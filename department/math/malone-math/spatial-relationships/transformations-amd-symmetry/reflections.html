Reflections

A reflection flips a figure across a line so that corresponding points lie the same perpendicular distance from that line on opposite sides. The line of reflection acts like a geometric mirror. Reflections preserve distance, angle measure, shape, and size, but unlike translations and rotations, they reverse orientation.

The Central Idea

A reflection maps every point to a mirror-image point across a fixed line.

That fixed line is called the line of reflection.

If a point \(P\) reflects to \(P'\) across a line \(\ell\), then \(\ell\) is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the point to its image:

\[ \boxed{\ell\text{ is the perpendicular bisector of }\overline{PP'}}. \]

Preimage and Image

The original figure is the preimage, and the reflected figure is the image.

If triangle \(ABC\) reflects to triangle \(A'B'C'\), then

\[ A\mapsto A',\qquad B\mapsto B',\qquad C\mapsto C'. \]

The Line of Reflection

The line of reflection plays a special role.

Every point on that line remains fixed:

\[ \boxed{P\in\ell\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P'=P}. \]

Points not on the line move to the opposite side by an equal perpendicular distance.

The Perpendicular-Bisector Condition

Suppose \(P\) reflects to \(P'\) across \(\ell\).

Then two facts hold:

  1. \(\overline{PP'}\) is perpendicular to \(\ell\);
  2. the midpoint of \(\overline{PP'}\) lies on \(\ell\).

Together, these conditions mean that \(\ell\) is the perpendicular bisector of \(\overline{PP'}\).

Equal Distance from the Mirror Line

If \(M\) is the midpoint of \(\overline{PP'}\), then

\[ PM=P'M. \]

Since \(M\) lies on the line of reflection, the preimage and image are equally far from the mirror line.

Reflection therefore creates bilateral balance across that line.

Reflection Across the \(x\)-Axis

Reflecting across the \(x\)-axis changes the sign of the \(y\)-coordinate:

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)}. \]

The horizontal coordinate remains unchanged because the reflection occurs vertically across the horizontal axis.

Example: Reflect Across the \(x\)-Axis

Reflect

\[ P=(-3,5) \]

across the \(x\)-axis.

Apply

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x,-y). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(-3,-5)}. \]

Reflection Across the \(y\)-Axis

Reflecting across the \(y\)-axis changes the sign of the \(x\)-coordinate:

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)}. \]

Example: Reflect Across the \(y\)-Axis

Reflect

\[ P=(4,-2) \]

across the \(y\)-axis.

Then

\[ \boxed{P'=(-4,-2)}. \]

Reflection Across the Line \(y=x\)

Reflecting across the line

\[ y=x \]

swaps the coordinates:

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)}. \]

This makes sense because points on \(y=x\) already have equal coordinates and therefore remain fixed.

Example: Reflect Across \(y=x\)

Reflect

\[ P=(2,-5) \]

across

\[ y=x. \]

Swap the coordinates:

\[ \boxed{P'=(-5,2)}. \]

Reflection Across the Line \(y=-x\)

Reflecting across

\[ y=-x \]

swaps the coordinates and changes both signs:

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(-y,-x)}. \]

The Four Standard Coordinate Reflections

The most common coordinate rules are:

\[ \boxed{ \text{\(x\)-axis: }(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y) } \] \[ \boxed{ \text{\(y\)-axis: }(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y) } \] \[ \boxed{ y=x:\ (x,y)\mapsto(y,x) } \] \[ \boxed{ y=-x:\ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,-x) }. \]

Reflection Across a Vertical Line

Consider the vertical line

\[ x=h. \]

A point \((x,y)\) reflected across this line keeps the same \(y\)-coordinate.

Its new \(x\)-coordinate is equally far from \(h\) on the other side:

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(2h-x,\ y)}. \]

Example: Reflect Across \(x=3\)

Reflect

\[ P=(7,-1) \]

across

\[ x=3. \]

Use

\[ x'=2(3)-7=-1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(-1,-1)}. \]

Reflection Across a Horizontal Line

Consider the horizontal line

\[ y=k. \]

A reflected point keeps the same \(x\)-coordinate and satisfies

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x,\ 2k-y)}. \]

Example: Reflect Across \(y=-2\)

Reflect

\[ P=(4,5) \]

across

\[ y=-2. \]

Then

\[ y'=2(-2)-5=-9. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(4,-9)}. \]

Reflections Are Rigid Motions

A reflection preserves the distance between every pair of points.

If \(P\) and \(Q\) reflect to \(P'\) and \(Q'\), then

\[ \boxed{P'Q'=PQ}. \]

Therefore, the reflected image is congruent to the preimage.

Reflections Preserve Angle Measure

If

\[ \angle ABC \]

reflects to

\[ \angle A'B'C', \]

then

\[ \boxed{ m\angle ABC=m\angle A'B'C' }. \]

Reflections Reverse Orientation

Reflections differ fundamentally from translations and rotations because they reverse orientation.

If the vertices of a polygon are listed counterclockwise before reflection, they are listed clockwise afterward.

Reflection is therefore an orientation-reversing isometry.

What a Reflection Preserves

A reflection preserves:

  • distance;
  • segment length;
  • angle measure;
  • collinearity;
  • betweenness;
  • parallelism;
  • perpendicularity;
  • area;
  • shape;
  • size.

It does not preserve orientation.

Fixed Points of a Reflection

Every point on the line of reflection is fixed.

Every point not on the line moves to a distinct mirror-image point.

Therefore, the set of fixed points of a plane reflection is exactly the line of reflection:

\[ \boxed{\text{Fix}(R)=\ell}. \]

Reflections of Lines

A line reflects to another line.

If the original line is the mirror line, it remains fixed as a set.

If a line is perpendicular to the mirror line, it also maps onto itself as a set, although most individual points on it move.

Other lines generally map to distinct lines.

Reflections Preserve Parallelism

If

\[ \ell\parallel m, \]

then after reflection,

\[ \boxed{\ell'\parallel m'}. \]

Although the directions may reverse in an oriented sense, the geometric relationship of parallelism remains unchanged.

Reflections Preserve Perpendicularity

If two lines meet at a right angle before reflection, their images also meet at a right angle:

\[ \ell\perp m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ell'\perp m'}. \]

Reflection as a Matrix Transformation

Reflections through lines passing through the origin can be represented by \(2\times2\) matrices.

For example, reflection across the \(x\)-axis is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Applying this matrix gives

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ -y \end{bmatrix}. \]

Matrix for Reflection Across the \(y\)-Axis

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} -1&0\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

This changes the sign of the \(x\)-coordinate while preserving the \(y\)-coordinate.

Matrix for Reflection Across \(y=x\)

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} 0&1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Multiplication by this matrix exchanges the coordinates.

Reflection Across a Line Through the Origin

Suppose the mirror line passes through the origin and makes an angle \(\theta\) with the positive \(x\)-axis.

Reflection across that line is represented by

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \cos2\theta&\sin2\theta\\ \sin2\theta&-\cos2\theta \end{bmatrix} }. \]

The appearance of \(2\theta\) reflects the geometry of turning toward the mirror line and then equally far to the other side.

Why the Reflection Matrix Has Determinant \(-1\)

For a reflection matrix \(M\),

\[ \det(M)=-1. \]

The absolute value

\[ |\det(M)|=1 \]

indicates that area is preserved.

The negative sign records the reversal of orientation.

This gives an algebraic signature of reflection.

Reflections Are Their Own Inverses

Reflecting twice across the same line returns every point to its starting position.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{R_\ell^{-1}=R_\ell} \]

and

\[ \boxed{R_\ell\circ R_\ell=I}. \]

Such a transformation is called an involution.

Example: Reflect Twice

Begin with

\[ P=(3,-4). \]

Reflect across the \(x\)-axis:

\[ (3,-4)\mapsto(3,4). \]

Reflect again across the \(x\)-axis:

\[ (3,4)\mapsto(3,-4). \]

Thus the original point is recovered.

Composing Two Reflections Across Intersecting Lines

Two reflections across intersecting lines combine to form a rotation.

If the mirror lines intersect at an angle \(\theta\), then the composition produces a rotation through

\[ \boxed{2\theta} \]

about their point of intersection.

The direction depends on the order of the reflections.

Example: Perpendicular Reflection Lines

Reflect across the \(x\)-axis and then across the \(y\)-axis.

The axes meet at

\[ 90^\circ. \]

Twice this angle is

\[ 180^\circ. \]

Therefore, the composition is a

\[ \boxed{180^\circ} \]

rotation about the origin.

Coordinate Check of Two Perpendicular Reflections

Begin with

\[ (x,y). \]

Reflect across the \(x\)-axis:

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x,-y). \]

Then reflect across the \(y\)-axis:

\[ (x,-y)\mapsto(-x,-y). \]

The result is exactly the coordinate rule for a \(180^\circ\) rotation:

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)}. \]

Composing Reflections Across Parallel Lines

Two reflections across distinct parallel lines combine to form a translation.

If the distance between the mirror lines is \(d\), the resulting translation has magnitude

\[ \boxed{2d}. \]

Its direction is perpendicular to the mirror lines and depends on the order of reflection.

Why Parallel Reflections Produce Translation

The first reflection moves a point across one mirror line.

The second reflection moves it across the parallel mirror line by an equal perpendicular rule.

Every point experiences the same net displacement, which is precisely the defining property of a translation.

Reflection Symmetry

A figure has reflection symmetry, or line symmetry, if reflection across some line maps the entire figure onto itself.

Such a line is called a line of symmetry.

Example: Isosceles Triangle

A non-equilateral isosceles triangle has one line of reflection symmetry.

That line passes through:

  • the vertex between the equal sides;
  • the midpoint of the base.

It is simultaneously an altitude, median, angle bisector, and perpendicular bisector of the base.

Example: Equilateral Triangle

An equilateral triangle has

\[ \boxed{3} \]

lines of reflection symmetry.

Each line passes through a vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.

Example: Square

A square has

\[ \boxed{4} \]

lines of reflection symmetry:

  • two through opposite vertices;
  • two through midpoints of opposite sides.

Regular Polygons

Every regular \(n\)-gon has

\[ \boxed{n} \]

lines of reflection symmetry.

The exact placement of those lines depends on whether \(n\) is even or odd, but the total number is always \(n\).

Reflection Symmetry and Functions

Graph symmetry can often be interpreted through reflection.

A graph symmetric about the \(y\)-axis satisfies

\[ \boxed{f(-x)=f(x)}. \]

Such functions are called even functions.

Origin Symmetry Is Not Reflection Symmetry

A graph satisfying

\[ f(-x)=-f(x) \]

has symmetry about the origin.

This is equivalent to a \(180^\circ\) rotation, not a reflection across a line.

Such functions are called odd functions.

Reflecting Function Graphs Across the \(x\)-Axis

If

\[ y=f(x), \]

reflection across the \(x\)-axis gives

\[ \boxed{y=-f(x)}. \]

Every output changes sign.

Reflecting Function Graphs Across the \(y\)-Axis

Reflection across the \(y\)-axis gives

\[ \boxed{y=f(-x)}. \]

Every input is replaced by its opposite.

Reflection Across \(y=x\) and Inverse Relations

Reflecting the graph of a relation across

\[ y=x \]

exchanges the roles of \(x\) and \(y\).

Thus, if

\[ y=f(x), \]

swapping coordinates gives the inverse relation.

When the inverse relation is itself a function, its graph is the reflection of the original function across \(y=x\).

Reflections of Circles

A reflection sends a circle to another circle with the same radius.

The center of the image circle is simply the reflection of the original center.

If the mirror line passes through the center, the entire circle maps onto itself as a set.

Reflections Preserve Area and Volume

Since reflection is an isometry, it preserves planar area.

In three dimensions, reflection across a plane also preserves volume.

The object changes handedness or orientation, but not magnitude of area or volume.

Reflection in Three Dimensions

In the plane, reflection occurs across a line.

In three dimensions, reflection commonly occurs across a plane.

For example, reflection across the \(xy\)-plane sends

\[ \boxed{(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y,-z)}. \]

Other Coordinate-Plane Reflections in Space

Reflection across the \(yz\)-plane:

\[ \boxed{(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,z)}. \]

Reflection across the \(xz\)-plane:

\[ \boxed{(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,z)}. \]

Each rule changes the sign of the coordinate perpendicular to the mirror plane.

Reflections and Handedness

In three dimensions, reflection reverses handedness.

A right-handed coordinate arrangement becomes left-handed after an orientation-reversing reflection.

This idea becomes important in linear algebra, physics, chemistry, computer graphics, and the study of chirality.

Finding the Line of Reflection from Point Pairs

If \(P\mapsto P'\), then the mirror line must be the perpendicular bisector of

\[ \overline{PP'}. \]

If several point-image pairs are given, they should all determine the same perpendicular bisector when the transformation is a reflection.

Example: Recover the Reflection Line

Suppose

\[ P=(2,5) \]

reflects to

\[ P'=(2,-1). \]

The segment \(\overline{PP'}\) is vertical.

Its midpoint is

\[ \left(2,\frac{5+(-1)}{2}\right)=(2,2). \]

Its perpendicular bisector is therefore the horizontal line

\[ \boxed{y=2}. \]

Verifying a Reflection

To verify that a transformation is a reflection across a proposed line, check each corresponding point pair.

The proposed mirror line must:

  • pass through the midpoint of each point-image segment;
  • be perpendicular to each point-image segment.

If either condition fails, the proposed reflection is incorrect.

Common Error: Changing the Wrong Coordinate

Reflection across the \(x\)-axis changes \(y\), not \(x\):

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x,-y). \]

Reflection across the \(y\)-axis changes \(x\), not \(y\):

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,y). \]

It helps to remember that the coordinate perpendicular to the mirror axis changes sign.

Common Error: Confusing \(y=x\) with \(y=-x\)

Across

\[ y=x, \]

simply swap coordinates:

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(y,x). \]

Across

\[ y=-x, \]

swap and negate:

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,-x). \]

Common Error: Assuming Orientation Is Preserved

Reflection preserves shape and size, but reverses orientation.

A clockwise vertex order becomes counterclockwise, and vice versa.

This orientation reversal is one of the main structural differences between reflections and translations or rotations.

Common Error: Treating the Mirror Line as a Boundary

A reflection line is not merely a divider between two figures.

It must satisfy the precise perpendicular-bisector condition for every corresponding point pair.

Visual similarity alone is not enough.

Common Error: Moving Points Along the Mirror Line

Every point on the line of reflection is fixed.

If a proposed reflection moves a point that lies on the mirror line, the construction is incorrect.

Common Error: Reflecting by the Wrong Distance

A point and its image must lie equal perpendicular distances from the mirror line.

If \(P\) is \(4\) units from the mirror line, then \(P'\) must also be \(4\) units from it on the opposite side.

A Reliable Procedure for Standard Coordinate Reflections

  1. Identify the line of reflection.
  2. Choose the corresponding coordinate rule.
  3. Apply the same rule to every point.
  4. Check that points on the mirror line remain fixed.
  5. Check equal perpendicular distances to the mirror line.
  6. Verify that shape and size are preserved.
  7. Confirm that orientation is reversed.

A Reliable Construction Procedure

To reflect a point \(P\) across a line \(\ell\):

  1. draw the perpendicular from \(P\) to \(\ell\);
  2. let \(M\) be the point where that perpendicular meets \(\ell\);
  3. continue along the same perpendicular to the opposite side;
  4. locate \(P'\) so that \(MP'=MP\);
  5. repeat for the remaining points of the figure.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding the Mirror Line

  1. Choose a preimage point and its image.
  2. Find the midpoint of the segment joining them.
  3. Find the slope or direction of that segment.
  4. Construct the perpendicular line through the midpoint.
  5. Repeat with another point-image pair.
  6. Confirm that the same line results.

Example: Full Coordinate Workflow

Let

\[ A=(1,2),\qquad B=(4,2),\qquad C=(2,5). \]

Reflect the triangle across the \(y\)-axis.

Use

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,y). \]

Then

\[ A'=(-1,2), \] \[ B'=(-4,2), \] \[ C'=(-2,5). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ A'=(-1,2),\quad B'=(-4,2),\quad C'=(-2,5) }. \]

Example: Verify the Mirror Condition

For

\[ A=(1,2) \]

and

\[ A'=(-1,2), \]

the midpoint is

\[ \left(\frac{1+(-1)}{2},\frac{2+2}{2}\right)=(0,2). \]

This midpoint lies on the \(y\)-axis.

The segment \(\overline{AA'}\) is horizontal, so it is perpendicular to the vertical \(y\)-axis.

Thus the perpendicular-bisector condition is satisfied.

What to Remember

A reflection flips a figure across a mirror line.

For every point \(P\) and image \(P'\),

\[ \boxed{ \text{the mirror line is the perpendicular bisector of }\overline{PP'} }. \]

The essential facts are:

  • points on the mirror line remain fixed;
  • points and images lie equal perpendicular distances from the mirror line;
  • reflections preserve distance and angle measure;
  • reflections preserve shape, size, parallelism, and perpendicularity;
  • reflections reverse orientation;
  • every reflection is its own inverse;
  • two intersecting reflections compose to a rotation;
  • two parallel reflections compose to a translation;
  • reflection symmetry means a figure maps onto itself across a mirror line;
  • reflection matrices have determinant \(-1\).

The most useful structural habit is: find the mirror line and verify the perpendicular-bisector relationship for corresponding points.
