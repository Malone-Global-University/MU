Similarity Transformations

A similarity transformation changes a figure in a way that preserves its shape while allowing its size and position to change. Rigid motions preserve distance exactly, while dilations multiply every distance by one common scale factor. Combining these ideas produces the full family of Euclidean similarity transformations and gives a transformation-based meaning to similar figures.

The Central Idea

A similarity transformation multiplies every distance by the same positive factor.

If a transformation \(S\) sends

\[ P\mapsto P' \qquad\text{and}\qquad Q\mapsto Q', \]

then there is a constant

\[ k>0 \]

such that

\[ \boxed{P'Q'=k\,PQ} \]

for every pair of points \(P,Q\).

The Similarity Ratio

The constant

\[ k \]

is called the similarity ratio or scale factor.

It measures how all lengths change under the transformation.

  • \(k>1\): enlargement;
  • \(0
  • \(k=1\): rigid motion.

Rigid Motions Are Similarities

A rigid motion preserves every distance:

\[ P'Q'=PQ. \]

Therefore, every rigid motion is a similarity transformation with

\[ \boxed{k=1}. \]

Dilations Are Similarities

A dilation multiplies all lengths by the magnitude of its scale factor.

Therefore, a dilation with scale factor \(k>0\) satisfies

\[ \boxed{P'Q'=k\,PQ}. \]

Dilations are the basic non-rigid similarity transformations.

Similarity Transformations Combine Dilation and Rigid Motion

A general plane similarity transformation can be understood as a dilation combined with one or more rigid motions.

Conceptually:

\[ \boxed{ \text{similarity transformation} = \text{dilation} + \text{isometry} }. \]

The rigid-motion part may translate, rotate, or reflect the figure, while the dilation controls size.

What Similarity Transformations Preserve

Similarity transformations preserve:

  • angle measure;
  • collinearity;
  • parallelism;
  • perpendicularity;
  • shape;
  • ratios of corresponding lengths;
  • ratios of lengths within the same figure.

They do not generally preserve actual distance, perimeter, area, or volume.

Angle Measure Is Preserved

If

\[ \angle ABC \]

maps to

\[ \angle A'B'C', \]

then

\[ \boxed{ m\angle ABC=m\angle A'B'C' }. \]

This is one of the defining geometric features of similar figures.

Corresponding Lengths Are Proportional

If a similarity transformation has ratio \(k\), then every corresponding segment satisfies

\[ \boxed{ \frac{A'B'}{AB}=k }. \]

The same ratio appears for every corresponding pair of lengths.

Similar Figures

Two figures are similar if one can be mapped onto the other by a similarity transformation.

For triangles,

\[ \boxed{ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle A'B'C' } \]

means corresponding angles are equal and corresponding side lengths are proportional.

Similarity as Transformation

The transformation viewpoint gives a compact definition:

\[ \boxed{ F\sim G \iff \text{some similarity transformation maps }F\text{ onto }G }. \]

This unifies the usual proportional-side and equal-angle conditions.

Congruence Is a Special Case of Similarity

Congruent figures have the same shape and the same size.

Similar figures have the same shape but may differ in size.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{congruence} \subset \text{similarity} }. \]

Congruence corresponds to similarity ratio

\[ k=1. \]

Example: Dilation Followed by Translation

Begin with

\[ P=(2,1). \]

First dilate about the origin by

\[ k=3. \]

This gives

\[ (6,3). \]

Then translate by

\[ \langle -4,2\rangle. \]

The final image is

\[ \boxed{P'=(2,5)}. \]

The Combined Coordinate Rule

If we dilate about the origin by \(k\) and then translate by

\[ \langle a,b\rangle, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(kx+a,\ ky+b) }. \]

The similarity ratio is

\[ |k|. \]

Dilation Followed by Rotation

A dilation about the origin by \(k\), followed by a rotation through \(\theta\), gives

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} x'\\ y' \end{bmatrix} = k \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Every length is multiplied by \(|k|\), while every direction is rotated by \(\theta\).

Dilation Followed by Reflection

A dilation may also be combined with reflection.

For example, reflect across the \(x\)-axis after dilating by \(k\):

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(kx,-ky) }. \]

This preserves angles and length ratios while reversing orientation.

Direct and Opposite Similarities

Similarity transformations can be divided by orientation.

  • direct similarities preserve orientation;
  • opposite similarities reverse orientation.

A dilation followed by a translation or rotation is direct.

A dilation combined with a reflection is opposite.

Orientation and the Sign of a Determinant

For a linear similarity transformation in the plane, the determinant helps identify orientation.

If the scale factor is \(k>0\), then the absolute value of the determinant is

\[ \boxed{k^2}. \]

A positive determinant preserves orientation, while a negative determinant reverses orientation.

Matrix Form of a Linear Similarity

A linear similarity fixing the origin can be written

\[ \boxed{A=kQ} \]

where \(k>0\) and \(Q\) is an orthogonal matrix.

The matrix \(Q\) performs a rotation or reflection, while \(k\) performs the uniform scaling.

The Matrix Test for Similarity

If

\[ A=kQ \]

with

\[ Q^TQ=I, \]

then

\[ A^TA = k^2Q^TQ = k^2I. \]

Thus a linear transformation is a similarity when

\[ \boxed{A^TA=k^2I} \]

for some \(k>0\).

Why This Matrix Condition Works

For a vector \(\mathbf{v}\),

\[ \|A\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \mathbf{v}^TA^TA\mathbf{v}. \]

If

\[ A^TA=k^2I, \]

then

\[ \|A\mathbf{v}\|^2 = k^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \|A\mathbf{v}\|=k\|\mathbf{v}\| }. \]

Every length is scaled by the same factor.

The General Affine Similarity Form

A plane similarity transformation can be written

\[ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{x})=kQ\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} } \]

where:

  • \(k>0\) is the similarity ratio;
  • \(Q\) is orthogonal;
  • \(\mathbf{b}\) is a translation vector.

Translation Does Not Change the Scale Factor

Suppose

\[ T(\mathbf{x})=kQ\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}. \]

For two points \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\),

\[ T(\mathbf{x})-T(\mathbf{y}) = kQ(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}). \]

The translation vector cancels.

Therefore, the distance scale depends only on \(kQ\).

Example: Identify the Similarity Ratio from a Rule

Consider

\[ T(x,y)=(-2y+5,\ 2x-3). \]

The linear part is

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 0&-2\\ 2&0 \end{bmatrix} = 2 \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The second matrix is a \(90^\circ\) rotation.

Therefore, the transformation is a similarity with ratio

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

Finding the Similarity Ratio from Corresponding Sides

If

\[ AB \]

corresponds to

\[ A'B', \]

then

\[ \boxed{ k=\frac{A'B'}{AB} }. \]

The same ratio must hold for every corresponding side.

Example: Similarity Ratio

Suppose

\[ AB=8 \]

and

\[ A'B'=20. \]

Then

\[ k=\frac{20}{8}=\frac52. \]

Therefore, the similarity ratio is

\[ \boxed{\frac52}. \]

Perimeter Under Similarity

Every length is multiplied by \(k\), so perimeter also scales by \(k\).

If the original perimeter is \(P\), then

\[ \boxed{P'=kP}. \]

Area Under Similarity

Area scales by the square of the similarity ratio:

\[ \boxed{A'=k^2A}. \]

Therefore, similar figures with side-length ratio \(k\) have area ratio

\[ \boxed{k^2}. \]

Volume Under Similarity

In three dimensions, volume scales by

\[ \boxed{k^3}. \]

Therefore, similar solids with linear scale ratio \(k\) have volume ratio

\[ \boxed{k^3}. \]

Example: Area Ratio

Two similar polygons have similarity ratio

\[ k=3. \]

Their area ratio is

\[ 3^2=9. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\frac{A'}{A}=9}. \]

Example: Volume Ratio

Two similar solids have linear scale ratio

\[ k=4. \]

Their volume ratio is

\[ 4^3=64. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\frac{V'}{V}=64}. \]

Similarity Preserves Ratios Within a Figure

Suppose two lengths \(a\) and \(b\) in the original figure map to

\[ ka \quad\text{and}\quad kb. \]

Then

\[ \frac{ka}{kb} = \frac{a}{b}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{a'}{b'}=\frac{a}{b} }. \]

Similarity Preserves Parallelism

Rigid motions preserve parallelism, and dilations preserve parallelism.

Therefore, their compositions also preserve parallelism:

\[ \ell\parallel m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ell'\parallel m'}. \]

Similarity Preserves Perpendicularity

Since similarity transformations preserve angle measure,

\[ 90^\circ \]

remains

\[ 90^\circ. \]

Thus,

\[ \ell\perp m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ell'\perp m'}. \]

Similarity Preserves Collinearity

Straight lines map to straight lines under Euclidean similarity transformations.

Therefore, collinear points remain collinear.

Similarity Preserves Shape, Not Necessarily Size

The central distinction between similarity and congruence is size.

Similarity preserves all angle relationships and all proportional length relationships, but it permits one common change of scale.

Composition of Similarity Transformations

The composition of two similarity transformations is another similarity transformation.

If the first has ratio

\[ k_1 \]

and the second has ratio

\[ k_2, \]

then the composition has ratio

\[ \boxed{k_1k_2}. \]

Why Similarity Ratios Multiply

Suppose an original length is \(L\).

After the first transformation,

\[ L_1=k_1L. \]

After the second,

\[ L_2=k_2L_1. \]

Therefore,

\[ L_2=k_2k_1L. \]

Hence the combined ratio is

\[ \boxed{k_1k_2}. \]

Inverse Similarity Transformations

Every nondegenerate similarity transformation has an inverse.

If the similarity ratio is \(k\), then the inverse has similarity ratio

\[ \boxed{\frac1k}. \]

The inverse also reverses any translation, rotation, or reflection components in the appropriate order.

Example: Inverse Similarity Ratio

If a similarity enlarges all lengths by

\[ \frac52, \]

then the inverse reduces them by

\[ \boxed{\frac25}. \]

Order of Composition Still Matters

Similarity transformations do not generally commute.

For example, a dilation about one center followed by a translation may differ from the same translation followed by the dilation.

Always interpret

\[ S_2\circ S_1 \]

as applying \(S_1\) first and \(S_2\) second.

Example: Dilation and Translation Do Not Always Commute

Begin with

\[ P=(1,0). \]

Dilate by \(2\) about the origin, then translate right by \(3\):

\[ (1,0)\mapsto(2,0)\mapsto(5,0). \]

Reverse the order:

\[ (1,0)\mapsto(4,0)\mapsto(8,0). \]

Since

\[ 5\neq8, \]

the transformations do not commute.

Similarity Transformations and Triangle Similarity

Classical triangle similarity criteria can be interpreted through transformations.

If two triangles satisfy an appropriate similarity criterion, one can be mapped to the other using a dilation together with rigid motions.

AA Similarity

If two angles of one triangle equal two angles of another, then the third angles are also equal.

The triangles have the same shape and differ only by scale and rigid positioning.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{AA}\Longrightarrow\text{similar triangles}}. \]

SAS Similarity

If two pairs of corresponding sides are proportional and the included angles are equal, then the triangles are similar.

Symbolically,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{AB}{A'B'} = \frac{AC}{A'C'} \quad\text{and}\quad \angle A\cong\angle A' }. \]

SSS Similarity

If all three pairs of corresponding sides are proportional, then the triangles are similar.

\[ \boxed{ \frac{AB}{A'B'} = \frac{BC}{B'C'} = \frac{CA}{C'A'} }. \]

Transformation Proof of Similarity

To prove two figures similar using transformations, one useful strategy is:

  1. apply a dilation to match size;
  2. apply rigid motions to match position and orientation;
  3. show the transformed figure coincides with the target figure.

This directly demonstrates the existence of a similarity transformation.

Example: Mapping One Triangle to Another

Suppose

\[ A=(0,0),\quad B=(2,0),\quad C=(0,3) \]

and

\[ A'=(4,1),\quad B'=(8,1),\quad C'=(4,7). \]

First dilate the original triangle about the origin by

\[ k=2. \]

This gives

\[ (0,0),\quad(4,0),\quad(0,6). \]

Then translate by

\[ \langle4,1\rangle. \]

The vertices become

\[ (4,1),\quad(8,1),\quad(4,7). \]

Therefore, the triangles are similar with ratio

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

Finding a Similarity Center in a Pure Dilation

When the similarity transformation is a dilation alone, corresponding point pairs lie on lines through one center.

The center can be found by intersecting lines such as

\[ AA' \quad\text{and}\quad BB'. \]

More general similarities need not have this simple center structure.

Spiral Similarity: A Gentle Preview

A dilation and rotation about the same center can be combined into a transformation sometimes called a spiral similarity.

It simultaneously changes size and direction.

In complex-number notation, such a transformation about the origin has the form

\[ \boxed{z'=k e^{i\theta}z}. \]

Similarity in the Complex Plane

In the complex plane, a direct similarity can be written

\[ \boxed{z'=az+b} \]

with

\[ a\neq0. \]

The magnitude

\[ |a| \]

gives the similarity ratio, while the argument of \(a\) gives the rotation angle.

Opposite Similarities in the Complex Plane

Orientation-reversing similarities may be represented using complex conjugation:

\[ \boxed{z'=a\overline{z}+b}. \]

The conjugation contributes reflection, while multiplication by \(a\) controls scale and rotation.

Similarity in Three Dimensions

The same principle extends to space.

A three-dimensional similarity transformation scales every distance by one common factor and may combine that scaling with rigid motion.

Lengths scale by \(k\), areas by \(k^2\), and volumes by \(k^3\).

Similarity and Scale Models

Scale drawings and physical models rely on similarity transformations.

If a model uses scale ratio

\[ k=\frac{1}{100}, \]

then every model length is one hundredth of the corresponding real length.

Angles remain unchanged.

Similarity and Maps

Maps use a constant ratio between map distances and actual distances.

For a map scale of

\[ 1:50{,}000, \]

one unit on the map represents \(50{,}000\) of the same units on the ground.

This is similarity reasoning expressed through scale.

Similarity and Photography

Resizing an image uniformly preserves its proportions.

If both horizontal and vertical dimensions are multiplied by the same factor, the result is a similarity transformation in the geometric sense.

Nonuniform resizing changes proportions and is not a Euclidean similarity.

Uniform Versus Nonuniform Scaling

The rule

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(3x,3y) \]

is a similarity transformation.

The rule

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(3x,2y) \]

is not, because horizontal and vertical distances scale differently.

Angles and shape may be distorted.

One Counterexample Can Disprove Similarity

To disprove that a transformation is a similarity, it is enough to find two pairs of segments whose length ratios are different.

A true similarity must use one common scale factor throughout.

Common Error: Assuming Equal Angles Mean Congruence

Equal corresponding angles establish shape, not necessarily size.

Two triangles may have equal corresponding angles but side lengths in a ratio of

\[ 2:1. \]

They are similar, not congruent.

Common Error: Using Different Scale Factors for Different Sides

A similarity transformation uses one common scale factor.

If one side doubles while another triples, the transformation is not a Euclidean similarity.

Common Error: Confusing Area Ratio with Length Ratio

If the length ratio is

\[ k, \]

then the area ratio is

\[ \boxed{k^2}, \]

not \(k\).

Common Error: Confusing Volume Ratio with Length Ratio

For similar solids, if the length ratio is

\[ k, \]

then the volume ratio is

\[ \boxed{k^3}. \]

Common Error: Assuming Similarity Preserves Orientation

Some similarity transformations preserve orientation and others reverse it.

A dilation combined with reflection still preserves shape and scale ratios, but reverses clockwise and counterclockwise order.

Common Error: Assuming Similarity Preserves Distance

Only similarities with

\[ k=1 \]

preserve distance exactly.

General similarities preserve proportional distance, not equal distance.

A Reliable Procedure for Identifying Similar Figures

  1. Match corresponding vertices or features.
  2. Compare corresponding angles.
  3. Compute ratios of corresponding lengths.
  4. Verify that one common ratio works throughout.
  5. Check whether a sequence of dilation and rigid motions can map one figure onto the other.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding a Similarity Ratio

  1. Choose one pair of corresponding nonzero lengths.
  2. Divide image length by preimage length.
  3. Use the result as the candidate ratio \(k\).
  4. Test the same ratio on additional corresponding lengths.
  5. Reject similarity if the ratios disagree.

A Reliable Procedure for Coordinate Similarities

  1. Identify the dilation or scale component.
  2. Identify any rotation, reflection, or translation component.
  3. Apply transformations in the stated order.
  4. Track corresponding points consistently.
  5. Verify that all distances scale by one common factor.
  6. Check whether orientation is preserved or reversed.

A Reliable Procedure for Matrix Similarities

  1. Separate the linear part from any translation.
  2. Compute \(A^TA\).
  3. Check whether the result equals \(k^2I\) for some \(k>0\).
  4. If so, the linear part is a similarity with ratio \(k\).
  5. Use the determinant sign to determine orientation.

Example: Full Coordinate Workflow

Let

\[ A=(1,1),\qquad B=(3,1),\qquad C=(1,4). \]

First dilate by

\[ k=2 \]

about the origin.

This gives

\[ A_1=(2,2), \] \[ B_1=(6,2), \] \[ C_1=(2,8). \]

Then translate by

\[ \langle -1,3\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ A'=(1,5), \] \[ B'=(5,5), \] \[ C'=(1,11). \]

The final triangle is similar to the original with ratio

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

Example: Verify the Ratio

In the previous example,

\[ AB=2. \]

After the similarity transformation,

\[ A'B'=4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{A'B'}{AB} = \frac42 = 2. \]

This agrees with the dilation scale factor.

What to Remember

A similarity transformation multiplies every distance by one common factor:

\[ \boxed{ d(S(P),S(Q))=k\,d(P,Q) }. \]

The essential facts are:

  • similarity transformations preserve angle measure;
  • all corresponding lengths scale by one common factor \(k\);
  • rigid motions are similarities with \(k=1\);
  • dilations are the basic non-rigid similarities;
  • general similarities combine dilation with rigid motion;
  • perimeters scale by \(k\);
  • areas scale by \(k^2\);
  • volumes scale by \(k^3\);
  • compositions multiply similarity ratios;
  • inverse similarities use reciprocal ratios;
  • similar figures are exactly figures related by a similarity transformation.

The most important structural principle is: similarity preserves shape by applying one common scale to all distances while preserving every angle.
