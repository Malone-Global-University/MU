Rotations

A rotation turns a figure around a fixed point called the center of rotation. Every point moves through the same angle, while its distance from the center remains unchanged. Rotations preserve shape, size, angle measure, distance, and orientation, making them rigid motions and one of the fundamental transformations of geometry.

The Central Idea

A rotation turns every point of a figure around a fixed point by the same angle.

The fixed point is the center of rotation.

If a point \(P\) rotates to \(P'\) around a center \(C\), then

\[ \boxed{CP=CP'} \]

and the directed angle from \(CP\) to \(CP'\) is the angle of rotation.

Three Pieces Determine a Rotation

A rotation is determined by three pieces of information:

  1. the center of rotation;
  2. the magnitude of the angle;
  3. the direction of the turn.

In the plane, the direction is usually described as counterclockwise or clockwise.

Positive and Negative Rotation

By the standard mathematical convention,

  • counterclockwise rotation is positive;
  • clockwise rotation is negative.

Thus,

\[ 90^\circ \]

means a \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise turn, while

\[ -90^\circ \]

means a \(90^\circ\) clockwise turn.

Rotations and Directed Angles

Because rotation has direction, its angle is naturally treated as a directed angle.

For example,

\[ 270^\circ \]

counterclockwise and

\[ -90^\circ \]

clockwise produce the same final orientation.

More generally, rotation angles that differ by an integer multiple of a full turn are equivalent:

\[ \boxed{\theta\sim\theta+360^\circ k} \]

for any integer \(k\).

Rotations in Radians

Rotations may also be measured in radians.

Important equivalences include

\[ 90^\circ=\frac{\pi}{2}, \] \[ 180^\circ=\pi, \] \[ 270^\circ=\frac{3\pi}{2}, \] \[ 360^\circ=2\pi. \]

The geometric meaning is the same whether the angle is measured in degrees or radians.

Preimage and Image

As with other transformations, the original figure is the preimage and the resulting figure is the image.

If triangle \(ABC\) rotates to triangle \(A'B'C'\), then

\[ A\mapsto A',\qquad B\mapsto B',\qquad C\mapsto C'. \]

The Center Stays Fixed

The center of rotation is a fixed point.

If the rotation has center \(C\), then

\[ \boxed{C\mapsto C}. \]

This distinguishes a nontrivial rotation from a nonzero translation, which has no fixed points.

Every Point Stays the Same Distance from the Center

If \(P\) rotates to \(P'\) around \(C\), then

\[ CP=CP'. \]

Therefore, \(P\) and \(P'\) lie on the same circle centered at \(C\).

A rotation may be understood as moving each point along such a circle by the same angular amount.

The Angle Condition

If \(P\) rotates to \(P'\) through an angle \(\theta\) about \(C\), then

\[ \boxed{\angle PCP'=\theta} \]

when the angle is interpreted with the specified direction.

Both the distance condition and the angle condition are needed to determine the image point.

Rotation About the Origin

Coordinate rotations are especially simple when the center is the origin.

A point

\[ P=(x,y) \]

can be viewed as the position vector

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix}. \]

Rotating this vector changes its direction while preserving its length.

A \(90^\circ\) Counterclockwise Rotation

Rotating

\[ (x,y) \]

\(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about the origin gives

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x)}. \]

The old \(y\)-coordinate becomes the new negative \(x\)-coordinate, and the old \(x\)-coordinate becomes the new \(y\)-coordinate.

Example: \(90^\circ\) Counterclockwise

Rotate

\[ P=(4,1) \]

\(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about the origin.

Apply

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,x). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(-1,4)}. \]

A \(180^\circ\) Rotation

Rotating a point \(180^\circ\) about the origin gives

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)}. \]

Both coordinates change sign.

Geometrically, the image lies directly opposite the original point across the origin.

Example: \(180^\circ\)

Rotate

\[ P=(-3,5) \]

\(180^\circ\) about the origin.

\[ (-3,5)\mapsto(3,-5). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{P'=(3,-5)}. \]

A \(270^\circ\) Counterclockwise Rotation

Rotating

\[ (x,y) \]

\(270^\circ\) counterclockwise is equivalent to rotating \(90^\circ\) clockwise.

The coordinate rule is

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(y,-x)}. \]

The Three Standard Quarter-Turn Rules

About the origin:

\[ \boxed{ 90^\circ:\ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,x) } \] \[ \boxed{ 180^\circ:\ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y) } \] \[ \boxed{ 270^\circ:\ (x,y)\mapsto(y,-x) } \]

These rules are worth recognizing quickly, but they are consequences of a more general rotation formula.

The General Rotation Formula

A counterclockwise rotation by an angle \(\theta\) about the origin sends

\[ (x,y) \]

to

\[ \boxed{ \left( x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta,\ x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta \right) }. \]

This formula works for any real angle \(\theta\).

The Rotation Matrix

The general formula can be written using matrix multiplication:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} x'\\ y' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} }. \]

The matrix

\[ R_\theta= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \]

is called a rotation matrix.

Recovering the \(90^\circ\) Rule from the Matrix

For

\[ \theta=90^\circ, \]

we have

\[ \cos90^\circ=0,\qquad \sin90^\circ=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ R_{90^\circ} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Applying this matrix gives

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -y\\ x \end{bmatrix}, \]

which reproduces

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x)}. \]

Why the General Formula Works

Write a point in polar form:

\[ x=r\cos\phi,\qquad y=r\sin\phi. \]

Rotating by \(\theta\) changes the angle from \(\phi\) to \(\phi+\theta\), while \(r\) remains unchanged.

Thus,

\[ x'=r\cos(\phi+\theta), \] \[ y'=r\sin(\phi+\theta). \]

Using the angle-addition identities produces

\[ x'=x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta, \] \[ y'=x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta. \]

The coordinate formula is therefore a direct expression of circular motion.

Example: Rotate by \(60^\circ\)

Rotate

\[ P=(2,0) \]

\(60^\circ\) counterclockwise about the origin.

Use

\[ \cos60^\circ=\frac12,\qquad \sin60^\circ=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}. \]

Then

\[ x' = 2\left(\frac12\right) - 0\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = 1, \] \[ y' = 2\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) + 0\left(\frac12\right) = \sqrt3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(1,\sqrt3)}. \]

Rotations Preserve Distance from the Origin

Suppose

\[ P=(x,y) \]

rotates to

\[ P'=(x',y'). \]

A rotation about the origin preserves

\[ x^2+y^2. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{x'^2+y'^2=x^2+y^2}. \]

This is the coordinate form of the fact that the point remains on the same circle centered at the origin.

Rotations Are Rigid Motions

Rotations preserve distances between every pair of points.

If \(P\) and \(Q\) rotate to \(P'\) and \(Q'\), then

\[ \boxed{P'Q'=PQ}. \]

Therefore, every figure is congruent to its rotated image.

Rotations Preserve Angle Measure

If

\[ \angle ABC \]

rotates to

\[ \angle A'B'C', \]

then

\[ \boxed{ m\angle ABC=m\angle A'B'C' }. \]

This is another consequence of rotation being an isometry.

Rotations Preserve Orientation

A rotation turns a figure without reversing it.

If polygon vertices are listed counterclockwise before rotation, they remain counterclockwise afterward.

Rotations are therefore orientation-preserving rigid motions.

What a Rotation Preserves

Rotations preserve:

  • distance;
  • segment length;
  • angle measure;
  • collinearity;
  • betweenness;
  • parallelism;
  • perpendicularity;
  • area;
  • shape;
  • size;
  • orientation.

These are invariants of rotation.

Rotations of Lines

A rotation sends a line to another line.

Unlike a translation, the new line usually has a different direction.

If the original line passes through the center of rotation, then the image line also passes through the center.

A line not passing through the center may rotate to a parallel line only for special rotation angles such as \(180^\circ\).

Rotating Direction Vectors

Suppose a segment has direction vector

\[ \mathbf{v}. \]

Under a rotation by \(\theta\), the direction vector becomes

\[ \boxed{R_\theta\mathbf{v}}. \]

Its magnitude stays unchanged, but its direction turns by \(\theta\).

Rotation About a Point Other Than the Origin

Suppose the center of rotation is

\[ C=(h,k). \]

To rotate a point around \(C\):

  1. translate the center to the origin;
  2. rotate about the origin;
  3. translate back.

This translate-rotate-translate-back strategy is fundamental.

The General Formula About \((h,k)\)

Begin with

\[ P=(x,y). \]

Relative to the center \((h,k)\), the point is

\[ (x-h,\ y-k). \]

Rotate that vector by \(\theta\), then add the center back:

\[ \boxed{ x' = h+(x-h)\cos\theta-(y-k)\sin\theta } \] \[ \boxed{ y' = k+(x-h)\sin\theta+(y-k)\cos\theta }. \]

Example: \(90^\circ\) About a Non-Origin Center

Rotate

\[ P=(5,3) \]

\(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about

\[ C=(2,1). \]

First translate relative to the center:

\[ P-C=(3,2). \]

Rotate \(90^\circ\):

\[ (3,2)\mapsto(-2,3). \]

Add the center back:

\[ (-2,3)+(2,1)=(0,4). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(0,4)}. \]

Checking the Center-Distance Condition

In the previous example,

\[ C=(2,1),\qquad P=(5,3),\qquad P'=(0,4). \]

Before rotation,

\[ CP=\sqrt{3^2+2^2}=\sqrt{13}. \]

After rotation,

\[ CP'=\sqrt{(-2)^2+3^2}=\sqrt{13}. \]

The distance from the center is preserved, as required.

Finding the Center of Rotation

If a point \(P\) maps to \(P'\) under a nontrivial rotation, then the center must be equally distant from both points.

Therefore, the center lies on the perpendicular bisector of

\[ \overline{PP'}. \]

Using two corresponding point pairs usually allows the center to be found as the intersection of two perpendicular bisectors.

Why Perpendicular Bisectors Locate the Center

If \(C\) is the center and \(P\mapsto P'\), then

\[ CP=CP'. \]

Every point equidistant from \(P\) and \(P'\) lies on the perpendicular bisector of \(\overline{PP'}\).

Hence the center must lie on that perpendicular bisector.

Finding the Angle of Rotation

Once the center \(C\) is known, the rotation angle can be found from the angle between

\[ \overrightarrow{CP} \]

and

\[ \overrightarrow{CP'}. \]

The direction of the turn determines whether the directed angle is positive or negative.

The Identity Rotation

A rotation through

\[ 0^\circ \]

changes nothing.

Likewise, a full turn of

\[ 360^\circ \]

returns every point to its original position.

Both represent the identity transformation:

\[ \boxed{R_0=R_{360^\circ}=I}. \]

Inverse Rotations

A rotation can be undone by rotating through the opposite angle about the same center.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{R_\theta^{-1}=R_{-\theta}}. \]

For example, the inverse of a \(70^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation is a \(70^\circ\) clockwise rotation.

Composing Rotations About the Same Center

If two rotations have the same center, their angles add.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ R_\beta\circ R_\alpha = R_{\alpha+\beta} }. \]

Angles may then be reduced modulo \(360^\circ\), or modulo \(2\pi\) in radians.

Example: Composition About the Same Center

Rotate \(40^\circ\) counterclockwise, then \(125^\circ\) counterclockwise about the same center.

The combined angle is

\[ 40^\circ+125^\circ=165^\circ. \]

Therefore, the composition is a single

\[ \boxed{165^\circ} \]

counterclockwise rotation about the same center.

Matrix Composition

Rotation matrices satisfy

\[ \boxed{R_\alpha R_\beta=R_{\alpha+\beta}}. \]

This matrix identity mirrors the geometric fact that rotations about the same center combine by angle addition.

It also reflects the trigonometric angle-addition formulas.

Rotations About the Same Center Commute

Since

\[ \alpha+\beta=\beta+\alpha, \]

rotations about the same center satisfy

\[ \boxed{ R_\alpha\circ R_\beta = R_\beta\circ R_\alpha }. \]

Rotations about different centers do not generally commute.

Composing Rotations About Different Centers

The composition of two plane rotations with different centers is more subtle.

In many cases, the result is another rotation. If the two angles sum to a full-turn equivalent angle of \(0^\circ\), the result may instead be a translation.

This is an early example of how simple transformations can combine to produce another familiar transformation.

Half-Turns

A \(180^\circ\) rotation is often called a half-turn.

About a center \(C\), the center is the midpoint of every segment joining a point to its image.

If

\[ P\mapsto P', \]

then

\[ \boxed{C=\text{midpoint of }\overline{PP'}}. \]

Coordinate Rule for a Half-Turn About \((h,k)\)

A \(180^\circ\) rotation about

\[ C=(h,k) \]

sends

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(2h-x,\ 2k-y) }. \]

This follows immediately from the midpoint relationship.

Example: Half-Turn About \((3,-1)\)

Rotate

\[ P=(7,4) \]

\(180^\circ\) about

\[ C=(3,-1). \]

Use

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(2h-x,2k-y). \]

Then

\[ x'=6-7=-1, \] \[ y'=-2-4=-6. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(-1,-6)}. \]

Rotational Symmetry

A figure has rotational symmetry if some rotation smaller than a full turn maps the figure onto itself.

The center of that rotation is called a center of rotational symmetry.

Order of Rotational Symmetry

The order of rotational symmetry is the number of times a figure matches itself during one full \(360^\circ\) turn.

If the smallest positive rotation angle that maps the figure onto itself is \(\theta\), then

\[ \boxed{ n=\frac{360^\circ}{\theta} } \]

provided \(\theta\) divides a full turn evenly.

Example: Equilateral Triangle

An equilateral triangle matches itself after rotations of

\[ 120^\circ,\qquad240^\circ,\qquad360^\circ. \]

Its smallest positive symmetry rotation is

\[ 120^\circ. \]

Therefore, its rotational symmetry has order

\[ \boxed{3}. \]

Example: Square

A square matches itself after

\[ 90^\circ,\qquad 180^\circ,\qquad 270^\circ,\qquad 360^\circ. \]

Its rotational symmetry has order

\[ \boxed{4}. \]

Regular Polygons

A regular \(n\)-gon has rotational symmetry of order

\[ \boxed{n}. \]

Its smallest positive rotational symmetry angle is

\[ \boxed{\frac{360^\circ}{n}}. \]

This follows from the equal angular spacing of its vertices around the center.

Rotation and the Unit Circle

The unit circle provides a natural model for rotation.

A point on the unit circle has coordinates

\[ (\cos\phi,\sin\phi). \]

Rotating by \(\theta\) produces

\[ \boxed{ (\cos(\phi+\theta),\sin(\phi+\theta)) }. \]

This connection links geometric transformation directly to trigonometry.

Rotation and Complex Numbers

In the complex plane, a point

\[ (x,y) \]

corresponds to

\[ z=x+iy. \]

Rotation by \(\theta\) about the origin can be performed by multiplying by

\[ e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{z'=e^{i\theta}z}. \]

This is a powerful later connection between geometry, algebra, and trigonometry.

Why Complex Multiplication Rotates

Write

\[ z=re^{i\phi}. \]

Then

\[ e^{i\theta}z = re^{i(\phi+\theta)}. \]

The magnitude \(r\) stays unchanged while the argument increases by \(\theta\).

That is exactly what a rotation does.

Rotation in Three Dimensions

In the plane, a rotation is described by a center and an angle.

In three dimensions, a rotation is generally described by an axis of rotation and an angle.

Points on the axis remain fixed, while other points move along circles centered on the axis.

Rotation About the \(z\)-Axis

A rotation through \(\theta\) about the \(z\)-axis sends

\[ (x,y,z) \]

to

\[ \boxed{ \left( x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta,\ x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta,\ z \right) }. \]

The \(z\)-coordinate is unchanged because the \(z\)-axis is the axis of rotation.

Rotation Matrices in Three Dimensions

A rotation about the \(z\)-axis can be written

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Similar matrices describe rotations about the \(x\)-axis and \(y\)-axis.

Three-dimensional rotations will become especially important in linear algebra, mechanics, computer graphics, and spatial modeling.

Rotations Preserve Area and Volume

Because rotations preserve all distances, they preserve area in the plane and volume in space.

A rotated region has the same area as its preimage, and a rotated solid has the same volume.

Rotations and Congruence

Any figure is congruent to its rotated image.

For example,

\[ \triangle ABC\cong\triangle A'B'C' \]

whenever \(A'B'C'\) is obtained from \(ABC\) by a rotation.

Rotations therefore provide another transformation-based model of congruence.

Rotations and Parallel Lines

If two lines are parallel before a rotation, their images remain parallel.

The direction of both lines changes by the same angle, so their directional relationship is preserved.

Thus,

\[ \ell\parallel m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ell'\parallel m'}. \]

Rotations and Perpendicular Lines

If two lines meet at a right angle, rotating both by the same amount leaves their angle difference unchanged.

Therefore,

\[ \ell\perp m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ell'\perp m'}. \]

Rotating a Circle

A rotation sends a circle to another circle with the same radius.

If the center of the circle coincides with the center of rotation, the entire circle maps onto itself.

Individual points move, but the circle as a set is invariant.

Fixed Points of a Nontrivial Plane Rotation

For a nonzero rotation angle that is not equivalent to a full turn, exactly one point in the plane remains fixed:

\[ \boxed{\text{the center of rotation}}. \]

This fact is often useful for identifying whether a transformation is a rotation.

Invariant Sets Under Rotation

Even when individual points move, an entire set may map onto itself.

Examples include:

  • a circle centered at the center of rotation;
  • a regular polygon under one of its symmetry rotations;
  • a rotationally symmetric pattern;
  • the entire plane.

Common Error: Rotating Around the Wrong Center

The same angle can produce completely different images when the center changes.

A \(90^\circ\) rotation about the origin is not the same transformation as a \(90^\circ\) rotation about \((3,2)\).

Always identify the center before applying a coordinate rule.

Common Error: Forgetting the Direction

A \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation and a \(90^\circ\) clockwise rotation are different.

About the origin,

\[ 90^\circ\text{ counterclockwise}: \quad (x,y)\mapsto(-y,x), \]

while

\[ 90^\circ\text{ clockwise}: \quad (x,y)\mapsto(y,-x). \]

Common Error: Memorizing Sign Changes Without Structure

The quarter-turn coordinate rules are useful, but pure memorization can lead to sign errors.

When uncertain, check the image of a simple reference point such as

\[ (1,0). \]

A \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise turn must send it to

\[ (0,1). \]

This immediately confirms the correct rule.

Common Error: Changing Distance from the Center

If a point begins \(r\) units from the center, its image must also be \(r\) units from the center.

If that distance changes, the transformation is not a pure rotation.

Common Error: Treating a Rotation Like a Translation

In a translation, every point has the same displacement vector.

In a rotation, displacement vectors usually differ because points move along different circular paths.

What remains common is the angle of rotation and the center-distance preservation.

Common Error: Assuming the Center Must Be Inside the Figure

The center of rotation may lie:

  • inside the figure;
  • on the figure;
  • outside the figure.

Its location is determined by the transformation, not by the boundary of the object being rotated.

A Reliable Procedure for Standard Coordinate Rotations

  1. Identify the center of rotation.
  2. Confirm whether the direction is clockwise or counterclockwise.
  3. Identify the angle.
  4. If the center is the origin, apply the appropriate coordinate rule.
  5. Apply the same rotation to every vertex or relevant point.
  6. Check that distances from the center are preserved.
  7. Check that the figure retains its shape and orientation.

A Reliable Procedure for Rotation About \((h,k)\)

  1. Subtract \((h,k)\) from the point coordinates.
  2. Rotate the resulting vector about the origin.
  3. Add \((h,k)\) back to the rotated coordinates.
  4. Repeat for every point.
  5. Verify equal center-distances before and after rotation.

A Reliable Procedure for Identifying a Rotation

  1. Match at least two preimage points with their image points.
  2. Construct or determine the perpendicular bisector of each connecting segment.
  3. Find the intersection of those perpendicular bisectors.
  4. Use that intersection as the candidate center.
  5. Verify equal distances from the center to each point-image pair.
  6. Determine the common directed angle.
  7. Confirm that the same angle works for all corresponding points.

Example: Full Coordinate Workflow

Let

\[ A=(1,2),\qquad B=(4,2),\qquad C=(2,5). \]

Rotate the triangle \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about the origin.

Use

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,x). \]

Then

\[ A'=( -2,1), \] \[ B'=(-2,4), \] \[ C'=(-5,2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ A'=(-2,1),\quad B'=(-2,4),\quad C'=(-5,2) }. \]

Example: Verify Distance Preservation

Using

\[ A=(1,2),\qquad B=(4,2), \]

the original segment length is

\[ AB=3. \]

After rotation,

\[ A'=(-2,1),\qquad B'=(-2,4), \]

so

\[ A'B'=3. \]

The segment length is unchanged.

What to Remember

A rotation turns every point through the same directed angle around one fixed center.

About the origin, the general rule is

\[ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto \left( x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta,\ x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta \right) }. \]

The essential facts are:

  • the center of rotation remains fixed;
  • every point remains the same distance from the center;
  • every point turns through the same directed angle;
  • rotations preserve distance and angle measure;
  • rotations preserve shape, size, and orientation;
  • rotations are rigid motions, or isometries;
  • the inverse rotation uses the opposite angle;
  • rotations about the same center compose by adding angles;
  • regular figures often exhibit rotational symmetry;
  • rotation matrices connect geometry with trigonometry and linear algebra.

The most useful structural habit is: identify the center first, then track both distance from the center and the directed angle of motion.
