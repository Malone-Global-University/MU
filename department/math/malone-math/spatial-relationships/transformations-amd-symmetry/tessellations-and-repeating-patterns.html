Tessellations and Repeating Patterns

A tessellation covers a surface with repeated shapes without gaps or overlaps. Repetition can be organized by translations, rotations, reflections, and glide reflections, turning simple geometric pieces into structured patterns. Tessellations connect angle measure, polygons, symmetry, lattices, periodicity, design, crystallography, architecture, and the broader study of transformation groups.

The Central Idea

A tessellation, or tiling, covers a surface completely using one or more shapes.

A valid tessellation has:

  • no gaps;
  • no overlaps.

In the plane, the tiles fit together so that every point of the region is covered exactly as intended.

Tiles and Tiling

The individual shapes used in a tessellation are called tiles.

The complete arrangement is the tiling.

Tiles may be:

  • regular polygons;
  • irregular polygons;
  • curved shapes;
  • composite geometric forms.

Local Fit and Global Coverage

A successful tessellation requires more than a few pieces fitting together locally.

The arrangement must extend consistently across the intended region.

This creates two related questions:

  1. Do the tiles fit around local vertices and edges?
  2. Can that arrangement continue without contradiction?

Why Angles Matter

When polygons meet around an interior point of a planar tessellation, the angles around that point must total

\[ \boxed{360^\circ}. \]

This is the basic angle test for many polygonal tessellations.

Interior Angle of a Regular Polygon

A regular \(n\)-gon has interior angle

\[ \boxed{ \alpha_n = \frac{(n-2)180^\circ}{n} }. \]

This formula allows us to test which regular polygons can meet around a point.

Regular Tessellations

A regular tessellation uses only one kind of regular polygon, arranged the same way at every vertex.

In the Euclidean plane, only three regular polygons tessellate by themselves:

  • equilateral triangles;
  • squares;
  • regular hexagons.

Why Equilateral Triangles Tessellate

Each interior angle of an equilateral triangle is

\[ 60^\circ. \]

Six such angles fit exactly around a point:

\[ 6(60^\circ)=360^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{equilateral triangles tessellate the plane}}. \]

Why Squares Tessellate

Each interior angle of a square is

\[ 90^\circ. \]

Four fit around a point:

\[ 4(90^\circ)=360^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{squares tessellate the plane}}. \]

Why Regular Hexagons Tessellate

Each interior angle of a regular hexagon is

\[ 120^\circ. \]

Three fit around a point:

\[ 3(120^\circ)=360^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{regular hexagons tessellate the plane}}. \]

Why Regular Pentagons Do Not Tessellate by Themselves

A regular pentagon has interior angle

\[ \frac{(5-2)180^\circ}{5} = 108^\circ. \]

But

\[ \frac{360^\circ}{108^\circ} = \frac{10}{3}, \]

which is not an integer.

Therefore, identical regular pentagons cannot meet edge-to-edge in the same way around every vertex to form a regular tessellation.

The Regular-Tessellation Equation

Suppose \(m\) identical regular \(n\)-gons meet at each vertex.

Then

\[ m\alpha_n=360^\circ. \]

Substituting the regular-polygon angle formula gives

\[ \boxed{ m\frac{(n-2)180^\circ}{n}=360^\circ }. \]

Simplifying the Regular-Tessellation Condition

Divide by \(180^\circ\):

\[ m\frac{n-2}{n}=2. \]

Rearranging,

\[ mn-2m=2n, \] \[ mn-2m-2n=0. \]

Add \(4\):

\[ (m-2)(n-2)=4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(m-2)(n-2)=4}. \]

The Three Integer Solutions

With

\[ m\ge3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad n\ge3, \]

the positive factor pairs of \(4\) give:

\[ (m-2,n-2)=(1,4),(2,2),(4,1). \]

Therefore,

\[ (m,n)=(3,6),(4,4),(6,3). \]

These correspond exactly to:

  • 3 hexagons;
  • 4 squares;
  • 6 triangles.

Vertex Configuration

A tessellation can be described by listing the polygons that meet around a vertex.

For example,

\[ 3.3.3.3.3.3 \]

means six triangles meet around each vertex.

Similarly,

\[ 4.4.4.4 \]

describes the square tiling.

Semiregular Tessellations

A semiregular tessellation, also called an Archimedean tiling, uses more than one type of regular polygon while keeping the same cyclic arrangement at every vertex.

The polygons must still fit so that the angles around each vertex total

\[ 360^\circ. \]

Example: Triangle-Square-Hexagon Pattern

Consider the vertex configuration

\[ 3.4.6.4. \]

The corresponding interior angles are

\[ 60^\circ,\quad90^\circ,\quad120^\circ,\quad90^\circ. \]

Their sum is

\[ 60^\circ+90^\circ+120^\circ+90^\circ = 360^\circ. \]

The local angle condition is therefore satisfied.

Angle Sum Is Necessary but Not Always Sufficient

A collection of polygon angles totaling

\[ 360^\circ \]

is necessary for an edge-to-edge vertex fit.

But a local fit does not automatically prove that the pattern extends consistently across the entire plane.

Global compatibility must also be checked.

Monohedral Tessellations

A monohedral tessellation uses only one tile shape, though the tile need not be a regular polygon.

Copies may be translated, rotated, or reflected to cover the plane.

Every Triangle Tessellates the Plane

Any triangle can tessellate the Euclidean plane.

One construction pairs two congruent copies to form a parallelogram.

Parallelograms then tile the plane by translation.

Every Quadrilateral Tessellates the Plane

Any quadrilateral can tessellate the plane.

Copies can be rotated by \(180^\circ\) around side midpoints so that adjacent edges fit, producing a repeating arrangement.

This fact is broader than the special case of regular polygons.

Transformations Build Tessellations

Tessellations are naturally constructed using transformations.

Common operations include:

  • translation;
  • rotation;
  • reflection;
  • glide reflection.

These transformations move one tile into positions occupied by congruent tiles.

Translation in Tessellations

Many tessellations repeat through translation.

If a tile or repeating block is shifted by a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) and the complete pattern remains unchanged, then

\[ \boxed{T_{\mathbf{v}}(F)=F}. \]

Two-Dimensional Translation Lattices

A periodic planar tiling usually has two independent translation vectors

\[ \mathbf{u} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf{v}. \]

Then every vector of the form

\[ \boxed{m\mathbf{u}+n\mathbf{v}} \]

with integers \(m,n\) is also a translation symmetry.

Fundamental Region

A fundamental region is a smallest or conveniently chosen piece whose transformed copies generate the complete repeating pattern.

It acts like a geometric building block for the entire tessellation.

Fundamental Parallelogram

For a translation lattice generated by independent vectors

\[ \mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}, \]

the parallelogram spanned by those vectors is often used as a fundamental translation cell.

Translating that cell by all lattice vectors covers the plane.

Area of a Fundamental Parallelogram

If

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle u_1,u_2\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{v}=\langle v_1,v_2\rangle, \]

then the area of the fundamental parallelogram is

\[ \boxed{ |u_1v_2-u_2v_1| }. \]

This determinant measures the area repeated by the translation lattice.

Example: Square Lattice

Let

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle a,0\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{v}=\langle0,a\rangle. \]

Then the fundamental cell has area

\[ |a\cdot a-0| = \boxed{a^2}. \]

Rotation in Tessellations

Some repeating patterns contain points around which the entire tiling can be rotated and remain unchanged.

Such points are centers of rotational symmetry.

Square tilings, for example, contain quarter-turn and half-turn symmetry centers.

Reflection in Tessellations

A tessellation may have lines across which the entire pattern reflects onto itself.

These are reflection axes of the pattern.

Reflection axes may pass through tile centers, vertices, edges, or repeating motifs.

Glide Reflection in Repeating Patterns

A glide reflection combines reflection across a line with a translation parallel to that line.

Some repeating strip and wallpaper patterns have glide-reflection symmetry even when ordinary reflection symmetry is absent.

Frieze Patterns

A frieze pattern repeats indefinitely in one principal direction.

Every frieze pattern has translational symmetry.

It may also have:

  • reflection symmetry;
  • half-turn symmetry;
  • glide-reflection symmetry.

Wallpaper Patterns

A wallpaper pattern repeats in two independent directions across the plane.

Its full symmetry may combine:

  • translations;
  • rotations;
  • reflections;
  • glide reflections.

Wallpaper Groups: A Gentle Preview

Mathematically, there are finitely many fundamentally different symmetry types for periodic planar patterns.

Their full classification produces the wallpaper groups.

This is a deeper application of transformation groups and planar symmetry.

Periodic Versus Aperiodic Patterns

A periodic tiling has nonzero translation symmetries.

An aperiodic tiling can cover the plane without repeating by any nonzero translation.

Thus, tessellation does not automatically imply translational periodicity.

Aperiodic Tessellations

Some tile sets force nonperiodic arrangements.

Such tilings can still show rich local structure and rotational motifs even though no translation maps the entire pattern onto itself.

This distinction is important in the broader study of order and repetition.

Tessellations on Other Surfaces

Tessellation is not limited to the Euclidean plane.

Tiles can also cover:

  • spheres;
  • cylinders;
  • tori;
  • hyperbolic surfaces.

The geometry of the surface changes which angle and tiling patterns are possible.

Euclidean Versus Spherical Tiling

On a sphere, triangle angle sums exceed

\[ 180^\circ. \]

Therefore, tiling conditions differ from those in the Euclidean plane.

The geometry of the underlying space matters.

Euclidean Versus Hyperbolic Tiling

In hyperbolic geometry, triangle angle sums are less than

\[ 180^\circ. \]

This allows many regular tessellations that cannot exist in the Euclidean plane.

Edge-to-Edge Tessellations

In an edge-to-edge tessellation, tiles meet along complete edges or at vertices.

One tile edge does not terminate in the middle of another tile edge.

This regularity simplifies vertex-angle analysis.

Non-Edge-to-Edge Tessellations

Some valid tessellations allow a vertex of one tile to meet the interior of another tile's edge.

Such tilings may still have no gaps or overlaps, but their local structure is more complicated.

Congruent and Noncongruent Tiles

A tessellation may use:

  • all congruent tiles;
  • several congruence classes of tiles;
  • several different shapes and sizes.

The defining requirement is complete coverage without improper overlap, not tile congruence.

Pattern Repetition and Motifs

A motif is a repeated visual or geometric unit.

The motif may be larger than one tile and may contain several shapes.

Transformations of the motif can generate the visible repeating pattern.

Local Symmetry Versus Global Symmetry

A small region of a pattern may appear symmetric even when the entire infinite pattern is not invariant under that same transformation.

Therefore, distinguish:

  • local motif symmetry;
  • global symmetry of the full tessellation.

Vertex Orbits

Under the symmetries of a tessellation, vertices may move among equivalent positions.

The set of positions reachable from one vertex is its orbit.

Uniform tessellations often have highly regular vertex orbits.

Symmetry and Equivalence of Tiles

Two tiles are symmetry-equivalent when some symmetry of the complete tiling maps one onto the other.

This is stronger than merely saying the tiles are congruent.

Colorings Can Reduce Symmetry

A geometric tiling may have many symmetries, but coloring its tiles can destroy some of them.

A symmetry of the colored pattern must preserve both geometry and color arrangement.

Example: Checkerboard Coloring

A square grid has many translation symmetries.

After alternating two colors like a checkerboard, a translation by one tile width exchanges the colors and no longer preserves the colored pattern.

A translation by two tile widths does preserve it.

Tessellations and Area

When a region is tiled by nonoverlapping tiles,

\[ \boxed{ \text{total area} = \sum \text{tile areas} }. \]

Tessellation therefore supports decomposition and accumulation methods for area.

Counting Tiles in a Finite Region

If a finite region is perfectly tiled by \(N\) congruent tiles of area \(A_t\), then

\[ \boxed{ A_{\text{region}}=NA_t. } \]

Conversely,

\[ \boxed{ N=\frac{A_{\text{region}}}{A_t} } \]

when the fit is exact.

Boundary Effects

Infinite periodic patterns may be simple in the interior but complicated when restricted to a finite boundary.

Cutting a tessellation to fit a bounded region may create partial tiles or destroy some global symmetries.

Tessellations and Coordinate Geometry

A lattice tiling can be studied using vectors and coordinates.

If a tile is generated from a fundamental tile by

\[ m\mathbf{u}+n\mathbf{v}, \]

then each pair of integers \((m,n)\) identifies a translated copy.

Example: Coordinate Lattice

Let

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle2,0\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{v}=\langle1,3\rangle. \]

Then the copy indexed by

\[ (m,n)=(2,-1) \]

is displaced by

\[ 2\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle4,0\rangle-\langle1,3\rangle = \boxed{\langle3,-3\rangle}. \]

Transforming Tile Edges

One way to design an irregular tessellating tile is to alter one edge and apply a matching transformation to an opposite edge.

For translation-based designs, a modification removed from one side can be translated to the opposite side.

The matching edges then fit in the repeated pattern.

Rotation-Based Tile Construction

A piece removed from one portion of a tile boundary can sometimes be rotated about a vertex and attached to another boundary segment.

Repeated rotated copies then fit around the corresponding vertices.

Reflection-Based Tile Construction

Boundary features may also be reflected across a line or edge so that neighboring tiles interlock as mirror images.

This technique connects artistic pattern design directly to geometric transformation rules.

Why Matching Transformations Matter

When an edge modification is transferred by an exact transformation, the neighboring copy inherits a matching boundary.

This is one reason transformations provide such a reliable language for constructing tessellations.

Tessellations in Architecture and Design

Tessellations appear in:

  • flooring;
  • brickwork;
  • mosaics;
  • facades;
  • textiles;
  • decorative borders;
  • digital patterns.

Geometry determines whether a design can repeat consistently.

Tessellations in Crystallography

Crystal structures exhibit repeated spatial arrangements.

Translation lattices and symmetry operations provide mathematical tools for describing those repeating structures.

In three dimensions, this leads to lattice and space-group concepts.

Tessellations and Computer Graphics

Digital graphics often repeat textures across surfaces using translation and coordinate wrapping.

Understanding periodicity helps avoid visible seams and mismatched edges.

Tessellations and Discrete Structure

A tessellation can be converted into a network:

  • vertices become nodes;
  • edges become connections;
  • tiles become faces.

This links geometry with graph theory and combinatorial topology.

Common Error: Thinking Any Repetition Is a Tessellation

A repeated pattern may leave uncovered gaps.

Tessellation requires complete coverage of the intended surface without gaps or improper overlaps.

Common Error: Thinking Every Tessellation Is Periodic

Some tessellations are aperiodic.

They cover the plane but possess no nonzero translation symmetry.

Common Error: Using \(180^\circ\) Instead of \(360^\circ\) Around a Point

Angles around an interior point of the plane total

\[ \boxed{360^\circ}, \]

not \(180^\circ\).

The \(180^\circ\) total applies to a straight angle.

Common Error: Assuming Any Regular Polygon Tessellates

A regular polygon must fit an integer number of times around a vertex in a regular tessellation.

Regular pentagons fail this test because

\[ 108^\circ \]

does not divide \(360^\circ\) evenly.

Common Error: Assuming Angle Sum Alone Proves a Tessellation

A valid \(360^\circ\) local angle sum is necessary in an edge-to-edge polygonal tiling, but the pattern must also extend consistently.

Local compatibility does not always guarantee global compatibility.

Common Error: Confusing a Tile with a Fundamental Region

A fundamental region may contain one tile, part of a tile, or several tiles, depending on the symmetry being studied.

The two ideas are related but not identical.

Common Error: Ignoring Color or Decoration

A transformation may preserve the underlying geometric tiling but fail to preserve a colored or decorated pattern.

Symmetry depends on what features are included in the object being studied.

A Reliable Procedure for Testing a Regular Polygon

  1. Find the polygon's interior angle.
  2. Divide \(360^\circ\) by that angle.
  3. Check whether the result is an integer at least \(3\).
  4. Verify that the local arrangement extends consistently.
  5. Conclude whether a regular tessellation is possible.

A Reliable Procedure for Testing a Mixed Vertex

  1. List the regular polygons meeting at the vertex.
  2. Find each interior angle.
  3. Add the angles.
  4. Require a total of \(360^\circ\).
  5. Check edge compatibility and global continuation.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding Translation Symmetry

  1. Identify a repeated tile or motif.
  2. Find a matching copy.
  3. Compute the displacement vector between corresponding points.
  4. Translate the entire pattern by that vector.
  5. Verify exact coincidence.
  6. Search for two independent fundamental translation vectors if the pattern repeats in two dimensions.

A Reliable Procedure for Analyzing a Tessellation

  1. Identify the tile shapes.
  2. Check gaps and overlaps.
  3. Analyze vertex configurations.
  4. Look for translation symmetries.
  5. Look for rotation centers.
  6. Look for reflection and glide-reflection axes.
  7. Determine whether the pattern is periodic or aperiodic.
  8. Identify a useful fundamental region.

Example: Full Analysis of the Square Tiling

Each square contributes

\[ 90^\circ \]

at a vertex, and four squares meet:

\[ 4(90^\circ)=360^\circ. \]

The vertex configuration is

\[ \boxed{4.4.4.4}. \]

The tiling has:

  • two independent translation directions;
  • quarter-turn and half-turn rotation centers;
  • multiple reflection axes;
  • periodic repetition across the entire plane.

Example: Full Analysis of the Regular Hexagon Tiling

Each regular hexagon contributes

\[ 120^\circ \]

at a vertex, and three meet:

\[ 3(120^\circ)=360^\circ. \]

The vertex configuration is

\[ \boxed{6.6.6}. \]

The pattern has translation, rotational, and reflection symmetries.

What to Remember

A tessellation covers a surface without gaps or improper overlaps.

For polygonal tiles meeting around an interior point of the Euclidean plane,

\[ \boxed{\text{angles around the point total }360^\circ}. \]

The essential facts are:

  • equilateral triangles, squares, and regular hexagons form the three regular Euclidean tessellations;
  • mixed regular polygons can form semiregular tessellations;
  • local angle fit must be combined with global compatibility;
  • translations, rotations, reflections, and glide reflections generate repeating tilings;
  • periodic plane patterns usually have two independent translation vectors;
  • integer combinations of those vectors form a lattice;
  • a fundamental region generates the larger repeating pattern;
  • not every tessellation is periodic;
  • coloring or decoration can reduce symmetry;
  • tessellations connect geometry with symmetry, area, lattices, graph theory, and transformation groups.

The most important structural idea is: a tessellation is not merely repeated shape—it is a globally compatible covering whose repetition is governed by geometry and transformations.
