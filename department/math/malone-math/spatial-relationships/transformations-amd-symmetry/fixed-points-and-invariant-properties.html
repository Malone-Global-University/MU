Fixed Points and Invariant Properties

Transformations move geometric objects, but they do not change everything. Some points may remain fixed, some lines or sets may map onto themselves, and important geometric quantities such as distance, angle, area, or orientation may be preserved. These unchanged features are called invariants, and they provide one of the most powerful ways to understand, compare, and classify transformations.

The Central Idea

A transformation changes the position or form of geometric objects, but some properties may remain unchanged.

A point \(P\) is a fixed point of a transformation \(T\) when

\[ \boxed{T(P)=P}. \]

A geometric property is invariant when it is preserved under the transformation.

Fixed Does Not Mean Unimportant

Fixed points often determine the structure of a transformation.

Examples include:

  • the center of a rotation;
  • every point on a reflection line;
  • the center of a dilation;
  • every point under the identity transformation.

Fixed-point structure can therefore help identify the transformation itself.

Invariant Does Not Always Mean Pointwise Fixed

A set may be invariant even when most of its individual points move.

For example, a circle centered at the center of a rotation maps onto itself under that rotation.

The circle is invariant as a set, even though most points on the circle are not fixed.

Fixed Point Versus Invariant Set

A fixed point satisfies

\[ T(P)=P. \]

An invariant set \(S\) satisfies

\[ \boxed{T(S)=S}. \]

The points of \(S\) may be rearranged while the set as a whole remains unchanged.

Pointwise Fixed Sets

A set is pointwise fixed when every one of its points is fixed individually.

For example, under reflection across a line \(\ell\),

\[ \boxed{T(P)=P\quad\text{for every }P\in\ell}. \]

The reflection line is therefore pointwise fixed.

Setwise Invariance

A set may map onto itself without being pointwise fixed.

Under a \(90^\circ\) rotation of a square about its center, the square maps onto itself, but its vertices move to other vertices.

The square is invariant setwise, not pointwise.

Fixed Points of a Translation

Consider a translation by

\[ \mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}. \]

A fixed point would have to satisfy

\[ P+\mathbf{v}=P. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}, \]

which contradicts the assumption.

Hence a nonzero translation has

\[ \boxed{\text{no fixed points}}. \]

Fixed Points of the Identity Translation

If the translation vector is

\[ \mathbf{0}, \]

every point remains unchanged.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{every point is fixed}}. \]

Invariant Lines Under Translation

A line translated parallel to itself maps onto itself as a set.

For example, translation by

\[ \langle3,0\rangle \]

leaves every horizontal line invariant as a set.

Individual points move, but the full line remains the same set.

Fixed Points of a Rotation

A nontrivial rotation in the plane has exactly one fixed point:

\[ \boxed{\text{the center of rotation}}. \]

Every other point moves around that center along a circle.

Invariant Circles Under Rotation

Every circle centered at the center of rotation is invariant under that rotation.

If \(C\) is the center and \(r\) is fixed, then

\[ \boxed{ \{P:CP=r\} } \]

maps onto itself.

Invariant Lines Under a Half-Turn

A \(180^\circ\) rotation about a point \(C\) maps every line through \(C\) onto itself as a set.

The points on the line generally move to opposite locations, but the entire line remains invariant.

Fixed Points of a Reflection

For reflection across a line \(\ell\), every point on \(\ell\) is fixed.

No point off \(\ell\) is fixed.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{Fix}(R_\ell)=\ell}. \]

Invariant Lines Under Reflection

The reflection line itself is invariant and pointwise fixed.

Any line perpendicular to the mirror line also maps onto itself as a set, although its off-axis points exchange sides.

Fixed Points of a Dilation

A nonidentity dilation has one fixed point:

\[ \boxed{\text{the center of dilation}}. \]

Every other point moves along the line joining it to the center.

Invariant Lines Under Dilation

Every line through the center of dilation maps onto itself as a set.

A line not through the center maps to a parallel line rather than to itself.

Fixed Points of a Glide Reflection

A nontrivial glide reflection has no fixed points.

The reflection component reverses orientation, while the translation along the mirror direction prevents any point from remaining in place.

Fixed-Point Classification of Plane Isometries

Fixed points help distinguish the basic plane isometries:

  • identity: every point fixed;
  • nonzero translation: no fixed points;
  • nontrivial rotation: one fixed point;
  • reflection: one fixed line;
  • glide reflection: no fixed points.

Invariant Properties of Rigid Motions

Every rigid motion preserves:

  • distance;
  • segment length;
  • angle measure;
  • collinearity;
  • betweenness;
  • parallelism;
  • perpendicularity;
  • area;
  • shape;
  • size.

Distance as a Fundamental Invariant

For an isometry \(T\),

\[ \boxed{ d(T(P),T(Q))=d(P,Q) } \]

for every pair of points.

Many other invariants follow from this single condition.

Angle Measure as an Invariant

Rigid motions and similarity transformations preserve angle measure.

Thus, if

\[ \angle ABC \]

maps to

\[ \angle A'B'C', \]

then

\[ \boxed{ m\angle ABC=m\angle A'B'C' }. \]

Orientation as an Invariant or Reversed Property

Some transformations preserve orientation, while others reverse it.

Orientation-preserving examples include:

  • translations;
  • rotations;
  • positive dilations;
  • direct similarities.

Orientation-reversing examples include:

  • reflections;
  • glide reflections;
  • opposite similarities.

Area as an Invariant of Isometries

Rigid motions preserve area:

\[ \boxed{A'=A}. \]

Similarity transformations do not generally preserve area, but scale it by

\[ k^2. \]

Volume as an Invariant of Spatial Isometries

Three-dimensional rigid motions preserve volume:

\[ \boxed{V'=V}. \]

Spatial similarities scale volume by

\[ k^3. \]

Collinearity Is Preserved

If three points lie on one line before a Euclidean isometry or similarity, their images remain collinear.

Therefore, straight-line incidence is an invariant of these transformation families.

Parallelism Is Preserved by Similarities

Rigid motions and dilations preserve parallelism.

Therefore, all Euclidean similarity transformations preserve it:

\[ \ell\parallel m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ell'\parallel m'}. \]

Perpendicularity Is Preserved by Similarities

Since similarity transformations preserve angle measure, they preserve right angles:

\[ \ell\perp m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ell'\perp m'}. \]

Ratios of Lengths as Similarity Invariants

A similarity transformation multiplies all lengths by the same factor \(k\).

Therefore, ratios of lengths remain unchanged:

\[ \frac{k a}{k b} = \frac{a}{b}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{a'}{b'}=\frac{a}{b} }. \]

What Dilations Preserve

A dilation preserves:

  • angle measure;
  • shape;
  • collinearity;
  • parallelism;
  • ratios of corresponding lengths;
  • ratios of lengths within the figure.

It generally does not preserve actual length, area, or volume.

What Reflections Preserve and Reverse

Reflection preserves:

  • distance;
  • angle measure;
  • area;
  • shape;
  • size.

But reflection reverses orientation.

Thus invariants and reversals can coexist in one transformation.

Symmetry Is Invariance of a Figure

A transformation is a symmetry of a figure \(F\) when

\[ \boxed{T(F)=F}. \]

The figure as a whole is invariant under the transformation.

This is the central connection between invariance and symmetry.

Rotational Symmetry as Set Invariance

A square is invariant under rotations of

\[ 90^\circ,\quad180^\circ,\quad270^\circ,\quad360^\circ \]

about its center.

Its vertices move, but the square as a set remains unchanged.

Reflection Symmetry as Set Invariance

If reflection across a line \(\ell\) maps a figure \(F\) onto itself, then \(\ell\) is a line of symmetry.

Symbolically,

\[ \boxed{R_\ell(F)=F}. \]

Translational Symmetry as Set Invariance

Infinite repeating patterns can remain unchanged under nonzero translations.

If

\[ T_{\mathbf{v}}(F)=F \]

for some nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\), then the pattern has translational symmetry.

Periodic Invariance

A transformation may not fix a figure after one application but may return it after repeated application.

If

\[ T^n(F)=F \]

for some positive integer \(n\), then the figure exhibits a periodic relationship under \(T\).

Example: Quarter-Turn Periodicity

For a square centered at the origin, let \(R\) be a \(90^\circ\) rotation.

Then

\[ R^4=I. \]

The square returns to its original pointwise arrangement after four quarter-turns.

Invariant Equations

Sometimes a geometric set is described by an equation that remains unchanged under a transformation.

For example, the circle

\[ x^2+y^2=r^2 \]

is invariant under every rotation about the origin because rotations preserve

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2}. \]

Example: Reflection Invariance of a Parabola

Consider

\[ y=x^2. \]

Reflection across the \(y\)-axis replaces \(x\) by \(-x\):

\[ y=(-x)^2=x^2. \]

The equation is unchanged.

Therefore, the parabola is invariant under reflection across the \(y\)-axis.

Even Functions and Reflection Invariance

A function satisfying

\[ \boxed{f(-x)=f(x)} \]

has a graph invariant under reflection across the \(y\)-axis.

Such functions are called even functions.

Odd Functions and Half-Turn Invariance

A function satisfying

\[ \boxed{f(-x)=-f(x)} \]

has a graph invariant under a \(180^\circ\) rotation about the origin.

Such functions are called odd functions.

Invariant Direction

A transformation may preserve a direction even when it moves every point.

For example, a translation by a horizontal vector preserves the horizontal direction.

Dilations preserve every line direction through their center.

Invariant Lines and Eigenvectors: A Gentle Preview

In linear algebra, a vector direction that remains on the same line after a transformation is related to an eigenvector.

If

\[ A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}, \]

then the line through the origin in direction \(\mathbf{v}\) is invariant under \(A\).

The vector may change magnitude or reverse direction, but it stays on the same line.

Example: Reflection Eigen-Directions

Reflect across the \(x\)-axis:

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Horizontal vectors satisfy

\[ A \begin{bmatrix} x\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Vertical vectors satisfy

\[ A \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ y \end{bmatrix} = - \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ y \end{bmatrix}. \]

Both coordinate-axis directions are invariant as lines.

Fixed Vectors Versus Invariant Directions

A fixed vector satisfies

\[ A\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}. \]

An invariant direction only requires

\[ A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v} \]

for some scalar \(\lambda\).

Therefore, fixed vectors are a special case of invariant directions.

Solving for Fixed Points Algebraically

If a transformation is given by a coordinate rule \(T(x,y)\), fixed points satisfy

\[ \boxed{T(x,y)=(x,y)}. \]

This creates a system of equations whose solutions are exactly the fixed points.

Example: Fixed Points of a Reflection

Consider

\[ T(x,y)=(x,-y). \]

Set

\[ (x,-y)=(x,y). \]

Then

\[ -y=y, \]

so

\[ y=0. \]

Therefore, the fixed-point set is

\[ \boxed{y=0}, \]

the \(x\)-axis.

Example: Fixed Point of a Rotation

Consider a \(90^\circ\) rotation about the origin:

\[ T(x,y)=(-y,x). \]

Fixed points satisfy

\[ (-y,x)=(x,y). \]

Therefore,

\[ x=-y \]

and

\[ y=x. \]

Combining these gives

\[ x=y=0. \]

Thus the only fixed point is

\[ \boxed{(0,0)}. \]

Example: No Fixed Point for a Translation

Consider

\[ T(x,y)=(x+4,y-1). \]

A fixed point would require

\[ x+4=x \]

and

\[ y-1=y. \]

These equations are impossible.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{there are no fixed points}}. \]

Fixed Points of an Affine Transformation

Suppose

\[ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}. \]

A fixed point satisfies

\[ A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{x}. \]

Rearranging gives

\[ \boxed{ (I-A)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} }. \]

Solving this system reveals the fixed-point set.

Fixed Sets Can Have Different Dimensions

Depending on the transformation, the fixed-point set may be:

  • empty;
  • one point;
  • a line;
  • a plane;
  • the entire space.

The dimension of the fixed set often provides strong classification information.

Fixed Sets in Three Dimensions

In three-dimensional geometry:

  • a rotation about an axis fixes every point on that axis;
  • a reflection across a plane fixes every point in that plane;
  • a nonzero translation has no fixed points;
  • the identity fixes all of space.

Invariant Distance from an Axis

Under rotation about an axis in three dimensions, each point maintains its perpendicular distance from the axis.

This distance is an invariant of the rotation.

Invariant Distance from a Center

Under rotation about a center \(C\),

\[ \boxed{CP=CP'}. \]

Distance from the center is invariant even though the point itself may move.

Invariant Midpoints Under Isometries

If \(M\) is the midpoint of \(\overline{AB}\), then under an isometry, \(M'\) is the midpoint of \(\overline{A'B'}\).

This follows from preservation of collinearity, betweenness, and equal distances.

Invariant Ratios Along a Line Under Affine Maps

Affine transformations preserve ratios of directed segments along the same line.

This is a broader invariant than Euclidean distance and helps explain why midpoints remain midpoints under affine transformations.

Different Transformation Families Preserve Different Structures

A useful hierarchy is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{isometries} \subset \text{similarities} \subset \text{affine transformations} \subset \text{projective transformations} }. \]

Each larger family preserves fewer geometric properties but permits more general transformations.

Isometry Invariants

Isometries preserve metric structure:

  • distance;
  • angle;
  • area;
  • shape;
  • size.

Similarity Invariants

Similarities preserve:

  • angle;
  • shape;
  • parallelism;
  • ratios of lengths.

Absolute length is generally not invariant.

Affine Invariants

Affine transformations preserve:

  • collinearity;
  • parallelism;
  • midpoints;
  • ratios of directed segments along one line.

They need not preserve length or angle.

Projective Invariants

Projective transformations preserve incidence relationships such as which points lie on which lines.

They generally do not preserve Euclidean length, angle, or parallelism.

This shows how invariants organize transformation geometry from restrictive to broad.

Invariants as Diagnostic Tools

Suppose a transformation preserves:

  • all distances;
  • all angles;
  • area.

These invariants strongly indicate an isometry.

If lengths change but all angles and length ratios remain constant, the transformation is more likely a similarity.

Using Fixed Points to Identify a Transformation

Suppose a plane transformation is known to be an isometry.

If it has:

  • one fixed point and preserves orientation, suspect rotation;
  • a fixed line and reverses orientation, suspect reflection;
  • no fixed points and preserves orientation, suspect translation;
  • no fixed points and reverses orientation, suspect glide reflection.

Using Orientation as a Diagnostic Tool

Orientation quickly separates rigid motions into two groups.

Preserve orientation:

\[ \boxed{\text{translations and rotations}} \]

Reverse orientation:

\[ \boxed{\text{reflections and glide reflections}}. \]

Using Invariant Lines as a Diagnostic Tool

Invariant lines can reveal the geometry of a transformation.

Examples:

  • reflection: mirror line pointwise fixed;
  • dilation: every line through center invariant;
  • half-turn: every line through center invariant as a set;
  • translation: lines parallel to the translation vector invariant as sets.

Common Error: Confusing Fixed with Invariant

A point is fixed only if it remains exactly where it started.

A set can be invariant even if its points move.

A rotated circle is a standard example: the circle remains the same set, while individual points move around it.

Common Error: Assuming Every Invariant Line Is Pointwise Fixed

A line through the center of a \(180^\circ\) rotation maps onto itself, but points on that line generally move.

The line is invariant setwise, not pointwise.

Common Error: Assuming No Fixed Points Means Translation

A nonzero translation has no fixed points, but so does a glide reflection.

Orientation must also be checked.

Common Error: Assuming One Fixed Point Always Means Rotation

A nonidentity dilation also has one fixed point: its center.

Distance preservation distinguishes rotation from dilation.

Common Error: Treating Area as Invariant Under Every Similarity

Similarity preserves shape but generally changes area.

If the similarity ratio is \(k\), then

\[ A'=k^2A. \]

Area is invariant only when

\[ k=1. \]

Common Error: Ignoring Orientation

Two transformations may preserve the same distances and angles but differ in whether they reverse orientation.

Reflection and rotation are both isometries, but only reflection reverses orientation.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding Fixed Points

  1. Write the transformation rule \(T(P)\).
  2. Set \(T(P)=P\).
  3. Translate the equality into coordinate equations.
  4. Solve the resulting system.
  5. Interpret the solution geometrically.
  6. Check whether the solution set is empty, a point, a line, or something larger.

A Reliable Procedure for Testing an Invariant Set

  1. Describe the set precisely.
  2. Apply the transformation to a general point of the set.
  3. Show that the image remains in the set.
  4. Check whether every point of the target set is reached.
  5. Conclude \(T(S)=S\) only when the full set maps onto itself.

A Reliable Procedure for Identifying Preserved Properties

  1. Identify the transformation family.
  2. Check whether distance is preserved or merely scaled.
  3. Check angle measure.
  4. Check orientation.
  5. Check parallelism and perpendicularity.
  6. Check area or volume behavior.
  7. Separate pointwise invariance from setwise invariance.

A Reliable Procedure for Classifying by Invariants

  1. Determine whether distances are preserved.
  2. If yes, classify as an isometry and inspect orientation.
  3. Count or describe fixed points.
  4. Look for fixed or invariant lines.
  5. If distances scale uniformly instead, test for similarity.
  6. Use preserved properties to narrow the transformation type.

Example: Full Fixed-Point Workflow

Consider

\[ T(x,y)=(-x+6,-y+4). \]

Fixed points satisfy

\[ -x+6=x \]

and

\[ -y+4=y. \]

Therefore,

\[ 2x=6, \qquad 2y=4. \]

Hence

\[ x=3, \qquad y=2. \]

The unique fixed point is

\[ \boxed{(3,2)}. \]

The rule is a \(180^\circ\) rotation about \((3,2)\).

Example: Full Invariant-Set Workflow

Consider a rotation about the origin and the circle

\[ x^2+y^2=25. \]

A rotation preserves

\[ x^2+y^2. \]

Therefore, any point satisfying

\[ x^2+y^2=25 \]

is sent to another point satisfying the same equation.

Hence the circle is invariant under every rotation about the origin.

What to Remember

A fixed point satisfies

\[ \boxed{T(P)=P}, \]

while an invariant set satisfies

\[ \boxed{T(S)=S}. \]

The essential facts are:

  • fixed points remain individually unchanged;
  • invariant sets may remain unchanged even while their points move;
  • translations, rotations, reflections, and dilations have different fixed-point structures;
  • distance is the defining invariant of isometries;
  • angle measure and length ratios are central invariants of similarities;
  • orientation may be preserved or reversed;
  • invariant lines and sets help classify transformations;
  • symmetry is invariance of a figure under a transformation;
  • fixed points can be found by solving \(T(P)=P\);
  • matrix methods connect invariant directions with eigenvectors.

The most important structural distinction is: ask separately what points stay fixed, what sets map onto themselves, and what geometric quantities remain unchanged.
