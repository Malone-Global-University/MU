Dilations

A dilation changes the size of a figure while preserving its shape. Every point moves along a line through a fixed center, and every distance from that center is multiplied by the same scale factor. Dilations are not usually rigid motions, but they preserve angle measure and create similar figures, making them fundamental to scale, similarity, coordinate geometry, modeling, and geometric transformations.

The Central Idea

A dilation resizes a figure relative to a fixed point called the center of dilation.

If \(C\) is the center and \(P\) maps to \(P'\), then \(C\), \(P\), and \(P'\) are collinear, and

\[ \boxed{CP'=|k|\,CP} \]

where \(k\) is the scale factor.

Two Pieces Determine a Dilation

A dilation is determined by:

  1. the center of dilation;
  2. the scale factor \(k\).

Once these are known, the image of every point is fixed.

The Scale Factor

The scale factor tells how distances from the center change.

If

\[ CP'=|k|CP, \]

then:

  • \(|k|>1\) enlarges distances from the center;
  • \(0<|k|<1\) reduces distances from the center;
  • \(|k|=1\) preserves distance from the center.

Positive Scale Factors

If

\[ k>0, \]

then \(P'\) lies on the same ray from \(C\) through \(P\).

For example, if

\[ k=2, \]

then

\[ CP'=2CP. \]

The image lies twice as far from the center in the same direction.

Negative Scale Factors

If

\[ k<0, \]

then \(P'\) lies on the opposite ray through the center.

Its distance from the center is still multiplied by \(|k|\).

For example, \(k=-2\) places the image twice as far from the center but on the opposite side.

The Center Is Fixed

The center of dilation always maps to itself.

If the center is \(C\), then

\[ \boxed{C\mapsto C}. \]

This follows because the distance from the center to itself is zero, and multiplying zero by any scale factor still gives zero.

Dilation About the Origin

When the center of dilation is the origin, the coordinate rule is especially simple.

A dilation by scale factor \(k\) sends

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(kx,ky)}. \]

Every coordinate is multiplied by the same factor.

Example: Enlargement About the Origin

Dilate

\[ P=(3,-2) \]

by scale factor

\[ k=4. \]

Then

\[ P'=(4\cdot3,\ 4\cdot(-2)). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(12,-8)}. \]

Example: Reduction About the Origin

Dilate

\[ P=(8,6) \]

by

\[ k=\frac12. \]

Then

\[ P'=\left(4,3\right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(4,3)}. \]

Example: Negative Scale Factor

Dilate

\[ P=(2,5) \]

about the origin by

\[ k=-3. \]

Then

\[ P'=(-6,-15). \]

The image is three times as far from the origin and lies on the opposite ray.

Dilation About a General Center

Suppose the center is

\[ C=(h,k_0). \]

To dilate a point \(P=(x,y)\) by scale factor \(s\):

  1. subtract the center coordinates;
  2. multiply the resulting vector by \(s\);
  3. add the center back.

The General Coordinate Formula

A dilation of

\[ P=(x,y) \]

about

\[ C=(h,k_0) \]

by scale factor \(s\) gives

\[ \boxed{ x'=h+s(x-h) } \] \[ \boxed{ y'=k_0+s(y-k_0) }. \]

Example: Dilation About a Non-Origin Center

Dilate

\[ P=(5,4) \]

about

\[ C=(1,2) \]

by scale factor

\[ s=2. \]

First find the vector from the center:

\[ P-C=(4,2). \]

Double it:

\[ 2(4,2)=(8,4). \]

Add the center back:

\[ (1,2)+(8,4)=(9,6). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(9,6)}. \]

Dilating an Entire Figure

To dilate a polygon, apply the same center and scale factor to every vertex.

If triangle

\[ ABC \]

maps to

\[ A'B'C', \]

then each point satisfies the same dilation rule.

Example: Dilating a Triangle

Let

\[ A=(1,1),\qquad B=(4,1),\qquad C=(2,3). \]

Dilate about the origin by

\[ k=2. \]

Then

\[ A'=(2,2), \] \[ B'=(8,2), \] \[ C'=(4,6). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ A'=(2,2),\quad B'=(8,2),\quad C'=(4,6) }. \]

Dilations Preserve Angle Measure

Although a dilation usually changes lengths, it preserves angle measure.

If

\[ \angle ABC \]

maps to

\[ \angle A'B'C', \]

then

\[ \boxed{ m\angle ABC=m\angle A'B'C' }. \]

Dilations Create Similar Figures

Because corresponding angles are equal and corresponding side lengths are proportional, a figure and its dilated image are similar.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle A'B'C' }. \]

Dilation is therefore the fundamental transformation underlying geometric similarity.

How Lengths Scale

If a segment has length \(L\), then after dilation by scale factor \(k\), its image has length

\[ \boxed{L'=|k|L}. \]

The absolute value appears because geometric length is nonnegative.

Example: Scaling a Segment

Suppose

\[ AB=7 \]

and a dilation has scale factor

\[ k=3. \]

Then

\[ A'B'=3(7)=21. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A'B'=21}. \]

How Perimeters Scale

Every side length is multiplied by \(|k|\), so perimeter is also multiplied by \(|k|\).

If the original perimeter is \(P\), then

\[ \boxed{P'=|k|P}. \]

How Areas Scale

Area is two-dimensional, so a dilation by scale factor \(k\) multiplies area by

\[ \boxed{k^2}. \]

If the original area is \(A\), then

\[ \boxed{A'=k^2A}. \]

Why Area Scales by \(k^2\)

Consider a rectangle with side lengths \(a\) and \(b\).

Its area is

\[ A=ab. \]

After dilation, the side lengths become

\[ |k|a \quad\text{and}\quad |k|b. \]

Therefore,

\[ A'=(|k|a)(|k|b)=k^2ab. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{A'=k^2A}. \]

Example: Area Scaling

A figure has area

\[ 12 \]

square units and is dilated by

\[ k=3. \]

The new area is

\[ 3^2(12)=9(12)=108. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A'=108}. \]

How Volumes Scale

Volume is three-dimensional, so a spatial dilation by scale factor \(k\) multiplies volume by

\[ \boxed{|k|^3}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{V'=|k|^3V}. \]

Example: Volume Scaling

A solid has volume

\[ 10 \]

cubic units and is dilated by

\[ k=2. \]

Then

\[ V'=2^3(10)=80. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{V'=80}. \]

The General Dimensional Scaling Law

In \(n\) dimensions, an \(n\)-dimensional measure scales by

\[ \boxed{|k|^n}. \]

Thus:

  • length scales by \(|k|\);
  • area scales by \(k^2\);
  • volume scales by \(|k|^3\);
  • higher-dimensional measure follows the same pattern.

Dilations Preserve Collinearity

If points \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) are collinear, then their images under a dilation are also collinear.

Therefore, dilations preserve straightness and incidence relationships.

Dilations Preserve Betweenness for Positive Scale Factors

For

\[ k>0, \]

the order of points along a line is preserved.

If \(B\) lies between \(A\) and \(C\), then \(B'\) lies between \(A'\) and \(C'\).

Negative Scale Factors and Order

A negative scale factor places points on opposite rays through the center.

The geometry remains similar, but directed order relative to the center is reversed.

This is why negative dilations behave like a positive dilation combined with a half-turn about the center.

Dilations Preserve Parallelism

If two lines are parallel before dilation, their images remain parallel.

Thus,

\[ \ell\parallel m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ell'\parallel m'}. \]

Dilations of Lines Through the Center

If a line passes through the center of dilation, that line maps onto itself as a set.

Individual points usually move, but they remain on the same line through the center.

Dilations of Lines Not Through the Center

A line that does not pass through the center maps to a parallel line.

Therefore, for a nonzero dilation:

\[ \boxed{ \text{line through center}\mapsto\text{same line} } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \text{line not through center}\mapsto\text{parallel line}. } \]

Dilations Are Usually Not Rigid Motions

A rigid motion preserves every distance.

A dilation changes distances by \(|k|\), so it is rigid only when

\[ |k|=1. \]

Therefore, ordinary enlargements and reductions are non-rigid transformations.

Special Case: \(k=1\)

If

\[ k=1, \]

every point remains unchanged.

The dilation is the identity transformation:

\[ \boxed{D_1=I}. \]

Special Case: \(k=-1\)

A dilation with

\[ k=-1 \]

sends every point to the opposite side of the center at the same distance.

This is exactly a

\[ \boxed{180^\circ} \]

rotation about the center.

Special Case: \(k=0\)

If one formally allows

\[ k=0, \]

every point maps to the center:

\[ P\mapsto C. \]

This collapses the entire plane to one point and is not invertible.

Many geometry treatments reserve the word dilation for nonzero scale factors so that distinct points do not all collapse together.

Orientation Under Dilation

For

\[ k>0, \]

orientation is preserved.

For

\[ k<0, \]

the point configuration passes through the center, but in the plane the corresponding linear map still has determinant \(k^2>0\), so orientation is preserved.

A negative planar dilation is a positive dilation followed by a \(180^\circ\) rotation, and rotations preserve orientation.

Dilation as Scalar Multiplication

About the origin, dilation is simply scalar multiplication of vectors.

If

\[ \mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{p}'=k\mathbf{p} }. \]

The Dilation Matrix

A dilation about the origin by scale factor \(k\) has matrix

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} k&0\\ 0&k \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Thus,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} k&0\\ 0&k \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} kx\\ ky \end{bmatrix}. \]

The Determinant and Area Scaling

The determinant of the dilation matrix is

\[ \det \begin{bmatrix} k&0\\ 0&k \end{bmatrix} = k^2. \]

This matches the geometric fact that area scales by

\[ \boxed{k^2}. \]

Inverse Dilations

A nonzero dilation can be undone.

If the original scale factor is

\[ k\neq0, \]

then the inverse scale factor is

\[ \boxed{\frac1k}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{D_k^{-1}=D_{1/k}} \]

when the center is unchanged.

Example: Inverse Dilation

A figure is dilated by

\[ k=5. \]

To return to the original figure, dilate about the same center by

\[ \boxed{\frac15}. \]

Composing Dilations About the Same Center

Dilations about the same center combine by multiplying scale factors.

If the first scale factor is \(a\) and the second is \(b\), then

\[ \boxed{ D_b\circ D_a=D_{ab} }. \]

Example: Composition of Dilations

First dilate by

\[ 3, \]

then by

\[ \frac12. \]

The combined scale factor is

\[ 3\left(\frac12\right)=\frac32. \]

Therefore, the composition is

\[ \boxed{D_{3/2}}. \]

Dilations About the Same Center Commute

Since multiplication of real numbers is commutative,

\[ ab=ba. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ D_a\circ D_b=D_b\circ D_a } \]

when the dilations share the same center.

Dilations and Similarity Transformations

A similarity transformation preserves shape while allowing size to change by a constant scale factor.

A dilation is the basic example.

More general similarity transformations can combine dilation with rigid motions such as translations, rotations, and reflections.

Finding the Scale Factor from Corresponding Lengths

If a segment of length \(L\) maps to a corresponding segment of length \(L'\), then

\[ \boxed{ |k|=\frac{L'}{L} }. \]

Additional positional information is needed to determine whether \(k\) is positive or negative.

Example: Recover the Scale Factor

Suppose

\[ AB=6 \]

and

\[ A'B'=15. \]

Then

\[ |k|=\frac{15}{6}=\frac52. \]

Thus the magnitude of the scale factor is

\[ \boxed{\frac52}. \]

Finding the Scale Factor from Center Distances

If the center \(C\) is known and

\[ P\mapsto P', \]

then

\[ \boxed{ |k|=\frac{CP'}{CP} }. \]

The side of the center on which \(P'\) lies determines the sign.

Finding the Center of Dilation

If a figure and its dilated image are given, corresponding points lie on lines through the center.

Therefore, extend lines through corresponding pairs such as

\[ AA',\qquad BB',\qquad CC'. \]

Their common intersection is the center of dilation.

Why Corresponding-Point Lines Meet at the Center

By definition, each image point lies on the same line through the center as its preimage.

Therefore,

\[ C,\ A,\ A' \]

are collinear,

\[ C,\ B,\ B' \]

are collinear, and so on.

This concurrency provides a practical way to identify the center.

Dilating a Circle

A circle of radius \(r\) dilated by scale factor \(k\) becomes a circle of radius

\[ \boxed{r'=|k|r}. \]

The center of the circle is itself dilated according to the same rule as every other point.

Example: Dilating a Circle About Its Own Center

Suppose a circle has radius

\[ r=5 \]

and is dilated about its center by

\[ k=\frac32. \]

The center remains fixed, while the new radius is

\[ r'=\frac32(5)=\frac{15}{2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{r'=\frac{15}{2}}. \]

Dilations of Function Graphs

Graphs may also be stretched or compressed through dilations.

A vertical dilation by factor \(a\) sends

\[ y=f(x) \]

to

\[ \boxed{y=af(x)}. \]

A horizontal dilation requires changing the input and must be interpreted carefully.

Horizontal Scaling of Graphs

To stretch a graph horizontally by factor \(b>0\), write

\[ \boxed{y=f\left(\frac{x}{b}\right)}. \]

To compress horizontally by factor \(b>1\), write

\[ \boxed{y=f(bx)}. \]

The reciprocal appearance comes from solving for the original input that produces the new horizontal location.

Uniform Versus Nonuniform Scaling

A true geometric dilation uses the same scale factor in every direction.

If horizontal and vertical directions are scaled differently, such as

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(2x,3y), \]

the transformation is not a dilation in the strict Euclidean sense.

It is a nonuniform scaling, which generally does not preserve angles or similarity.

Dilations in Three Dimensions

About the origin, a spatial dilation by scale factor \(k\) sends

\[ \boxed{ (x,y,z)\mapsto(kx,ky,kz) }. \]

Every distance from the origin is multiplied by \(|k|\).

Example: Dilation in Space

Dilate

\[ P=(2,-1,4) \]

by

\[ k=3. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{P'=(6,-3,12)}. \]

Similarity Ratios

In similar figures, the common ratio of corresponding side lengths is the magnitude of the dilation scale factor.

If

\[ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle A'B'C', \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \frac{A'B'}{AB} = \frac{B'C'}{BC} = \frac{C'A'}{CA} = |k| }. \]

Dilations and Map Scale

Maps and scale drawings use dilation conceptually.

If one unit on a drawing represents \(s\) units in reality, then lengths are related by a constant scale factor.

The same proportional reasoning applies to architectural plans, models, diagrams, and scientific visualizations.

Dilations and Measurement Error

When a scaled model is measured, any measurement error in the model is also scaled when converted back to the original size.

If the real object is \(k\) times larger than the model, then an absolute measurement error \(e\) in the model corresponds to approximately

\[ \boxed{|k|e} \]

in the full-scale object.

Common Error: Adding Instead of Multiplying

A dilation changes coordinates multiplicatively, not additively.

About the origin with scale factor \(3\),

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(3x,3y), \]

not

\[ (x+3,y+3). \]

The latter is a translation.

Common Error: Scaling Only One Coordinate

A uniform dilation in the plane multiplies both coordinates by the same scale factor.

The rule

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(2x,y) \]

is not a dilation about the origin because horizontal and vertical directions are scaled differently.

Common Error: Forgetting the Center

The rule

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(kx,ky) \]

applies only when the center is the origin.

For another center, subtract the center, scale, and then add the center back.

Common Error: Scaling Area by \(k\)

Lengths scale by \(|k|\), but area scales by

\[ k^2. \]

For example, doubling all lengths multiplies area by

\[ 2^2=4, \]

not by \(2\).

Common Error: Scaling Volume by \(k^2\)

Volume is three-dimensional, so it scales by

\[ |k|^3. \]

Tripling every linear dimension multiplies volume by

\[ 3^3=27. \]

Common Error: Assuming Dilations Are Rigid

Dilations preserve angles and shape, but generally change lengths.

Therefore, an enlargement or reduction is not a rigid motion unless

\[ |k|=1. \]

Common Error: Confusing Similarity with Congruence

A dilated figure is always similar to its preimage when \(k\neq0\), but it is congruent only when

\[ |k|=1. \]

Similarity preserves shape; congruence preserves both shape and size.

A Reliable Procedure for Dilating Coordinates

  1. Identify the center of dilation.
  2. Identify the scale factor.
  3. If the center is the origin, multiply every coordinate by the scale factor.
  4. If the center is not the origin, subtract the center coordinates first.
  5. Multiply the relative coordinates by the scale factor.
  6. Add the center coordinates back.
  7. Repeat for every point of the figure.

A Reliable Procedure for Checking a Dilation

  1. Verify that corresponding points line up with one common center.
  2. Compare center-to-point distances.
  3. Confirm that all distance ratios have the same magnitude.
  4. Check whether image points lie on the same or opposite rays as required by the sign of \(k\).
  5. Verify that corresponding angles are equal.
  6. Check that corresponding side lengths are proportional.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding the Center and Scale Factor

  1. Draw or extend lines through corresponding point pairs.
  2. Find their common intersection; this is the candidate center.
  3. Measure or compute \(CP\) and \(CP'\) for one corresponding pair.
  4. Compute \(|k|=CP'/CP\).
  5. Determine the sign from whether the image lies on the same or opposite ray.
  6. Verify the same scale factor with another point pair.

Example: Full Coordinate Workflow

Let

\[ A=(1,2),\qquad B=(4,2),\qquad C=(2,5). \]

Dilate the triangle about the origin by

\[ k=\frac32. \]

Then

\[ A'=\left(\frac32,3\right), \] \[ B'=(6,3), \] \[ C'=\left(3,\frac{15}{2}\right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ A'=\left(\frac32,3\right),\quad B'=(6,3),\quad C'=\left(3,\frac{15}{2}\right) }. \]

Example: Verify Side-Length Scaling

In the previous triangle,

\[ AB=3. \]

After dilation,

\[ A'B' = 6-\frac32 = \frac92. \]

The ratio is

\[ \frac{A'B'}{AB} = \frac{9/2}{3} = \frac32. \]

This matches the scale factor.

What to Remember

A dilation resizes a figure from a fixed center using one scale factor.

About the origin,

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(kx,ky) }. \]

The essential facts are:

  • the center of dilation remains fixed;
  • corresponding points lie on lines through the center;
  • distances from the center scale by \(|k|\);
  • all lengths scale by \(|k|\);
  • areas scale by \(k^2\);
  • volumes scale by \(|k|^3\);
  • angle measure is preserved;
  • dilations produce similar figures;
  • dilations are generally not rigid motions;
  • inverse dilations use reciprocal scale factors;
  • compositions about the same center multiply scale factors.

The most useful structural habit is: identify the center and scale factor first, then track every point along its line through the center.

Where We Go Next

Translations, rotations, and reflections preserve distance, while dilations preserve shape and angle measure but allow size to change. Together these transformations provide the foundation for classifying broader geometric motions.
