Composition and Inverse Transformations

Geometric transformations can be performed one after another, combined into a single transformation, or reversed to recover an original figure. Composition provides the language for multi-step motion, while inverse transformations explain how those motions are undone. Together they reveal the algebraic structure behind translations, rotations, reflections, dilations, rigid motions, and similarity transformations.

The Central Idea

A composition of transformations applies two or more transformations in sequence.

If \(T_1\) is applied first and \(T_2\) second, the composition is written

\[ \boxed{T_2\circ T_1}. \]

An inverse transformation reverses a transformation and returns every image point to its original location.

Transformations Behave Like Functions

A geometric transformation sends each input point to an output point.

We may write

\[ T(P)=P'. \]

If another transformation \(S\) is then applied,

\[ S(P')=P''. \]

The complete process is

\[ \boxed{(S\circ T)(P)=S(T(P))}. \]

Read Composition from Right to Left

The notation

\[ S\circ T \]

means:

  1. apply \(T\);
  2. then apply \(S\).

This is the same convention used for composition of ordinary functions.

Example: Translation Followed by Rotation

Let

\[ P=(1,2). \]

First translate by

\[ \langle 3,-1\rangle. \]

Then

\[ P_1=(4,1). \]

Now rotate \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about the origin:

\[ (4,1)\mapsto(-1,4). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P''=(-1,4)}. \]

The Order Usually Matters

In general,

\[ \boxed{S\circ T\neq T\circ S}. \]

Transformation composition is usually noncommutative.

Changing the order may change the final location, orientation, or even the visible type of the combined transformation.

Example: Reverse the Order

Start again with

\[ P=(1,2). \]

First rotate \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise:

\[ (1,2)\mapsto(-2,1). \]

Then translate by

\[ \langle3,-1\rangle: \] \[ (-2,1)\mapsto(1,0). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{P''=(1,0)}. \]

This differs from the previous result \((-1,4)\).

Some Transformations Do Commute

Although composition is not generally commutative, some special pairs do commute.

For example, two translations satisfy

\[ T_{\mathbf{u}}\circ T_{\mathbf{v}} = T_{\mathbf{v}}\circ T_{\mathbf{u}} \]

because vector addition is commutative.

Composition Is Associative

When three transformations are composed,

\[ (T_3\circ T_2)\circ T_1 = T_3\circ(T_2\circ T_1). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ T_3\circ T_2\circ T_1 } \]

may be written without ambiguity about grouping, although the order itself must still be preserved.

The Identity Transformation

The identity transformation leaves every point unchanged.

\[ \boxed{I(P)=P}. \]

It acts as the neutral element for composition:

\[ \boxed{I\circ T=T\circ I=T}. \]

What Is an Inverse Transformation?

If \(T\) sends

\[ P\mapsto P', \]

then its inverse \(T^{-1}\) sends

\[ P'\mapsto P. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ T^{-1}\circ T=I } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ T\circ T^{-1}=I. } \]

Inverse Does Not Mean Reciprocal

The notation

\[ T^{-1} \]

means the inverse transformation.

It does not mean

\[ \frac{1}{T}. \]

This distinction is the same one encountered with inverse functions.

Inverse of a Translation

A translation by vector

\[ \mathbf{v} \]

is undone by translation by

\[ -\mathbf{v}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ T_{\mathbf{v}}^{-1}=T_{-\mathbf{v}} }. \]

Example: Inverse Translation

Suppose

\[ T(x,y)=(x+7,y-4). \]

The translation vector is

\[ \langle7,-4\rangle. \]

Negate it:

\[ \langle-7,4\rangle. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ T^{-1}(x,y)=(x-7,y+4) }. \]

Inverse of a Rotation

A rotation through angle \(\theta\) about a center \(C\) is undone by a rotation through

\[ -\theta \]

about the same center.

\[ \boxed{R_\theta^{-1}=R_{-\theta}}. \]

Equivalent Inverse Rotation Angles

Because rotation angles are equivalent modulo a full turn, the inverse of a \(70^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation may be described as either

\[ -70^\circ \]

or

\[ 290^\circ. \]

Both produce the same inverse motion.

Reflections Are Self-Inverse

Reflecting twice across the same line returns every point to its original position.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{R_\ell^{-1}=R_\ell}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{R_\ell\circ R_\ell=I}. \]

Inverse of a Dilation

A dilation with nonzero scale factor \(k\) is undone by a dilation about the same center with scale factor

\[ \frac1k. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{D_k^{-1}=D_{1/k}}. \]

Why Zero-Scale Collapse Has No Inverse

If a transformation sends every point to one center point, many different inputs have the same output.

The original point cannot be recovered uniquely.

Therefore, a zero-scale collapse is not invertible.

Inverse of a Composition

Suppose

\[ S\circ T \]

applies \(T\) first and \(S\) second.

To undo the process, we must undo \(S\) first and then undo \(T\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ (S\circ T)^{-1} = T^{-1}\circ S^{-1} }. \]

Why the Inverse Order Reverses

Imagine putting on a coat and then a backpack.

To reverse the process, the backpack must come off before the coat.

Transformations behave the same way:

\[ \text{first }T,\text{ then }S \]

is reversed by

\[ \text{first }S^{-1},\text{ then }T^{-1}. \]

Example: Inverse of a Two-Step Transformation

Suppose a transformation:

  1. translates by \(\langle4,-2\rangle\);
  2. then rotates \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about the origin.

To undo it:

  1. rotate \(90^\circ\) clockwise;
  2. then translate by \(\langle-4,2\rangle\).

Composition of Two Translations

Let

\[ T_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{u} \]

and

\[ T_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{v}. \]

Then

\[ T_{\mathbf{v}}(T_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{p})) = \mathbf{p}+\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ T_{\mathbf{v}}\circ T_{\mathbf{u}} = T_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}} }. \]

Example: Combine Two Translations

Translate by

\[ \langle5,3\rangle \]

and then by

\[ \langle-2,7\rangle. \]

Add the vectors:

\[ \langle5,3\rangle+\langle-2,7\rangle = \langle3,10\rangle. \]

Thus the composition is

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x+3,y+10)}. \]

Composition of Rotations About the Same Center

Rotations about one common center combine by adding directed angles:

\[ \boxed{ R_\beta\circ R_\alpha = R_{\alpha+\beta} }. \]

The result may then be reduced modulo

\[ 360^\circ. \]

Example: Combine Rotations

Rotate \(140^\circ\) counterclockwise and then \(250^\circ\) counterclockwise about the same center.

Add:

\[ 140^\circ+250^\circ=390^\circ. \]

Reduce by one full turn:

\[ 390^\circ-360^\circ=30^\circ. \]

Therefore, the composition is

\[ \boxed{30^\circ} \]

counterclockwise about the same center.

Composition of Dilations About the Same Center

Dilations about the same center combine by multiplying scale factors:

\[ \boxed{ D_b\circ D_a=D_{ab} }. \]

Example: Combine Dilations

First dilate by

\[ \frac32, \]

then by

\[ 4. \]

Multiply the scale factors:

\[ \frac32(4)=6. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{D_4\circ D_{3/2}=D_6}. \]

Two Reflections Across Intersecting Lines

If two reflection lines intersect at angle \(\theta\), their composition is a rotation through

\[ \boxed{2\theta}. \]

The intersection of the lines becomes the center of rotation.

The order determines the direction.

Two Reflections Across Parallel Lines

If two reflection lines are parallel and \(d\) units apart, their composition is a translation of magnitude

\[ \boxed{2d}. \]

The translation is perpendicular to the mirror lines.

Reflection Followed by Translation

A reflection followed by a translation parallel to the mirror line can produce a glide reflection.

This is an orientation-reversing rigid motion with no fixed points.

Composition Can Simplify

A long sequence of transformations may sometimes be replaced by one simpler transformation.

Examples include:

  • several translations becoming one translation;
  • several rotations about one center becoming one rotation;
  • two reflections becoming a rotation or translation;
  • a transformation followed by its inverse becoming the identity.

Cancellation

A transformation followed immediately by its inverse cancels:

\[ \boxed{T^{-1}\circ T=I}. \]

Similarly,

\[ \boxed{T\circ T^{-1}=I}. \]

This is the transformation analogue of algebraic cancellation.

Example: Cancel a Translation Pair

Translate by

\[ \langle6,-3\rangle \]

and then by

\[ \langle-6,3\rangle. \]

The net vector is

\[ \langle0,0\rangle. \]

Therefore, the composition is

\[ \boxed{I}. \]

Example: Cancel a Rotation Pair

Rotate \(75^\circ\) counterclockwise about \(C\), then rotate \(75^\circ\) clockwise about the same center.

The directed angles add to

\[ 0^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{R_{-75^\circ}\circ R_{75^\circ}=I}. \]

Repeated Transformations

Repeated composition of one transformation can be written using powers.

For example,

\[ T^2=T\circ T \]

and

\[ T^3=T\circ T\circ T. \]

This notation is especially useful in symmetry and iteration.

Repeated Translation

If \(T_{\mathbf{v}}\) translates by \(\mathbf{v}\), then

\[ \boxed{ T_{\mathbf{v}}^n=T_{n\mathbf{v}} }. \]

Repeating the same translation \(n\) times multiplies its displacement by \(n\).

Repeated Rotation

If \(R_\theta\) rotates about a fixed center, then

\[ \boxed{ R_\theta^n=R_{n\theta} }. \]

Angles are interpreted modulo a full turn.

Repeated Reflection

Since a reflection is self-inverse,

\[ R_\ell^2=I. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ R_\ell^n= \begin{cases} I,&n\text{ even},\\ R_\ell,&n\text{ odd}. \end{cases} } \]

Repeated Dilation

If \(D_k\) is a dilation about a fixed center, then

\[ \boxed{D_k^n=D_{k^n}}. \]

Repeated dilation therefore produces geometric growth or decay of distances.

Coordinate Composition

Coordinate rules can be composed by substitution.

Suppose

\[ T(x,y)=(x+2,y-1) \]

and

\[ R(x,y)=(-y,x). \]

To find

\[ R\circ T, \]

substitute the output of \(T\) into \(R\).

Example: Compose Coordinate Rules

Begin with

\[ T(x,y)=(x+2,y-1). \]

Apply

\[ R(u,v)=(-v,u). \]

Substitute

\[ u=x+2,\qquad v=y-1. \]

Then

\[ (R\circ T)(x,y) = (-(y-1),x+2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ (R\circ T)(x,y)=(1-y,x+2) }. \]

Reverse Coordinate Composition

For the same transformations,

\[ T\circ R \]

gives

\[ R(x,y)=(-y,x), \]

followed by

\[ T(-y,x)=(-y+2,x-1). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ (T\circ R)(x,y)=(2-y,x-1) }. \]

This differs from \(R\circ T\).

Finding an Inverse Coordinate Rule

One reliable method is to:

  1. write the image coordinates;
  2. solve for the original coordinates;
  3. rewrite the result as the inverse rule.

Example: Find an Inverse Rule Algebraically

Suppose

\[ T(x,y)=(2x+3,2y-5). \]

Write

\[ u=2x+3, \qquad v=2y-5. \]

Solve:

\[ x=\frac{u-3}{2}, \] \[ y=\frac{v+5}{2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ T^{-1}(x,y) = \left( \frac{x-3}{2}, \frac{y+5}{2} \right) }. \]

Checking an Inverse by Composition

The strongest check is to compose the candidate inverse with the original transformation.

A correct inverse must satisfy

\[ \boxed{ T^{-1}(T(x,y))=(x,y) } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ T(T^{-1}(x,y))=(x,y). } \]

Matrix Composition

Linear transformations represented by matrices compose through matrix multiplication.

If

\[ T_1(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x} \]

and

\[ T_2(\mathbf{x})=B\mathbf{x}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ (T_2\circ T_1)(\mathbf{x})=BA\mathbf{x} }. \]

Matrix Order Matches Transformation Order

The matrix nearest the vector acts first.

Thus,

\[ BA\mathbf{x} \]

means:

  1. apply \(A\);
  2. then apply \(B\).

This matches ordinary composition notation.

Matrix Multiplication Is Not Generally Commutative

In general,

\[ \boxed{AB\neq BA}. \]

This algebraic fact reflects the geometric reality that the order of transformations usually matters.

Inverse Matrices

If a linear transformation has invertible matrix \(A\), then its inverse transformation has matrix

\[ \boxed{A^{-1}}. \]

By definition,

\[ A^{-1}A=AA^{-1}=I. \]

Inverse of a Rotation Matrix

Rotation matrices are orthogonal, so

\[ R_\theta^{-1}=R_\theta^T. \]

In fact,

\[ \boxed{ R_\theta^{-1}=R_{-\theta} }. \]

Inverse of a Reflection Matrix

A reflection matrix \(M\) satisfies

\[ M^2=I. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{M^{-1}=M}. \]

Affine Composition

Many geometric transformations have affine form

\[ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}. \]

Suppose

\[ T_1(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} \]

and

\[ T_2(\mathbf{x})=C\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ (T_2\circ T_1)(\mathbf{x}) = CA\mathbf{x}+C\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{d} }. \]

Inverse of an Affine Transformation

If

\[ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} \]

and \(A\) is invertible, then

\[ \boxed{ T^{-1}(\mathbf{y}) = A^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}) }. \]

This formula says: first undo the translation, then undo the linear transformation.

Homogeneous Coordinates: A Useful Preview

In computer graphics, affine transformations can be written as matrix multiplications by adding one coordinate.

For example, translation by \(\langle a,b\rangle\) becomes

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&a\\ 0&1&b\\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Multiple transformations can then be composed by multiplying their matrices.

Why Composition Matters in Computer Graphics

A digital object may need to be:

  • scaled;
  • rotated;
  • translated;
  • reflected;
  • positioned relative to another object.

Combining these operations into one transformation matrix makes repeated processing efficient.

Composition and Rigid Motions

The composition of rigid motions is always another rigid motion.

Therefore, if

\[ T_1,\ T_2,\ldots,T_n \]

each preserve distance, then

\[ \boxed{ T_n\circ\cdots\circ T_2\circ T_1 } \]

also preserves distance.

Composition and Similarity Transformations

Similarity transformations also remain similarities under composition.

If their scale factors are

\[ k_1,k_2,\ldots,k_n, \]

the combined scale factor is

\[ \boxed{k_1k_2\cdots k_n}. \]

Inverse Transformations Preserve the Same Family

The inverse of a rigid motion is a rigid motion.

The inverse of a nondegenerate similarity transformation is another similarity transformation.

Thus transformation families remain structurally stable under both composition and inversion.

Transformation Equations Can Be Simplified

Consider

\[ T_{\langle2,1\rangle} \circ T_{\langle-2,-1\rangle}. \]

The vectors cancel, so

\[ \boxed{ T_{\langle2,1\rangle} \circ T_{\langle-2,-1\rangle} =I }. \]

Transformation notation can therefore be manipulated structurally much like algebraic expressions.

But Cancellation Requires the Correct Order

In a noncommutative setting, we cannot freely move transformations past one another just to make inverse pairs adjacent.

From

\[ A\circ B\circ A^{-1}, \]

we cannot simply cancel \(A\) and \(A^{-1}\), because \(B\) lies between them.

Conjugation: A Gentle Preview

An expression of the form

\[ \boxed{T\circ S\circ T^{-1}} \]

is called a conjugate of \(S\).

Geometrically, conjugation often changes the location or orientation of a transformation while preserving its basic type.

This idea becomes important in symmetry groups.

Example: Moving a Rotation Center

A rotation about a point \(C\neq(0,0)\) can be built by:

  1. translating \(C\) to the origin;
  2. rotating about the origin;
  3. translating back.

Symbolically, this has the form

\[ \boxed{ T_C\circ R_\theta\circ T_{-C} }. \]

Example: Moving a Dilation Center

A dilation about a point \(C\) can similarly be constructed by

\[ \boxed{ T_C\circ D_k\circ T_{-C} }. \]

Translate the center to the origin, dilate, then translate back.

Decomposing a Transformation

Sometimes a complicated transformation is easier to understand by breaking it into familiar components.

For example, a similarity might be decomposed into:

  1. a dilation;
  2. a rotation;
  3. a translation.

Decomposition helps reveal scale, orientation, fixed points, and geometric meaning.

Different Decompositions May Describe the Same Map

One transformation can sometimes be expressed by different sequences of simpler transformations.

The important question is not whether the intermediate steps look the same, but whether the final rule sends every point to the same image.

How to Prove Two Compositions Are Equal

To show

\[ S=T, \]

prove that

\[ \boxed{S(P)=T(P)} \]

for every point \(P\) in the domain.

Coordinate rules or transformation theorems can often establish this efficiently.

Example: Two Reflections Equal a Half-Turn

Reflect across the \(x\)-axis:

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x,-y). \]

Then reflect across the \(y\)-axis:

\[ (x,-y)\mapsto(-x,-y). \]

The final rule is

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)}, \]

exactly a \(180^\circ\) rotation about the origin.

Tracking an Entire Figure

For a multi-step transformation of a polygon, apply every stage to every vertex.

A useful organizational pattern is:

\[ A\to A_1\to A_2, \] \[ B\to B_1\to B_2, \] \[ C\to C_1\to C_2. \]

Intermediate labels reduce bookkeeping errors.

Example: Multi-Step Triangle Transformation

Let

\[ A=(1,1),\qquad B=(3,1),\qquad C=(1,4). \]

First reflect across the \(x\)-axis:

\[ A_1=(1,-1), \] \[ B_1=(3,-1), \] \[ C_1=(1,-4). \]

Then translate by

\[ \langle2,5\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ A'=(3,4), \] \[ B'=(5,4), \] \[ C'=(3,1). \]

Finding the Inverse of the Triangle Transformation

The original sequence was:

  1. reflect across the \(x\)-axis;
  2. translate by \(\langle2,5\rangle\).

The inverse sequence is:

  1. translate by \(\langle-2,-5\rangle\);
  2. reflect across the \(x\)-axis.

Applying that inverse to \(A',B',C'\) returns \(A,B,C\).

Common Error: Reading Composition Left to Right

The expression

\[ S\circ T \]

does not mean apply \(S\) first.

It means

\[ \boxed{S(T(P))}, \]

so \(T\) acts first.

Common Error: Assuming Order Never Matters

Translations commute with other translations, but many mixed transformations do not.

Never reverse the order unless a property or calculation proves that the transformations commute.

Common Error: Undoing in the Same Order

If the forward sequence is

\[ T_3\circ T_2\circ T_1, \]

then the inverse is

\[ \boxed{ T_1^{-1}\circ T_2^{-1}\circ T_3^{-1} }. \]

The operations are inverted and their order is reversed.

Common Error: Forgetting the Center

The inverse of a rotation or dilation must use the same center as the original transformation.

Changing the center produces a different transformation.

Common Error: Calling Every Transformation Invertible

A transformation is invertible only when each image point determines one unique original point.

A collapse that sends many points to one point cannot be reversed uniquely.

Common Error: Confusing \(T^{-1}\) with \(1/T\)

The superscript \(-1\) denotes reversal of a transformation.

It is not ordinary numerical reciprocation.

Common Error: Multiplying Matrices in the Wrong Order

If \(A\) acts first and \(B\) acts second, the combined matrix is

\[ \boxed{BA}, \]

not \(AB\).

The rightmost matrix acts first.

Common Error: Canceling Nonadjacent Transformations

In

\[ T\circ S\circ T^{-1}, \]

\(T\) and \(T^{-1}\) cannot simply be removed because \(S\) lies between them.

Cancellation requires the inverse pair to compose directly.

A Reliable Procedure for Composition

  1. Read the composition from right to left.
  2. Write the transformations in execution order.
  3. Apply the first transformation to the point or figure.
  4. Record intermediate coordinates when useful.
  5. Apply the next transformation to the current image.
  6. Continue until all transformations are complete.
  7. Simplify the combined transformation when possible.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding an Inverse Sequence

  1. List the forward transformations in order.
  2. Reverse that order.
  3. Replace each transformation by its inverse.
  4. Keep the same centers or mirror lines where required.
  5. Compose the inverse steps in the reversed order.
  6. Verify that the composition returns the identity.

A Reliable Procedure for Simplifying a Composition

  1. Identify transformations of the same type and compatible center or axis.
  2. Combine translation vectors when possible.
  3. Add rotation angles about a common center.
  4. Multiply dilation factors about a common center.
  5. Recognize standard pairs of reflections.
  6. Cancel adjacent inverse pairs.
  7. Do not reorder transformations without justification.

A Reliable Procedure for Verifying an Inverse

  1. Choose the original transformation \(T\).
  2. Write the proposed inverse \(T^{-1}\).
  3. Compute \(T^{-1}\circ T\).
  4. Confirm that every point returns to itself.
  5. When necessary, also compute \(T\circ T^{-1}\).
  6. Conclude that the inverse is correct only if the identity results.

Example: Full Composition Workflow

Let

\[ P=(2,3). \]

Perform the following:

  1. translate by \(\langle-1,2\rangle\);
  2. rotate \(90^\circ\) clockwise about the origin;
  3. reflect across the \(x\)-axis.

First translation:

\[ (2,3)\mapsto(1,5). \]

Then \(90^\circ\) clockwise:

\[ (1,5)\mapsto(5,-1). \]

Then reflection across the \(x\)-axis:

\[ (5,-1)\mapsto(5,1). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(5,1)}. \]

Example: Construct the Inverse Workflow

The forward steps were:

  1. translate by \(\langle-1,2\rangle\);
  2. rotate \(90^\circ\) clockwise;
  3. reflect across the \(x\)-axis.

Reverse and invert them:

  1. reflect across the \(x\)-axis;
  2. rotate \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise;
  3. translate by \(\langle1,-2\rangle\).

Applying these steps to \((5,1)\) returns

\[ \boxed{(2,3)}. \]

What to Remember

Composition applies transformations in sequence:

\[ \boxed{ (S\circ T)(P)=S(T(P)) }. \]

Inverse transformations undo those sequences:

\[ \boxed{ T^{-1}\circ T=T\circ T^{-1}=I }. \]

The essential facts are:

  • composition is read from right to left;
  • the order of transformations usually matters;
  • composition is associative;
  • the identity transformation changes nothing;
  • inverse translations use opposite vectors;
  • inverse rotations use opposite angles about the same center;
  • reflections are self-inverse;
  • inverse dilations use reciprocal scale factors;
  • the inverse of a composition reverses both operations and order;
  • compatible transformations can often be combined into one simpler transformation;
  • matrix multiplication provides an algebraic model of transformation composition.

The most important operational rule is: perform a composition in the stated order, but undo it in the reverse order.

Where We Go Next

Composition tells us how transformations combine, while inverses tell us how those transformations are reversed. The next step is to examine what remains unchanged even while points move.
