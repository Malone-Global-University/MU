Line, Rotational, and Translational Symmetry

A figure has symmetry when a transformation moves its points but leaves the figure as a whole unchanged. Reflection produces line symmetry, rotation produces rotational symmetry, and translation produces translational symmetry. These patterns reveal structure, repetition, balance, and invariance across geometry, art, architecture, crystallography, tiling, algebra, and mathematical classification.

The Central Idea

A symmetry of a figure is a transformation that maps the figure onto itself.

If \(F\) is a figure and \(T\) is a transformation, then \(T\) is a symmetry of \(F\) when

\[ \boxed{T(F)=F}. \]

Individual points may move, but the complete figure remains unchanged as a set.

Symmetry Is Setwise Invariance

A symmetric figure does not require every point to remain fixed.

For example, under a \(90^\circ\) rotation of a square, each vertex moves to another vertex.

Yet the square still occupies exactly the same set of points.

Thus symmetry is fundamentally a form of setwise invariance.

The Identity Symmetry

Every figure has at least one symmetry: the identity transformation.

\[ \boxed{I(F)=F}. \]

The identity moves nothing, so it always maps a figure onto itself.

When discussing nontrivial symmetry, we usually look for transformations other than the identity.

Three Major Symmetry Types

This lesson focuses on:

  • line symmetry from reflection;
  • rotational symmetry from rotation;
  • translational symmetry from translation.

A single figure or pattern may possess more than one type.

Line Symmetry

A figure has line symmetry, or reflection symmetry, when reflection across a line maps the figure onto itself.

If \(\ell\) is such a line, then

\[ \boxed{R_\ell(F)=F}. \]

The line \(\ell\) is called a line of symmetry or axis of symmetry.

What a Line of Symmetry Does

For every point \(P\) on one side of the symmetry line, there is a matching point \(P'\) on the other side.

The symmetry line is the perpendicular bisector of

\[ \overline{PP'}. \]

Points lying directly on the symmetry line remain fixed.

Example: Isosceles Triangle

A non-equilateral isosceles triangle has one line of symmetry.

It passes through:

  • the vertex between the equal sides;
  • the midpoint of the base.

Along this line, the two equal sides exchange places under reflection.

Example: Equilateral Triangle

An equilateral triangle has

\[ \boxed{3} \]

lines of symmetry.

Each line passes through one vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.

Example: Square

A square has

\[ \boxed{4} \]

lines of symmetry.

Two pass through opposite vertices, and two pass through midpoints of opposite sides.

Example: Rectangle

A non-square rectangle has

\[ \boxed{2} \]

lines of symmetry.

They pass through the midpoints of opposite sides.

The diagonals are not symmetry lines unless the rectangle is a square.

Example: Rhombus

A non-square rhombus has

\[ \boxed{2} \]

lines of symmetry.

These are its diagonals.

Example: Regular Polygons

A regular \(n\)-gon has

\[ \boxed{n} \]

lines of reflection symmetry.

The arrangement of the axes depends on whether \(n\) is even or odd.

Regular Polygons with Odd \(n\)

If \(n\) is odd, each symmetry axis passes through:

  • one vertex;
  • the midpoint of the opposite side.

There are \(n\) such axes.

Regular Polygons with Even \(n\)

If \(n\) is even, half of the symmetry axes pass through pairs of opposite vertices, while the other half pass through midpoints of opposite sides.

The total number remains

\[ \boxed{n}. \]

Circles Have Infinitely Many Lines of Symmetry

Every diameter of a circle lies on a line of reflection symmetry.

Since infinitely many lines pass through the center,

\[ \boxed{\text{a circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry}}. \]

Testing Line Symmetry with Coordinates

A figure is symmetric across the \(y\)-axis when reflection

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,y) \]

leaves the figure unchanged as a set.

Similarly, symmetry across the \(x\)-axis uses

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x,-y). \]

Even Functions and \(y\)-Axis Symmetry

A function \(f\) is even when

\[ \boxed{f(-x)=f(x)}. \]

This means the graph is invariant under reflection across the \(y\)-axis.

Example: \(y=x^2\)

For

\[ f(x)=x^2, \]

we have

\[ f(-x)=(-x)^2=x^2=f(x). \]

Therefore, the parabola has \(y\)-axis symmetry.

Rotational Symmetry

A figure has rotational symmetry when a rotation by some angle smaller than a full turn maps the figure onto itself.

If \(R_\theta\) is such a rotation, then

\[ \boxed{R_\theta(F)=F}. \]

The Center of Rotational Symmetry

A rotational symmetry occurs about a fixed point called the center of rotational symmetry.

For regular polygons, this point is the ordinary geometric center.

Order of Rotational Symmetry

The order of rotational symmetry is the number of distinct orientations during one full turn that map the figure onto itself.

If the smallest positive symmetry angle is \(\theta\), then

\[ \boxed{ n=\frac{360^\circ}{\theta} }. \]

Smallest Positive Rotation Angle

If the rotational symmetry has order \(n\), then the smallest positive rotation angle is

\[ \boxed{ \theta_{\min}=\frac{360^\circ}{n} }. \]

Example: Equilateral Triangle

An equilateral triangle maps onto itself under rotations of

\[ 120^\circ,\qquad240^\circ,\qquad360^\circ. \]

Its rotational symmetry has order

\[ \boxed{3}. \]

Example: Square

A square maps onto itself after

\[ 90^\circ,\quad180^\circ,\quad270^\circ,\quad360^\circ. \]

Therefore, its rotational symmetry has order

\[ \boxed{4}. \]

Example: Rectangle

A non-square rectangle maps onto itself after a half-turn:

\[ 180^\circ. \]

Therefore, its rotational symmetry has order

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

Example: Regular \(n\)-Gon

A regular \(n\)-gon has rotational symmetry of order

\[ \boxed{n}. \]

Its smallest positive symmetry angle is

\[ \boxed{\frac{360^\circ}{n}}. \]

Circles Have Continuous Rotational Symmetry

A circle maps onto itself under every rotation about its center.

Therefore, a circle has rotational symmetry for every angle

\[ \theta. \]

This is sometimes described as continuous rotational symmetry.

Half-Turn Symmetry

A figure has central symmetry when a \(180^\circ\) rotation maps it onto itself.

The rotation center is then called a center of symmetry.

Parallelograms Have Half-Turn Symmetry

Every parallelogram has rotational symmetry of order

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

Its center of symmetry is the intersection of its diagonals.

Odd Functions and Half-Turn Symmetry

A function \(f\) is odd when

\[ \boxed{f(-x)=-f(x)}. \]

This means the graph is invariant under a \(180^\circ\) rotation about the origin.

Example: \(y=x^3\)

For

\[ f(x)=x^3, \]

we have

\[ f(-x)=(-x)^3=-x^3=-f(x). \]

Therefore, the graph has half-turn symmetry about the origin.

Testing Rotational Symmetry with Coordinates

To test a \(180^\circ\) rotational symmetry about the origin, apply

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)}. \]

If the transformed figure is the same set, then the figure has origin symmetry.

Translational Symmetry

A figure or pattern has translational symmetry when a nonzero translation maps it onto itself.

If

\[ T_{\mathbf{v}}(F)=F \]

for some

\[ \mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}, \]

then \(\mathbf{v}\) is a translation symmetry vector.

Finite Figures Usually Lack Translational Symmetry

A bounded finite figure cannot usually map onto itself after a nonzero translation because the entire figure shifts to a different location.

Translational symmetry is most common in infinite repeating patterns.

Repeating One-Dimensional Patterns

Consider an infinite pattern repeated every \(d\) units along a line.

Translation by

\[ \boxed{\langle d,0\rangle} \]

maps the pattern onto itself.

So do translations by integer multiples:

\[ \boxed{\langle nd,0\rangle,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}}. \]

The Fundamental Translation

In a one-dimensional repeating pattern, the smallest positive translation distance that maps the pattern onto itself is often called a fundamental period or fundamental translation length.

All larger translation symmetries may be generated by integer multiples of this displacement.

Translational Symmetry in the Plane

A two-dimensional periodic pattern may have two independent translation vectors

\[ \mathbf{u} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf{v}. \]

Then translations by

\[ \boxed{m\mathbf{u}+n\mathbf{v}} \]

for integers \(m,n\) also preserve the pattern.

Lattice Structure

The set

\[ \{m\mathbf{u}+n\mathbf{v}:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\} \]

forms a repeating grid of displacement vectors called a lattice.

Lattices provide the geometric backbone of many tessellations and crystal patterns.

Example: Square Grid

A square grid with spacing \(a\) is invariant under translations such as

\[ \langle a,0\rangle \]

and

\[ \langle0,a\rangle. \]

Therefore, it is also invariant under

\[ \boxed{\langle ma,na\rangle} \]

for any integers \(m,n\).

Translational Symmetry and Periodic Functions

A function \(f\) is periodic when there exists \(T>0\) such that

\[ \boxed{f(x+T)=f(x)} \]

for every \(x\) in its domain.

Geometrically, its graph is invariant under horizontal translation by

\[ \langle T,0\rangle. \]

Example: Sine

Since

\[ \sin(x+2\pi)=\sin x, \]

the graph of sine has horizontal translational symmetry by

\[ \boxed{\langle2\pi,0\rangle}. \]

Fundamental Period of a Function

The smallest positive period, when it exists, is called the fundamental period.

For sine and cosine, the fundamental period is

\[ \boxed{2\pi}. \]

Symmetry Types Can Coexist

A pattern may have line, rotational, and translational symmetry simultaneously.

For example, a square tiling of the plane has:

  • many reflection axes;
  • rotational centers;
  • horizontal and vertical translation symmetries.

A Single Figure Can Have Several Symmetries

A square has:

  • four reflection symmetries;
  • four rotational symmetries when the identity is included.

These transformations are distinct but all preserve the same figure.

Counting Symmetries of a Regular Polygon

A regular \(n\)-gon has:

  • \(n\) rotations that map it onto itself, including the identity;
  • \(n\) reflections.

Therefore, it has

\[ \boxed{2n} \]

rigid symmetries in total.

Example: Symmetries of a Square

For a square,

\[ n=4. \]

Therefore, it has

\[ 2n=8 \]

rigid symmetries:

  • 4 rotations;
  • 4 reflections.

Symmetry Transformations Can Be Composed

If \(S\) and \(T\) are symmetries of the same figure \(F\), then

\[ S(F)=F \]

and

\[ T(F)=F. \]

Therefore,

\[ (S\circ T)(F)=S(T(F))=S(F)=F. \]

So the composition is also a symmetry.

Inverse Symmetries

If \(T\) maps a figure onto itself, then \(T^{-1}\) also maps the figure onto itself.

Therefore, symmetries are closed under inversion as well as composition.

Symmetry and Group Structure: A Preview

The symmetries of a figure satisfy four important properties:

  • composition stays within the symmetry set;
  • composition is associative;
  • the identity symmetry is present;
  • every symmetry has an inverse symmetry.

These are exactly the structural ingredients of a group.

Why Symmetry Groups Matter

Symmetry groups organize all the ways a figure can remain unchanged under transformations.

Rather than studying individual symmetries separately, group structure studies how they combine.

This will become the culminating idea of this transformation unit.

Line Symmetry and Orientation

Reflection symmetry reverses orientation.

A clockwise traversal of a symmetric figure becomes counterclockwise after reflection.

The figure as a set remains unchanged, even though its oriented structure is reversed.

Rotational Symmetry and Orientation

Rotations preserve orientation.

Therefore, rotational symmetries preserve clockwise and counterclockwise ordering.

Translational Symmetry and Orientation

Translations preserve orientation.

A repeating pattern shifted by a symmetry translation retains the same directional arrangement.

Symmetry and Fixed Points

Different symmetry types have different fixed-point behavior.

  • reflection symmetry: every point on the mirror line is fixed;
  • nontrivial rotational symmetry: the center is fixed;
  • nonzero translational symmetry: no point is fixed.

Fixed-point structure helps identify the symmetry type.

Symmetry and Invariant Sets

Every symmetry preserves the complete figure as an invariant set:

\[ \boxed{T(F)=F}. \]

Smaller subsets may also be invariant, such as:

  • a reflection axis;
  • a rotation center;
  • an orbit of vertices under repeated rotations;
  • a repeating row within a translational pattern.

Orbits Under Symmetry

Repeatedly applying symmetries can move one point through a collection of equivalent positions.

That collection is called the point's orbit.

For a square, repeated \(90^\circ\) rotations move one vertex through all four vertices.

Example: Orbit of a Square Vertex

Label the vertices

\[ A,B,C,D. \]

Under repeated \(90^\circ\) rotations,

\[ A\mapsto B\mapsto C\mapsto D\mapsto A. \]

The orbit of \(A\) is

\[ \boxed{\{A,B,C,D\}}. \]

Symmetry in Tessellations

Tessellations cover the plane without gaps or overlaps.

Their repeating structure often produces:

  • translation symmetries;
  • rotation centers;
  • reflection axes;
  • glide-reflection symmetries.

The full classification of such repeating symmetries leads to wallpaper groups.

Symmetry in Nature and Design

Symmetry appears in:

  • flowers and biological forms;
  • crystals and molecular structures;
  • architecture and ornament;
  • logos and graphic design;
  • textiles and repeating patterns;
  • engineering components.

Mathematical symmetry provides a precise language for describing these structures.

Testing Symmetry by Transformation

Visual resemblance is useful, but a mathematical symmetry test asks whether a precise transformation leaves the figure unchanged.

The correct criterion is

\[ \boxed{T(F)=F}. \]

Testing a Candidate Reflection Axis

To verify a proposed line of symmetry:

  1. reflect representative points across the line;
  2. check that their images lie on the original figure;
  3. verify the same for the entire structure.

One matching pair is not enough.

Testing a Candidate Rotation

To verify rotational symmetry:

  1. identify the center;
  2. apply the proposed rotation angle;
  3. verify that every feature maps onto a corresponding feature;
  4. confirm that the full figure is unchanged as a set.

Testing a Candidate Translation

To verify translational symmetry:

  1. identify the displacement vector;
  2. translate the entire pattern;
  3. check that the shifted pattern coincides exactly with the original.

The vector must be nonzero for nontrivial translational symmetry.

Common Error: Calling Any Balanced Figure Symmetric

Informal balance is not enough.

Mathematical symmetry requires a specific transformation that maps the figure onto itself.

Common Error: Counting the Identity as a Nontrivial Rotation

A full \(360^\circ\) turn is always a symmetry, but rotational symmetry is usually considered nontrivial only when some smaller positive angle also works.

Common Error: Confusing Number of Symmetry Lines with Rotational Order

These quantities happen to agree for regular polygons, but they are conceptually different.

A non-square rectangle has:

\[ 2 \]

reflection axes and rotational order

\[ 2. \]

Other figures need not match this way.

Common Error: Assuming Every Figure Has Translational Symmetry

A nonzero translation moves a bounded finite figure away from its original position.

Translational symmetry is therefore mainly associated with infinite or periodic patterns.

Common Error: Confusing Period with Amplitude

For periodic graphs, the period measures horizontal repetition.

Amplitude measures vertical size.

Translational symmetry is connected to period, not amplitude.

Common Error: Assuming Symmetric Points Are Fixed Points

Under reflection, paired points usually exchange positions rather than stay fixed.

Only points on the reflection axis are fixed.

Common Error: Ignoring the Transformation Center

Rotational symmetry depends on the correct center.

A rotation about the wrong point may fail even if the angle itself is correct.

A Reliable Procedure for Line Symmetry

  1. Propose a candidate axis.
  2. Reflect key points across the axis.
  3. Check perpendicular-bisector relationships.
  4. Verify that every reflected feature coincides with the original figure.
  5. Count all distinct valid axes.

A Reliable Procedure for Rotational Symmetry

  1. Identify a candidate center.
  2. Determine the smallest angle that might map the figure onto itself.
  3. Rotate all major features by that angle.
  4. Verify exact coincidence.
  5. Compute the order using \(360^\circ/\theta_{\min}\).

A Reliable Procedure for Translational Symmetry

  1. Locate a repeated feature in the pattern.
  2. Measure the displacement to the next matching feature.
  3. Use that displacement as a candidate translation vector.
  4. Translate the entire pattern.
  5. Confirm exact overlap.
  6. Look for smaller nonzero vectors that might also work.

A Reliable Procedure for Classifying Symmetry

  1. Ask whether a reflection maps the figure onto itself.
  2. Ask whether a nontrivial rotation maps it onto itself.
  3. Ask whether a nonzero translation maps it onto itself.
  4. Record all successful transformation types.
  5. Separate finite symmetry from periodic symmetry when appropriate.

Example: Full Symmetry Analysis of a Square

A square has four reflection axes:

  • two diagonals;
  • two lines through midpoints of opposite sides.

It also has rotational symmetry under

\[ 90^\circ,\quad180^\circ,\quad270^\circ,\quad360^\circ. \]

Therefore, its rotational order is

\[ \boxed{4}. \]

As a bounded finite figure, it has no nonzero translational symmetry.

Example: Full Symmetry Analysis of a Square Grid

An infinite square grid has:

  • horizontal and vertical reflection axes;
  • diagonal reflection axes through suitable grid points;
  • rotational centers of order \(4\) at grid intersections and cell centers;
  • translations by integer combinations of the grid spacing vectors.

This illustrates how several symmetry types can coexist in a periodic pattern.

What to Remember

A symmetry is a transformation that leaves a figure unchanged as a set:

\[ \boxed{T(F)=F}. \]

The essential facts are:

  • line symmetry comes from reflection;
  • rotational symmetry comes from rotation;
  • translational symmetry comes from nonzero translation;
  • a line of symmetry acts as a reflection axis;
  • rotational order counts matching orientations in one full turn;
  • the smallest rotation angle is \(360^\circ/n\) for order \(n\);
  • translational symmetry is characteristic of repeating patterns;
  • periodic functions exhibit translational symmetry in their graphs;
  • multiple symmetry types can coexist;
  • symmetries are closed under composition and inversion;
  • the full set of symmetries of a figure forms a group.

The most important structural idea is: symmetry means that a nontrivial transformation can move the parts while leaving the whole unchanged.
