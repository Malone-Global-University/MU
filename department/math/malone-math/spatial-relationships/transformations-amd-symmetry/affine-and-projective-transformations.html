Affine and Projective Transformations

Rigid motions preserve distance, similarities preserve shape up to scale, and affine transformations preserve straight-line structure and parallelism. Projective transformations go further, modeling perspective and preserving incidence even when parallel lines appear to meet. Together these transformation families form a hierarchy that explains which geometric properties survive as transformations become more general.

The Central Idea

Different transformation families preserve different amounts of geometric structure.

A useful hierarchy is

\[ \boxed{ \text{isometries} \subset \text{similarities} \subset \text{affine transformations} \subset \text{projective transformations} }. \]

As we move to the right, more transformations are allowed, but fewer Euclidean properties remain invariant.

What Is an Affine Transformation?

An affine transformation in the plane has the form

\[ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} } \]

where \(A\) is a \(2\times2\) matrix and \(\mathbf{b}\) is a translation vector.

For a nondegenerate affine transformation, \(A\) is invertible.

Coordinate Form

Writing

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} a&b\\ c&d \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} e\\ f \end{bmatrix}, \]

gives

\[ \boxed{ x'=ax+by+e } \] \[ \boxed{ y'=cx+dy+f }. \]

Affine Transformations Include Familiar Transformations

Affine transformations include:

  • translations;
  • rotations;
  • reflections;
  • dilations;
  • nonuniform scalings;
  • shears;
  • compositions of these transformations.

What Affine Transformations Preserve

Nondegenerate affine transformations preserve:

  • points;
  • lines;
  • collinearity;
  • parallelism;
  • ratios of directed segments along the same line;
  • midpoints;
  • concurrency of lines.

What Affine Transformations Need Not Preserve

General affine transformations do not necessarily preserve:

  • length;
  • angle measure;
  • perpendicularity;
  • circles;
  • shape in the Euclidean similarity sense;
  • area exactly.

Lines Map to Lines

One of the defining geometric features of affine transformations is that straight lines remain straight.

If \(P\) and \(Q\) lie on a line and

\[ X=(1-t)P+tQ, \]

then

\[ T(X) = (1-t)T(P)+tT(Q). \]

Thus affine combinations remain affine combinations.

Affine Combinations

An affine combination of points \(P_1,\ldots,P_n\) has the form

\[ \boxed{ \lambda_1P_1+\cdots+\lambda_nP_n } \]

where

\[ \boxed{ \lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_n=1. } \]

Affine transformations preserve such combinations.

Midpoints Are Preserved

The midpoint of \(P\) and \(Q\) is

\[ M=\frac12P+\frac12Q. \]

Since the coefficients sum to \(1\),

\[ T(M) = \frac12T(P)+\frac12T(Q). \]

Therefore, \(T(M)\) is the midpoint of the transformed segment.

Ratios Along a Line Are Preserved

Suppose \(B\) divides segment \(AC\) in a fixed directed ratio.

Because affine transformations preserve affine combinations, that same directed ratio is preserved after transformation.

This is stronger than midpoint preservation and weaker than full distance preservation.

Parallelism Is Preserved

Let two lines have the same direction vector \(\mathbf{v}\).

Under the linear part \(A\), both direction vectors become

\[ A\mathbf{v}. \]

Therefore, the transformed lines remain parallel.

Thus

\[ \boxed{ \ell\parallel m \Longrightarrow \ell'\parallel m' }. \]

Nonuniform Scaling

Consider

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(2x,3y) }. \]

Horizontal lengths are multiplied by \(2\), while vertical lengths are multiplied by \(3\).

This is affine but not a Euclidean similarity because one common scale factor does not apply in all directions.

Example: Nonuniform Scaling

Apply

\[ T(x,y)=(2x,3y) \]

to

\[ P=(1,2). \]

Then

\[ \boxed{P'=(2,6)}. \]

Shears

A shear slants a figure while preserving straight lines and parallelism.

A horizontal shear has the form

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x+ky,y) }. \]

Horizontal Shear Matrix

The matrix is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} 1&k\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Horizontal lines remain horizontal, while other lines may tilt.

Vertical Shear

A vertical shear has the form

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x,y+kx) }. \]

Its matrix is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ k&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Example: Horizontal Shear

Let

\[ T(x,y)=(x+2y,y). \]

Then

\[ T(1,3)=(1+6,3). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{T(1,3)=(7,3)}. \]

A Square Can Become a Parallelogram

Under a shear, a square may become a nonrectangular parallelogram.

The transformation preserves:

  • opposite-side parallelism;
  • midpoints;
  • collinearity.

But right angles and side lengths may change.

Area Scaling Under Affine Transformations

If the linear part is \(A\), planar area is multiplied by

\[ \boxed{|\det A|}. \]

Thus affine transformations generally scale area by one constant factor throughout the plane.

Example: Area Scale Factor

Let

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} 2&1\\ 0&3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \det A=6. \]

Therefore, every planar area is multiplied by

\[ \boxed{6}. \]

Orientation Under Affine Transformations

For an invertible affine transformation:

  • \(\det A>0\): orientation is preserved;
  • \(\det A<0\): orientation is reversed.

The determinant sign therefore distinguishes direct and opposite affine transformations.

Degenerate Affine Transformations

If

\[ \det A=0, \]

the transformation collapses dimension.

A plane may collapse to a line or point.

Such a map is affine but not invertible.

Affine Inverses

If

\[ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} \]

with \(A\) invertible, then

\[ \boxed{ T^{-1}(\mathbf{y}) = A^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}) }. \]

Composition of Affine Transformations

Suppose

\[ T_1(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} \]

and

\[ T_2(\mathbf{x})=C\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ (T_2\circ T_1)(\mathbf{x}) = CA\mathbf{x}+C\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{d} }. \]

The composition is again affine.

Affine Transformations Preserve Parallelograms

Because parallel lines remain parallel, a parallelogram maps to another parallelogram under a nondegenerate affine transformation.

Rectangles may become slanted, but opposite sides remain parallel.

Circles Need Not Remain Circles

Under nonuniform scaling or shear, a circle may become an ellipse.

Thus circularity is not an affine invariant.

Conic Sections Under Affine Maps

Affine transformations send nondegenerate conic sections to conic sections of the same broad affine type.

For example, ellipses remain ellipses, while parabolas remain parabolas under invertible affine transformations.

What Is a Projective Transformation?

A projective transformation, or projectivity, is a more general transformation that preserves incidence relationships between points and lines.

It models perspective effects in which parallel lines may appear to converge.

Incidence Is the Core Projective Invariant

If a point \(P\) lies on a line \(\ell\), then after a projective transformation,

\[ \boxed{ P\in\ell \Longrightarrow P'\in\ell' }. \]

Collinearity and concurrence remain meaningful even when metric geometry changes dramatically.

Parallelism Is Not Projectively Invariant

In Euclidean geometry, distinct parallel lines never meet.

Under perspective projection, they may appear to meet at a vanishing point.

Therefore, ordinary parallelism is not preserved by general projective transformations.

Perspective as Projection

Imagine projecting points from a center of projection onto an image plane.

Straight lines project to straight lines, but lengths and angles generally change.

This geometric process motivates projective transformations.

Vanishing Points

Parallel lines in three-dimensional space may project to lines that meet in an image.

Their apparent meeting point is called a vanishing point.

Different families of parallel lines may have different vanishing points.

Horizon Line

In perspective geometry, vanishing points associated with horizontal directions in a scene often lie on a common horizon line.

This is a projective-geometric explanation of a familiar visual effect.

Projective Geometry Adds Points at Infinity

One elegant way to treat parallel lines is to imagine that each parallel family meets at a point at infinity.

This extends the Euclidean plane into the projective plane.

In that setting, every pair of distinct lines meets in exactly one projective point.

Homogeneous Coordinates

Projective geometry is naturally expressed using homogeneous coordinates.

The ordinary point

\[ (x,y) \]

can be represented by

\[ \boxed{[x:y:1]}. \]

Equivalent Homogeneous Coordinates

Homogeneous coordinates are defined only up to a nonzero common scale.

Thus

\[ [X:Y:W] \]

and

\[ \boxed{[\lambda X:\lambda Y:\lambda W]} \]

represent the same projective point whenever

\[ \lambda\neq0. \]

Recovering Ordinary Coordinates

If

\[ W\neq0, \]

the projective point

\[ [X:Y:W] \]

corresponds to the ordinary Cartesian point

\[ \boxed{ \left(\frac{X}{W},\frac{Y}{W}\right) }. \]

Points at Infinity

Homogeneous points with

\[ W=0 \]

represent directions at infinity.

These points provide a projective home for the vanishing directions of Euclidean parallel lines.

Projective Transformation Matrix

A planar projective transformation can be represented by an invertible \(3\times3\) matrix:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} X'\\ Y'\\ W' \end{bmatrix} = H \begin{bmatrix} X\\ Y\\ W \end{bmatrix} }. \]

The matrix \(H\) is defined up to a nonzero scalar multiple.

General Planar Homography

Let

\[ H= \begin{bmatrix} a&b&c\\ d&e&f\\ g&h&i \end{bmatrix}. \]

For ordinary coordinates \((x,y)\), the transformed point is

\[ \boxed{ x'= \frac{ax+by+c}{gx+hy+i} } \] \[ \boxed{ y'= \frac{dx+ey+f}{gx+hy+i} }. \]

Why Projective Rules Look Fractional

The denominator

\[ gx+hy+i \]

comes from converting homogeneous coordinates back to ordinary Cartesian coordinates.

When \(g=h=0\), the denominator is constant and the transformation reduces to an affine transformation.

Affine Transformations as Projective Transformations

An affine transformation

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(ax+by+c,dx+ey+f) \]

can be represented projectively by

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} a&b&c\\ d&e&f\\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{affine transformations} \subset \text{projective transformations}. } \]

Translations in Homogeneous Coordinates

Translation by

\[ \langle a,b\rangle \]

has homogeneous matrix

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&a\\ 0&1&b\\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

This allows translation to be composed by ordinary matrix multiplication.

Rotation in Homogeneous Coordinates

Rotation through \(\theta\) about the origin is represented by

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Scaling in Homogeneous Coordinates

Uniform or nonuniform scaling can be written

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} s_x&0&0\\ 0&s_y&0\\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

When

\[ s_x=s_y, \]

the scaling is uniform.

Composition Through Homogeneous Matrices

If \(H_1\) acts first and \(H_2\) second, the combined transformation is

\[ \boxed{H_2H_1}. \]

This unifies translation, rotation, scaling, shear, and perspective transformations in one matrix framework.

Projective Transformations Preserve Cross Ratio

One of the fundamental numerical invariants of projective geometry is the cross ratio of four collinear points.

For suitable coordinates \(a,b,c,d\), one common form is

\[ \boxed{ (A,B;C,D) = \frac{(c-a)(d-b)}{(c-b)(d-a)} }. \]

Unlike ordinary distance ratios, the cross ratio is preserved by projective transformations.

Why Cross Ratio Matters

Projective transformations may distort ordinary lengths dramatically.

The cross ratio survives those distortions, providing a numerical invariant suited to projective geometry.

This is a deeper analogue of distance invariants in Euclidean geometry.

Conics Under Projective Transformations

Projective transformations map nondegenerate conics to nondegenerate conics.

From a projective viewpoint, circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas belong to one broader conic family.

Perspective Can Change Conic Type

A circle viewed under perspective may appear as an ellipse.

More generally, projective transformations may convert one affine conic type into another while preserving the larger projective conic structure.

Affine Versus Projective Preservation

Affine geometry preserves parallelism.

Projective geometry does not.

Both preserve:

  • points;
  • lines;
  • collinearity;
  • incidence;
  • concurrency.

Comparing the Four Main Families

A useful summary is:

  • isometries: preserve distance and angle;
  • similarities: preserve angle and length ratios;
  • affine transformations: preserve lines and parallelism;
  • projective transformations: preserve incidence and cross ratio.

Metric Structure Versus Incidence Structure

Euclidean geometry emphasizes metric quantities such as distance and angle.

Projective geometry strips away much of that metric information and focuses on relationships such as:

  • which points lie on which lines;
  • which lines intersect;
  • how configurations correspond under projection.

Affine Coordinates and Barycentric Reasoning

Affine combinations provide the basis for barycentric-style descriptions of points inside triangles and other simplices.

If

\[ \lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3=1, \]

then

\[ P=\lambda_1A+\lambda_2B+\lambda_3C \]

is transformed using the same coefficients under any affine map.

Centroids Are Affine Invariants

The centroid of triangle \(ABC\) is

\[ G=\frac13A+\frac13B+\frac13C. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ T(G) = \frac13T(A)+\frac13T(B)+\frac13T(C) }. \]

An affine transformation sends a triangle centroid to the centroid of the transformed triangle.

Affine Images of Coordinate Grids

A rectangular coordinate grid may become a slanted parallelogram grid under an affine transformation.

Straightness and parallel families survive even though right angles may not.

Projective Images of Coordinate Grids

Under a projective transformation, parallel grid lines may converge toward vanishing points.

The grid can appear to narrow with distance, modeling visual perspective.

One-Point Perspective

In one-point perspective, one principal family of parallel lines converges to a single vanishing point.

Lines parallel to the picture plane may remain parallel in the image.

Two-Point Perspective

In two-point perspective, two principal direction families converge to two different vanishing points.

This is common when viewing the corner of a rectangular object.

Three-Point Perspective

In three-point perspective, three principal direction families converge to three vanishing points.

This can model views looking strongly upward or downward as well as across a scene.

Affine Transformations in Computer Graphics

Computer graphics uses affine transformations for:

  • moving objects;
  • rotating them;
  • resizing them;
  • shearing them;
  • placing them in scenes.

Projective Transformations in Imaging

Projective transformations are used in:

  • camera modeling;
  • image rectification;
  • perspective correction;
  • document scanning;
  • panorama construction;
  • computer vision.

Example: Perspective Correction

A photographed rectangular page may appear as a quadrilateral because of perspective.

A projective transformation can map the four visible corners to the corners of a rectangle, producing a front-facing corrected image.

Four Point Correspondences

In general position, four point correspondences in the plane can determine a projective transformation up to scale.

This is why selecting four corners is sufficient for many perspective correction tasks.

Affine Transformations Need Fewer Constraints

An affine transformation in the plane has six effective parameters.

Three noncollinear point correspondences provide enough coordinate equations to determine those parameters.

Projective Transformations Have More Freedom

A \(3\times3\) homography matrix has nine entries, but one overall nonzero scale is irrelevant.

Therefore, it has

\[ \boxed{8} \]

effective degrees of freedom.

Fixed Points of Affine Transformations

For

\[ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}, \]

fixed points satisfy

\[ \boxed{ (I-A)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} }. \]

Depending on the transformation, the solution set may be empty, a point, a line, or larger.

Invariant Directions Under Affine Maps

If

\[ A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}, \]

then the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\) is invariant under the linear part.

This connects affine transformation geometry with eigenvectors.

Projective Fixed Points and Directions

In projective geometry, fixed points may include finite points or points at infinity.

A preserved direction in affine geometry can be represented by a fixed point at infinity in projective geometry.

Common Error: Calling Every Matrix Transformation Affine

A linear map

\[ \mathbf{x}\mapsto A\mathbf{x} \]

is affine, but general projective coordinate rules may involve division by a linear expression.

Those are more general than affine maps.

Common Error: Assuming Affine Means Angle-Preserving

Shears and nonuniform scalings are affine but can change angles.

Angle preservation belongs to the more restrictive similarity family.

Common Error: Assuming Affine Means Distance-Preserving

Distance preservation characterizes isometries, not general affine maps.

An affine transformation may stretch different directions by different factors.

Common Error: Assuming Projective Maps Preserve Parallelism

Perspective is precisely where parallel lines may appear to converge.

Parallelism is affine-invariant but not projective-invariant.

Common Error: Treating Vanishing Points as Ordinary Intersections

A vanishing point represents the image of a direction under perspective.

In the original Euclidean scene, the corresponding parallel lines do not meet at a finite point.

Common Error: Forgetting Homogeneous Scale Equivalence

The projective coordinates

\[ [2:4:2] \]

and

\[ [1:2:1] \]

represent the same projective point because they differ by a nonzero common factor.

Common Error: Confusing Local Appearance with Preserved Geometry

A rectangle may appear as a trapezoid in perspective.

That visual change does not mean projective incidence relationships have been lost.

A Reliable Procedure for Applying an Affine Transformation

  1. Write the point as a coordinate vector.
  2. Multiply by the matrix \(A\).
  3. Add the translation vector \(\mathbf{b}\).
  4. Repeat for all relevant vertices.
  5. Check preserved properties such as collinearity and parallelism.

A Reliable Procedure for Classifying an Affine Map

  1. Identify the matrix \(A\) and translation \(\mathbf{b}\).
  2. Compute \(\det A\).
  3. Check whether \(A^TA=I\) for an isometry.
  4. Check whether \(A^TA=k^2I\) for a similarity.
  5. If neither holds but \(A\) is invertible, classify it as a more general affine transformation.
  6. Inspect matrix entries for shear or nonuniform scaling behavior.

A Reliable Procedure for Homogeneous Coordinates

  1. Convert \((x,y)\) to \([x:y:1]\).
  2. Multiply by the \(3\times3\) transformation matrix.
  3. Obtain \([X':Y':W']\).
  4. If \(W'\neq0\), divide by \(W'\).
  5. Write the Cartesian point \((X'/W',Y'/W')\).

A Reliable Procedure for Comparing Transformation Families

  1. Ask whether distance is preserved.
  2. If not, ask whether all distances scale uniformly.
  3. If not, ask whether parallelism is preserved.
  4. If not, ask whether incidence and collinearity are preserved.
  5. Use the strongest preserved properties to identify the smallest applicable family.

Example: Full Affine Workflow

Let

\[ T(x,y)=(2x+y+1,\ x+3y-2). \]

Apply \(T\) to

\[ P=(2,-1). \]

Then

\[ x'=2(2)+(-1)+1=4, \] \[ y'=2+3(-1)-2=-3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{T(2,-1)=(4,-3)}. \]

Example: Determine the Area Scale

The linear part of the previous transformation is

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} 2&1\\ 1&3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Its determinant is

\[ \det A=6-1=5. \]

Therefore, areas are multiplied by

\[ \boxed{5}. \]

Example: Full Projective Workflow

Consider the homography

\[ H= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0&1&0\\ \frac12&0&1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Apply it to

\[ P=(2,3). \]

In homogeneous coordinates,

\[ P=[2:3:1]. \]

Then

\[ H \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 3\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 3\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Divide by the final coordinate:

\[ \boxed{ P'=\left(1,\frac32\right) }. \]

What to Remember

An affine transformation has the form

\[ \boxed{T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}}, \]

while a projective transformation is naturally represented in homogeneous coordinates by an invertible \(3\times3\) matrix.

The essential facts are:

  • affine transformations preserve lines, collinearity, parallelism, and affine ratios;
  • shears and nonuniform scalings are affine but not generally similarities;
  • area under an affine map scales by \(|\det A|\);
  • the determinant sign controls orientation;
  • projective transformations preserve incidence rather than Euclidean metric structure;
  • parallel lines may meet at vanishing points under projective maps;
  • homogeneous coordinates represent projective points up to nonzero scale;
  • affine transformations are a special case of projective transformations;
  • projective transformations preserve cross ratio;
  • homogeneous matrices unify affine and perspective transformations.

The most important structural idea is: the more general the transformation family, the fewer geometric properties remain invariant.
