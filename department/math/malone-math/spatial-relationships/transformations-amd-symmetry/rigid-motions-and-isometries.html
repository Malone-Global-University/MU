Rigid Motions and Isometries

A rigid motion moves a figure without changing any distance. Translations, rotations, and reflections are the basic rigid motions of the plane, and their compositions produce the full family of Euclidean isometries. Studying them together reveals a powerful unifying idea: geometric congruence can be understood as the ability to move one figure exactly onto another without stretching, shrinking, or distorting it.

The Central Idea

A rigid motion is a transformation that preserves every distance.

If a transformation \(T\) sends

\[ P\mapsto P' \qquad\text{and}\qquad Q\mapsto Q', \]

then rigid motion requires

\[ \boxed{P'Q'=PQ} \]

for every pair of points \(P\) and \(Q\).

Isometry Means Distance Preservation

The mathematical term isometry means a transformation that preserves distance.

Therefore, in Euclidean geometry,

\[ \boxed{\text{rigid motion}=\text{isometry}}. \]

The two terms emphasize slightly different viewpoints:

  • rigid motion emphasizes movement without deformation;
  • isometry emphasizes preservation of distance.

The Basic Plane Isometries

The principal rigid motions of the plane are:

  • translations;
  • rotations;
  • reflections.

Compositions of these transformations create additional isometries, including glide reflections.

Translations

A translation moves every point by the same vector.

In coordinates,

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x+a,y+b) }. \]

Translations preserve distance, angle measure, shape, size, parallelism, and orientation.

Rotations

A rotation turns every point through the same directed angle around a fixed center.

About the origin,

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto \left( x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta,\ x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta \right) }. \]

Rotations preserve distance and orientation.

Reflections

A reflection flips a figure across a line.

For each point \(P\) and image \(P'\), the mirror line is the perpendicular bisector of

\[ \overline{PP'}. \]

Reflections preserve distance but reverse orientation.

Dilations Are Not Usually Isometries

A dilation multiplies lengths by the magnitude of its scale factor.

If

\[ L'=|k|L, \]

then distances are preserved only when

\[ |k|=1. \]

Therefore, ordinary enlargements and reductions are not rigid motions.

Distance Preservation Is the Defining Test

A transformation may preserve some geometric properties without being an isometry.

The decisive condition is:

\[ \boxed{ d(T(P),T(Q))=d(P,Q) } \]

for every pair of points \(P,Q\).

Coordinate Form of the Isometry Condition

Suppose

\[ P=(x_1,y_1), \qquad Q=(x_2,y_2). \]

Their distance satisfies

\[ PQ^2 = (x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2. \]

A coordinate transformation is an isometry if this quantity remains unchanged for all point pairs.

Rigid Motions Preserve Segment Length

Since every pairwise distance is preserved, every segment keeps its length.

If

\[ A\mapsto A', \qquad B\mapsto B', \]

then

\[ \boxed{A'B'=AB}. \]

Rigid Motions Preserve Angle Measure

Isometries preserve angles.

If

\[ \angle ABC \]

maps to

\[ \angle A'B'C', \]

then

\[ \boxed{ m\angle ABC=m\angle A'B'C' }. \]

Rigid Motions Preserve Collinearity

If points lie on one straight line before a rigid motion, their images lie on one straight line afterward.

Thus, isometries preserve collinearity.

Rigid Motions Preserve Betweenness

Suppose \(B\) lies between \(A\) and \(C\).

Then

\[ AB+BC=AC. \]

Since an isometry preserves all three distances,

\[ A'B'+B'C'=A'C'. \]

Therefore, \(B'\) lies between \(A'\) and \(C'\).

Rigid Motions Preserve Parallelism

Parallel lines remain parallel under rigid motions.

Thus,

\[ \ell\parallel m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ell'\parallel m'}. \]

Rigid Motions Preserve Perpendicularity

Since right angles remain right angles,

\[ \ell\perp m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ell'\perp m'}. \]

Rigid Motions Preserve Area

A rigid motion does not stretch or compress a planar region.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A'=A}. \]

The same principle extends to volume in three-dimensional rigid motions.

Rigid Motions Preserve Shape and Size

Because both distances and angles are preserved, the image has exactly the same shape and size as the preimage.

In everyday language, the figure may be moved, turned, or flipped, but it is not deformed.

Rigid Motions and Congruence

Two figures are congruent when one can be mapped exactly onto the other by a rigid motion or a composition of rigid motions.

Thus, transformation geometry gives a powerful definition:

\[ \boxed{ A\cong B \iff \text{some isometry maps }A\text{ onto }B }. \]

Congruence as Motion

Traditional congruence language compares corresponding lengths and angles.

The transformation viewpoint asks a different but equivalent question:

Can one figure be moved onto the other without changing any distance?

If yes, the figures are congruent.

Orientation

Rigid motions divide naturally into two classes:

  • orientation-preserving isometries;
  • orientation-reversing isometries.

This distinction describes whether clockwise and counterclockwise order are retained or reversed.

Orientation-Preserving Isometries

Translations and rotations preserve orientation.

Their compositions also preserve orientation.

In the plane, the orientation-preserving Euclidean isometries are translations and rotations, including the identity as a special case.

Orientation-Reversing Isometries

Reflections reverse orientation.

A reflection followed by a suitable translation along the mirror direction produces another orientation-reversing isometry called a glide reflection.

Glide Reflections

A glide reflection combines:

  1. a reflection across a line;
  2. a translation parallel to that line.

Glide reflections preserve distance but reverse orientation.

Why Glide Reflections Matter

Glide reflections appear naturally in repeating patterns.

A sequence of footprints is a familiar informal example: one footprint may be reflected to switch left and right, then translated forward.

In mathematics, glide reflections are one of the basic classifications of plane isometries.

Classification of Plane Isometries

Every Euclidean isometry of the plane is one of four basic types:

  1. translation;
  2. rotation;
  3. reflection;
  4. glide reflection.

The identity transformation may be viewed as a zero translation or a zero-angle rotation.

Fixed Points Help Classify Isometries

The fixed-point structure of an isometry provides useful information.

  • a nonzero translation has no fixed points;
  • a nontrivial rotation has exactly one fixed point;
  • a reflection fixes an entire line;
  • a glide reflection has no fixed points.

The Identity Isometry

The identity transformation fixes every point:

\[ \boxed{I(P)=P}. \]

It preserves distance trivially and acts as the neutral element for composition:

\[ I\circ T=T\circ I=T. \]

Every Isometry Is Invertible

A rigid motion can always be undone.

Therefore, every isometry has an inverse that is also an isometry.

Examples include:

  • the inverse of a translation uses the opposite vector;
  • the inverse of a rotation uses the opposite angle;
  • a reflection is its own inverse.

Composition of Isometries

If

\[ T_1 \]

and

\[ T_2 \]

are isometries, then their composition

\[ \boxed{T_2\circ T_1} \]

is also an isometry.

Distance remains preserved through each stage.

Why Composition Preserves Distance

Let \(P,Q\) be any two points.

Since \(T_1\) is an isometry,

\[ d(T_1(P),T_1(Q))=d(P,Q). \]

Since \(T_2\) is also an isometry,

\[ d(T_2(T_1(P)),T_2(T_1(Q))) = d(T_1(P),T_1(Q)). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ d((T_2\circ T_1)(P),(T_2\circ T_1)(Q)) = d(P,Q) }. \]

Two Translations Compose to a Translation

If

\[ T_{\mathbf{u}} \]

and

\[ T_{\mathbf{v}} \]

are translations, then

\[ \boxed{ T_{\mathbf{v}}\circ T_{\mathbf{u}} = T_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}} }. \]

Two Rotations About the Same Center

Rotations about the same center compose by adding angles:

\[ \boxed{ R_\beta\circ R_\alpha = R_{\alpha+\beta} }. \]

Two Reflections Across Intersecting Lines

Two reflections across lines meeting at angle \(\theta\) compose to a rotation through

\[ \boxed{2\theta}. \]

The intersection point becomes the center of rotation.

Two Reflections Across Parallel Lines

Two reflections across distinct parallel lines compose to a translation.

If the lines are \(d\) units apart, the translation distance is

\[ \boxed{2d}. \]

Composition Can Change the Visible Type

The result of composing rigid motions may belong to a different named category than either component.

Examples:

  • reflection + reflection can produce rotation;
  • reflection + reflection can produce translation;
  • reflection + translation can produce glide reflection.

This makes composition central to transformation geometry.

Order of Composition Matters

In general,

\[ T_2\circ T_1 \]

need not equal

\[ T_1\circ T_2. \]

Transformation composition is therefore not generally commutative.

Certain special pairs, such as translations, do commute.

Example: Translation Then Rotation

Begin with

\[ P=(1,0). \]

First translate by

\[ \langle 2,0\rangle, \]

giving

\[ (3,0). \]

Then rotate \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about the origin:

\[ (3,0)\mapsto(0,3). \]

The final image is

\[ \boxed{(0,3)}. \]

Reverse the Order

Start again with

\[ P=(1,0). \]

First rotate \(90^\circ\):

\[ (1,0)\mapsto(0,1). \]

Then translate by

\[ \langle 2,0\rangle: \] \[ (0,1)\mapsto(2,1). \]

The final image is

\[ \boxed{(2,1)}. \]

Since

\[ (0,3)\neq(2,1), \]

the two operations do not commute.

Isometries and Coordinate Matrices

Linear isometries fixing the origin are represented by matrices that preserve vector length and angle.

Such matrices satisfy

\[ \boxed{A^TA=I}. \]

These are called orthogonal matrices.

Why \(A^TA=I\) Matters

For a vector \(\mathbf{v}\),

\[ \|A\mathbf{v}\|^2 = (A\mathbf{v})^T(A\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v}^TA^TA\mathbf{v}. \]

If

\[ A^TA=I, \]

then

\[ \|A\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{v}\|^2. \]

Therefore, lengths are preserved.

Determinants of Plane Isometries

For an orthogonal \(2\times2\) matrix,

\[ \boxed{\det A=\pm1}. \]

The sign identifies orientation:

  • \(\det A=1\): orientation preserving;
  • \(\det A=-1\): orientation reversing.

Rotation Matrices Have Determinant \(1\)

The rotation matrix

\[ R_\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \]

satisfies

\[ \det R_\theta = \cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta = 1. \]

This matches the fact that rotations preserve orientation.

Reflection Matrices Have Determinant \(-1\)

Reflection across the \(x\)-axis has matrix

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Its determinant is

\[ \boxed{-1}, \]

recording orientation reversal.

Translations Require an Affine Form

A nonzero translation does not fix the origin, so it cannot be represented by a \(2\times2\) matrix alone.

Instead, write

\[ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} }. \]

For a translation,

\[ A=I. \]

The General Affine Isometry Form

Every Euclidean plane isometry can be written in affine form as

\[ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} } \]

where

\[ A^TA=I. \]

The orthogonal matrix \(A\) controls rotation or reflection, while \(\mathbf{b}\) controls translation.

Testing an Affine Transformation for Isometry

Consider

\[ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}. \]

The translation vector \(\mathbf{b}\) does not affect differences between points:

\[ T(\mathbf{x})-T(\mathbf{y}) = A(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}). \]

Therefore, \(T\) is an isometry exactly when \(A\) preserves vector length.

Example: Is This Matrix an Isometry?

Consider

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Its columns are perpendicular unit vectors, so

\[ A^TA=I. \]

Therefore, the matrix defines an isometry.

In fact, it is a \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation.

Example: A Non-Isometry

Consider

\[ T(x,y)=(2x,y). \]

The distance from

\[ (0,0) \]

to

\[ (1,0) \]

is \(1\).

After transformation, the points become

\[ (0,0) \quad\text{and}\quad (2,0), \]

whose distance is \(2\).

Therefore, this transformation is not an isometry.

One Counterexample Is Enough

To prove that a transformation is not an isometry, it is enough to find one pair of points whose distance changes.

The isometry condition must hold for

\[ \boxed{\text{every pair of points}}. \]

Rigid Motions and Circles

Isometries send circles to circles of the same radius.

If a circle has center \(C\) and radius \(r\), its image has center \(T(C)\) and radius

\[ \boxed{r}. \]

Rigid Motions and Polygons

A rigid motion preserves every side length and every interior angle of a polygon.

Therefore, the image polygon is congruent to the original polygon.

Rigid Motions and Symmetry

A symmetry of a figure is an isometry that maps the figure onto itself.

Depending on the figure, symmetries may include:

  • the identity;
  • rotations;
  • reflections;
  • translations for repeating infinite patterns;
  • glide reflections for certain periodic patterns.

Symmetry as an Isometry of a Figure

If \(F\) is a geometric figure and \(T\) is an isometry, then \(T\) is a symmetry of \(F\) when

\[ \boxed{T(F)=F}. \]

The figure as a whole remains unchanged even though individual points may move.

Rigid Motions Form a Transformation System

Euclidean isometries have several important structural properties:

  • composing isometries gives another isometry;
  • the identity is an isometry;
  • every isometry has an inverse;
  • composition is associative.

These properties prepare the way for the later study of symmetry groups.

Rigid Motion Versus Similarity Transformation

A rigid motion preserves distance exactly:

\[ d'=d. \]

A similarity transformation preserves distance up to one common scale:

\[ d'=|k|d. \]

Thus every isometry is a similarity transformation with scale factor

\[ \boxed{|k|=1}, \]

but not every similarity transformation is an isometry.

Rigid Motion Versus Affine Transformation

Affine transformations preserve lines and parallelism but may stretch, shear, or scale figures.

Isometries are a much more restrictive subclass because they preserve actual Euclidean distance.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{isometries}\subset\text{affine transformations} }. \]

Rigid Motion Versus Projective Transformation

Projective transformations preserve incidence relationships but may alter lengths, angles, parallelism, and shape.

Rigid motions preserve far more metric structure.

This hierarchy helps organize different transformation families according to what they preserve.

Hierarchy of Preservation

Different transformation families preserve different amounts of geometric information.

A useful broad hierarchy is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{isometries} \subset \text{similarities} \subset \text{affine transformations} \subset \text{projective transformations} }. \]

Each broader family preserves fewer metric properties but permits more general transformations.

Common Error: Assuming Every Shape-Preserving Map Is Rigid

Dilations preserve shape but change size.

Therefore, shape preservation alone does not imply isometry.

The defining test is exact distance preservation.

Common Error: Checking Only One Segment

A transformation is an isometry only if every pairwise distance is preserved.

One preserved segment does not prove the entire transformation is rigid.

A general proof must establish the distance condition for arbitrary points, or appeal to a known isometry rule.

Common Error: Assuming Orientation Must Be Preserved

Reflections and glide reflections are rigid motions even though they reverse orientation.

Orientation preservation is not required for isometry.

Common Error: Confusing Fixed Points with Rigid Motion

Different rigid motions have different fixed-point structures.

A rigid motion may have:

  • no fixed points;
  • one fixed point;
  • an entire line of fixed points;
  • every point fixed.

Fixed points help classify the motion but do not define rigidity.

Common Error: Assuming Compositions Commute

The order of rigid motions often matters.

Translation followed by rotation usually differs from rotation followed by translation.

Always read

\[ T_2\circ T_1 \]

as “apply \(T_1\) first, then \(T_2\).”

Common Error: Treating a Glide Reflection as a Simple Reflection

A glide reflection has no fixed mirror line because the translation step moves points along the line after reflection.

It remains orientation reversing and distance preserving, but its fixed point structure differs from an ordinary reflection.

A Reliable Procedure for Testing a Transformation

  1. Identify the transformation rule.
  2. Determine whether it is a known rigid motion or composition of rigid motions.
  3. If necessary, choose arbitrary points \(P,Q\).
  4. Compute their original distance.
  5. Transform both points.
  6. Compute the image distance.
  7. Verify equality for arbitrary points, not merely one example.

A Reliable Procedure for Classifying a Plane Isometry

  1. Determine whether orientation is preserved or reversed.
  2. Check the fixed-point structure.
  3. If orientation is preserved and there are no fixed points, suspect a translation.
  4. If orientation is preserved and there is one fixed point, suspect a rotation.
  5. If orientation is reversed and an entire line is fixed, suspect a reflection.
  6. If orientation is reversed and there are no fixed points, suspect a glide reflection.
  7. Verify the classification using the transformation rule.

Example: Verify a Translation Is an Isometry

Consider

\[ T(x,y)=(x+a,y+b). \]

Let

\[ P=(x_1,y_1), \qquad Q=(x_2,y_2). \]

Then

\[ T(P)=(x_1+a,y_1+b), \] \[ T(Q)=(x_2+a,y_2+b). \]

The coordinate differences are unchanged:

\[ (x_2+a)-(x_1+a)=x_2-x_1, \] \[ (y_2+b)-(y_1+b)=y_2-y_1. \]

Therefore, the distance is preserved.

Example: Verify a Reflection Is an Isometry

Reflect across the \(x\)-axis:

\[ T(x,y)=(x,-y). \]

For

\[ P=(x_1,y_1), \qquad Q=(x_2,y_2), \]

the squared image distance is

\[ (x_2-x_1)^2+((-y_2)-(-y_1))^2. \]

Since

\[ (-y_2)-(-y_1)=-(y_2-y_1), \]

squaring removes the sign:

\[ \boxed{ (x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2 }. \]

Thus reflection preserves distance.

Example: Classify from Fixed Points and Orientation

Suppose an isometry preserves orientation and has exactly one fixed point.

A nonzero translation has no fixed points, while a nontrivial rotation has exactly one.

Therefore, the isometry must be a

\[ \boxed{\text{rotation}}. \]

What to Remember

A rigid motion, or isometry, preserves every distance:

\[ \boxed{ d(T(P),T(Q))=d(P,Q) }. \]

The essential facts are:

  • translations, rotations, and reflections are basic plane isometries;
  • glide reflections are also plane isometries;
  • rigid motions preserve lengths and angle measures;
  • they preserve collinearity, betweenness, parallelism, and perpendicularity;
  • they preserve area, shape, and size;
  • rigid motions map figures to congruent figures;
  • translations and rotations preserve orientation;
  • reflections and glide reflections reverse orientation;
  • compositions and inverses of isometries are isometries;
  • fixed-point structure helps classify plane isometries;
  • linear isometries are represented by orthogonal matrices.

The most important structural principle is: if every pairwise distance is preserved, the transformation is a rigid motion.

Where We Go Next

Rigid motions preserve both shape and size. Dilations preserve shape while changing size by a common scale factor. Combining these ideas leads to the broader family of transformations that preserve similarity.
