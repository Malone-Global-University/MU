Translations

A translation moves every point of a figure the same distance in the same direction. The figure changes position, but not shape, size, orientation, angle measure, or distance. Translations are among the simplest geometric transformations, and they provide a natural entry point into the study of rigid motions, symmetry, coordinate rules, and transformation composition.

The Central Idea

A translation slides every point of a geometric object by the same displacement.

If a point \(P\) moves to a point \(P'\), then every other point of the figure moves by exactly the same directed segment.

\[ \boxed{\overrightarrow{PP'}=\overrightarrow{QQ'}=\overrightarrow{RR'}=\cdots} \]

Nothing is turned, reflected, stretched, or compressed. The entire figure is shifted as one rigid object.

Transformations as Rules

A geometric transformation is a rule that sends each point of a figure to another point.

We may write

\[ T:P\mapsto P' \]

to mean that the transformation \(T\) sends the point \(P\) to its image \(P'\).

Under a translation, every point receives the same displacement.

Preimage and Image

The original figure is called the preimage. The resulting figure is called the image.

If triangle \(ABC\) is translated to triangle \(A'B'C'\), then

\[ A\mapsto A',\qquad B\mapsto B',\qquad C\mapsto C'. \]

The prime marks indicate corresponding image points.

A Translation Is Determined by a Vector

A translation is completely determined by a translation vector.

In the plane, let

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle a,b\rangle. \]

The component \(a\) gives the horizontal displacement, and the component \(b\) gives the vertical displacement.

Every point moves by the same vector \(\mathbf{v}\).

The Coordinate Rule

A translation by the vector

\[ \langle a,b\rangle \]

sends the point \((x,y)\) to

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x+a,\ y+b)}. \]

This formula is the basic coordinate rule for translation in the plane.

Reading the Translation Vector

Suppose

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x+5,\ y-2). \]

The translation vector is

\[ \boxed{\langle 5,-2\rangle}. \]

Every point moves \(5\) units to the right and \(2\) units downward.

Example: Translating a Point

Translate

\[ P=(-3,4) \]

by the vector

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 6,-5\rangle. \]

Apply the coordinate rule:

\[ P'=(-3+6,\ 4-5). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(3,-1)}. \]

Example: Translating a Triangle

Let

\[ A=(1,2),\qquad B=(4,2),\qquad C=(2,5). \]

Translate the triangle by

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle -3,4\rangle. \]

Then

\[ A'=(1-3,\ 2+4)=(-2,6), \] \[ B'=(4-3,\ 2+4)=(1,6), \] \[ C'=(2-3,\ 5+4)=(-1,9). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ A'=(-2,6),\quad B'=(1,6),\quad C'=(-1,9) }. \]

Every Point Moves Equally

A defining feature of translation is that corresponding displacement vectors are equal.

For the triangle above,

\[ \overrightarrow{AA'} = \overrightarrow{BB'} = \overrightarrow{CC'} = \langle -3,4\rangle. \]

This equality distinguishes a translation from transformations in which different points move in different directions or by different distances.

Translations Are Rigid Motions

A rigid motion, or isometry, preserves distance.

If \(P\) and \(Q\) are translated to \(P'\) and \(Q'\), then

\[ \boxed{P'Q'=PQ}. \]

Because all distances are preserved, the image of a figure is congruent to the original figure.

Why Distance Is Preserved

Let

\[ P=(x_1,y_1),\qquad Q=(x_2,y_2), \]

and translate both points by \(\langle a,b\rangle\).

Then

\[ P'=(x_1+a,y_1+b), \] \[ Q'=(x_2+a,y_2+b). \]

Their coordinate differences are

\[ (x_2+a)-(x_1+a)=x_2-x_1 \]

and

\[ (y_2+b)-(y_1+b)=y_2-y_1. \]

Therefore, the distance formula gives exactly the same distance before and after translation.

Translations Preserve Angle Measure

Because translations are rigid motions, they preserve angle measure.

If

\[ \angle ABC \]

is translated to

\[ \angle A'B'C', \]

then

\[ \boxed{ m\angle ABC=m\angle A'B'C' }. \]

Translations Preserve Parallelism

A translation sends a line to a parallel line, unless the line is shifted along itself.

Consequently, if

\[ \ell\parallel m, \]

then their translated images satisfy

\[ \boxed{\ell'\parallel m'}. \]

Slopes remain unchanged whenever the lines are described in ordinary Cartesian coordinates.

Translations Preserve Collinearity

If points \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) lie on the same line before translation, then their images \(A'\), \(B'\), and \(C'\) also lie on one line.

Thus translation preserves collinearity.

Translations Preserve Betweenness

Suppose \(B\) lies between \(A\) and \(C\).

A translation preserves both the line and the relevant distances, so \(B'\) lies between \(A'\) and \(C'\).

The order of points along a line is therefore preserved.

Translations Preserve Orientation

A translation does not reverse a figure.

If the vertices of a polygon are listed counterclockwise before the translation, their images are still listed counterclockwise afterward.

Therefore, translations are orientation-preserving transformations.

What a Translation Preserves

A translation preserves all of the following:

  • distance;
  • segment length;
  • angle measure;
  • collinearity;
  • betweenness;
  • parallelism;
  • perpendicularity;
  • area;
  • shape;
  • size;
  • orientation.

These preserved properties are called invariants of the transformation.

Translation as Vector Addition

A point can itself be represented by its position vector.

If

\[ \mathbf{p}= \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} \]

and the translation vector is

\[ \mathbf{v}= \begin{bmatrix} a\\ b \end{bmatrix}, \]

then the translated point is

\[ \boxed{\mathbf{p}'=\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{v}}. \]

Translation is therefore ordinary vector addition applied uniformly to every point.

Finding a Translation Vector from Two Points

If a point

\[ P=(x_1,y_1) \]

is translated to

\[ P'=(x_2,y_2), \]

then the translation vector is

\[ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{PP'} = \langle x_2-x_1,\ y_2-y_1\rangle }. \]

Example: Recovering the Translation Rule

Suppose

\[ P=(-2,7) \]

is translated to

\[ P'=(4,3). \]

The displacement is

\[ \langle 4-(-2),\ 3-7\rangle = \langle 6,-4\rangle. \]

Therefore, the translation rule is

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x+6,\ y-4)}. \]

Verifying That Two Figures Are Related by Translation

To verify that one figure is a translation of another, compare corresponding displacement vectors.

If

\[ A\mapsto A',\qquad B\mapsto B',\qquad C\mapsto C', \]

then a translation exists exactly when

\[ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{AA'} = \overrightarrow{BB'} = \overrightarrow{CC'} }. \]

One matching pair of points is not enough. The same displacement must work for every point.

Example: Is It a Translation?

Suppose

\[ A=(1,1),\quad B=(5,1),\quad C=(2,4), \]

and

\[ A'=(4,3),\quad B'=(8,3),\quad C'=(5,6). \]

Compute the displacements:

\[ \overrightarrow{AA'}=\langle 3,2\rangle, \] \[ \overrightarrow{BB'}=\langle 3,2\rangle, \] \[ \overrightarrow{CC'}=\langle 3,2\rangle. \]

Because all three are equal, the second triangle is a translation of the first.

Translations of Lines

Consider a nonvertical line

\[ y=mx+c. \]

Translate the plane by \(\langle a,b\rangle\).

Since

\[ x'=x+a,\qquad y'=y+b, \]

we have

\[ x=x'-a,\qquad y=y'-b. \]

Substitute into the original equation:

\[ y'-b=m(x'-a)+c. \]

Therefore,

\[ y'=mx'+(c+b-ma). \]

The slope \(m\) is unchanged.

Horizontal and Vertical Translations

A horizontal translation has the form

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x+a,y)}. \]

A vertical translation has the form

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x,y+b)}. \]

General translations combine both motions:

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x+a,y+b)}. \]

Translations of Graphs

The graph of a function can also be translated.

If

\[ y=f(x), \]

then translating the graph horizontally by \(h\) and vertically by \(k\) gives

\[ \boxed{y=f(x-h)+k}. \]

The sign inside the function is opposite the direction of the horizontal shift:

  • \(f(x-h)\) shifts right by \(h\);
  • \(f(x+h)\) shifts left by \(h\);
  • \(f(x)+k\) shifts up by \(k\);
  • \(f(x)-k\) shifts down by \(k\).

Why the Horizontal Sign Looks Reversed

Suppose the graph of

\[ y=f(x) \]

is moved \(h\) units to the right.

A point that originally occurred at input \(x_0\) now occurs at input \(x_0+h\).

To recover the original input from the new input \(x\), we use

\[ x-h. \]

That is why a rightward graph translation is written

\[ \boxed{y=f(x-h)}. \]

Example: Translating a Parabola

Start with

\[ y=x^2. \]

Translate the graph \(3\) units right and \(4\) units up.

The translated equation is

\[ \boxed{y=(x-3)^2+4}. \]

The vertex moves from

\[ (0,0) \]

to

\[ (3,4). \]

Translations in Three Dimensions

The same idea extends directly to space.

A translation by

\[ \langle a,b,c\rangle \]

sends

\[ \boxed{ (x,y,z)\mapsto(x+a,\ y+b,\ z+c) }. \]

Every point receives the same three-dimensional displacement.

Example: Translation in Space

Translate

\[ P=(2,-1,5) \]

by

\[ \langle -4,3,2\rangle. \]

Then

\[ P'=(2-4,\ -1+3,\ 5+2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P'=(-2,2,7)}. \]

The Identity Translation

The translation vector

\[ \mathbf{0}=\langle 0,0\rangle \]

moves every point by zero displacement.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x,y)}. \]

This is the identity transformation.

Inverse Translations

Every translation can be undone by translating in the opposite direction.

If

\[ T_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{v}, \]

then its inverse is

\[ \boxed{ T_{\mathbf{v}}^{-1}=T_{-\mathbf{v}} }. \]

For example, the inverse of translation by

\[ \langle 5,-2\rangle \]

is translation by

\[ \langle -5,2\rangle. \]

Composing Two Translations

Two translations performed one after the other combine into a single translation.

If

\[ T_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{u} \]

and

\[ T_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{v}, \]

then

\[ T_{\mathbf{v}}\!\left(T_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{p})\right) = \mathbf{p}+\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ T_{\mathbf{v}}\circ T_{\mathbf{u}} = T_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}} }. \]

Example: Composing Translations

First translate by

\[ \langle 4,1\rangle, \]

then by

\[ \langle -2,5\rangle. \]

Add the vectors:

\[ \langle 4,1\rangle+\langle -2,5\rangle = \langle 2,6\rangle. \]

The combined transformation is

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x+2,\ y+6)}. \]

Translations Commute with Other Translations

Vector addition is commutative:

\[ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ T_{\mathbf{v}}\circ T_{\mathbf{u}} = T_{\mathbf{u}}\circ T_{\mathbf{v}} }. \]

The order of two translations does not affect the final position.

This convenient property does not hold for every pair of geometric transformations.

Fixed Points of a Translation

A fixed point is a point that remains in its original location after a transformation.

For a translation by \(\langle a,b\rangle\), a fixed point would have to satisfy

\[ (x+a,y+b)=(x,y). \]

Therefore,

\[ a=0,\qquad b=0. \]

Hence a nonzero translation has

\[ \boxed{\text{no fixed points}}. \]

Only the identity translation fixes every point.

Invariant Sets Under Translation

A nonzero translation has no fixed individual points, but some larger sets can map onto themselves.

For example, a line translated in a direction parallel to itself maps onto the same line.

Repeating patterns and tessellations may also remain unchanged as complete sets after particular translations.

Translational Symmetry

A figure has translational symmetry if a nonzero translation maps the entire figure onto itself.

Infinite repeating patterns frequently have this property.

A simple example is an evenly spaced infinite row of identical marks. If the spacing is \(d\), then translation by

\[ \langle d,0\rangle \]

maps the pattern onto itself.

Translations and Tessellations

Tessellations often repeat a tile across the plane by translation.

If one tile is copied using two independent translation vectors

\[ \mathbf{u} \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{v}, \]

then many copies can be reached using integer combinations

\[ \boxed{m\mathbf{u}+n\mathbf{v}}, \]

where \(m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\).

This idea will return later when we study repeating patterns and symmetry groups.

Translations and Coordinate Geometry

Translation provides a useful bridge between geometric reasoning and coordinate methods.

A geometric statement such as

“move the figure \(4\) units left and \(7\) units up”

becomes the algebraic rule

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x-4,y+7)}. \]

Coordinates make the transformation precise and easy to verify.

Translations and Congruence

Because a translation is a rigid motion, any figure is congruent to its translated image.

For example,

\[ \triangle ABC\cong\triangle A'B'C' \]

whenever \(A'B'C'\) is obtained from \(ABC\) by translation.

Transformations therefore provide a motion-based way to understand congruence.

Translations of Geometric Objects

A translation can be applied to any geometric set:

  • points;
  • segments;
  • rays;
  • lines;
  • angles;
  • polygons;
  • circles;
  • curves;
  • regions;
  • three-dimensional solids.

The same translation vector applies to every point of the object.

Translating a Circle

Consider the circle

\[ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2. \]

Its center is

\[ (h,k). \]

Translating the circle by \(\langle a,b\rangle\) moves the center to

\[ (h+a,k+b) \]

while leaving the radius unchanged.

The translated equation is

\[ \boxed{ (x-(h+a))^2+(y-(k+b))^2=r^2 }. \]

Translations Preserve Area and Volume

Since translation preserves every distance, it also preserves derived measurements such as area and volume.

If a planar region has area \(A\), then its translated image also has area \(A\).

If a solid has volume \(V\), then its translated image also has volume \(V\).

A Translation Does Not Change Direction Vectors

Suppose a directed segment runs from \(A\) to \(B\).

Its direction vector is

\[ \overrightarrow{AB}=B-A. \]

After translating both endpoints by \(\mathbf{v}\),

\[ \overrightarrow{A'B'} = (B+\mathbf{v})-(A+\mathbf{v}) = B-A. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\overrightarrow{A'B'}=\overrightarrow{AB}}. \]

This compact vector calculation explains many of the invariants of translation at once.

Translations and Matrices

Ordinary \(2\times2\) matrices represent linear transformations that keep the origin fixed.

A nonzero translation moves the origin:

\[ (0,0)\mapsto(a,b). \]

Therefore, a general translation cannot be represented by multiplication by a \(2\times2\) matrix alone.

This is an important distinction between linear transformations and affine transformations.

Homogeneous Coordinates: A Gentle Preview

In computer graphics and projective geometry, translations can be placed into matrix form by adding an extra coordinate.

Represent

\[ (x,y) \]

as

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then translation by \(\langle a,b\rangle\) can be written

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&a\\ 0&1&b\\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x+a\\ y+b\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

This technique will become more important when affine and projective transformations are studied later.

Common Error: Moving Different Points Differently

A translation must apply one identical displacement to every point.

If

\[ A\mapsto A' \]

uses the vector

\[ \langle 3,2\rangle, \]

then every other point must also move by

\[ \langle 3,2\rangle. \]

If different vertices receive different displacement vectors, the transformation is not a translation.

Common Error: Reversing a Coordinate Sign

The rule

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x-4,y+3) \]

means

  • move \(4\) units left;
  • move \(3\) units up.

The coordinate changes describe the direction directly for point translations.

Common Error: Confusing Point Translation with Graph Notation

For a point, the rule

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x+3,y) \]

moves the point \(3\) units right.

For a function graph,

\[ y=f(x-3) \]

also moves the graph \(3\) units right.

The different-looking signs arise because one formula updates point coordinates directly, while the other rewrites the input needed to produce the translated graph.

Common Error: Assuming Every Congruent Figure Is a Translation

Congruence alone does not prove that two figures are related by translation.

A congruent image may instead have been produced by a rotation, reflection, or composition of rigid motions.

To verify a translation, corresponding points must have equal displacement vectors.

Common Error: Looking for a Fixed Point

A nonzero translation has no fixed points.

If a problem describes a transformation with a fixed point and nontrivial motion, then the transformation cannot be a pure nonzero translation.

A Reliable Procedure for Translating Coordinates

  1. Identify the translation vector.
  2. Separate its horizontal and vertical components.
  3. Add the horizontal component to every \(x\)-coordinate.
  4. Add the vertical component to every \(y\)-coordinate.
  5. Repeat the same rule for every point of the figure.
  6. Check that all corresponding displacement vectors agree.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding a Translation

  1. Choose one pair of corresponding points.
  2. Subtract preimage coordinates from image coordinates.
  3. Use the result as the candidate translation vector.
  4. Test the same vector on every remaining corresponding point.
  5. If all displacements match, the transformation is a translation.
  6. If even one displacement differs, it is not a translation.

Example: Full Coordinate Workflow

Let

\[ A=(-1,2),\qquad B=(3,2),\qquad C=(1,6). \]

Translate the triangle \(5\) units right and \(3\) units down.

The vector is

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 5,-3\rangle. \]

Apply it to every vertex:

\[ A'=(-1+5,\ 2-3)=(4,-1), \] \[ B'=(3+5,\ 2-3)=(8,-1), \] \[ C'=(1+5,\ 6-3)=(6,3). \]

Check:

\[ \overrightarrow{AA'} = \overrightarrow{BB'} = \overrightarrow{CC'} = \langle 5,-3\rangle. \]

Therefore, the translation has been performed consistently.

Example: Recovering a Translation from a Polygon

Suppose a quadrilateral has corresponding vertices

\[ A=(0,1),\quad A'=(-4,6), \] \[ B=(3,1),\quad B'=(-1,6), \] \[ C=(4,4),\quad C'=(0,9), \] \[ D=(1,5),\quad D'=(-3,10). \]

Using the first pair,

\[ \overrightarrow{AA'} = \langle -4,5\rangle. \]

The same difference occurs for every remaining pair, so the translation is

\[ \boxed{(x,y)\mapsto(x-4,y+5)}. \]

What to Remember

A translation moves every point by the same vector:

\[ \boxed{ T_{\langle a,b\rangle}(x,y) = (x+a,y+b) }. \]

The essential facts are:

  • a translation is completely determined by its displacement vector;
  • every point moves the same distance in the same direction;
  • translations preserve distance and angle measure;
  • translations preserve shape, size, parallelism, and orientation;
  • translations are rigid motions, or isometries;
  • the inverse translation uses the opposite vector;
  • composing translations adds their vectors;
  • a nonzero translation has no fixed points;
  • repeating figures may possess translational symmetry.

The most useful coordinate habit is simple: identify the translation vector first, then apply that exact vector to every point.
