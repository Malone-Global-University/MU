Three-Dimensional Coordinates

Two coordinates describe position in a plane. Three coordinates describe position in ordinary Euclidean space. By adding a third perpendicular axis, coordinate geometry extends naturally from points, lines, and curves in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) to points, lines, planes, surfaces, and spatial motion in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

The Central Idea

A point in three-dimensional Cartesian space is represented by an ordered triple

\[ \boxed{(x,y,z)}. \]

The three coordinates describe position along three mutually perpendicular directions:

  • \(x\): position along the \(x\)-axis;
  • \(y\): position along the \(y\)-axis;
  • \(z\): position along the \(z\)-axis.

The order matters just as it does with ordered pairs.

From the Plane to Space

In two dimensions, the coordinate plane uses

\[ (x,y). \]

Adding a third perpendicular coordinate produces

\[ (x,y,z). \]

The set of all ordered triples of real numbers is written

\[ \boxed{\mathbb{R}^3=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}. \]

The Three Coordinate Axes

Three-dimensional Cartesian space contains three coordinate axes:

  • the \(x\)-axis;
  • the \(y\)-axis;
  • the \(z\)-axis.

They intersect at one point and are pairwise perpendicular.

The Origin

The three coordinate axes intersect at the origin

\[ \boxed{O=(0,0,0)}. \]

This point is the reference location from which all three coordinates are measured.

The \(x\)-Axis in Three Dimensions

Every point on the \(x\)-axis has

\[ y=0 \]

and

\[ z=0. \]

Therefore the \(x\)-axis can be described as

\[ \boxed{(x,0,0)}. \]

The \(y\)-Axis in Three Dimensions

Every point on the \(y\)-axis has

\[ x=0,\qquad z=0. \]

Thus the \(y\)-axis consists of points

\[ \boxed{(0,y,0)}. \]

The \(z\)-Axis

Every point on the \(z\)-axis has

\[ x=0,\qquad y=0. \]

Therefore the \(z\)-axis is described by

\[ \boxed{(0,0,z)}. \]

The Coordinate Planes

Every pair of coordinate axes determines a coordinate plane.

The three coordinate planes are:

\[ \boxed{xy\text{-plane}: z=0}, \] \[ \boxed{xz\text{-plane}: y=0}, \] \[ \boxed{yz\text{-plane}: x=0}. \]

Each coordinate plane is a two-dimensional copy of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) embedded inside \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Reading an Ordered Triple

Consider

\[ P=(3,-2,5). \]

Its coordinates say:

  • \(x=3\);
  • \(y=-2\);
  • \(z=5\).

The point is located by combining all three signed coordinate displacements.

Plotting a Point in Space

To plot

\[ (4,2,3), \]

conceptually begin at the origin and move according to the three coordinates.

One convenient interpretation is:

  1. locate \((4,2,0)\) in the \(xy\)-plane;
  2. move \(3\) units parallel to the \(z\)-axis.

The final point is \((4,2,3)\).

Negative Coordinates in Space

Negative coordinates indicate motion in the negative direction of the corresponding axis.

For

\[ (-3,4,-2), \]

the point lies in the negative \(x\)-direction, positive \(y\)-direction, and negative \(z\)-direction from the origin.

Octants

The three coordinate planes divide space into eight regions called octants.

Each octant corresponds to one of the eight possible sign combinations of

\[ (x,y,z). \]

The first octant is conventionally

\[ \boxed{x>0,\qquad y>0,\qquad z>0}. \]

Octant Naming Is Less Standard Than Quadrant Naming

In two dimensions, Quadrants I through IV have a widely standardized numbering convention.

In three dimensions, only the first octant is universally named in a consistent way.

For the other seven regions, sign descriptions such as

\[ (-,+,-) \]

are often clearer than relying on an octant number.

Points on Coordinate Planes

A point lies on a coordinate plane when one coordinate is zero.

For example,

\[ (3,-5,0) \]

lies on the \(xy\)-plane because

\[ z=0. \]

Likewise,

\[ (0,4,7) \]

lies on the \(yz\)-plane.

Points on Coordinate Axes

A point lies on a coordinate axis when two of its coordinates are zero.

For example,

\[ (0,-6,0) \]

lies on the \(y\)-axis.

Projection onto the \(xy\)-Plane

To project

\[ (x,y,z) \]

onto the \(xy\)-plane, set

\[ z=0. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y,0)}. \]

This removes the height component while preserving horizontal location in the \(xy\)-plane.

Projection onto the \(xz\)-Plane

Projection onto the \(xz\)-plane sets

\[ y=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,0,z)}. \]

Projection onto the \(yz\)-Plane

Projection onto the \(yz\)-plane sets

\[ x=0. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{(x,y,z)\mapsto(0,y,z)}. \]

Distance from the Origin

For

\[ P=(x,y,z), \]

the distance from the origin is

\[ \boxed{ OP=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2} }. \]

This is the three-dimensional extension of

\[ \sqrt{x^2+y^2} \]

in the plane.

Deriving the Three-Dimensional Distance Formula

Let

\[ P=(x_1,y_1,z_1) \]

and

\[ Q=(x_2,y_2,z_2). \]

The coordinate differences are

\[ \Delta x=x_2-x_1, \] \[ \Delta y=y_2-y_1, \] \[ \Delta z=z_2-z_1. \]

Applying the Pythagorean relationship twice gives

\[ d^2=(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2+(\Delta z)^2. \]

Three-Dimensional Distance Formula

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ d= \sqrt{ (x_2-x_1)^2+ (y_2-y_1)^2+ (z_2-z_1)^2 } }. \]

Distance remains nonnegative and does not depend on which endpoint is listed first.

Example: Distance in Space

Find the distance between

\[ A=(1,2,3) \]

and

\[ B=(5,-1,9). \]

Then

\[ \Delta x=4,\qquad \Delta y=-3,\qquad \Delta z=6. \]

Therefore,

\[ d= \sqrt{4^2+(-3)^2+6^2} = \sqrt{61}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{AB=\sqrt{61}}. \]

Midpoint in Three Dimensions

The midpoint formula extends coordinate by coordinate.

For

\[ P=(x_1,y_1,z_1) \]

and

\[ Q=(x_2,y_2,z_2), \]

the midpoint is

\[ \boxed{ M= \left( \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}, \frac{z_1+z_2}{2} \right) }. \]

Example: Midpoint in Space

Find the midpoint of

\[ (-2,4,7) \]

and

\[ (6,-2,1). \]

Average corresponding coordinates:

\[ M= \left( \frac{-2+6}{2}, \frac{4+(-2)}{2}, \frac{7+1}{2} \right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{M=(2,1,4)}. \]

Displacement Vectors

The displacement from

\[ P=(x_1,y_1,z_1) \]

to

\[ Q=(x_2,y_2,z_2) \]

is

\[ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle x_2-x_1,\, y_2-y_1,\, z_2-z_1 \rangle }. \]

The distance \(PQ\) is the magnitude of this vector.

Vector Magnitude in Three Dimensions

For

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle a,b,c\rangle, \]

its magnitude is

\[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2} }. \]

This is the same Pythagorean structure as the distance formula.

Lines in Three Dimensions

A line in space can be described by a point

\[ \mathbf{r}_0= \langle x_0,y_0,z_0\rangle \]

and a nonzero direction vector

\[ \mathbf{v}= \langle a,b,c\rangle. \]

Its vector equation is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0+t\mathbf{v} }. \]

Parametric Equations of a Spatial Line

The vector equation becomes

\[ \boxed{x=x_0+at}, \] \[ \boxed{y=y_0+bt}, \] \[ \boxed{z=z_0+ct}. \]

As \(t\) ranges over the real numbers, these equations generate every point on the line.

Example: Spatial Line

A line passes through

\[ (1,-2,4) \]

with direction vector

\[ \langle3,5,-1\rangle. \]

Its parametric equations are

\[ \boxed{ x=1+3t,\qquad y=-2+5t,\qquad z=4-t }. \]

Planes in Three Dimensions

A plane is a flat two-dimensional surface extending infinitely through three-dimensional space.

A standard plane equation is

\[ \boxed{Ax+By+Cz=D}, \]

where \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) are not all zero.

Normal Vector of a Plane

For

\[ Ax+By+Cz=D, \]

the vector

\[ \boxed{\mathbf{n}=\langle A,B,C\rangle} \]

is perpendicular to the plane.

This vector is called the plane's normal vector.

Point-Normal Form of a Plane

If a plane passes through

\[ (x_0,y_0,z_0) \]

and has normal vector

\[ \langle A,B,C\rangle, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ A(x-x_0)+ B(y-y_0)+ C(z-z_0)=0 }. \]

Example: Equation of a Plane

Find the plane through

\[ (2,1,-3) \]

with normal vector

\[ \langle4,-2,5\rangle. \]

Use point-normal form:

\[ 4(x-2)-2(y-1)+5(z+3)=0. \]

Simplify:

\[ 4x-2y+5z+9=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{4x-2y+5z=-9}. \]

Coordinate Planes as Special Plane Equations

The coordinate planes are special cases of

\[ Ax+By+Cz=D. \]

Specifically,

\[ \boxed{xy\text{-plane}: z=0}, \] \[ \boxed{xz\text{-plane}: y=0}, \] \[ \boxed{yz\text{-plane}: x=0}. \]

Planes Parallel to Coordinate Planes

The equation

\[ z=k \]

describes a plane parallel to the \(xy\)-plane.

Likewise,

\[ y=k \]

is parallel to the \(xz\)-plane, and

\[ x=k \]

is parallel to the \(yz\)-plane.

Spheres

A sphere is the set of all points in three-dimensional space at a fixed distance \(r\) from a fixed center

\[ (h,k,\ell). \]

The distance condition is

\[ \sqrt{ (x-h)^2+ (y-k)^2+ (z-\ell)^2 }=r. \]

Standard Equation of a Sphere

Squaring gives

\[ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+ (y-k)^2+ (z-\ell)^2 = r^2 }. \]

This is the direct three-dimensional analogue of the circle equation.

Example: Reading a Sphere Equation

Consider

\[ (x-2)^2+(y+3)^2+(z-5)^2=49. \]

The center is

\[ \boxed{(2,-3,5)} \]

and the radius is

\[ \boxed{7}. \]

Sphere Centered at the Origin

If the center is the origin, the equation becomes

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2+z^2=r^2}. \]

Every point satisfying this equation lies exactly \(r\) units from \((0,0,0)\).

Cross-Sections of a Sphere

Intersecting a sphere with a coordinate plane produces a circle when the plane passes through or sufficiently near the sphere.

For

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2=25, \]

setting

\[ z=0 \]

gives

\[ x^2+y^2=25. \]

The \(xy\)-cross-section is therefore a circle of radius \(5\).

Traces of Surfaces

A trace is the intersection of a surface with a selected plane.

Setting one coordinate equal to a constant can reduce a three-dimensional surface equation to a two-dimensional curve.

Traces are a powerful way to understand surfaces that are difficult to visualize all at once.

Example: Traces of a Paraboloid

Consider

\[ z=x^2+y^2. \]

Setting

\[ y=0 \]

gives

\[ z=x^2, \]

a parabola in the \(xz\)-plane.

Setting

\[ z=c \]

gives

\[ x^2+y^2=c, \]

a circle when \(c>0\).

Cylinders in Three Dimensions

An equation involving only two of the three coordinates often describes a cylinder extending parallel to the missing coordinate axis.

For example,

\[ x^2+y^2=9 \]

contains no \(z\).

Therefore it describes a circular cylinder of radius \(3\) extending parallel to the \(z\)-axis.

Why a Missing Variable Creates Extension

In

\[ x^2+y^2=9, \]

the value of \(z\) is unrestricted.

Any point

\[ (x,y) \]

satisfying the circle equation may be paired with every real value of

\[ z. \]

The two-dimensional circle is therefore extended through space.

Distance to Coordinate Planes

For a point

\[ P=(x,y,z), \]

the perpendicular distance to the \(xy\)-plane is

\[ \boxed{|z|}. \]

Similarly, the distances to the other coordinate planes are

\[ \boxed{|y|} \]

for the \(xz\)-plane and

\[ \boxed{|x|} \]

for the \(yz\)-plane.

Distance to Coordinate Axes

The distance from

\[ (x,y,z) \]

to the \(z\)-axis depends only on the \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates:

\[ \boxed{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}. \]

Analogously, the distance to the \(x\)-axis is

\[ \boxed{\sqrt{y^2+z^2}}, \]

and the distance to the \(y\)-axis is

\[ \boxed{\sqrt{x^2+z^2}}. \]

Equality of Ordered Triples

Two ordered triples are equal exactly when corresponding coordinates are equal:

\[ \boxed{ (a,b,c)=(d,e,f) \iff a=d,\ b=e,\ c=f }. \]

For example, if

\[ (x,2y,z-1)=(4,10,6), \]

then

\[ \boxed{x=4,\qquad y=5,\qquad z=7}. \]

Spatial Coordinates and Data

Three coordinates can encode more than physical location.

A point

\[ (x,y,z) \]

may represent three measured variables in a data set, three state variables in a model, or three components of a vector.

Coordinate geometry therefore provides a language that extends well beyond literal physical space.

From Three Dimensions to Higher Dimensions

The pattern continues.

A point in \(n\)-dimensional Cartesian space is represented by

\[ \boxed{ (x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n) }. \]

Its distance from the origin is

\[ \boxed{ \sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots+x_n^2} }. \]

Visualization becomes difficult, but the algebraic structure remains consistent.

Cylindrical Coordinates Preview

Three-dimensional space can also be described using cylindrical coordinates

\[ \boxed{(r,\theta,z)}. \]

The first two coordinates are polar coordinates in the \(xy\)-plane, while \(z\) records vertical position.

The conversions are

\[ x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta, \qquad z=z. \]

Spherical Coordinates Preview

Spherical coordinates describe a point using one radial distance and two angles.

Different fields use different angle conventions, so notation must always be defined carefully.

The central idea is that three-dimensional location can be expressed in terms of distance from the origin and angular direction rather than three perpendicular distances.

Coordinate Systems Are Representations

A geometric point exists independently of the particular coordinates used to describe it.

Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates can assign different numerical descriptions to the same location.

Choosing a coordinate system is therefore a modeling decision.

Common Error: Reordering the Coordinates

The points

\[ (2,3,5) \]

and

\[ (5,3,2) \]

are generally different.

The first, second, and third coordinates have assigned geometric roles and cannot be reordered arbitrarily.

Common Error: Confusing an Axis with a Plane

The equation

\[ z=0 \]

describes the entire \(xy\)-plane, not the \(x\)-axis.

The \(x\)-axis requires both

\[ y=0 \]

and

\[ z=0. \]

Common Error: Omitting a Coordinate Difference in Distance

Three-dimensional distance requires all three coordinate changes:

\[ \sqrt{ (\Delta x)^2+ (\Delta y)^2+ (\Delta z)^2 }. \]

Using only \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\) computes the distance between projections onto the \(xy\)-plane, not the full spatial distance.

Common Error: Treating a Sphere as a Circle

The equation

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2=25 \]

describes a sphere in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

The equation

\[ x^2+y^2=25 \]

describes a circle in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), but in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) it describes a cylinder because \(z\) is unrestricted.

Common Error: Assuming Octant Numbering Is Universal

The sign pattern of a point is always unambiguous.

Octant numbering beyond the first octant is not as standardized as quadrant numbering in the plane.

When precision matters, state the coordinate signs directly.

A Reliable Procedure for Reading a Point in Space

  1. Read the \(x\)-coordinate.
  2. Read the \(y\)-coordinate.
  3. Read the \(z\)-coordinate.
  4. Preserve the order \((x,y,z)\).
  5. Use coordinate signs to determine directional placement.
  6. Check whether any zero coordinate places the point on a coordinate plane or axis.

A Reliable Procedure for Spatial Distance

  1. Label the two points consistently.
  2. Compute \(x_2-x_1\).
  3. Compute \(y_2-y_1\).
  4. Compute \(z_2-z_1\).
  5. Square all three differences.
  6. Add the squares.
  7. Take the nonnegative square root.
  8. Simplify the radical if possible.

What to Remember

Three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates use

\[ \boxed{(x,y,z)}. \]

The coordinate planes are

\[ \boxed{z=0,\qquad y=0,\qquad x=0}. \]

Distance between

\[ (x_1,y_1,z_1) \]

and

\[ (x_2,y_2,z_2) \]

is

\[ \boxed{ d= \sqrt{ (x_2-x_1)^2+ (y_2-y_1)^2+ (z_2-z_1)^2 } }. \]

The midpoint is

\[ \boxed{ \left( \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}, \frac{z_1+z_2}{2} \right) }. \]

A sphere centered at \((h,k,\ell)\) has equation

\[ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2+(z-\ell)^2=r^2 }. \]

The key principle is that the familiar coordinate ideas from the plane extend coordinate by coordinate into space.
