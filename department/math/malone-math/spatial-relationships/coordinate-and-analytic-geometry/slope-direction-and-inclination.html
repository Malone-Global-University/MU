Slope, Direction, and Inclination

Distance tells us how far apart two points are. Slope tells us how a line or segment is oriented by comparing vertical change with horizontal change. This signed rate connects geometric direction with algebra and leads directly to equations of lines.

The Central Idea

Suppose two distinct points are

\[ P=(x_1,y_1) \]

and

\[ Q=(x_2,y_2). \]

Their horizontal change is

\[ \Delta x=x_2-x_1, \]

and their vertical change is

\[ \Delta y=y_2-y_1. \]

When \(\Delta x\ne0\), the slope is

\[ \boxed{ m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} }. \]

Slope compares vertical change to horizontal change.

Rise Over Run

Slope is often remembered as

\[ \boxed{ m=\frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} }. \]

The rise is the vertical change, and the run is the horizontal change.

Because both changes are signed, slope carries information about direction, not merely steepness.

Example: Positive Slope

Find the slope through

\[ (1,2) \]

and

\[ (5,10). \]

Then

\[ \Delta y=10-2=8, \] \[ \Delta x=5-1=4. \]

Therefore,

\[ m=\frac{8}{4}=\boxed{2}. \]

For every one unit of horizontal movement to the right, the line rises two units.

Positive Slope

A line with positive slope rises as \(x\) increases.

Moving from left to right, the line goes upward.

For example,

\[ m=\frac{3}{2} \]

means that a run of \(2\) units to the right corresponds to a rise of \(3\) units.

Positive slope represents increasing vertical position as horizontal position increases.

Negative Slope

A line with negative slope falls as \(x\) increases.

Moving from left to right, the line goes downward.

For example,

\[ m=-\frac{4}{3} \]

can be interpreted as a run of \(3\) units to the right and a fall of \(4\) units.

Example: Negative Slope

Find the slope through

\[ (-2,7) \]

and

\[ (4,-5). \]

Compute

\[ \Delta y=-5-7=-12, \] \[ \Delta x=4-(-2)=6. \]

Thus,

\[ m=\frac{-12}{6}=\boxed{-2}. \]

Zero Slope

A horizontal line has no vertical change.

If

\[ y_2-y_1=0 \]

and

\[ x_2-x_1\ne0, \]

then

\[ m=\frac{0}{x_2-x_1}=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{horizontal lines have slope }0}. \]

Example: Zero Slope

Consider

\[ (-3,5) \]

and

\[ (8,5). \]

The \(y\)-coordinates are equal, so

\[ \Delta y=5-5=0. \]

Hence,

\[ m=\frac{0}{11}=\boxed{0}. \]

Undefined Slope

A vertical line has no horizontal change.

If

\[ x_2-x_1=0, \]

then the slope expression would require division by zero:

\[ \frac{y_2-y_1}{0}. \]

Division by zero is undefined.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{vertical lines have undefined slope}}. \]

Example: Undefined Slope

Consider

\[ (4,-2) \]

and

\[ (4,9). \]

The \(x\)-coordinates are equal, so

\[ x_2-x_1=4-4=0. \]

The segment is vertical, and its slope is undefined.

Slope Is a Ratio, Not a Length

Distance and slope answer different questions.

Distance measures separation:

\[ d=\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}. \]

Slope measures relative change:

\[ m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}. \]

A long segment and a short segment can have the same slope if they point in the same line direction.

Slope Does Not Depend on Which Point Is First

Suppose

\[ m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}. \]

Reversing the point order gives

\[ \frac{y_1-y_2}{x_1-x_2}. \]

Both numerator and denominator change sign:

\[ \frac{-(y_2-y_1)}{-(x_2-x_1)} = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}. \]

Therefore the slope is unchanged.

Consistency of Subtraction Order

When calculating slope, the subtraction order must match.

Either use

\[ \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} \]

or

\[ \frac{y_1-y_2}{x_1-x_2}. \]

Both are correct.

What is incorrect is mixing the orders:

\[ \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_1-x_2}. \]

That introduces an unwanted negative sign.

Slope as a Rate of Change

If \(y\) depends on \(x\), then slope describes how much \(y\) changes for each unit change in \(x\).

For example,

\[ m=5 \]

means

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=5. \]

If \(x\) increases by \(1\), \(y\) increases by \(5\).

This makes slope both a geometric quantity and an applied rate.

Units of Slope

If coordinates represent measured quantities, slope can have units.

Suppose horizontal position is measured in seconds and vertical position in meters. Then

\[ m=\frac{\text{meters}}{\text{second}}. \]

In that context, slope represents a velocity-like rate.

If both axes use the same unit, slope is a ratio of like units and may be dimensionless.

Steepness and the Magnitude of Slope

For nonvertical lines, the magnitude

\[ |m| \]

measures steepness relative to the horizontal axis.

A line with

\[ |m|=5 \]

is steeper than a line with

\[ |m|=\frac12, \]

provided the graph uses comparable axis scaling.

Graph Scale Can Distort Visual Steepness

A graph can visually stretch one axis more than the other.

When unequal display scales are used, a line may appear steeper or flatter than its numerical slope suggests.

The algebraic slope remains

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}, \]

regardless of how the graph is displayed.

This is one reason numerical calculation is more reliable than visual estimation alone.

Direction from the Sign of the Slope

The sign of the slope summarizes left-to-right behavior.

  • If \(m>0\), the line rises from left to right.
  • If \(m<0\), the line falls from left to right.
  • If \(m=0\), the line is horizontal.
  • If the slope is undefined, the line is vertical.

These four cases provide the basic directional classification of straight lines in the Cartesian plane.

Direction Vectors and Slope

A displacement from one point on a line to another can be written as

\[ (\Delta x,\Delta y). \]

This ordered pair describes a direction along the line.

When \(\Delta x\ne0\),

\[ m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}. \]

Multiplying both components by the same nonzero number changes the size of the displacement but not the line direction.

Example: Equivalent Direction Changes

Suppose a line has

\[ m=\frac23. \]

A possible direction change is

\[ (\Delta x,\Delta y)=(3,2). \]

Other equivalent direction changes include

\[ (6,4),\qquad(9,6),\qquad(-3,-2). \]

Each gives the same ratio:

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac23. \]

Angle of Inclination

A nonvertical line can also be described by its angle of inclination.

This is the angle \(\theta\) measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis to the line, with

\[ 0^\circ\le\theta<180^\circ. \]

Horizontal lines have

\[ \theta=0^\circ. \]

Vertical lines have

\[ \theta=90^\circ. \]

Slope and the Tangent of the Inclination

For a nonvertical line,

\[ \boxed{m=\tan\theta}. \]

This follows from the right triangle formed by horizontal and vertical change:

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}. \]

Thus slope is the tangent of the line's angle of inclination.

Finding Inclination from Slope

For a nonvertical line, the inclination can be found from

\[ \tan\theta=m. \]

When the principal inverse tangent gives an angle in the correct inclination range, we may write

\[ \theta=\arctan(m). \]

For negative slopes, the inclination lies in Quadrant II and therefore requires care when converting from an inverse-tangent calculator result.

Example: Inclination of a Positive-Slope Line

Suppose

\[ m=1. \]

Then

\[ \tan\theta=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\theta=45^\circ}. \]

Example: Inclination of a Negative-Slope Line

Suppose

\[ m=-1. \]

The line falls from left to right, so its inclination must lie between \(90^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\).

Since

\[ \tan 135^\circ=-1, \]

the inclination is

\[ \boxed{\theta=135^\circ}. \]

Vertical Lines and Inclination

A vertical line has inclination

\[ \theta=90^\circ. \]

Since

\[ \tan90^\circ \]

is undefined, the corresponding slope is also undefined.

This agreement between trigonometry and coordinate geometry is not accidental. The two descriptions encode the same orientation.

Parallel Lines

Distinct nonvertical parallel lines have the same direction and therefore the same slope.

\[ \boxed{m_1=m_2}. \]

For example, lines with slopes

\[ \frac34 \]

and

\[ \frac34 \]

are parallel unless they are actually the same line.

Parallel Vertical Lines

Vertical lines are also parallel to one another.

Their slopes are all undefined, so the ordinary numerical equation

\[ m_1=m_2 \]

is not used to compare them.

Instead, their shared vertical direction establishes parallelism.

Perpendicular Lines

For two nonvertical lines with nonzero slopes, perpendicularity is characterized by

\[ \boxed{m_1m_2=-1}. \]

Equivalently, their slopes are negative reciprocals.

If

\[ m_1=\frac23, \]

then a perpendicular slope is

\[ m_2=-\frac32. \]

Horizontal and Vertical Lines Are Perpendicular

A horizontal line has slope

\[ 0, \]

while a vertical line has undefined slope.

Their slopes are not negative reciprocals in the ordinary numerical sense, but their directions differ by

\[ 90^\circ. \]

Therefore horizontal and vertical lines are perpendicular.

Example: Testing Parallelism

Find the slope of the segment through

\[ A=(1,4),\qquad B=(5,10). \]

Then

\[ m_{AB}=\frac{10-4}{5-1}=\frac64=\frac32. \]

Now consider

\[ C=(-2,1),\qquad D=(2,7). \]

Its slope is

\[ m_{CD}=\frac{7-1}{2-(-2)}=\frac64=\frac32. \]

The segments have equal slopes, so their supporting lines are parallel.

Example: Testing Perpendicularity

Suppose one line has slope

\[ m_1=4 \]

and another has slope

\[ m_2=-\frac14. \]

Their product is

\[ 4\left(-\frac14\right)=-1. \]

Therefore the lines are perpendicular.

Slope and Collinearity

Three points are collinear when they lie on the same line.

One coordinate method is to compare slopes.

If

\[ A=(x_1,y_1),\quad B=(x_2,y_2),\quad C=(x_3,y_3), \]

and the relevant slopes satisfy

\[ m_{AB}=m_{BC}, \]

then the three points lie on the same nonvertical line.

Vertical collinearity is handled by checking that the \(x\)-coordinates are equal.

Example: Testing Collinearity

Consider

\[ A=(0,1),\qquad B=(2,5),\qquad C=(5,11). \]

First,

\[ m_{AB}=\frac{5-1}{2-0}=2. \]

Next,

\[ m_{BC}=\frac{11-5}{5-2}=2. \]

Since the slopes match,

\[ \boxed{A,\ B,\text{ and }C\text{ are collinear}}. \]

Slope as a Local Direction Rule

A slope tells how to generate more points on the same line.

If

\[ m=\frac25, \]

then from any known point on the line we may move

\[ 5\text{ units right and }2\text{ units up} \]

to reach another point.

We could also move

\[ 5\text{ units left and }2\text{ units down}. \]

Both moves preserve the line direction.

Slope and Linear Functions

For a linear function of the form

\[ y=mx+b, \]

the coefficient \(m\) is the slope.

The value of \(m\) determines the line's direction and steepness, while \(b\) determines where the line crosses the \(y\)-axis.

The next lesson will develop this equation and related line forms systematically.

Average Rate of Change

For two points on any graph,

\[ (x_1,f(x_1)) \]

and

\[ (x_2,f(x_2)), \]

the slope of the secant line is

\[ \frac{f(x_2)-f(x_1)}{x_2-x_1}. \]

This is the average rate of change of the function over the interval.

For a straight line, this slope is constant everywhere. For a curved graph, it can change from one interval to another.

Constant Slope Characterizes a Line

A nonvertical straight line has the same slope between any two distinct points on it.

This constancy is one of the defining features of a linear relationship.

If the slope between sampled points changes, the points do not all lie on one nonvertical straight line.

Common Error: Flipping Rise and Run

Slope is

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}, \]

not

\[ \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta y}. \]

The vertical change belongs in the numerator and the horizontal change in the denominator.

Common Error: Mixing Subtraction Orders

Suppose the points are

\[ (1,3) \]

and

\[ (5,11). \]

Correct:

\[ \frac{11-3}{5-1}=\frac84=2. \]

Incorrect:

\[ \frac{11-3}{1-5}=\frac8{-4}=-2. \]

The numerator and denominator must use the same point order.

Common Error: Saying a Vertical Line Has Infinite Slope

A vertical line does not have an ordinary real-number slope.

Its slope calculation requires division by zero:

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{0}. \]

Therefore its slope is

\[ \boxed{\text{undefined}}, \]

not an ordinary number called infinity.

Common Error: Confusing Negative Slope with Negative Coordinates

A line can have negative slope even if all of the coordinates in a particular visible portion of the graph are positive.

Slope depends on

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}, \]

not on whether the coordinates themselves are positive or negative.

Common Error: Treating Steepness as Sign

The sign of \(m\) indicates direction.

The magnitude

\[ |m| \]

indicates steepness for nonvertical lines.

For example,

\[ m=-7 \]

is steep and decreasing, while

\[ m=\frac17 \]

is shallow and increasing.

Common Error: Using Negative Reciprocals for Parallel Lines

Equal slopes indicate parallel direction:

\[ m_1=m_2. \]

Negative reciprocal slopes indicate perpendicular direction:

\[ m_1m_2=-1. \]

These two tests should not be confused.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding Slope

  1. Label the two points consistently.
  2. Compute \(y_2-y_1\).
  3. Compute \(x_2-x_1\) using the same point order.
  4. Form \(\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\).
  5. Simplify the ratio when possible.
  6. If the denominator is zero, report the slope as undefined.
  7. Check that the sign matches the line's left-to-right direction.

A Reliable Procedure for Interpreting Slope

  1. Inspect the sign of the slope.
  2. Use the sign to determine increasing, decreasing, horizontal, or vertical behavior.
  3. Use \(|m|\) to compare steepness among nonvertical lines.
  4. Interpret the numerator as vertical change.
  5. Interpret the denominator as horizontal change.
  6. Attach units when the coordinate axes represent measured quantities.
  7. When needed, connect slope to inclination through \(m=\tan\theta\).

What to Remember

For two points

\[ (x_1,y_1) \]

and

\[ (x_2,y_2), \]

the slope is

\[ \boxed{ m= \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} }. \]
  • slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change;
  • positive slope rises from left to right;
  • negative slope falls from left to right;
  • horizontal lines have slope \(0\);
  • vertical lines have undefined slope;
  • equal slopes indicate parallel nonvertical lines;
  • negative reciprocal slopes indicate perpendicular nonvertical lines;
  • for a nonvertical line, \(m=\tan\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle of inclination.

The core interpretation is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{slope} = \frac{\text{change in vertical position}} {\text{change in horizontal position}} }. \]
