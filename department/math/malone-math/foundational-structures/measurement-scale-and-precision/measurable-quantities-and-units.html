Measurable Quantities and Units

A measurement is more than a number. It tells us how much of a particular quantity is present by comparing that quantity with an agreed unit.

A Number Needs Meaning

Suppose someone tells you:

\[ 12. \]

Twelve what?

The number could mean:

  • \(12\) meters,
  • \(12\) seconds,
  • \(12\) kilograms,
  • \(12\) dollars,
  • or simply a count of \(12\) objects.

The numerical value alone does not identify the measured quantity.

A measurement combines a numerical value with a unit:

\[ \boxed{ \text{measurement} = \text{number} \times \text{unit} } \]

For example,

\[ 5\text{ m} \]

means five units of length, where each unit is one meter.

What Is a Quantity?

A quantity is a property that can be described by an amount, magnitude, count, or other numerical value.

Examples include:

  • length,
  • mass,
  • time,
  • temperature,
  • area,
  • speed,
  • energy,
  • number of objects.

Some quantities are measured against units. Others, such as counts, may be dimensionless.

Measurable Quantities

A measurable quantity is one whose magnitude can be compared with a standard unit.

If a table is measured to be

\[ 2.4\text{ m} \]

long, then its length has been compared with the meter.

The value

\[ 2.4 \]

tells how many meter-units correspond to that length.

Measurement therefore depends on a comparison:

\[ \boxed{ \text{quantity} = \text{numerical value}\times\text{chosen unit} } \]

Units

A unit is an agreed standard amount used to measure a quantity.

Examples:

Quantity Example Unit Symbol
Lengthmeter\(\mathrm{m}\)
Timesecond\(\mathrm{s}\)
Masskilogram\(\mathrm{kg}\)
Temperaturekelvin\(\mathrm{K}\)
Electric currentampere\(\mathrm{A}\)

A unit does not merely label a number. It tells us what comparison standard was used.

Numerical Value and Unit

A measurement can be separated conceptually into:

\[ Q=nU, \]

where:

  • \(Q\) is the quantity,
  • \(n\) is the numerical value,
  • \(U\) is the unit.

For example,

\[ Q=3.5\text{ m} \]

has numerical value

\[ n=3.5 \]

and unit

\[ U=\text{m}. \]

If we change units, the numerical value usually changes even though the underlying quantity does not.

Measurement as Comparison with a Scale

Dimensions

A dimension identifies the type of physical quantity being measured.

Common dimensions include:

  • length \(L\),
  • mass \(M\),
  • time \(T\),
  • temperature \(\Theta\).

Meters and feet are different units, but both measure the same dimension: length.

\[ [\text{meter}]=[\text{foot}]=L. \]

Seconds measure time:

\[ [\text{second}]=T. \]

Dimensions are more fundamental than the particular unit chosen.

Base and Derived Quantities

Some quantities are treated as foundational within a measurement system. Others are built from them.

Base Quantity

Length is commonly treated as a base quantity.

Derived Quantity

Area is built from two length measurements:

\[ A=\ell w. \]

Its dimension is

\[ [A]=L^2. \]

Velocity combines length and time:

\[ v=\frac{d}{t}, \]

so

\[ [v]=LT^{-1}. \]

Derived units reflect the same algebraic structure.

Unit Consistency

Quantities can be added or subtracted directly only when their units are compatible.

For example,

\[ 3\text{ m}+2\text{ m}=5\text{ m}. \]

But

\[ 3\text{ m}+2\text{ s} \]

does not represent an ordinary sum of like quantities.

Convert before Adding

We may add

\[ 2\text{ m}+50\text{ cm}, \]

but first it is useful to use a common unit:

\[ 50\text{ cm}=0.5\text{ m}. \]

Then

\[ 2\text{ m}+0.5\text{ m} = \boxed{2.5\text{ m}}. \]

Different Units, Same Dimension

The same quantity can be represented in different units.

For example,

\[ 1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}. \]

The numerical values differ:

\[ 1\neq100, \]

but the physical length is the same because the units differ.

\[ \boxed{ 1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm} } \]

This is another example of the distinction between a mathematical object and its representation: the quantity is unchanged while its numerical-unit form changes.

Counts versus Measurements

Counting and measuring both produce numbers, but they are not identical processes.

Count

A box contains exactly

\[ 12 \]

pencils.

The value is discrete and usually exact.

Measurement

A pencil may be measured as

\[ 17.8\text{ cm}. \]

That value depends on the measuring instrument and its precision.

Counts are often dimensionless. Measurements usually carry units and measurement uncertainty.

Exact and Measured Values

Some numerical values are exact by definition or counting.

For example, one dozen means exactly

\[ 12. \]

But a measured length such as

\[ 4.72\text{ cm} \]

is normally understood as a reported measurement with finite precision.

The written digits do not imply infinite accuracy.

This distinction will matter later when we study:

  • precision,
  • uncertainty,
  • significant figures,
  • rounding,
  • measurement error.

Compound Units

Derived quantities often use compound units.

Speed

\[ \frac{\text{meters}}{\text{second}} = \mathrm{m/s}. \]

Area

\[ \mathrm{m}\cdot\mathrm{m} = \mathrm{m^2}. \]

Volume

\[ \mathrm{m^3}. \]

Acceleration

\[ \frac{\mathrm{m/s}}{\mathrm{s}} = \mathrm{m/s^2}. \]

The units reflect how the underlying quantities are multiplied and divided.

Units as Algebraic Factors

Units can often be handled algebraically.

Suppose a car travels

\[ 150\text{ km} \]

in

\[ 3\text{ h}. \]

Its average speed is

\[ v= \frac{150\text{ km}}{3\text{ h}} = 50\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}. \]

The numbers divide:

\[ \frac{150}{3}=50, \]

and the units divide:

\[ \frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}. \]

This parallel treatment is the foundation of dimensional analysis and unit conversion.

Practical Applications

Construction

Lengths, areas, volumes, and tolerances must be recorded with compatible units.

Science

Measurements are meaningful only when quantity, numerical value, and unit are reported clearly.

Medicine and Laboratory Work

Concentrations, masses, volumes, and rates depend critically on correct unit interpretation.

Business

Quantities such as dollars per unit, cost per hour, and revenue per customer are compound-unit rates.

Engineering

Unit consistency provides a powerful check on formulas and calculations.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Identify Number and Unit

For

\[ 7.5\text{ kg}, \]

the numerical value is

\[ \boxed{7.5} \]

and the unit is

\[ \boxed{\mathrm{kg}}. \]

Example 2: Add Compatible Measurements

\[ 4.2\text{ m}+1.7\text{ m} = \boxed{5.9\text{ m}}. \]

Example 3: Convert before Adding

Find

\[ 3\text{ m}+40\text{ cm}. \]

Convert:

\[ 40\text{ cm}=0.4\text{ m}. \]

Then:

\[ 3+0.4=3.4. \] \[ \boxed{3.4\text{ m}}. \]

Example 4: Derived Unit

A rectangle has

\[ \ell=5\text{ m} \]

and

\[ w=3\text{ m}. \]

Its area is

\[ A=(5\text{ m})(3\text{ m}) = 15\text{ m}^2. \] \[ \boxed{A=15\text{ m}^2}. \]

Example 5: Rate

A machine produces \(240\) parts in \(8\) hours.

\[ \frac{240\text{ parts}}{8\text{ h}} = \boxed{30\text{ parts/h}}. \]

Example 6: Dimension Check

Suppose

\[ d=vt. \]

If velocity has dimension

\[ LT^{-1} \]

and time has dimension

\[ T, \]

then

\[ [vt] = (LT^{-1})(T) = L. \]

So the right-hand side has the dimension of length, matching \(d\).

Common Errors

1. Dropping Units Too Early

Units help track meaning and catch mistakes.

2. Adding Incompatible Quantities

Length and time cannot ordinarily be added as though they were like terms.

3. Adding Compatible Quantities before Converting Units

Convert to a common unit first when necessary.

4. Confusing Numerical Value with Quantity

The numerical value changes when the unit changes, even though the measured quantity may remain the same.

5. Treating a Measured Value as Infinitely Exact

Measurements have finite precision.

6. Forgetting Squared or Cubed Units

Area and volume require

\[ \mathrm{unit}^2 \]

and

\[ \mathrm{unit}^3, \]

respectively.

for seconds.

Compatible Addition

Compute

\[ 2.3\text{ kg}+1.2\text{ kg}. \]
Check Answer \[ \boxed{3.5\text{ kg}}. \]

Compute

\[ 1\text{ m}+25\text{ cm} \]

in meters.

Check Answer \[ 25\text{ cm}=0.25\text{ m}, \] \[ 1+0.25=\boxed{1.25\text{ m}}. \]

What unit results from

\[ (4\text{ m})(7\text{ m})? \]
Check Answer \[ \boxed{\mathrm{m^2}}. \]

A runner travels \(10\) kilometers in \(2\) hours. What is the average speed?

Check Answer \[ \frac{10\text{ km}}{2\text{ h}} = \boxed{5\text{ km/h}}. \]

The Deeper Idea

Measurement is another form of representation.

The physical or abstract quantity is the object.

The numerical value and unit are the representation.

\[ \boxed{ \text{quantity} \longleftrightarrow \text{number + unit} } \]

Changing from meters to centimeters changes the representation but not the underlying length.

This parallels the representation ideas developed earlier:

  • fractions and decimals can represent the same number,
  • Cartesian and polar coordinates can represent the same point,
  • different units can represent the same measured quantity.

The new challenge is that measurements also introduce scale, precision, and uncertainty. Those ideas build on the same distinction between object and representation.

Summary

  • A quantity is a property described by an amount or magnitude.
  • A measurable quantity is compared with an agreed unit.
  • A measurement can be represented as \[ Q=nU, \] where \(n\) is the numerical value and \(U\) is the unit.
  • Units identify the standard used for comparison.
  • Dimensions identify the type of quantity being measured.
  • Different units can measure the same dimension.
  • Derived quantities combine base quantities algebraically.
  • Compatible quantities should be expressed in compatible units before addition or subtraction.
  • Counts are often exact and dimensionless; measurements usually involve units and finite precision.
  • Compound units arise naturally from multiplication and division of quantities.
  • Units can often be manipulated algebraically during calculations.
  • Changing units changes the numerical representation but not the underlying quantity.

A measurement is meaningful because number and unit work together. Keeping units visible helps preserve meaning, verify calculations, and prepare for later work with conversion, precision, uncertainty, and dimensional analysis.
