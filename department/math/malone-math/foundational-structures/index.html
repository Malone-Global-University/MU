Purpose: Establish the language, objects, laws, representations, and reusable tools needed throughout mathematics. This bucket prevents later explorations from being interrupted by explanations of prerequisite concepts.

Foundational Structures introduces ideas operationally at their earliest useful point. Students learn to recognize, describe, represent, and use a tool at an appropriate level before its deeper theory is developed under Algebraic Relationships, Spatial Relationships, Discrete and Stochastic Relationships, or Modeling and Analysis.

The governing progression is:

[ \text{recognition and terminology} \longrightarrow \text{representation} \longrightarrow \text{light computation} \longrightarrow \text{practical use} \longrightarrow \text{interbranch application} \longrightarrow \text{formal development} ]

A curriculum scope matrix should identify the current level at which each tool is being used, where it will reappear, and where its deeper theory will be developed. A separate logic progression matrix should map the movement from naive reasoning to increasing levels of rigor.

0.1 Mathematical Language and Objects

Establish what mathematics studies and how mathematical ideas are named, classified, and communicated.

Mathematics

Mathematics is the study of structure, quantity, space, change, uncertainty, and the relationships among them.

It provides a precise language for:

  • Describing objects and patterns
  • Comparing quantities and structures
  • Representing relationships
  • Reasoning from assumptions
  • Calculating and transforming information
  • Building models of real and abstract systems
  • Discovering what must be true

A concise instructional definition is:

Mathematics is a system of language, relationships, operations, and reasoning used to represent and understand patterns, quantities, structures, space, change, and uncertainty.

Mathematical objects

Mathematical objects are the things mathematics talks about, studies, compares, represents, and transforms.

  • Numbers
  • Points
  • Shapes
  • Symbols
  • Expressions
  • Equations
  • Sets
  • Relations
  • Functions
  • Sequences
  • Vectors
  • Matrices
  • Graphs and networks
  • Spaces
  • Mathematical structures

A useful distinction is:

  • A mathematical object is what is being studied.
  • A property describes an object.
  • An operation changes or combines objects.
  • A relationship connects objects.
  • A representation displays or encodes an object.

Language and classification

  • Definitions and conventions
  • Symbols and notation
  • Variables, constants, parameters, and coefficients
  • Terms, expressions, equations, formulas, and identities
  • Statements and open sentences
  • Equality, equivalence, and identity
  • Classification and mathematical properties
  • Examples and nonexamples
  • Counterexamples
  • Finite and infinite objects
  • Concrete and abstract descriptions
  • Mathematical abstraction
  • Reading mathematical notation
  • Writing mathematical explanations

0.3 Numbers and Quantities

Develop numbers as objects representing quantity, order, magnitude, measurement, position, and algebraic possibility.

Number systems

  • Counting numbers
  • Natural numbers
  • Whole numbers
  • Integers
  • Rational numbers
  • Irrational numbers
  • Real numbers
  • Imaginary numbers
  • Complex numbers
  • Positive and negative numbers
  • Number-system inclusion
  • Closure within number systems
  • Extension of number systems

The progression should explain why number systems expand:

[ \mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{W} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C} ]

Under the Malone Math convention, (\mathbb{N}) denotes the positive counting numbers and (\mathbb{W}) includes zero. Each extension allows previously impossible operations or equations to be handled.

Forms and properties of quantity

  • Fractions
  • Decimals
  • Ratios
  • Percentages
  • Place value
  • Magnitude
  • Absolute value
  • Signed quantities
  • Exact values
  • Approximate values
  • Measured values
  • Units and dimensions
  • Scalars

Scalars are initially treated as zero-dimensional coefficients or multipliers. They later scale vectors and eventually become named quantities contrasted with vector quantities, such as speed compared with velocity.

0.7 Representations and Mathematical Spaces

Teach students to move among representations rather than treating each representation as a separate topic. Each representation is introduced at the simplest level needed for immediate use.

Core representations

  1. Natural language
  2. Numerical examples
  3. Symbolic notation
  4. Tables
  5. Lists and sequences
  6. Diagrams
  7. Number lines
  8. Coordinate graphs
  9. Geometric constructions
  10. Algorithms
  11. Data displays

The purpose is representational fluency: recognizing that one object or relationship may appear in verbal, numerical, symbolic, tabular, sequential, graphical, geometric, or procedural form.

Coordinate frameworks

  1. Coordinates and ordered pairs
  2. Axes, origins, units, and scales
  3. The Cartesian plane
  4. Quadrants
  5. Position, distance, and direction
  6. The real plane
  7. The complex plane
  8. Polar representation
  9. Dimensions and coordinates
  10. Introduction to higher-dimensional representation

The purpose is orientation within mathematical space. Students learn what these frameworks are and how to locate or represent simple objects within them. Detailed geometry, transformations, vector analysis, and multidimensional modeling develop later.

Structured representations

  1. Tuples
  2. Vectors
  3. Matrices
  4. Arrays
  5. Coordinate lists
  6. Networks and graphs
  7. Trees

These objects are introduced as organized containers for mathematical information. Students should recognize, read, construct, and perform a few immediately useful operations with them before their deeper theories are developed.

Translation among representations

  1. Words to symbols
  2. Symbols to tables
  3. Tables to graphs
  4. Lists to sequences
  5. Coordinates to geometric objects
  6. Data to arrays or matrices
  7. Relationships to networks or graphs
  8. Procedures to algorithms
  9. Comparing equivalent representations
  10. Choosing an efficient representation

A single relationship can be translated through several forms:

  • Natural language: Each output is twice the input.
  • Numerical examples: (1\mapsto2,\ 2\mapsto4,\ 3\mapsto6)
  • Symbolic notation: (y=2x)
  • Table: corresponding (x)- and (y)-values
  • Sequence: (2,4,6,8,\ldots)
  • Coordinate list: ((1,2),(2,4),(3,6),\ldots)
  • Graph: points lying on a line
  • Matrix or array: values organized for computation
  • Algorithm: input (x), multiply by (2), and return (y)

0.10 Problem Solving and Verification

Create a common operating method for all later explorations.

General problem-solving process

  1. Identify the known information.
  2. Identify the unknown quantity or relationship.
  3. Classify the mathematical objects involved.
  4. Identify units, conditions, and restrictions.
  5. Select an appropriate representation.
  6. Choose an operation, law, model, or algorithm.
  7. Carry out the method.
  8. Check the calculation.
  9. Interpret the result.
  10. Test whether the answer is reasonable.
  11. Communicate the reasoning clearly.

Verification methods

  • Estimation
  • Substitution
  • Inverse operations
  • Alternative representations
  • Boundary testing
  • Dimensional checks
  • Graphical checks
  • Special cases
  • Counterexamples
  • Independent solution methods
  • Testing assumptions
  • Checking domain restrictions
  • Comparing exact and approximate answers

Problem solving should be practical, explanatory, and connected to meaningful applications. Verification is treated as part of solving rather than as an optional final gesture.

Internal progression

This is a recommended learning flow, not an inflexible prerequisite chain. Explorations can revisit any strand whenever a concept requires another layer of intuition, skill, or formalization.

Recurring tool progression

Stage Student use Curriculum role
Encounter Recognize the object and its purpose Prevent later terminology detours
Represent Read, construct, and translate basic forms Build orientation and fluency
Compute Perform a few immediately useful operations Make the object operational
Apply Use the tool in meaningful problems Establish practical value
Revisit Use it across branches and representations Build familiarity and intuition
Formalize Study deeper properties, limits, and theory Reach mature mathematical treatment

Boundary with later buckets

Foundational treatment Later development
Define variables, expressions, equations, and identities Manipulate, transform, and solve them under Algebraic Relationships
Define functions as mappings and perform light evaluations Study function families, variation, transformations, equations, and models later
Introduce the number line, coordinate planes, and mathematical spaces Analyze curves, shapes, motion, and spatial transformations later
Introduce scalars as coefficients and multipliers Scale vectors and distinguish scalar from vector quantities in later mathematics and physics
Introduce vectors and matrices as structured tools Use them in transformations, systems, networks, data, and multidimensional models later
Introduce sequences as ordered objects and representations Study recurrence, series, convergence, and discrete structures later
Introduce sets, relations, and probability language Develop counting, finite structures, probability models, and inference later
Introduce algorithms and computational thinking Analyze numerical and computational methods within their later mathematical purposes
Introduce approximation, measurement error, and tolerance Develop numerical analysis, model sensitivity, and applied approximation later
Introduce naive logic, informal proof, and verification Develop formal proof methods and mathematical theories at increasing levels of rigor

Governing editorial principle

Introduce mathematical objects, terminology, representations, and tools at their earliest useful point. Use them immediately at an appropriate level, revisit them across branches, and increase their sophistication as the learner's mathematical maturity grows.
