Definitions, Axioms, Postulates, and Theorems

Seeing how mathematical language, assumptions, and proof build a theory.

The Big Idea

Mathematics is not a loose collection of facts. It is built in layers. Some statements establish what words mean. Some provide starting assumptions. Others are conclusions justified from those meanings and assumptions.

Four terms help us see this structure:

  • Definitions establish meaning.
  • Axioms provide accepted starting assumptions.
  • Postulates usually serve the same logical role as axioms, often within a particular subject such as geometry.
  • Theorems are propositions proved from definitions, axioms, and earlier results.

Once these roles are separated, a proof becomes easier to understand: it is a documented path from the starting framework to a new conclusion.

A Quick Structural Comparison

Roles within a mathematical theory
Item Main job Proved within the theory? Typical form
Definition Assigns precise meaning to a term or symbol No; it stipulates usage “An integer is even iff…”
Axiom States a starting assumption No; it is adopted in the current theory Equality, order, or algebraic structure rule
Postulate States a starting assumption, often for a specific subject No; it is adopted in the current theory Geometric incidence or parallel rule
Theorem Records a proved mathematical consequence Yes Hypotheses followed by a conclusion

The names indicate function, not importance. A short definition may support thousands of theorems, while a famous theorem may depend on several modest lemmas.

Definitions Establish Meaning

A definition specifies how a term or symbol will be used. It gives the necessary and sufficient conditions for applying that term within the stated context.

For example:

\[ n\text{ is even} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists k\in\mathbb{Z}\,(n=2k). \]

This definition tells us exactly which integers receive the label even. It does not report an experimental discovery; it fixes a mathematical meaning that later arguments may use.

Definitions are not normally proved as theorems. We evaluate them by asking whether they are clear, noncircular, consistent with the surrounding theory, and useful for the intended classification.

What Makes a Definition Work?

A strong mathematical definition should be:

  • Precise: it gives an objective membership test.
  • Exact: it includes all intended objects and excludes unintended ones.
  • Noncircular: it does not secretly use the term being defined.
  • Context-aware: it identifies the domain and active conventions.
  • Compatible: it does not conflict with definitions already in force.

“A square is a nice-looking quadrilateral” is too subjective. “A square is a quadrilateral with four congruent sides and four right angles” gives checkable conditions.

Definitions may vary across contexts. Some authors include \(0\) among the natural numbers and others do not. Either convention can work if it is stated and used consistently.

Not Every Term Can Be Defined

If every term were defined using earlier terms, the chain would either continue forever or eventually become circular. A formal system therefore begins with a small collection of primitive or undefined terms.

In elementary geometry, words such as point, line, and lies on may be treated as primitive. Their behavior is controlled by axioms and examples rather than reduced to still earlier geometric definitions.

“Undefined” does not mean meaningless. It means the term is foundational within that system. Its relationships are specified by the assumptions of the theory.

Axioms Supply Starting Assumptions

An axiom is a proposition accepted as a starting point within a mathematical theory. It is not proved from earlier statements in that same theory.

Historically, axioms were sometimes described as self-evident truths. Modern mathematics uses a more flexible view: axioms are explicit assumptions that determine the kind of structure being studied.

For example, one familiar property of equality is:

\[ \forall a,\quad a=a. \]

This is the reflexive property. Other axioms may describe how an operation behaves, how points and lines relate, or which sets exist.

An axiom in one theory may be provable as a theorem in a stronger theory. “Axiom” names its role in the current framework, not an eternal label attached to the sentence.

Postulates Usually Play the Same Logical Role

A postulate is also an accepted starting assumption. In many modern texts, axiom and postulate are interchangeable.

A traditional distinction calls very general assumptions axioms and subject-specific assumptions postulates. Geometry often preserves this language. Euclid’s parallel postulate, for example, concerns the behavior of parallel lines in Euclidean geometry.

The terminology varies, but the logical role is the same: neither an axiom nor a postulate is proved from earlier results within the current theory. Both help define what follows.

Theorems Are Proved Consequences

A theorem is a mathematical proposition established by proof. The proof may use:

  • definitions,
  • axioms or postulates,
  • rules of logic, and
  • theorems proved earlier.

Many theorems have conditional form:

\[ \text{If the hypotheses hold, then the conclusion follows.} \]

The theorem does not promise the conclusion outside its hypotheses. Reading those hypotheses carefully is part of using the result correctly.

A Small Theorem Built from a Definition

Theorem. The sum of two even integers is even.

Let \(m\) and \(n\) be even integers. By the definition of evenness, there are integers \(a\) and \(b\) such that

\[ m=2a \qquad\text{and}\qquad n=2b. \]

Then

\[ m+n=2a+2b=2(a+b). \]

The integers are closed under addition, so \(a+b\in\mathbb{Z}\). Therefore \(m+n\) has the form \(2k\) for an integer \(k=a+b\). By the definition of evenness, \(m+n\) is even.

This proof is short, but its architecture is visible: hypotheses activate a definition, algebra transforms the expression, closure keeps the new object in the domain, and the definition identifies the conclusion.

Lemmas, Corollaries, and Propositions

Proved results receive different labels according to how they are used.

Common names for proved results
Name Typical role
Theorem A result presented as a major or central conclusion
Lemma A proved result used mainly to help prove another result
Corollary A result that follows quickly from a theorem
Proposition A proved result, often of moderate scope

These labels do not mark different kinds of truth. All are proved propositions. A result may be a lemma in one presentation and a theorem in another because the label reflects its role in the argument.

Conjectures and Open Problems

A conjecture is a proposition believed to be true but not yet proved. It may be supported by patterns, calculations, computer searches, partial results, or analogy.

Evidence is valuable, but it does not change a conjecture into a theorem. A universal claim can survive millions of tests and still fail at an untested case.

Once a valid proof is found and checked, the conjecture becomes a theorem. If a counterexample is found, the conjecture is false as stated and may need revision.

An open problem is a mathematical question whose resolution is not yet known. Some open problems ask whether a conjecture is true; others ask for a construction, classification, or optimal value.

“Hypothesis” Has Two Common Uses

In a conditional theorem, a hypothesis is an assumption placed before the conclusion:

\[ p\to q. \]

Here \(p\) is the hypothesis and \(q\) is the conclusion. The word does not mean that \(p\) is uncertain; it means the theorem applies when \(p\) holds.

In empirical science, hypothesis often means a proposed explanation to be tested against evidence. The mathematical and scientific uses are related but not identical. Context tells us which role is intended.

How the Parts Work Together

A typical dependency chain
Layer Contribution Example
Primitive terms Supply foundational objects and relations Point, line, incidence
Definitions Create exact classifications from available terms Even integer, triangle, function
Axioms and postulates State the permitted starting relationships Equality rules, parallel postulate
Logic Controls valid inference Modus ponens, contradiction
Theorems Record consequences proved from earlier layers Angle-sum and divisibility results

The actual structure is a network rather than a single ladder. One theorem may depend on several earlier results, and one definition may support many different branches.

Changing an Axiom Can Change the Theory

Axioms determine which mathematical world is being studied. Change an assumption, and different theorems may follow.

In Euclidean geometry, the familiar parallel postulate supports the theorem that a triangle’s interior angles sum to \(180^\circ\). Geometries built with different parallel behavior can produce triangle angle sums greater than or less than \(180^\circ\).

This does not mean mathematics has abandoned truth. It means truth is evaluated relative to a stated system. A theorem says, in effect, “given these definitions and assumptions, this conclusion follows.”

Models Give Axioms an Interpretation

A model is a collection of objects and relations that satisfies the axioms of a theory. Models show that abstract symbols can describe coherent structures.

For example, the ordinary real number line models familiar axioms of ordered arithmetic. Points represent real numbers, order corresponds to position, and addition behaves consistently with translation along the line.

A model can demonstrate that a collection of axioms is jointly satisfiable. Different models of the same axioms may look different while sharing the structural properties expressed by the theory.

Consistency, Independence, and Completeness

Mathematicians may evaluate an axiomatic system using several deeper ideas:

  • Consistency: the axioms do not allow both a proposition and its negation to be proved.
  • Independence: a particular axiom cannot be proved from the remaining axioms.
  • Completeness: in a specified sense, every relevant statement can be settled by the system.

These ideas become technically demanding in formal logic, but the basic questions are approachable: Do the starting rules conflict? Is one assumption redundant? Are there meaningful questions the rules cannot decide?

Definitions, Conventions, and Axioms Are Not the Same

A definition fixes meaning. A convention chooses among workable representations or usages. An axiom states a starting proposition whose consequences will be studied.

Three different foundational choices
Choice Example What it controls
Definition An even integer has the form \(2k\) Which integers are called even
Convention Whether \(\mathbb{N}\) includes \(0\) How notation is used in context
Axiom An operation has an identity element Which structures satisfy the theory

The categories can interact, but asking what job a sentence performs usually reveals the correct label.

Laws, Rules, Principles, and Identities

Mathematical results are sometimes called laws, rules, principles, formulas, or identities. These names often reflect subject tradition or use rather than a separate place in the logical hierarchy.

The distributive law

\[ a(b+c)=ab+ac \]

may be adopted as an axiom for a structure, proved from a more foundational construction, or used as a familiar rule in elementary algebra. Its status depends on the framework.

When precision matters, ask: Is this sentence defining notation, being assumed, or being proved?

A Theorem-Reading Routine

  1. Identify the domain. What kinds of objects are involved?
  2. Locate the definitions. Which technical terms have exact meanings?
  3. Separate hypotheses from the conclusion.
  4. List the permitted starting facts. Which axioms and earlier results are available?
  5. Follow each inference. What justifies the next step?
  6. Check scope. Does the theorem apply to the current case?
  7. Notice dependencies. Which parts would fail if an assumption changed?
  8. Distinguish proof from evidence. Examples may guide the idea without establishing every case.

This routine turns a theorem from a statement to memorize into a reusable reasoning tool.

Common Confusions

Are axioms simply facts that everyone agrees are obvious?

Not necessarily. In modern mathematics, axioms are explicit starting assumptions for a theory. They may be intuitive, abstract, or chosen to study a particular structure.

What is the difference between an axiom and a postulate?

They usually play the same logical role. A traditional usage reserves “postulate” for subject-specific assumptions, especially in geometry, but terminology varies.

Can a definition be proved?

A definition itself stipulates meaning. We may prove that another description is equivalent to the definition, but that equivalence is a theorem, not the original definition.

Is a lemma less true than a theorem?

No. Both are proved results. “Lemma” usually indicates that the result is being used to support another theorem.

Can the same proposition be an axiom in one system and a theorem in another?

Yes. The label describes the proposition’s role within a particular framework.

Working Summary

Definitions establish meaning, axioms and postulates establish starting assumptions, and theorems record conclusions established by proof. Primitive terms prevent definitions from becoming infinite or circular, while rules of logic connect the starting framework to derived results.

Lemmas, corollaries, and propositions are also proved results; conjectures remain unproved. Models interpret axioms, and changing an axiom may change the mathematical theory. The key question is always structural: is this sentence defining, assuming, or deriving?

Check Your Understanding

What job does the definition of an even integer perform?

It gives the exact condition for using the word “even”: an integer \(n\) is even exactly when \(n=2k\) for some integer \(k\).

Why is an axiom not proved within its own theory?

It is one of the theory’s starting assumptions. Proof begins from axioms, definitions, and logical rules. The same statement may be proved in a stronger surrounding theory.

What distinguishes a theorem from a conjecture?

A theorem has a valid proof. A conjecture is proposed as true but has not yet been proved.

Why can changing the parallel postulate change geometric theorems?

Theorems are consequences of the chosen assumptions. Different assumptions about parallel lines define different geometric systems with different consequences.

What three questions help classify a foundational sentence?

Ask whether the sentence is defining meaning, being accepted as a starting assumption, or being proved from earlier material.
