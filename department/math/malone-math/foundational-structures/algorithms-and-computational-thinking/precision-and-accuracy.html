Precision and Accuracy

A measurement can be repeatable without being correct, and it can be close to the true value without being especially repeatable. Precision and accuracy describe different qualities of measurement, and good quantitative work keeps them separate.

Close Together Is Not the Same as Close to Correct

Suppose the accepted length of a calibration block is

\[ 10.00\text{ cm}. \]

One instrument gives repeated readings:

\[ 9.52,\quad9.51,\quad9.52,\quad9.53,\quad9.52\text{ cm}. \]

These values are tightly clustered.

That suggests good precision.

But the cluster is far from

\[ 10.00\text{ cm}. \]

So the measurements are not very accurate.

\[ \boxed{ \text{precision asks: how consistent?} } \] \[ \boxed{ \text{accuracy asks: how close to the true or accepted value?} } \]

Precision

Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another, or how finely a quantity is resolved or reported.

A set of readings such as

\[ 12.31,\quad12.32,\quad12.31,\quad12.30\text{ cm} \]

shows little spread.

That is evidence of high repeatability and therefore relatively high precision.

Precision concerns the consistency of the measurement process.

Accuracy

Accuracy describes closeness to the true, accepted, or reference value.

If the accepted value is

\[ 12.30\text{ cm} \]

and a measurement is

\[ 12.29\text{ cm}, \]

the measurement is quite accurate.

Accuracy requires a reference for comparison.

Without some accepted or independently justified value, we can discuss repeatability more directly than absolute accuracy.

The Four Common Cases

Resolution and Precision

Instrument resolution affects how finely differences can be observed or reported.

A ruler marked every millimeter can distinguish smaller changes than a ruler marked every centimeter.

However:

\[ \boxed{ \text{fine resolution does not guarantee accuracy} } \]

A digital instrument may display many decimal places while still being systematically miscalibrated.

Displayed detail and trustworthy detail are not automatically the same.

Random Variation

Repeated measurements often vary slightly because of random influences:

  • small changes in reading position,
  • electrical noise,
  • environmental fluctuation,
  • human reaction time,
  • uncontrolled experimental variation.

Random variation tends to create spread among repeated measurements.

More random variation generally reduces precision.

Repeating measurements and summarizing them can help characterize this spread.

Systematic Error and Bias

A systematic error shifts measurements consistently in one direction.

Examples include:

  • a scale that always reads \(0.5\) kg too high,
  • a ruler whose zero mark is worn away,
  • a sensor with a persistent offset,
  • a method that consistently favors one result.

Systematic error can produce measurements that are very precise but not accurate.

The repeated values may cluster tightly around the wrong value.

Calibration

Calibration compares an instrument or measurement process with a trusted reference standard.

Suppose a \(100.00\)-g reference mass produces a reading of

\[ 100.42\text{ g}. \]

The instrument shows a positive offset of

\[ 100.42-100.00 = 0.42\text{ g}. \]

Calibration can reveal systematic error and support correction or adjustment.

Calibration improves confidence in accuracy, but it does not eliminate all sources of uncertainty.

Absolute Error

If \(x_m\) is a measured value and \(x_r\) is an accepted reference value, then the signed error is

\[ x_m-x_r. \]

The absolute error is

\[ \boxed{ |x_m-x_r| } \]

and has the same unit as the measured quantity.

Example

Measured value:

\[ 24.8\text{ cm}. \]

Reference value:

\[ 25.0\text{ cm}. \]

Absolute error:

\[ |24.8-25.0| = \boxed{0.2\text{ cm}}. \]

Relative and Percent Error

Absolute error does not tell us how large the error is relative to the size of the quantity.

Relative error is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{|x_m-x_r|}{|x_r|} } \]

when \(x_r\neq0\).

Percent error is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{|x_m-x_r|}{|x_r|} \times100\% } \]

Example

Using

\[ x_m=24.8, \qquad x_r=25.0, \]

we get

\[ \frac{0.2}{25.0}=0.008. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{0.8\%} \]

percent error.

Repeatability and Reproducibility

Repeatability concerns agreement when measurements are repeated under nearly the same conditions.

Reproducibility concerns agreement when conditions change, such as:

  • different operators,
  • different instruments,
  • different laboratories,
  • different days.

A method may be repeatable within one setup but less reproducible across different setups.

Both ideas contribute to understanding practical precision.

Tolerance and Acceptable Range

Engineering and manufacturing often specify an acceptable range rather than one exact target value.

For example,

\[ 10.00\pm0.05\text{ mm} \]

corresponds to the interval

\[ 9.95\text{ mm} \leq x \leq 10.05\text{ mm}. \]

A measured part may be considered acceptable if it falls inside the tolerance range.

Tolerance is a design or acceptance requirement. Measurement uncertainty is a property of the measurement process. They should not be confused.

Reporting Measurements Responsibly

A measurement report should not imply more certainty than the process supports.

Good reporting may include:

  • appropriate units,
  • appropriate decimal places or significant figures,
  • an uncertainty estimate when relevant,
  • a description of the measurement method,
  • the reference or calibration standard when needed.

A long calculator output such as

\[ 12.438917263 \]

does not mean the experiment measured nine meaningful decimal places.

\[ \boxed{ \text{reported digits should reflect justified precision} } \]

Practical Applications

Manufacturing

Precision determines whether repeated parts are made consistently; accuracy determines whether those parts match design specifications.

Science

Researchers distinguish random variation from systematic bias when evaluating experimental results.

Medicine and Laboratory Work

Calibration and measurement uncertainty matter when instruments report concentrations, masses, temperatures, or pressures.

Surveying and Construction

Repeated measurements may be precise, but incorrect instrument setup can still shift all results.

Data Collection

Sensors may produce highly repeatable data that nevertheless require calibration against a trusted reference.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Precise but Inaccurate

Accepted value:

\[ 50.0\text{ g}. \]

Measurements:

\[ 47.8,\quad47.9,\quad47.8,\quad47.9\text{ g}. \]

The measurements are tightly clustered but far from \(50.0\) g.

They are

\[ \boxed{\text{precise but inaccurate}}. \]

Example 2: Accurate on Average, Less Precise

Accepted value:

\[ 10.0\text{ cm}. \]

Measurements:

\[ 9.6,\quad10.4,\quad9.8,\quad10.2\text{ cm}. \]

The readings spread around the accepted value.

The process may be reasonably accurate overall but less precise than a tightly clustered set.

Example 3: Absolute Error

Measured:

\[ 8.92\text{ V}. \]

Reference:

\[ 9.00\text{ V}. \] \[ |8.92-9.00| = \boxed{0.08\text{ V}}. \]

Example 4: Percent Error

\[ \frac{0.08}{9.00}\times100\% \approx \boxed{0.89\%}. \]

Example 5: Calibration Offset

A thermometer reads

\[ 1.3^\circ\text{C} \]

in a reference environment known to be

\[ 0.0^\circ\text{C}. \]

The observed offset is

\[ \boxed{+1.3^\circ\text{C}}. \]

Example 6: Tolerance Check

A shaft specification is

\[ 20.00\pm0.03\text{ mm}. \]

Acceptable interval:

\[ 19.97\leq d\leq20.03. \]

A measured diameter of

\[ 20.02\text{ mm} \]

falls inside the tolerance.

\[ \boxed{\text{acceptable}} \]

Common Errors

1. Using Precision and Accuracy as Synonyms

Precision concerns consistency; accuracy concerns closeness to a reference value.

2. Assuming Many Decimal Places Guarantee Accuracy

Fine display resolution does not eliminate calibration error or bias.

3. Assuming Tight Clustering Means Correctness

A systematic offset can make a precise set inaccurate.

4. Using Absolute Error without Considering Scale

An error of \(1\) unit may be huge for a quantity near \(2\), but tiny for a quantity near \(1{,}000{,}000\).

5. Confusing Tolerance with Measurement Uncertainty

Tolerance specifies what is acceptable; uncertainty describes what the measurement process can support.

6. Treating One Lucky Measurement as Proof of Accuracy

One reading can happen to fall close to the reference value even if the method is poorly controlled.

Practice

Classify the Measurements

Accepted value:

\[ 100.0. \]

Measurements:

\[ 92.1,\quad92.2,\quad92.1,\quad92.2. \]

Are they precise, accurate, both, or neither?

Check Answer \[ \boxed{\text{precise but inaccurate}} \]

because they cluster tightly but far from the accepted value.

Absolute Error

Measured value:

\[ 19.7\text{ cm}. \]

Reference value:

\[ 20.0\text{ cm}. \]
Check Answer \[ |19.7-20.0| = \boxed{0.3\text{ cm}}. \]

Percent Error

Using the previous values, find the percent error.

Check Answer \[ \frac{0.3}{20.0}\times100\% = \boxed{1.5\%}. \]

Calibration

A scale reads \(502\) g for a \(500\)-g reference mass. What is the observed offset?

Check Answer \[ 502-500 = \boxed{+2\text{ g}}. \]

Tolerance

A dimension must satisfy

\[ 15.00\pm0.10\text{ mm}. \]

Is \(15.08\) mm acceptable?

Check Answer

Yes. The acceptable interval is

\[ 14.90\leq x\leq15.10, \]

and \(15.08\) lies inside it.

The Deeper Idea

Precision and accuracy describe two different relationships.

Precision compares measurements with one another.

Accuracy compares measurements with a reference.

\[ \boxed{ \text{precision} \longleftrightarrow \text{internal consistency} } \] \[ \boxed{ \text{accuracy} \longleftrightarrow \text{external correctness} } \]

This distinction is a useful general reasoning pattern.

A method can be internally consistent yet systematically wrong. A single result can be close to the correct value without coming from a reliable process.

Good quantitative work therefore asks both:

  • Are the measurements stable?
  • Are they centered where they should be?

That combination of repeatability and reference comparison is what turns raw measurement into trustworthy evidence.

Summary

  • Precision describes agreement among repeated measurements and the fineness of measurement reporting.
  • Accuracy describes closeness to a true, accepted, or reference value.
  • A measurement process can be precise but inaccurate.
  • A process can be accurate on average while showing relatively poor precision.
  • Instrument resolution affects reported detail but does not guarantee accuracy.
  • Random variation tends to reduce precision.
  • Systematic error can shift measurements away from the reference while preserving tight clustering.
  • Calibration compares a measurement system with a trusted standard.
  • Absolute error is \[ |x_m-x_r|. \]
  • Relative error is \[ \frac{|x_m-x_r|}{|x_r|}. \]
  • Percent error expresses relative error as a percentage.
  • Repeatability concerns repeated measurements under similar conditions; reproducibility concerns agreement across changed conditions.
  • Tolerance specifies an acceptable range and is distinct from measurement uncertainty.
  • Reported digits should reflect justified precision rather than raw calculator output.

Precision tells us how consistently a measurement process behaves. Accuracy tells us whether it is centered near the value it is supposed to measure. Reliable measurement requires attention to both.
