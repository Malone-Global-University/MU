The Complex Plane

A complex number can be treated not only as an algebraic expression \(a+bi\), but also as a point in a plane. This representation turns real and imaginary parts into coordinates and gives complex arithmetic a geometric meaning.

From \(\mathbb{R}^2\) to \(\mathbb{C}\)

A point in the real plane is written

\[ (a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^2. \]

A complex number is written

\[ z=a+bi, \]

where

\[ i^2=-1. \]

The complex plane identifies these two forms:

\[ \boxed{ a+bi \longleftrightarrow (a,b) } \]

The real part becomes the horizontal coordinate, and the imaginary part becomes the vertical coordinate.

\[ \operatorname{Re}(z)=a, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=b. \]

This gives the set of complex numbers a natural geometric representation:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{C} \text{ can be represented as a plane} } \]

Complex Numbers as Coordinates

Every complex number has the form

\[ z=a+bi, \]

where \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\).

The ordered pair

\[ (a,b) \]

contains exactly the same two real components.

Algebraic Form

\[ z=a+bi \]

Coordinate Form

\[ z\leftrightarrow(a,b) \]

Components

\[ \operatorname{Re}(z)=a,\qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=b \]

This representation is sometimes called an Argand diagram or Argand plane.

Real and Imaginary Axes

The horizontal axis is the real axis.

Points on it have imaginary part \(0\):

\[ a+0i=a. \]

The vertical axis is the imaginary axis.

Points on it have real part \(0\):

\[ 0+bi=bi. \]

Plotting Complex Numbers

To plot

\[ z=a+bi, \]

use \(a\) as the horizontal coordinate and \(b\) as the vertical coordinate.

For example:

\[ -2+4i \longleftrightarrow (-2,4). \]

So move \(2\) units left along the real axis and \(4\) units upward along the imaginary axis.

Purely Real and Purely Imaginary Numbers

A real number such as

\[ 5 \]

is plotted at

\[ (5,0). \]

A purely imaginary number such as

\[ -3i \]

is plotted at

\[ (0,-3). \]

Equality of Complex Numbers

Two complex numbers are equal exactly when their real parts and imaginary parts are equal.

\[ \boxed{ a+bi=c+di \iff a=c \text{ and } b=d } \]

This is the same coordinatewise equality used for ordered pairs:

\[ (a,b)=(c,d). \]

Example

If

\[ x+3i=5+yi, \]

then

\[ x=5 \]

and

\[ y=3. \]

Modulus

The modulus of

\[ z=a+bi \]

is its distance from the origin.

By the Pythagorean theorem,

\[ \boxed{ |z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2} } \]

For

\[ z=3+4i, \]

we get

\[ |z| = \sqrt{3^2+4^2} = 5. \]

So modulus is the complex-plane version of distance from zero.

Argument

A nonzero complex number also has a direction from the origin.

The angle that its position vector makes with the positive real axis is called an argument of \(z\).

We write

\[ \arg(z)=\theta \]

for an argument, and often

\[ \operatorname{Arg}(z) \]

for a chosen principal argument.

The number \(0\) has modulus \(0\), but its direction is undefined because there is no unique angle from the origin to itself.

Complex Conjugation

The complex conjugate of

\[ z=a+bi \]

is

\[ \boxed{ \overline{z}=a-bi } \]

Geometrically, conjugation reflects a point across the real axis:

\[ (a,b) \longmapsto (a,-b). \]

Thus,

\[ 3+2i \]

and

\[ 3-2i \]

are mirror images across the real axis.

Useful Identity

\[ z\overline{z} = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2+b^2 = |z|^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ |z|^2=z\overline{z} } \]

Addition as Vector Addition

Let

\[ z_1=a+bi \]

and

\[ z_2=c+di. \]

Then

\[ z_1+z_2 = (a+c)+(b+d)i. \]

In coordinate form,

\[ (a,b)+(c,d) = (a+c,b+d). \]

So complex addition is exactly coordinatewise addition in the plane.

Geometrically, the same rule can be viewed as vector addition.

Subtraction and Distance

The difference between complex numbers is

\[ z_1-z_2. \]

Its modulus gives the distance between the corresponding points:

\[ \boxed{ d(z_1,z_2) = |z_1-z_2| } \]

Example

Let

\[ z_1=5+4i \]

and

\[ z_2=2i. \]

Then

\[ z_1-z_2 = 5+2i. \]

So

\[ d(z_1,z_2) = |5+2i| = \sqrt{29}. \]

This is the same Euclidean distance formula expressed in complex notation.

Multiplication and Rotation

Complex multiplication has a geometric interpretation that ordinary coordinatewise multiplication does not.

Multiplying by \(i\) sends

\[ 1\mapsto i, \] \[ i\mapsto -1, \] \[ -1\mapsto -i, \] \[ -i\mapsto1. \]

Geometrically, multiplication by \(i\) rotates points by

\[ 90^\circ \]

counterclockwise around the origin.

Example

Let

\[ z=2+i. \]

Then

\[ iz = i(2+i) = 2i+i^2 = -1+2i. \]

The point

\[ (2,1) \]

has rotated to

\[ (-1,2). \]

More generally, complex multiplication combines scaling and rotation.

Rectangular and Polar Views

The form

\[ z=a+bi \]

uses rectangular coordinates.

The same nonzero complex number can be described by

  • its modulus \(r=|z|\),
  • and an argument \(\theta\).

Since

\[ a=r\cos\theta \]

and

\[ b=r\sin\theta, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ z = r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) } \]

This is a polar representation of the same point.

Thus the complex plane connects Cartesian and polar coordinates naturally.

The Real Numbers inside the Complex Plane

Every real number is also a complex number with imaginary part \(0\):

\[ a=a+0i. \]

So the real numbers appear inside \(\mathbb{C}\) as the horizontal axis.

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C} } \]

The complex plane does not discard the real number line. It extends it by adding an independent imaginary direction.

This mirrors the earlier progression:

\[ \mathbb{R} \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}^2. \]

But now the two-dimensional plane also carries complex multiplication, giving it additional algebraic structure.

Geometric Sets in the Complex Plane

Conditions on complex numbers often describe familiar geometric sets.

Circle Centered at the Origin

\[ |z|=5 \]

describes all points \(5\) units from the origin.

Disk

\[ |z|\leq5 \]

describes the circle together with its interior.

Circle Centered at \(z_0\)

\[ |z-z_0|=r \]

describes all points a distance \(r\) from \(z_0\).

Real Axis

\[ \operatorname{Im}(z)=0. \]

Imaginary Axis

\[ \operatorname{Re}(z)=0. \]

Complex notation can therefore describe geometry very compactly.

Practical Applications

Electrical Engineering

Complex numbers are used to represent oscillating voltages, currents, and impedances, where magnitude and phase are both important.

Signal Processing

Complex representations make periodic signals, frequencies, and phase relationships easier to analyze.

Physics

Complex amplitudes appear in wave mechanics, quantum mechanics, and many models involving oscillation.

Control Systems

The locations of complex roots and poles help describe stability and dynamic behavior.

Geometry and Transformations

Complex multiplication gives a compact language for rotations and scalings in the plane.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Convert to Coordinates

Represent

\[ z=-3+4i \]

as an ordered pair.

\[ \boxed{(-3,4)}. \]

Example 2: Find Real and Imaginary Parts

For

\[ z=7-2i, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=7, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=-2 }. \]

Example 3: Find the Modulus

Let

\[ z=5-12i. \]

Then

\[ |z| = \sqrt{5^2+(-12)^2} = \sqrt{25+144} = \boxed{13}. \]

Example 4: Find the Conjugate

For

\[ z=-4+7i, \]

the conjugate is

\[ \boxed{ \overline{z}=-4-7i }. \]

Example 5: Add Complex Numbers Geometrically

Let

\[ z_1=2+3i \]

and

\[ z_2=-1+4i. \]

Then

\[ z_1+z_2 = (2-1)+(3+4)i = \boxed{1+7i}. \]

In coordinate form,

\[ (2,3)+(-1,4)=(1,7). \]

Example 6: Distance between Complex Numbers

Find the distance between

\[ z_1=4+i \]

and

\[ z_2=1-3i. \]

First,

\[ z_1-z_2 = (4+i)-(1-3i) = 3+4i. \]

Therefore,

\[ d(z_1,z_2) = |3+4i| = \boxed{5}. \]

Common Errors

1. Treating \(a+bi\) as Two Unrelated Numbers

The real and imaginary parts together form one complex number.

2. Plotting \(a+bi\) as \((b,a)\)

The real part is horizontal and the imaginary coefficient is vertical:

\[ a+bi \longleftrightarrow (a,b). \]

3. Forgetting That \(i^2=-1\)

This identity controls complex multiplication.

4. Confusing Modulus with Real Part

For

\[ z=a+bi, \]

the modulus is

\[ |z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}, \]

not simply \(a\).

5. Treating Conjugation as Negating the Entire Number

Conjugation changes only the sign of the imaginary part:

\[ a+bi\mapsto a-bi. \]

6. Assuming Argument Is Unique

Angles differing by full rotations describe the same direction.

That is why complex arguments can differ by multiples of

\[ 2\pi. \]

The Deeper Idea

The complex plane combines two mathematical structures at once.

As a geometric space, it behaves like a two-dimensional real plane:

\[ a+bi \longleftrightarrow (a,b). \]

But as a number system, it also has multiplication:

\[ (a+bi)(c+di) \]

produces another complex number.

This extra multiplication gives geometry an algebraic action: complex multiplication can scale and rotate points.

\[ \boxed{ \text{complex number} = \text{point} + \text{magnitude} + \text{direction} + \text{algebra} } \]

That is why the complex plane is more than another coordinate diagram. It is a space where algebra and geometry become tightly intertwined.

Summary

  • Every complex number has the form \[ z=a+bi. \]
  • The complex number \[ a+bi \] corresponds to the point \[ (a,b). \]
  • The horizontal axis is the real axis and the vertical axis is the imaginary axis.
  • \[ \operatorname{Re}(z)=a, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=b. \]
  • Complex equality is coordinatewise: \[ a+bi=c+di \iff a=c,\ b=d. \]
  • The modulus is \[ |z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}. \]
  • The argument gives the direction of a nonzero complex number from the positive real axis.
  • The conjugate \[ \overline{z}=a-bi \] reflects \(z\) across the real axis.
  • Complex addition is coordinatewise and behaves like vector addition.
  • Distance between complex numbers is \[ |z_1-z_2|. \]
  • Multiplication by \(i\) rotates the plane by \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise.
  • Rectangular form \[ a+bi \] and polar form \[ r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) \] represent the same complex number in different coordinate styles.
  • The real numbers sit inside the complex plane as the real axis: \[ \mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}. \]

The complex plane extends the coordinate ideas of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) while adding a new algebraic structure. Complex numbers can be calculated with, plotted, measured, rotated, reflected, and used to describe geometric sets all within one unified representation.
