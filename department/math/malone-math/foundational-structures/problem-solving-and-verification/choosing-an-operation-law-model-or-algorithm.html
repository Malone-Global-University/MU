Choosing an Operation, Law, Model, or Algorithm

Once a problem has been understood and represented, the next question is: what kind of mathematical tool should act on it? Sometimes one operation is enough. Sometimes a law, model, or full algorithm is the better fit.

Choose the Tool That Matches the Structure

Suppose a tank contains \(300\text{ L}\) and drains at \(15\text{ L/min}\). How long until it is empty?

The problem can be represented as

\[ t=\frac{\text{volume}}{\text{rate}}. \]

The final computational step is a division:

\[ t=\frac{300}{15}=20\text{ min}. \]

Now consider a different question: a quantity grows by \(5\%\) every year for ten years. That problem is not naturally solved by one isolated operation. It is better represented by a model such as

\[ A=P(1+r)^n. \]

And if the task is to repeatedly refine an approximation until an error tolerance is met, the natural tool is an algorithm.

\[ \boxed{ \text{good strategy} = \text{problem structure} \longrightarrow \text{appropriate mathematical tool} } \]

Four Levels of Mathematical Action

Operation

A direct action such as addition, division, exponentiation, or radicalization.

Law or Identity

A reusable relationship that permits a valid transformation or inference.

Model

A mathematical representation of a real or abstract system.

Algorithm

An ordered procedure that may combine many operations, decisions, and repetitions.

These are not competing categories. They often work together.

An algorithm may use operations. A model may be manipulated using laws. A law may justify a step inside an algorithm.

Choosing an Operation

Choose a single operation when the structure is direct and the needed relationship is already clear.

Addition

Use addition when compatible quantities combine:

\[ 12+8=20. \]

Subtraction

Use subtraction for difference or removal:

\[ 20-8=12. \]

Multiplication

Use multiplication for repeated equal groups, scaling, or rectangular products:

\[ 6(4)=24. \]

Division

Use division for partitioning, rates, or inverse scaling:

\[ \frac{120\text{ miles}}{3\text{ h}} = 40\text{ mph}. \]

Exponentiation

Use exponentiation when repeated multiplicative structure is present:

\[ 2^5=32. \]

Radicalization

Use radicalization when undoing an exponent or solving a root relationship:

\[ x^2=49 \Longrightarrow x=\pm7. \]

Choosing a Law or Identity

A law is useful when a transformation is needed but the underlying relationship should remain equivalent.

Distributive Law

\[ a(b+c)=ab+ac. \]

Commutative Law

\[ a+b=b+a. \]

Exponent Law

\[ a^m a^n=a^{m+n}. \]

Pythagorean Relationship

\[ a^2+b^2=c^2. \]

A law tells us what transformation is valid. It does not necessarily solve the whole problem by itself.

Choosing a Model

A model describes a system using mathematical objects and relationships.

Examples:

\[ d=rt \]

for constant-rate motion,

\[ A=P(1+r)^n \]

for discrete compound growth, and

\[ y=mx+b \]

for linear relationships.

A model is appropriate when the problem is about how quantities relate, not merely about performing one arithmetic step.

Choosing an Algorithm

An algorithm is appropriate when the task requires a sequence of steps, branching, repetition, or systematic refinement.

Example: Greatest Common Divisor

Euclid's algorithm repeatedly replaces a pair of integers by a smaller pair:

\[ (a,b) \to (b,a\bmod b) \]

until the remainder is zero.

The result is the greatest common divisor.

No single operation captures the entire procedure.

A Method-Selection Flow

Recognizing Problem Structure

Useful clues include:

  • Are quantities merely being combined or separated?
  • Does an equation need to be transformed?
  • Is the problem describing a relationship among changing quantities?
  • Does the solution require repeated steps?
  • Are decisions or cases involved?
  • Is an approximation being refined?

Structure should drive method choice.

Do Not Choose by Surface Words Alone

Words such as “more,” “per,” “of,” or “times” can be useful clues, but they are not reliable substitutes for understanding the relationship.

Example

“Ten is three more than a number.”

The relationship is:

\[ 10=x+3. \]

To find \(x\), the useful inverse operation is subtraction:

\[ x=10-3. \]

The word “more” appears in the statement, but the solving operation is subtraction.

Direct and Inverse Relationships

Often the problem gives the result of an operation and asks for the original input.

Then an inverse operation is natural.

Forward Process Inverse Process
additionsubtraction
subtractionaddition
multiplicationdivision
divisionmultiplication
exponentiationradicalization or logarithmic methods, depending on the structure

Choosing the inverse operation is often equivalent to asking:

What action will undo the relationship that produced the known value?

When One Operation Is Not Enough

Many problems require a sequence.

Example

A store discounts an \$80 item by \(25\%\) and then adds \(8\%\) sales tax.

This requires multiple steps:

\[ 80(0.75)=60, \] \[ 60(1.08)=64.8. \]

The final price is

\[ \boxed{\$64.80}. \]

A multi-step strategy may still be simple, but it is already more than one isolated operation.

Model Selection

Models should match the assumptions of the situation.

For example, constant-speed motion may use

\[ d=rt. \]

But if speed changes substantially over time, a constant-rate model may be inappropriate.

Likewise:

  • linear models fit constant additive change,
  • exponential models fit constant multiplicative change,
  • inverse models fit relationships such as \(y=k/x\),
  • quadratic models fit many area, projectile, and curvature situations.

Choosing a model means matching the mathematical pattern to the system.

Algorithm Selection

Two algorithms can solve the same problem but differ in speed, reliability, or clarity.

For example, to find an approximate root:

  • bisection is robust when a sign-changing interval is known,
  • Newton's method may converge faster when a good starting value and derivative are available.

So algorithm choice may depend on:

  • available information,
  • required accuracy,
  • guarantees of convergence,
  • computational cost,
  • implementation difficulty.

Criteria for Comparing Methods

Ask:

  • Fit: Does the method match the problem structure?
  • Correctness: Is it valid under the stated conditions?
  • Efficiency: Does it avoid unnecessary work?
  • Clarity: Can the reasoning be followed?
  • Precision: Does it produce the accuracy required?
  • Robustness: Does it behave well across the intended domain?

The shortest method is not always the best method. The most advanced method is not always the best method either.

When to Change Methods

Change strategy when:

  • the algebra becomes unnecessarily complicated,
  • the representation hides useful structure,
  • a chosen model violates assumptions,
  • an algorithm fails to converge,
  • a simpler identity or operation becomes visible.

A good solver is committed to the problem, not to the first method attempted.

Verify the Chosen Method

Verification should check both the answer and the method.

Ask:

  • Did the method use the right relationship?
  • Were the assumptions satisfied?
  • Did each transformation preserve validity?
  • Do units remain consistent?
  • Can the answer be checked another way?

Example

If a distance problem is modeled by

\[ d=rt \]

with

\[ r=50\text{ mph}, \qquad t=3\text{ h}, \]

then

\[ d=150\text{ miles}. \]

Units verify the model:

\[ \frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}} \cdot \text{hour} = \text{miles}. \]

Worked Examples

Example 1: Choose an Operation

A box contains \(24\) pencils shared equally among \(6\) students.

Structure: equal partition.

Operation: division.

\[ \frac{24}{6} = \boxed4. \]

Example 2: Choose a Law

Simplify:

\[ 3(x+4). \]

Structure: product over a sum.

Law: distributive law.

\[ 3(x+4) = \boxed{3x+12}. \]

Example 3: Choose a Model

A savings account begins with \$1000 and grows by \(4\%\) per year for \(5\) years.

Structure: repeated multiplicative growth.

Model:

\[ A=P(1+r)^n. \] \[ A=1000(1.04)^5. \] \[ A\approx\boxed{\$1216.65}. \]

Example 4: Choose an Algorithm

Find the greatest common divisor of \(84\) and \(30\).

Use Euclid's algorithm:

\[ 84=2(30)+24, \] \[ 30=1(24)+6, \] \[ 24=4(6)+0. \] \[ \boxed{\gcd(84,30)=6}. \]

Example 5: Change Strategy

Solve:

\[ x^2-9=0. \]

One possible route is the quadratic formula, but the structure is immediately factorable:

\[ (x-3)(x+3)=0. \]

So

\[ \boxed{x=\pm3}. \]

The simpler law-and-factor strategy is more efficient here.

Example 6: Choose between Two Algorithms

Suppose a continuous function changes sign on \([1,2]\), and a guaranteed root approximation is required.

Bisection is a natural choice because each step preserves a sign-changing interval and halves its width.

Newton's method may be faster, but bisection offers the more direct guarantee under the stated information.

Common Errors

1. Choosing an Operation from a Keyword

Interpret the relationship before deciding what operation is needed.

2. Using a Formula without Checking Its Assumptions

A model is valid only under the conditions it represents.

3. Using a Multi-Step Algorithm for a One-Step Problem

Extra machinery can hide a simple structure.

4. Forcing One Operation to Solve an Entire Multi-Step Problem

Some problems genuinely require a sequence of actions.

5. Choosing the Most Familiar Method Instead of the Best-Fitting One

Method choice should follow structure.

6. Refusing to Change Methods

A poor strategy should be replaced when a better one becomes visible.

7. Verifying Only the Arithmetic

The chosen model, law, or algorithm must also be valid.

Practice

Operation

A \(150\text{ km}\) trip takes \(3\text{ h}\). Which operation gives average speed once the relationship is known?

Check Answer \[ \boxed{\text{division}} \]

because

\[ v=\frac{d}{t}. \]

Law

Which law transforms

\[ 5(x+2) \]

into

\[ 5x+10? \]
Check Answer \[ \boxed{\text{distributive law}}. \]

Model

A quantity doubles every hour. Is a linear or exponential model more natural?

Check Answer \[ \boxed{\text{exponential model}} \]

because the change is multiplicative.

Algorithm

A task requires repeatedly refining an estimate until the error is below \(10^{-6}\). What kind of tool is most natural?

Check Answer \[ \boxed{\text{an iterative algorithm}} \]

Method Change

If a chosen method creates much more algebra than another valid method, should you keep the first one merely because you started with it?

Check Answer

No. Choose the method that is valid, clear, and efficient for the structure of the problem.

The Deeper Idea

Mathematical problem solving is partly the art of matching structures.

\[ \boxed{ \text{problem structure} \longleftrightarrow \text{tool structure} } \]

A difference suggests subtraction. A repeated multiplicative pattern suggests exponentiation or an exponential model. A valid transformation may require a law. A repeated decision process may require an algorithm.

This connects directly to the previous stages of the Malone Math framework:

  • known and unknown information tells us what must be connected,
  • objects and restrictions tell us which methods are admissible,
  • representation exposes the structure,
  • method choice acts on that structure,
  • verification checks that the chosen tool really fit the problem.

The goal is not to memorize which exercise “belongs” to which method. The goal is to recognize why a method fits.

Summary

  • An operation performs a direct mathematical action.
  • A law or identity justifies a valid transformation or reusable relationship.
  • A model represents how quantities or objects relate in a system.
  • An algorithm organizes a sequence of operations, decisions, or repetitions.
  • Problem structure should determine which level of tool is appropriate.
  • Surface keywords can mislead if the underlying relationship is not understood.
  • Inverse operations are useful when undoing a known forward process.
  • Many problems require multiple steps rather than one isolated operation.
  • Models must match the assumptions of the situation.
  • Algorithms can be compared by correctness, efficiency, robustness, accuracy, and clarity.
  • A solver should change methods when the current strategy is unnecessarily difficult or invalid.
  • Verification should check not only the arithmetic but also whether the law, model, or algorithm was appropriate.

The strategic question is not simply “What do I do next?” It is “What kind of mathematical tool matches the structure I have uncovered?” Once that match is clear, the solution often becomes much more direct.
