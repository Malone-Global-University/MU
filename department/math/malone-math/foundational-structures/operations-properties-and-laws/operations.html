Operations: The Six Operations

Six fundamental ways to combine, separate, scale, compare, exponentiate, and recover quantities.

The Big Idea

An operation is a rule or process that takes mathematical inputs and produces an output. Arithmetic begins with six closely connected operations:

  1. addition,
  2. subtraction,
  3. multiplication,
  4. division,
  5. exponentiation, and
  6. radicalization.

These operations do more than calculate answers. They describe change, comparison, scaling, grouping, repeated growth, area, volume, rates, and the recovery of unknown values.

They also form three natural inverse pairs: addition with subtraction, multiplication with division, and exponentiation with radicalization—subject to important domain and sign restrictions.

A Note on “Radicalization”

Malone Math uses radicalization as the process noun parallel to addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and exponentiation.

Other texts may say taking a root, extracting a root, or evaluating a radical. The underlying operation is the same: given a radicand and an index, determine the principal root when it is defined in the chosen number system.

The terminology is meant to keep the six operations grammatically and conceptually parallel. Conventional terms such as radical, root, radicand, and index remain fully in use.

Addition: Combining Quantities

Addition combines compatible quantities into a total:

\[ 7+5=12. \]

The numbers \(7\) and \(5\) are addends, and \(12\) is their sum.

Addition can represent accumulation, joining groups, forward change, and displacement along a number line. It also extends beyond positive quantities:

\[ 7+(-5)=2. \]

When units are present, only compatible quantities may be added. Three meters plus forty centimeters is meaningful after conversion; three meters plus forty seconds is not ordinary addition of like quantities.

Subtraction: Removing, Comparing, and Finding Change

Subtraction finds what remains, how far apart two values are in a directed sense, or what change connects them:

\[ 12-5=7. \]

Here \(12\) is the minuend, \(5\) is the subtrahend, and \(7\) is the difference.

Subtraction can always be rewritten as addition of an additive inverse:

\[ a-b=a+(-b). \]

This connection explains why subtracting a negative becomes addition:

\[ 8-(-3)=8+3=11. \]

Multiplication: Scaling and Grouping

Multiplication scales one quantity by another:

\[ 4\times6=24. \]

The inputs are factors, and the result is the product.

For positive whole numbers, multiplication can be understood as repeated addition:

\[ 4\times6=6+6+6+6. \]

Repeated addition is only the beginning. Multiplication also describes fractional scaling, area, probability, proportional change, unit conversion, and the interaction of algebraic factors. Multiplying by \(\frac12\) does not repeat a quantity; it scales the quantity to half its size.

Division: Partitioning and Comparing

Division asks how many groups, how large each group, or how one quantity compares with another:

\[ 24\div6=4. \]

The number \(24\) is the dividend, \(6\) is the divisor, and \(4\) is the quotient.

Division by a nonzero number can be rewritten as multiplication by its reciprocal:

\[ \frac ab=a\left(\frac1b\right), \qquad b\ne0. \]

In whole-number division, a remainder may also be reported:

\[ 17=3(5)+2. \]

Thus \(17\div5\) has whole-number quotient \(3\) and remainder \(2\), while exact rational division gives \(\frac{17}{5}=3.4\).

Division by Zero Is Undefined

Division \(a\div b\) asks for a number \(q\) satisfying

\[ bq=a. \]

If \(b=0\) and \(a\ne0\), no value of \(q\) works because \(0q=0\) for every \(q\). If \(a=0\) as well, every \(q\) satisfies \(0q=0\), so no unique quotient is determined.

Therefore,

\[ \frac a0 \]

is undefined in ordinary arithmetic. Moving to a larger familiar number system does not repair division by zero.

Exponentiation: Raising a Base

Exponentiation combines a base with an exponent:

\[ 3^4=81. \]

For a positive integer exponent,

\[ 3^4=3\cdot3\cdot3\cdot3. \]

The expression \(3^4\) and its value \(81\) may be called a power; more specifically, \(3\) is raised to the fourth power.

Exponentiation extends beyond repeated multiplication. Zero, negative, rational, irrational, and complex exponents are defined through consistent extensions of the exponent laws, with appropriate domain restrictions.

Zero and Negative Exponents

For \(a\ne0\),

\[ a^0=1. \]

This preserves the quotient law:

\[ \frac{a^m}{a^m}=a^{m-m}=a^0=1. \]

A negative exponent indicates a reciprocal:

\[ a^{-n}=\frac1{a^n}, \qquad a\ne0. \]

A negative exponent does not automatically make the value negative. For example, \(2^{-3}=\frac18>0\).

Radicalization: Recovering a Base

Radicalization asks which principal value, when raised to a stated positive-integer power, produces the radicand:

\[ \sqrt[3]{27}=3 \qquad\text{because}\qquad 3^3=27. \]

In \(\sqrt[n]{a}\), \(n\) is the index, \(a\) is the radicand, and the radical expression denotes the principal \(n\)th root when it is defined in the chosen domain.

The index \(2\) is normally omitted:

\[ \sqrt{a}=\sqrt[2]{a}. \]

Radicalization connects naturally to rational exponents:

\[ \sqrt[n]{a}=a^{1/n} \]

whenever the expression is defined consistently in the selected number system.

A Principal Root Is Not Every Equation Solution

The radical symbol denotes a principal root. Thus

\[ \sqrt9=3, \]

not \(\pm3\).

By contrast, the equation

\[ x^2=9 \]

has two real solutions:

\[ x=3 \qquad\text{or}\qquad x=-3. \]

Both values square to \(9\), but the square-root function returns the nonnegative principal root.

Even and Odd Roots Behave Differently

Every real number has one real odd-index principal root:

\[ \sqrt[3]{-8}=-2. \]

A negative real number has no real even-index root because every even power of a real number is nonnegative:

\[ \sqrt{-9}\notin\mathbb{R}. \]

In the complex numbers, \(\sqrt{-9}=3i\) under the usual principal-root convention.

Even roots also create an absolute-value correction:

\[ \sqrt{x^2}=|x| \]

for real \(x\), because a principal square root is nonnegative.

Inverse operations are central to solving equations: perform the same legal inverse operation on both sides to isolate the unknown.

Exponentiation Creates Two Inverse Questions

In

\[ a^x=b, \]

radicalization helps when the exponent is known and the base is unknown. For example,

\[ x^3=125 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x=\sqrt[3]{125}=5. \]

A logarithm answers the other inverse question: the base and result are known, but the exponent is unknown:

\[ 2^x=8 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x=\log_2 8=3. \]

Radicalization and logarithms reverse different parts of exponentiation. This lesson counts radicalization among the six basic arithmetic processes; logarithms will later extend the inverse toolkit.

Identity Values: Zero and One

An identity value leaves another value unchanged under an operation.

Zero is the additive identity:

\[ a+0=0+a=a. \]

One is the multiplicative identity:

\[ a\cdot1=1\cdot a=a. \]

Related facts include

\[ a-0=a,\qquad \frac a1=a,\qquad a^1=a,\qquad \sqrt[1]{a}=a. \]

These are not all two-sided identity laws for their operations. For example, \(0-a\ne a\) in general and \(1/a\ne a\) in general.

Multiplication Distributes Over Addition

The distributive law connects multiplication to addition and subtraction:

\[ a(b+c)=ab+ac \]

and

\[ a(b-c)=ab-ac. \]

For example,

\[ 7(20+3)=7(20)+7(3)=140+21=161. \]

Distribution is not a universal permission to split every operation:

\[ \sqrt{a+b}\ne\sqrt a+\sqrt b \]

in general.

An Operation May Leave the Current Number System

Whether an operation is available depends on the domain:

  • \(3-5=-2\) leaves the whole numbers but remains in the integers.
  • \(3\div5=\frac35\) leaves the integers but remains rational.
  • \(\sqrt2\) leaves the rational numbers but remains real.
  • \(\sqrt{-1}=i\) leaves the real numbers but remains complex.
  • \(\frac{1}{0}\) is undefined rather than belonging to a still larger familiar system.

Before performing an operation, ask which number system is being used and whether the result is allowed within it.

Sign Rules Belong to the Operation

Addition and subtraction of signed numbers are easiest to understand as movement and combination of opposites. Multiplication and division follow a compact sign pattern:

Signs of products and quotients

NegativePositiveNegative

Exponentiation requires attention to grouping:

\[ (-3)^2=9, \qquad -3^2=-(3^2)=-9. \]

Parentheses determine whether the negative sign is part of the base.

A Preview of Operation Order

An expression containing several operations needs a shared evaluation convention. In ordinary notation:

  1. evaluate grouping symbols,
  2. perform exponentiation and radicalization,
  3. perform multiplication and division from left to right, and
  4. perform addition and subtraction from left to right.

A fraction bar, radical bar, exponent position, and function notation also create grouping. For example,

\[ \frac{a+b}{c} \]

divides the entire numerator \(a+b\) by \(c\).

Operation order is a notation convention, not a claim that one operation is inherently more important than another.

A Practical Operation Routine

  1. Identify the operation requested by the situation.
  2. Name the inputs and preserve their order.
  3. Check the number system and domain restrictions.
  4. Use grouping symbols to make the structure clear.
  5. Estimate the expected size and sign.
  6. Perform the operation.
  7. Attach and simplify units when quantities are measured.
  8. Check with an inverse operation or substitution.
  9. Interpret the result in the original context.

Common Confusions

Is multiplication always repeated addition?

No. Repeated addition explains multiplication by positive whole numbers, but multiplication also represents fractional scaling, area, proportions, and many other structures.

Can subtraction and division be reversed?

No. Operand order matters: \(a-b\ne b-a\) and \(a\div b\ne b\div a\) in general.

Is \(\sqrt9=\pm3\)?

No. \(\sqrt9=3\) is the principal square root. The equation \(x^2=9\) has the two solutions \(x=\pm3\).

Does \(a^0=1\) include \(a=0\)?

The ordinary rule is stated for \(a\ne0\). The expression \(0^0\) is left undefined in many elementary settings and assigned values only in particular contexts.

Can a radical be distributed over addition?

No. In general, \(\sqrt{a+b}\ne\sqrt a+\sqrt b\). For example, \(\sqrt{9+16}=5\), while \(\sqrt9+\sqrt{16}=7\).

Are exponentiation and radicalization perfect inverses?

They reverse one another only with domain and principal-root restrictions. For real \(x\), \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\), not always \(x\).

Why is division by zero not infinity?

No unique ordinary number satisfies the required multiplication relationship. Limits may grow without bound near zero, but that does not define \(a/0\) as an ordinary quotient.

Is a negative exponent a negative result?

No. A negative exponent creates a reciprocal: \(a^{-n}=1/a^n\) for \(a\ne0\). The result’s sign depends on the base and exponent.

Working Summary

The six fundamental arithmetic processes are addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, exponentiation, and radicalization. Their results are sums, differences, products, quotients, powers, and principal roots.

The operations form inverse relationships, but they do not all obey the same laws. Addition and multiplication are commutative and associative; subtraction and division are not. Exponentiation and radicalization require careful attention to order, grouping, principal values, and domains.

Treat each operation as a meaningful transformation: identify the inputs, predict the result’s size and sign, perform the process, and check by reversing it when possible.

Check Your Understanding

Name the inputs and result in \(18-7=11\).

\(18\) is the minuend, \(7\) is the subtrahend, and \(11\) is the difference.

Rewrite \(12\div5\) as multiplication.

\(12\div5=12\left(\frac15\right)\).

Evaluate \(2^{-4}\).

\(2^{-4}=\frac1{2^4}=\frac1{16}\).

Evaluate \(\sqrt[3]{-125}\).

\(-5\), because \((-5)^3=-125\).

Solve \(x^2=49\) over the real numbers.

\(x=7\) or \(x=-7\). The equation has two solutions even though \(\sqrt{49}=7\).

Why is \(4+3\text{ m}\) not an ordinary sum?

One addend is dimensionless and the other has a length unit. Addition requires compatible kinds of quantity.

Which inverse operation checks \(9^3=729\)?

Radicalization: \(\sqrt[3]{729}=9\).

Does \((-2)^4\) equal \(-2^4\)?

No. \((-2)^4=16\), while \(-2^4=-(2^4)=-16\).
