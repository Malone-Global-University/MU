Multiplication

Combining equal groups, scaling quantities, pairing choices, and building new measurements.

The Big Idea

Multiplication combines two factors to produce a product. It can describe equal groups, scale a quantity, measure a rectangular arrangement, connect a rate with an amount, or count all possible pairings.

\[ a\times b=c \]

says that \(a\) and \(b\) are factors and \(c\) is their product.

Repeated addition provides a useful beginning: four groups of three objects contain \(3+3+3+3=12\) objects. But multiplication grows beyond whole-number repetition. Expressions such as \(\tfrac12\times8\), \((-2)\times5\), \(1.03\times250\), and \(\pi r^2\) are better understood as scaling, measurement, or structure.

Equal Groups and Repeated Addition

Four bags with six oranges in each bag contain

\[ 4\times6=6+6+6+6=24 \]

oranges. In this interpretation, one factor counts the groups and the other gives the size of each group.

Repeated addition explains multiplication by nonnegative whole numbers, but it does not naturally explain “one-half groups” or “negative three groups.” The scaling interpretation handles those cases more smoothly.

Multiplication as Scaling

In \(k\times a\), the factor \(k\) can be viewed as a scale factor applied to \(a\).

How a real scale factor changes magnitude and direction
Scale factor \(k\) Effect Example
\(k>1\) Enlarges the magnitude \(1.5\times8=12\)
\(k=1\) Leaves the value unchanged \(1\times8=8\)
\(0<k<1\) Shrinks the magnitude \(\tfrac12\times8=4\)
\(k=0\) Collapses the value to zero \(0\times8=0\)
\(k<0\) Scales and reverses direction or sign \((-2)\times8=-16\)

This view explains why multiplication does not always make a number larger.

Arrays and Area

A rectangular array with \(4\) rows and \(7\) positions per row contains

\[ 4\times7=28 \]

positions. Rotating the array produces \(7\) rows of \(4\), which helps make \(4\times7=7\times4\) visible.

The same structure measures rectangular area:

\[ \text{area}=\text{length}\times\text{width}. \]

A rectangle \(3\text{ m}\) by \(5\text{ m}\) has area \(15\text{ m}^2\). Multiplying two lengths creates a new kind of quantity: square units.

Rates Turn Multiplication into a Model

A rate tells how much of one quantity corresponds to one unit of another. Multiplying the rate by the number of units produces a total:

\[ \text{total}=\text{rate}\times\text{amount}. \]

At \(\$18\) per hour for \(7.5\) hours,

\[ \frac{\$18}{\text{h}}\times7.5\text{ h}=\$135. \]

The hour units cancel, leaving dollars. Units help reveal whether the intended multiplication makes sense.

Multiplying Signed Numbers

The sign of a real-number product
Factor signs Product sign Example
Positive and positive Positive \(3\times5=15\)
Positive and negative Negative \(3\times(-5)=-15\)
Negative and positive Negative \((-3)\times5=-15\)
Negative and negative Positive \((-3)\times(-5)=15\)

For several nonzero factors, an even number of negative factors gives a positive product; an odd number gives a negative product.

Why a Negative Times a Negative Is Positive

The sign rule is required if multiplication is to remain consistent with the distributive law. Since \((-3)+3=0\),

\[ (-4)\bigl((-3)+3\bigr)=(-4)\times0=0. \]

Distributing gives

\[ (-4)(-3)+(-4)(3)=0. \]

Because \((-4)(3)=-12\), the first product must be \(12\) so the terms cancel:

\[ (-4)(-3)=12. \]

One Preserves; Zero Collapses

One is the multiplicative identity because

\[ a\times1=1\times a=a. \]

Zero is an absorbing element for multiplication because

\[ a\times0=0\times a=0. \]

The zero-product property follows for real and complex numbers:

\[ ab=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a=0\text{ or }b=0. \]

This property becomes a central tool for solving factored equations.

The Core Properties of Multiplication

Fundamental multiplication laws for ordinary numbers
Property General form Meaning
Closure \(ab\) remains in the set Applies when the selected set is closed under multiplication
Commutative \(ab=ba\) Factor order does not change a numerical product
Associative \((ab)c=a(bc)\) Factor grouping does not change a numerical product
Identity \(a\cdot1=a\) Multiplying by one preserves a value
Inverse \(a\cdot\frac1a=1\), \(a\ne0\) A nonzero number times its reciprocal equals one
Distributive \(a(b+c)=ab+ac\) Multiplication acts across an addition

These laws hold for ordinary real-number multiplication. Other objects may use multiplication-like operations with different properties. Matrix multiplication, for example, is associative and distributive but generally not commutative.

Reordering and Regrouping

Commutativity and associativity allow convenient rearrangements:

\[ 25\times17\times4 =(25\times4)\times17 =100\times17 =1{,}700. \]

Commutativity changes order; associativity changes grouping. Multiplication of ordinary numbers has both properties, but division generally has neither.

The Distributive Law Connects Operations

The distributive law links multiplication with addition and subtraction:

\[ a(b+c)=ab+ac, \qquad a(b-c)=ab-ac. \]

It supports mental arithmetic:

\[ 7\times38 =7(40-2) =280-14 =266. \]

It also supports algebraic expansion and factoring:

\[ 3(x+5)=3x+15, \qquad 3x+15=3(x+5). \]

Expansion and factoring move through the same law in opposite directions.

Closure and Multiplicative Inverses

Natural numbers, integers, rational numbers, real numbers, and complex numbers are closed under multiplication. Multiplicative inverses require more care.

Every nonzero rational, real, or complex number has a reciprocal in the same number system. Most nonzero integers do not: the reciprocal of \(5\) is \(\tfrac15\), which is not an integer. Zero has no multiplicative inverse because no number multiplied by zero equals one.

The Place-Value Multiplication Algorithm

The standard written algorithm is a compact use of place value and distribution. Consider

\[ 347\times26. \]

Decompose \(26\) into \(20+6\):

\[ 347\times26 =347(20+6) =6{,}940+2{,}082 =9{,}022. \]

The partial product from the tens digit represents \(347\times20\), not \(347\times2\). The placeholder zero in a written layout records that place value.

Multiplying Decimals

First multiply as if the factors were whole numbers. Then use place value to position the decimal point.

\[ 2.4\times0.35 =\frac{24}{10}\times\frac{35}{100} =\frac{840}{1{,}000} =0.84. \]

The two factors contain three decimal places altogether, so the product has three places before simplifying the trailing zero.

Estimation guards against misplaced decimals: \(2.4\times0.35\) is a little less than \(2.4\times\tfrac12=1.2\), so \(8.4\) cannot be reasonable.

Multiplying Fractions

Multiply numerators and multiply denominators:

\[ \frac ab\times\frac cd=\frac{ac}{bd}, \qquad b,d\ne0. \]

Example:

\[ \frac34\times\frac25 =\frac6{20} =\frac3{10}. \]

The phrase “\(\tfrac34\) of \(\tfrac25\)” describes a scale factor applied to a quantity. Unlike addition, fraction multiplication does not require a common denominator.

Factors may be canceled before multiplying because cancellation divides a numerator and a denominator by the same nonzero factor:

\[ \frac{14}{15}\times\frac{25}{21} =\frac{2}{3}\times\frac{5}{3} =\frac{10}{9}. \]

Multiplying Mixed Numbers

Convert mixed numbers to improper fractions before multiplying:

\[ 2\frac13\times1\frac12 =\frac73\times\frac32 =\frac72 =3\frac12. \]

Multiplying the whole-number parts and fractional parts separately would omit the cross-products. Conversion keeps the entire value of each factor intact.

Multiplication Builds Units

Addition requires compatible units, but multiplication can combine different dimensions into a new quantity.

\[ 5\text{ m}\times3\text{ m}=15\text{ m}^2 \]

produces area, while

\[ 12\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\times4\text{ s}=48\text{ m} \]

produces distance. Numbers multiply, units multiply, and matching numerator and denominator units cancel. This is the foundation of dimensional analysis.

Multiplying Measured Values

A product cannot be more precise than its measured inputs justify. For introductory work, the final product is often rounded to the same number of significant figures as the least precise factor.

If a rectangle is measured as \(4.2\text{ m}\) by \(3.15\text{ m}\), then

\[ 4.2\times3.15=13.23\text{ m}^2. \]

Because \(4.2\) has two significant figures, an appropriately rounded report is approximately \(13\text{ m}^2\). Retain extra digits during the calculation and round at the end.

Multiplying in Scientific Notation

Multiply the coefficients and add the powers of ten:

\[ (a\times10^m)(b\times10^n) =(ab)\times10^{m+n}. \]

For example,

\[ (3.2\times10^5)(4\times10^{-3}) =12.8\times10^2 =1.28\times10^3. \]

The final coefficient is normalized so that it is at least \(1\) and less than \(10\).

Changing Factors Without Changing the Product

Multiplying one nonzero factor by \(k\) and dividing the other by \(k\) preserves the product:

\[ ab=(ak)\left(\frac{b}{k}\right), \qquad k\ne0. \]

This justifies doubling and halving:

\[ 24\times35 =12\times70 =6\times140 =840. \]

Estimate Before Trusting the Product

Round to convenient values:

\[ 398\times21 \approx400\times20 =8{,}000. \]

The exact product \(8{,}358\) is reasonable. A result of \(835.8\) or \(83{,}580\) would suggest a place-value error.

Also predict the sign. A product with one negative factor should not unexpectedly become positive.

Check Multiplication with Division

If

\[ ab=c \]

and the relevant factor is nonzero, then

\[ \frac cb=a \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac ca=b. \]

For example, \(36\times27=972\) can be checked by \(972\div27=36\). Estimation checks scale; division checks the exact inverse relationship.

Multiplication in Equations

To solve

\[ ax=b, \]

divide both sides by \(a\), provided \(a\ne0\):

\[ x=\frac ba. \]

For \(7x=42\), dividing both sides by \(7\) gives \(x=6\).

The case \(a=0\) must be handled separately. The equation \(0x=0\) is true for every \(x\), while \(0x=5\) has no solution.

Repeated Multiplication Leads Toward Exponentiation

A finite repeated product of the same factor can be compressed with an exponent:

\[ a\times a\times a\times a=a^4. \]

This introduces exponentiation by positive whole-number exponents. Exponentiation later extends to zero, negative, fractional, real, and complex exponents, so it eventually means more than repeated multiplication.

Multiplication Counts Pairings

If one decision has \(m\) possible outcomes and a second independent decision has \(n\), then the pair of decisions has

\[ m\times n \]

possible outcome pairs. Four sandwich choices and three drink choices produce \(4\times3=12\) meal combinations.

In set language, if \(A\) and \(B\) are finite, then their Cartesian product satisfies

\[ |A\times B|=|A|\,|B|. \]

This becomes the multiplication principle of counting.

Product Notation Compresses Long Products

The Greek capital letter pi, \(\Pi\), represents a structured product:

\[ \prod_{k=1}^{4}k =1\times2\times3\times4 =24. \]

The index \(k\) begins at \(1\), ends at \(4\), and takes each integer value in between. Product notation appears in factorials, probability, sequences, number theory, and calculus.

Multiplication with Vectors, Matrices, and Functions

A scalar multiplies a vector componentwise:

\[ k(a,b)=(ka,kb). \]

For example, \(-2(3,-4)=(-6,8)\). The factor changes the vector’s magnitude and, when negative, reverses its direction.

Functions multiply pointwise:

\[ (fg)(x)=f(x)g(x), \]

wherever both functions are defined.

Matrices have more than one multiplication-like operation. Entrywise multiplication combines matching entries, while standard matrix multiplication combines rows and columns. The standard product is generally not commutative:

\[ AB\ne BA \]

in many cases. The meaning of multiplication depends on the objects and the operation defined for them.

Words Do Not Decide the Operation by Themselves

Words such as each, times, of, area, and rate often suggest multiplication, but the relationship is more important than a keyword.

“A package contains \(6\) bottles, and there are \(8\) packages” gives \(6\times8=48\) bottles. But if \(48\) bottles are divided equally among \(8\) packages, the unknown is found by division. Write the relationship before choosing which operation solves for the missing value.

A Practical Multiplication Routine

  1. Identify what the factors represent: groups, scale, dimensions, rate and amount, or choices.
  2. Check the sign and units.
  3. Estimate the product’s approximate magnitude.
  4. Choose a helpful form: array, decomposition, fraction, scientific notation, or written algorithm.
  5. Reorder and regroup when the applicable laws permit it.
  6. Use distribution, cancellation, or factor compensation when useful.
  7. Compute the product.
  8. Check with estimation and division.
  9. Interpret the result, including any new unit or dimension.

Common Confusions

Does multiplication always make a number larger?

No. Multiplying a positive value by a factor between \(0\) and \(1\) makes it smaller; multiplying by \(1\) leaves it unchanged; multiplying by \(0\) gives zero.

Is multiplication just repeated addition?

Repeated addition models multiplication by nonnegative whole numbers. Scaling, area, rates, fractional factors, and products of more advanced objects require a broader view.

Why is a negative times a negative positive?

The rule is required for multiplication to remain consistent with additive inverses and the distributive law.

Do fractions need a common denominator before multiplication?

No. Multiply numerators and denominators, canceling common nonzero factors when convenient.

Why does area use square units?

Area multiplies one length by another, so the units multiply as well: \(\text{m}\times\text{m}=\text{m}^2\).

Can every nonzero number be canceled?

Cancellation requires division by a nonzero factor and a number system in which that division is defined. Zero can never be canceled by division.

Is every kind of multiplication commutative?

No. Ordinary real-number multiplication is commutative, but standard matrix multiplication and some other abstract products are not.

Does \(3x\) mean the number thirty-something?

No. Juxtaposition in algebra means multiplication: \(3x=3\times x\).

Working Summary

Multiplication combines factors into a product. It can represent equal groups, scaling, rectangular measurement, rate relationships, or paired choices.

For ordinary numbers, multiplication is commutative and associative, has identity \(1\), and distributes over addition and subtraction. Zero collapses every numerical product, while each nonzero real number has a reciprocal.

Place-value multiplication is organized distribution; fraction multiplication scales by parts; units multiply to create new dimensions. Estimate first, calculate carefully, and check the product by division.

Check Your Understanding

Name the parts of \(14\times6=84\).

\(14\) and \(6\) are factors; \(84\) is the product.

Evaluate \((-7)(-8)\).

Two negative factors produce a positive result: \((-7)(-8)=56\).

Use distribution to evaluate \(13\times48\).

\(13(50-2)=650-26=624\).

Use factor compensation to evaluate \(28\times25\).

Double \(25\) and halve \(28\): \(28\times25=14\times50=7\times100=700\).

Multiply \(\frac7{12}\times\frac{18}{35}\).

Cancel common factors: \(\frac7{12}\times\frac{18}{35}=\frac15\times\frac32=\frac3{10}\).

Multiply \(2.4\times0.06\).

\(24\times6=144\), with three decimal places altogether, so the product is \(0.144\).

What unit results from \(8\text{ m/s}\times15\text{ s}\)?

Seconds cancel, leaving meters: the product is \(120\text{ m}\).

Solve \(9x=117\).

Divide both sides by \(9\): \(x=13\).

Three routes can be paired with five departure times. How many route-time combinations are possible?

The multiplication principle gives \(3\times5=15\) combinations.

Multiply the vector \((4,-3)\) by the scalar \(-2\).

Multiply each component: \(-2(4,-3)=(-8,6)\).
