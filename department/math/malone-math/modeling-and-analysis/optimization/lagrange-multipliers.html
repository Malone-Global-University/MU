Lagrange Multipliers

Lagrange multipliers provide a systematic way to optimize a smooth objective function while requiring one or more smooth equality constraints. The method replaces direct substitution with a geometric condition: at a regular constrained optimum, the gradient of the objective must align with the gradient of the constraint. This turns constrained optimization into a system of equations that can often be solved directly.

The Central Idea

Suppose we want to optimize

\[ f(x,y) \]

subject to

\[ g(x,y)=c. \]

At many smooth constrained extrema,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \lambda \nabla g }. \]

The scalar \(\lambda\) is called a Lagrange multiplier.

Why the Gradients Must Align

The gradient

\[ \nabla f \]

points in the direction of greatest local increase of the objective.

Meanwhile,

\[ \nabla g \]

is perpendicular to the level curve

\[ g(x,y)=c. \]

At a constrained optimum, movement along the constraint cannot improve the objective to first order.

Therefore, the objective gradient must be normal to the constraint curve as well.

Geometric Interpretation

The level curves of the objective satisfy

\[ f(x,y)=k. \]

The feasible set is the constraint curve

\[ g(x,y)=c. \]

At a smooth constrained extremum, an objective contour typically becomes tangent to the constraint.

Their normal vectors are therefore parallel:

\[ \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g. \]

The Basic Lagrange System

For

\[ \text{optimize }f(x,y) \]

subject to

\[ g(x,y)=c, \]

solve

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} f_x &= \lambda g_x,\\ f_y &= \lambda g_y,\\ g(x,y) &= c. \end{aligned} } \]

The unknowns are

\[ x,\qquad y,\qquad\lambda. \]

The Lagrangian

The method can be organized using the Lagrangian.

One common convention is

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal{L}(x,y,\lambda) = f(x,y) - \lambda\bigl(g(x,y)-c\bigr) }. \]

Other sign conventions are also valid.

Stationarity of the Lagrangian

Differentiate the Lagrangian with respect to every unknown:

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x}=0, \] \[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial y}=0, \] \[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda}=0. \]

These equations recover

\[ \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g \]

together with the original constraint.

Example: Minimize Distance to the Origin

Minimize

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+y^2 \]

subject to

\[ x+y=10. \]

Define

\[ g(x,y)=x+y. \]

Then

\[ \nabla f= \begin{bmatrix} 2x\\ 2y \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \nabla g= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Solving the Distance Example

The multiplier condition gives

\[ 2x=\lambda, \] \[ 2y=\lambda, \]

together with

\[ x+y=10. \]

From the first two equations,

\[ x=y. \]

Therefore,

\[ 2x=10 \]

and

\[ x=y=5. \]

Thus the constrained minimizer is

\[ \boxed{(5,5)}. \]

Checking the Objective Value

At

\[ (5,5), \]

the objective is

\[ f(5,5) = 5^2+5^2 = 50. \]

Therefore, the minimum squared distance is

\[ \boxed{50}. \]

Example: Maximize a Product

Maximize

\[ f(x,y)=xy \]

subject to

\[ x+y=20. \]

The gradients are

\[ \nabla f= \begin{bmatrix} y\\ x \end{bmatrix}, \] \[ \nabla g= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Solving the Product Example

The Lagrange equations are

\[ y=\lambda, \] \[ x=\lambda, \] \[ x+y=20. \]

Hence

\[ x=y. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=y=10. \]

The objective value is

\[ f(10,10)=100. \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{(10,10)} \]

is the maximizing point.

Connection with the Rectangle Problem

If \(x\) and \(y\) are side lengths of a rectangle with perimeter \(40\), then

\[ 2x+2y=40 \]

simplifies to

\[ x+y=20. \]

Maximizing

\[ A=xy \]

therefore gives

\[ x=y=10. \]

Among rectangles with fixed perimeter, the square has maximum area.

Example: Constraint on a Circle

Maximize and minimize

\[ f(x,y)=x+y \]

subject to

\[ x^2+y^2=1. \]

Let

\[ g(x,y)=x^2+y^2. \]

Then

\[ \nabla f= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \nabla g= \begin{bmatrix} 2x\\ 2y \end{bmatrix}. \]

Solving the Circle Example

The equations are

\[ 1=2\lambda x, \] \[ 1=2\lambda y, \] \[ x^2+y^2=1. \]

The first two equations imply

\[ x=y. \]

Therefore,

\[ 2x^2=1, \]

so

\[ x=y=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt2}. \]

The candidate points are

\[ \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \frac{1}{\sqrt2} \right) \]

and

\[ \left( -\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, -\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \right). \]

Comparing the Circle Candidates

At the positive candidate,

\[ f = \sqrt2. \]

At the negative candidate,

\[ f = -\sqrt2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \max f=\sqrt2 } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \min f=-\sqrt2 }. \]

Lagrange Multipliers Produce Candidates

Solving

\[ \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g \]

identifies candidate constrained extrema.

The resulting points must still be:

  • checked for feasibility;
  • evaluated in the objective;
  • compared when several candidates exist;
  • interpreted in the context of the original problem.

Why the Method Does Not Automatically Classify Candidates

The multiplier equations do not by themselves tell us whether a point is a maximum, minimum, or another stationary constrained point.

Classification may require:

  • objective-value comparison;
  • geometric reasoning;
  • second-order conditions;
  • convexity or concavity;
  • compactness and existence theorems.

Multiple Equality Constraints

Suppose we optimize

\[ f(\mathbf{x}) \]

subject to

\[ g_1(\mathbf{x})=c_1 \]

and

\[ g_2(\mathbf{x})=c_2. \]

Then the stationarity condition becomes

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \lambda_1\nabla g_1 + \lambda_2\nabla g_2 }. \]

The Multi-Constraint Lagrangian

For equality constraints

\[ g_j(\mathbf{x})=c_j, \qquad j=1,\ldots,p, \]

define

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal{L} = f(\mathbf{x}) - \sum_{j=1}^{p} \lambda_j \bigl(g_j(\mathbf{x})-c_j\bigr) }. \]

We then set the derivatives of \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to the decision variables and multipliers equal to zero.

Example with Three Variables

Minimize

\[ f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2 \]

subject to

\[ x+y+z=12. \]

The gradients are

\[ \nabla f= \begin{bmatrix} 2x\\ 2y\\ 2z \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \nabla g= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Solving the Three-Variable Example

The Lagrange equations give

\[ 2x=\lambda, \] \[ 2y=\lambda, \] \[ 2z=\lambda. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=y=z. \]

Since

\[ x+y+z=12, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{x=y=z=4}. \]

Symmetry Can Simplify the Algebra

Symmetric objectives and constraints often imply symmetric candidate solutions.

In the previous example, the equations

\[ 2x=\lambda, \qquad 2y=\lambda, \qquad 2z=\lambda \]

immediately reveal

\[ x=y=z. \]

When Substitution Is Easier

Lagrange multipliers are powerful, but they are not always the simplest method.

If a constraint such as

\[ y=10-x \]

can be substituted easily into the objective, direct elimination may be faster.

Method choice should reflect the structure of the problem.

When Lagrange Multipliers Are Especially Useful

The method becomes especially useful when:

  • the equality constraint is difficult to solve explicitly;
  • several variables are involved;
  • several equality constraints are present;
  • the geometry of the constraint is naturally described implicitly;
  • a systematic symbolic method is preferred.

Regularity of the Constraint

The basic multiplier condition assumes the constraint is locally regular.

For a single constraint,

\[ g(\mathbf{x})=c, \]

we usually require

\[ \boxed{ \nabla g(\mathbf{x}^*)\ne\mathbf{0} }. \]

This ensures that the constraint has a well-defined local normal direction.

Singular Constraint Points

If

\[ \nabla g=\mathbf{0} \]

at a feasible point, the ordinary Lagrange condition may fail to identify that point.

Such singular points must be examined separately.

Example of a Singular Constraint

Consider

\[ g(x,y)=x^2+y^2 \]

with constraint

\[ g(x,y)=0. \]

The only feasible point is

\[ (0,0). \]

But

\[ \nabla g(0,0)=\mathbf{0}. \]

The point must therefore be recognized directly rather than relying only on the standard multiplier condition.

The Multiplier as a Sensitivity Measure

In many regular optimization problems, the Lagrange multiplier has an important interpretation.

Suppose the constraint is

\[ g(\mathbf{x})=c. \]

If the optimal value is

\[ V(c), \]

then under suitable conditions,

\[ \boxed{ \lambda \approx \frac{dV}{dc} } \]

up to the sign convention used in the Lagrangian.

Shadow-Price Interpretation

In economic and operational models, a multiplier can measure the marginal value of relaxing a binding constraint.

For example, if a constraint represents available labor hours, the multiplier can indicate how much the optimal objective would change if one additional unit of labor became available.

Units of a Lagrange Multiplier

Multiplier units follow from the objective and constraint.

If the objective is measured in dollars and the constraint parameter is measured in labor hours, then the multiplier may have units

\[ \frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{labor hour}}. \]

This supports its interpretation as marginal objective value per unit of constrained resource.

Sign Convention Matters

Some texts define

\[ \mathcal{L} = f-\lambda(g-c), \]

while others use

\[ \mathcal{L} = f+\lambda(g-c). \]

Both conventions are valid.

The sign of \(\lambda\) changes accordingly, while the candidate values of the decision variables do not.

Second-Order Analysis

First-order multiplier equations identify constrained stationary candidates.

More advanced classification can examine curvature only in directions that remain tangent to the constraint.

This produces constrained second-order optimality conditions.

Convexity and Lagrange Multipliers

In convex optimization, multiplier conditions become especially powerful.

When the objective and feasible geometry satisfy appropriate convexity assumptions, a stationary feasible candidate can often be certified as globally optimal.

Relationship to KKT Conditions

Lagrange multipliers handle equality constraints directly.

Inequality constraints require a broader framework.

The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions extend multiplier ideas to problems with constraints such as

\[ g_i(\mathbf{x})\le0. \]

Complementary Slackness Preview

For inequality constraints, inactive constraints should not influence first-order stationarity in the same way as active constraints.

KKT conditions capture this through

\[ \lambda_i g_i(\mathbf{x})=0. \]

This is called complementary slackness.

Example: Maximize Volume with a Surface Constraint

Suppose a rectangular box has dimensions

\[ x,\qquad y,\qquad z \]

and fixed total edge sum

\[ x+y+z=s. \]

The volume is

\[ V=xyz. \]

The multiplier equations reveal that at the symmetric optimum,

\[ x=y=z. \]

Therefore, equal dimensions maximize the volume under this symmetric constraint.

Why Equalization Often Appears

In symmetric constrained problems, the Lagrange equations often force several variables to become equal.

This reflects balance: when the objective and constraint treat variables symmetrically, an unequal allocation can often be improved by redistributing the constrained quantity.

Common Error: Forgetting the Original Constraint

The equations

\[ \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g \]

are not enough by themselves.

The original condition

\[ g(\mathbf{x})=c \]

must be solved simultaneously.

Common Error: Treating \(\lambda\) as the Objective Value

The multiplier \(\lambda\) is an auxiliary variable.

It is not generally the maximum or minimum objective value.

The objective must still be evaluated at the candidate point.

Common Error: Forgetting Multiple Candidates

The multiplier system may produce several feasible solutions.

All relevant candidates must be evaluated and compared.

Common Error: Assuming Every Lagrange Point Is an Extremum

A solution of the multiplier equations is a constrained stationary candidate.

It may require further classification.

Common Error: Dividing by a Quantity That Could Be Zero

Algebraic simplification can accidentally discard valid solutions.

If an equation contains a factor such as

\[ x(\cdots)=0, \]

do not divide by \(x\) until the case

\[ x=0 \]

has been considered.

Common Error: Using the Method at a Singular Constraint Point

When

\[ \nabla g=\mathbf{0}, \]

the ordinary geometric argument for gradient alignment breaks down.

Singular feasible points must be checked separately.

Common Error: Ignoring Domain Restrictions

Candidate points must satisfy:

  • the equality constraint;
  • the natural domain of the objective;
  • any sign or physical restrictions in the original problem.

A Reliable Lagrange Multiplier Workflow

  1. Write the objective function \(f\).
  2. Write each equality constraint in a clear form.
  3. Check domain and regularity conditions.
  4. Compute \(\nabla f\).
  5. Compute the gradient of each constraint.
  6. Set \(\nabla f\) equal to a linear combination of constraint gradients.
  7. Include every original constraint equation.
  8. Solve the complete system for the decision variables and multipliers.
  9. Check for singular feasible points that may have been missed.
  10. Discard infeasible or undefined candidates.
  11. Evaluate the objective at every remaining candidate.
  12. Classify or compare candidates.
  13. Interpret both the optimizer and multiplier values when meaningful.

What to Remember

Lagrange multipliers convert smooth equality-constrained optimization into a system of first-order equations.

The central condition is

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g }. \]
  • the objective and constraint gradients align at regular smooth constrained extrema;
  • the original constraint must always be included;
  • the multiplier is an auxiliary variable, not the objective value;
  • multiple equality constraints require multiple multipliers;
  • singular constraint points may require separate analysis;
  • multiplier solutions are candidates that still require classification or comparison;
  • multipliers often have useful sensitivity or shadow-price interpretations;
  • inequality constraints lead to the broader KKT framework.

The core geometric idea is

\[ \boxed{ \text{at a smooth constrained optimum, the objective contour is tangent to the constraint} }. \]
