Visualization and Interpretation

Computational models can generate thousands or millions of values. Visualization turns those values into patterns that can be inspected, compared, and communicated. Interpretation then connects those patterns back to the mathematics, assumptions, uncertainty, and purpose of the model. A good figure does not merely look clear; it preserves the structure of the data, makes important comparisons visible, and avoids suggesting conclusions that the simulation does not support.

The Central Idea

Visualization and interpretation form the final link between computation and understanding.

The basic process is

\[ \boxed{ \text{model output} \longrightarrow \text{visual representation} \longrightarrow \text{mathematical interpretation} }. \]

The visual representation should serve the question being asked rather than merely display everything the model produced.

Visualization Is Part of Analysis

A graph is not only a presentation device.

Visualization can reveal:

  • trends;
  • oscillations;
  • outliers;
  • instability;
  • transitions;
  • spatial concentration;
  • distribution shape;
  • parameter sensitivity;
  • unexpected numerical artifacts.

These observations can guide further computation and verification.

Start with the Question

The appropriate visualization depends on the quantity and relationship being studied.

Ask first:

  • Is the quantity changing over time?
  • Is the goal to compare categories or scenarios?
  • Is uncertainty important?
  • Is the output spatial?
  • Is the distribution of outcomes more important than the mean?
  • Are two variables being compared?
  • Is the goal to identify a threshold or regime change?

Tables Versus Graphs

Tables are useful when exact values matter.

Graphs are useful when patterns and relationships matter.

A compact table may be best for

\[ 5,\quad10,\quad15 \]

carefully chosen values, while a graph may be much more informative for a time series containing hundreds of points.

Time-Series Plots

If a model output changes through time, a line plot is usually a natural first visualization.

Plot pairs

\[ (t_n,y_n) \]

with time on the horizontal axis and the modeled quantity on the vertical axis.

A time-series plot can reveal growth, decay, oscillation, equilibrium, drift, and transient behavior.

Interpreting Slope

On a time-series graph, local slope represents a rate of change.

Between two simulation points,

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t} = \frac{y_{n+1}-y_n}{t_{n+1}-t_n}. \]

A steep positive slope indicates rapid increase, while a steep negative slope indicates rapid decrease.

Interpreting Curvature

Changes in slope indicate acceleration, deceleration, or nonlinear response.

A curve that becomes increasingly steep is behaving differently from a straight line even if both increase over the same interval.

Curvature can therefore reveal model mechanisms that a start-to-finish difference would hide.

Comparing Several Time Series

Multiple simulation scenarios can be plotted on the same axes when they share compatible units and scales.

For example,

\[ y(t;\theta_1), \qquad y(t;\theta_2), \qquad y(t;\theta_3) \]

can show how a parameter affects the entire trajectory rather than only the final value.

Avoid Overcrowding

A figure containing too many trajectories can become unreadable.

When hundreds of runs are available, alternatives include:

  • plotting selected representative runs;
  • plotting a mean or median trajectory;
  • showing quantile bands;
  • using small multiples;
  • summarizing final outcomes as a distribution.

Scatter Plots

A scatter plot displays pairs

\[ (x_i,y_i). \]

It is useful for studying relationships between two numerical variables.

Scatter plots can reveal:

  • positive association;
  • negative association;
  • nonlinear relationships;
  • clusters;
  • outliers;
  • heterogeneous variance.

Correlation Is Not Mechanism

A visible relationship in a scatter plot does not by itself establish causation.

Even within a simulation, an observed association may arise from:

  • a common parameter;
  • a shared constraint;
  • indirect interactions;
  • selection effects;
  • the model's built-in assumptions.

Interpretation should refer back to the model structure.

Histograms

A histogram summarizes the distribution of repeated simulation outcomes.

If Monte Carlo outputs are

\[ Y_1,Y_2,\ldots,Y_R, \]

a histogram groups these values into intervals and counts how many results fall within each interval.

It can reveal skewness, multiple modes, spread, and tail behavior.

Distribution Shape

Two models can have the same mean and very different distributions.

For example, both may satisfy

\[ E[Y]=10, \]

while one is tightly concentrated near \(10\) and the other has wide variability and a long upper tail.

The mean alone does not describe risk or uncertainty fully.

Box Plots

A box plot summarizes a distribution using quantiles.

It can compactly display:

  • median;
  • lower quartile;
  • upper quartile;
  • spread;
  • potential outliers.

Box plots are useful for comparing distributions across several scenarios.

Empirical Cumulative Distribution

An empirical cumulative distribution function reports the fraction of simulated outcomes less than or equal to a value \(y\).

It estimates

\[ F(y)=P(Y\le y). \]

This form is useful when probabilities and quantiles are more important than bin counts.

Tail Probabilities

If a threshold \(L\) matters, visualization should make exceedance behavior clear.

The relevant quantity may be

\[ P(Y>L). \]

A distribution plot can show where \(L\) lies relative to the simulated outcomes and how much probability mass lies beyond it.

Uncertainty Bands

A stochastic trajectory can be summarized by a central estimate and an uncertainty band.

For example, at time \(t\), plot:

  • median;
  • 25th and 75th percentiles;
  • or 5th and 95th percentiles.

The band communicates variation across simulation runs.

Confidence Intervals Versus Outcome Intervals

These should not be confused.

An interval describing the spread of simulated outcomes answers a question such as:

“Where do most modeled outcomes fall?”

A confidence interval for an estimated mean answers:

“How precisely has the mean been estimated from finite sampling?”

They describe different kinds of uncertainty.

Error Bars

Error bars should state what they represent.

They may indicate:

  • standard deviation;
  • standard error;
  • confidence interval;
  • minimum and maximum;
  • interquartile range;
  • measurement uncertainty.

Unlabeled error bars are ambiguous.

Spatial Fields

Many simulations produce a field

\[ u(x,y) \]

or

\[ u(x,y,z). \]

Spatial visualizations can reveal:

  • gradients;
  • hot spots;
  • fronts;
  • boundary layers;
  • localized concentrations;
  • symmetry or asymmetry.

Heat Maps

A heat map assigns a visual encoding to values on a two-dimensional grid.

For a discretized field

\[ u_{i,j}, \]

each cell or location is displayed according to its value.

Heat maps are useful for scanning large spatial arrays quickly.

Contour Plots

A contour line connects points with the same field value.

A contour satisfies

\[ u(x,y)=c. \]

Closely spaced contours indicate rapid spatial change, while widely spaced contours indicate slower variation.

Vector Fields

A model may produce vectors such as

\[ \mathbf{v}(x,y). \]

Arrows can communicate both direction and magnitude.

Vector-field displays are useful for:

  • velocity;
  • force;
  • flow;
  • electric fields;
  • gradients;
  • transport direction.

Streamlines and Trajectories

A trajectory shows the path followed by a state or object through space or phase space.

If

\[ \mathbf{x}(t) = (x(t),y(t)), \]

plotting \(y(t)\) against \(x(t)\) reveals the geometric path without using time directly as an axis.

Phase Portraits

For a two-state dynamical system

\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=f(x,y), \qquad \frac{dy}{dt}=g(x,y), \]

plotting trajectories in the \((x,y)\)-plane can reveal:

  • equilibria;
  • attractors;
  • cycles;
  • separatrices;
  • long-term qualitative behavior.

Network Visualization

Network models can be displayed with nodes and edges.

Visual encodings may represent:

  • node state;
  • degree;
  • community;
  • edge weight;
  • direction;
  • failure or infection status.

Network diagrams can be informative, but layout geometry should not be mistaken for physical distance unless the network is genuinely spatial.

Agent-Based Visualization

Agent-based models often benefit from animation or snapshots.

A visualization may show:

  • agent positions;
  • state categories;
  • movement paths;
  • clusters;
  • local interactions;
  • changes through time.

Animation can reveal mechanisms that are difficult to see in final summary statistics alone.

Visualization of Parameter Sweeps

A one-parameter sweep can be shown as

\[ Y(\theta). \]

A two-parameter sweep can be shown with:

  • heat maps;
  • contours;
  • response surfaces;
  • regime maps.

These displays reveal the shape of parameter space rather than one isolated result.

Regime Maps

If simulations are classified qualitatively, parameter space can be divided into regions.

For example,

\[ C(a,b) \in \{ \text{stable}, \text{oscillatory}, \text{divergent} \}. \]

A regime map shows where each type of behavior occurs.

Normalization

Variables with different scales may be easier to compare after normalization.

One common transformation is

\[ z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

Another is min-max scaling:

\[ z = \frac{x-x_{\min}} {x_{\max}-x_{\min}}. \]

The transformation should be stated clearly so the displayed values are not mistaken for the original units.

Dimensionless Visualization

Nondimensional quantities can make results from different systems directly comparable.

For example,

\[ \hat{x} = \frac{x}{L}, \qquad \hat{t} = \frac{t}{T}. \]

Plotting dimensionless variables can reveal common behavior hidden by different physical scales.

Logarithmic Axes

Logarithmic scales are useful when values span several orders of magnitude.

For example,

\[ 10^{-3},\quad 10^{-2},\quad 10^{-1},\quad 10^0,\quad 10^1. \]

Equal visual spacing then represents multiplicative rather than additive change.

Interpreting a Logarithmic Scale

A straight line on a semilog plot may indicate exponential behavior.

A straight line on a log-log plot may indicate a power-law relationship.

However, the interpretation depends on which axes are logarithmic and over what range the pattern holds.

Axis Limits Matter

Axis choices can strongly affect visual impression.

A narrow vertical range can make small differences look dramatic. A very wide range can hide meaningful variation.

Axis limits should support truthful comparison rather than exaggeration or suppression.

Zero Baselines

Bar charts generally rely on length to represent magnitude, so starting the quantitative axis at zero is often important.

Line plots do not always require a zero baseline because they often emphasize change and shape.

The choice should reflect the visual encoding being used.

Aspect Ratio

Stretching or compressing a graph can alter the apparent steepness of curves.

The numerical data do not change, but the visual impression does.

Aspect ratio should therefore be chosen with care, especially when slope is part of the interpretation.

Units and Labels

Every important axis should identify the quantity and units.

For example:

\[ t\;(\text{s}), \qquad T\;({}^\circ\text{C}). \]

Missing units make physical interpretation difficult and can hide conversion errors.

Legends and Direct Labels

When several series are shown, the reader must be able to identify them reliably.

This can be done with:

  • a legend;
  • direct line labels;
  • distinct marker forms;
  • panel titles;
  • clear annotations.

The visual distinction should not depend on one feature alone when accessibility is important.

Color Should Encode Meaning

Color can represent magnitude, category, sign, uncertainty, or state.

But color should be used consistently.

If larger values are mapped in one direction in one figure and the opposite direction in another, interpretation becomes harder.

Sequential and Diverging Scales

A sequential scale is appropriate when values increase from low to high.

A diverging scale is useful when values vary around a meaningful reference such as

\[ 0. \]

For example, positive and negative deviations may be visually separated around the zero point.

Do Not Encode Arbitrary Precision

A simulation output such as

\[ 3.141592817 \]

should not automatically be displayed with every digit.

The number of reported digits should reflect:

  • numerical accuracy;
  • measurement precision;
  • model uncertainty;
  • decision relevance.

Visualizing Numerical Error

Verification results can be visualized directly.

For example, plot error

\[ E(h) \]

against grid spacing \(h\).

A log-log error plot can help reveal the convergence rate of a numerical method.

Residual Plots

For validation, define residuals

\[ r_i = y_i^{\text{model}} - y_i^{\text{data}}. \]

Plotting \(r_i\) can reveal systematic patterns that a single error summary hides.

A model may have small average error yet consistently overpredict in one region and underpredict in another.

Observed Versus Predicted

A useful validation plot places observed values on one axis and model predictions on the other.

Perfect agreement would lie on

\[ y_{\text{predicted}} = y_{\text{observed}}. \]

Systematic departure from that relationship can reveal bias or scale problems.

Reference Lines

Reference lines make important comparisons explicit.

Examples include:

\[ y=0, \] \[ y=x, \]

a threshold

\[ y=L, \]

or a known benchmark value.

Such lines help connect the visualization to a mathematical criterion.

Annotations

Annotations can identify:

  • critical events;
  • parameter changes;
  • threshold crossings;
  • maximum or minimum values;
  • regime transitions;
  • known external events.

An annotation should explain an important feature, not clutter the figure.

Small Multiples

When several comparable scenarios must be shown, separate aligned panels can be clearer than placing every curve on one graph.

The panels should use consistent scales whenever direct comparison is intended.

Animation

Animation is useful when the process of change matters.

It can reveal:

  • movement;
  • propagation;
  • formation of clusters;
  • network cascades;
  • growth of spatial patterns.

Static summaries should often accompany animation so that important values can be inspected precisely.

Interactive Displays

Interactive tools can allow users to:

  • select parameter values;
  • zoom into regions;
  • inspect individual runs;
  • toggle model variables;
  • compare scenarios;
  • filter large datasets.

Interactivity is useful when it supports a clear analytical task.

Interpretation Requires Model Context

A graph cannot explain more than the model contains.

If a model assumes a fixed parameter

\[ r, \]

then a plotted trajectory reflects that assumption.

Interpretation should distinguish observed simulation behavior from conclusions about the real world.

Simulation Output Is Conditional

A model result is conditional on the inputs and assumptions used.

Conceptually,

\[ \boxed{ \text{output} = \text{result given the model, parameters, data, and numerical settings} }. \]

A graph should not be interpreted as an unconditional fact.

Distinguish Prediction from Scenario

Some simulations attempt to predict what is likely to happen.

Others ask what would happen under a specified hypothetical condition.

A scenario plot should be labeled as a scenario rather than presented as a forecast if no probability has been assigned to that scenario.

Distinguish Model Variability from Numerical Error

Variation across stochastic model runs is not the same as error caused by a coarse time step or mesh.

These sources should be analyzed separately:

\[ \text{stochastic variability} \neq \text{numerical discretization error}. \]

A visualization should make clear which uncertainty is being shown.

Distinguish Uncertainty from Sensitivity

Sensitivity asks how strongly the output changes when an input changes.

Uncertainty asks how uncertain the output is because inputs, parameters, data, or random processes are uncertain.

A highly sensitive parameter may contribute little uncertainty if it is known very precisely.

Look for Numerical Artifacts

Visualization can reveal numerical problems such as:

  • grid-scale oscillations;
  • checkerboard patterns;
  • negative concentrations;
  • unphysical spikes;
  • boundary discontinuities;
  • solver drift;
  • aliasing.

An unexpected visual pattern should trigger verification before interpretation.

Compare Resolutions Visually

Plotting solutions from several resolutions on the same axes can reveal whether the model is approaching a stable numerical result.

Compare, for example,

\[ u_h, \qquad u_{h/2}, \qquad u_{h/4}. \]

Large visible differences indicate that discretization still matters materially.

Difference Plots

Sometimes two solutions look nearly identical when overlaid.

Plotting their difference directly can be more informative:

\[ d(x) = u_1(x)-u_2(x). \]

This reveals where the solutions differ most strongly.

Relative Difference

When scale varies substantially, relative differences may be useful:

\[ r(x) = \frac{ u_1(x)-u_2(x) }{ u_{\text{ref}}(x) }. \]

Care is required when the reference value is near zero.

Visualizing Conservation

If a quantity should be conserved, plot it through time.

If

\[ M(t)=M_0, \]

then a plot of

\[ M(t)-M_0 \]

makes numerical drift easy to detect.

Visualizing Constraints

If a model requires

\[ x(t)\ge0, \]

include the boundary value \(0\) visibly when checking the result.

Constraint violations should not be hidden by an axis range that excludes them.

Common Error: Plotting Everything

More visual information is not always more insight.

A figure with hundreds of overlapping series may conceal the very pattern it is intended to show.

Choose summaries and comparisons that match the analytical question.

Common Error: Misleading Axis Scaling

Truncated or highly compressed axes can exaggerate or hide differences.

Axis choices should be justified by the kind of visual comparison being made.

Common Error: Hiding Uncertainty

Plotting only the mean trajectory of a stochastic model can create a false impression of certainty.

Show variability when it matters to the interpretation.

Common Error: Confusing Interpolation with Data

A smooth line connecting discrete simulation values may visually suggest that the model was evaluated continuously.

The reader should understand whether the line represents:

  • actual computed values;
  • interpolation;
  • a fitted curve;
  • an analytical solution.

Common Error: Using Visual Area for Precise Comparison

Humans generally compare aligned positions and lengths more precisely than areas or volumes.

When exact comparison matters, simple position- or length-based graphics are often preferable to decorative three-dimensional displays.

Common Error: Overinterpreting Apparent Patterns

Random variation can create clusters, streaks, or apparent trends.

A visual pattern in stochastic output should be supported with quantitative analysis before it is treated as a reliable model feature.

Common Error: Treating Visualization as Validation

A plausible-looking graph does not establish that a model is valid.

Validation requires systematic comparison with relevant evidence, not visual plausibility alone.

A Reliable Visualization Workflow

  1. State the analytical question.
  2. Identify the variables and units.
  3. Choose a visual form suited to the relationship.
  4. Preserve relevant scales and reference values.
  5. Show uncertainty when it matters.
  6. Label axes, units, scenarios, and statistical summaries clearly.
  7. Compare resolutions or benchmarks when verifying numerical behavior.
  8. Check for numerical artifacts before interpreting patterns.
  9. Use tables when exact values matter.
  10. Use plots when trends, distributions, or spatial structure matter.
  11. Generate visualizations reproducibly from stored outputs.
  12. Interpret every figure within the assumptions and purpose of the model.

What to Remember

Visualization makes computational output interpretable, but visual clarity must be matched by mathematical discipline.

  • choose the display according to the analytical question;
  • use time-series plots for temporal evolution;
  • use distributions for stochastic outcomes;
  • use spatial fields, contours, and trajectories for geometric structure;
  • show uncertainty rather than hiding it;
  • label quantities, units, scales, and reference values clearly;
  • distinguish numerical error, stochastic variability, and parameter uncertainty;
  • inspect visualizations for numerical artifacts before interpreting them;
  • generate figures reproducibly from the underlying model outputs;
  • interpret every result as conditional on the model and its assumptions.

The central principle is: use visualization to reveal mathematical structure without allowing the display to claim more than the model and evidence support.

Completing Computational Modeling and Simulation

Computational modeling and simulation brings together mathematical formulation, implementation, discretization, time evolution, stochastic sampling, grid and element methods, cellular and network structures, verification, parameter exploration, reproducibility, and interpretation.

The full modeling cycle can be summarized as

\[ \boxed{ \text{formulate} \rightarrow \text{implement} \rightarrow \text{discretize} \rightarrow \text{simulate} \rightarrow \text{verify} \rightarrow \text{validate} \rightarrow \text{explore} \rightarrow \text{reproduce} \rightarrow \text{interpret} }. \]

The strongest computational models are not defined merely by sophisticated algorithms. They are defined by a transparent chain of mathematical assumptions, numerical choices, evidence, tests, and interpretations that allows the result to be understood and challenged.
