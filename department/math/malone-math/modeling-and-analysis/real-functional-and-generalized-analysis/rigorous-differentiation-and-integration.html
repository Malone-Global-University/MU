Rigorous Differentiation and Integration

Elementary calculus teaches powerful computational rules for derivatives and integrals. Real analysis asks why those rules work, which hypotheses they require, and what can fail when those hypotheses are removed. This lesson develops differentiation and Riemann integration from precise limit and approximation definitions, then connects them through the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

The Central Idea

Differentiation studies local linear behavior. Integration studies accumulation through increasingly fine approximations. Both are limiting processes, so their rigorous development depends on the language of convergence, continuity, compactness, and completeness.

The central relationship is

\[ \boxed{ \text{local change} \xleftrightarrow{\quad\text{Fundamental Theorem}\quad} \text{global accumulation} }. \]

From Computational Calculus to Analysis

In a computational course, we often begin with formulas such as

\[ \frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1} \]

or

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

Analysis begins one level deeper. We ask:

  • What does the derivative actually mean?
  • When does a derivative exist?
  • Which functions are Riemann integrable?
  • Why do partitions approximate area correctly?
  • When may limits, derivatives, and integrals be interchanged?
  • What hypotheses make the Fundamental Theorem valid?

The Derivative at a Point

Let \(f\) be defined on an interval containing \(a\). The derivative of \(f\) at \(a\) is

\[ \boxed{ f'(a) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} } \]

provided this limit exists as a finite real number.

The quotient

\[ \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \]

is a difference quotient. The derivative is the limiting slope of these secant lines as the second point approaches \(a\).

An Equivalent Difference-Quotient Form

Writing \(x=a+h\) gives the equivalent definition

\[ \boxed{ f'(a) = \lim_{x\to a} \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a} }. \]

Both definitions express the same limiting process. One emphasizes the increment \(h\); the other emphasizes a nearby point \(x\).

Example: Deriving the Derivative of \(x^2\)

Let

\[ f(x)=x^2. \]

At \(a\),

\[ \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} = \frac{(a+h)^2-a^2}{h}. \]

Expand:

\[ \frac{2ah+h^2}{h} = 2a+h. \]

Therefore,

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{h\to 0}(2a+h) = \boxed{2a}. \]

Differentiability Is Stronger Than Continuity

If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\).

To see the structure, write

\[ f(x)-f(a) = \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}(x-a). \]

As \(x\to a\), differentiability gives

\[ \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}\to f'(a), \]

while

\[ x-a\to 0. \]

Hence

\[ f(x)-f(a)\to 0, \]

so \(f(x)\to f(a)\).

The Converse Is False

Continuity does not imply differentiability.

The function

\[ f(x)=|x| \]

is continuous at \(0\), but

\[ \lim_{h\to0^+}\frac{|h|}{h}=1 \]

while

\[ \lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{|h|}{h}=-1. \]

Since the one-sided limits disagree,

\[ \boxed{f'(0)\text{ does not exist}}. \]

One-Sided Derivatives

At an endpoint, or when investigating a corner, one-sided derivatives are useful:

\[ f'_+(a) = \lim_{h\to0^+} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \]

and

\[ f'_-(a) = \lim_{h\to0^-} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}. \]

At an interior point, the ordinary derivative exists exactly when both one-sided derivatives exist and are equal.

Differentiability as Linear Approximation

The derivative is more than a slope. It gives the best first-order linear approximation to a function near a point.

If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), then

\[ f(a+h) = f(a)+f'(a)h+r(h), \]

where the remainder satisfies

\[ \frac{r(h)}{h}\to0 \qquad \text{as }h\to0. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+o(h) }. \]

The Meaning of \(o(h)\)

The notation

\[ r(h)=o(h) \]

means

\[ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{r(h)}{h}=0. \]

Thus the error in the linear approximation is small compared with the size of the input increment itself.

Derivative Rules as Theorems

Once differentiability is defined by limits, familiar rules follow from limit laws.

If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at \(a\), then

\[ (f+g)'(a)=f'(a)+g'(a), \] \[ (cf)'(a)=cf'(a), \] \[ (fg)'(a)=f'(a)g(a)+f(a)g'(a), \]

and, when \(g(a)\ne0\),

\[ \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'(a) = \frac{f'(a)g(a)-f(a)g'(a)}{g(a)^2}. \]

Why the Product Rule Works

Begin with

\[ \frac{f(a+h)g(a+h)-f(a)g(a)}{h}. \]

Add and subtract \(f(a+h)g(a)\):

\[ \frac{ f(a+h)g(a+h)-f(a+h)g(a) + f(a+h)g(a)-f(a)g(a) }{h}. \]

Rearranging gives

\[ f(a+h) \frac{g(a+h)-g(a)}{h} + g(a) \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}. \]

Differentiability implies continuity, so \(f(a+h)\to f(a)\). Taking limits yields the product rule.

The Chain Rule

Suppose \(g\) is differentiable at \(a\) and \(f\) is differentiable at \(g(a)\). Then

\[ \boxed{ (f\circ g)'(a) = f'(g(a))g'(a) }. \]

The rigorous proof requires care because the usual quotient manipulation appears to divide by \(g(a+h)-g(a)\), which may occasionally be zero. A proof based on the linear-approximation form of differentiability avoids that difficulty cleanly.

Fermat's Theorem on Interior Extrema

Suppose \(f\) has a local maximum or local minimum at an interior point \(c\), and suppose \(f'(c)\) exists. Then

\[ \boxed{f'(c)=0}. \]

This theorem explains why differentiable interior extrema must occur at critical points. It does not say that every point with derivative zero is an extremum.

Rolle's Theorem

Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and

\[ f(a)=f(b). \]

Then there exists \(c\in(a,b)\) such that

\[ \boxed{f'(c)=0}. \]

The compactness of \([a,b]\) helps ensure that a continuous function attains maxima and minima, while differentiability converts an interior extremum into a zero derivative.

The Mean Value Theorem

Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\). Then there exists \(c\in(a,b)\) such that

\[ \boxed{ f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. \]

The instantaneous rate of change equals the average rate of change at least once somewhere inside the interval.

Why the Hypotheses Matter

The Mean Value Theorem requires:

  • continuity on the closed interval \([a,b]\);
  • differentiability throughout the open interval \((a,b)\).

A jump discontinuity, a corner, or another failure of differentiability may destroy the conclusion. In analysis, hypotheses are part of the theorem, not decorative assumptions.

Consequences of the Mean Value Theorem

If

\[ f'(x)=0 \]

throughout an interval, then \(f\) is constant on that interval.

If

\[ f'(x)>0 \]

throughout an interval, then \(f\) is strictly increasing there.

If

\[ |f'(x)|\le M, \]

then for all \(x,y\) in the interval,

\[ \boxed{ |f(x)-f(y)|\le M|x-y| }. \]

Thus a bounded derivative produces a Lipschitz bound.

Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem

Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\). Then there exists \(c\in(a,b)\) such that

\[ \bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)g'(c) = \bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)f'(c). \]

When \(g'(c)\ne0\) and \(g(b)\ne g(a)\), this may be written

\[ \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}. \]

This theorem is one route to rigorous versions of l'Hopital-type results.

Darboux's Theorem for Derivatives

Derivatives need not be continuous. Nevertheless, every derivative has the intermediate value property.

If \(f'\) takes values \(u\) and \(v\) at two points, then every value between \(u\) and \(v\) is also attained by \(f'\) somewhere between those points.

Therefore a derivative cannot have a jump discontinuity.

Higher Derivatives

If \(f'\) is differentiable, its derivative is the second derivative:

\[ f''=(f')'. \]

Continuing gives

\[ f^{(n)}. \]

Higher derivatives encode increasingly refined local behavior, but their existence must be established rather than assumed.

Taylor's Theorem

If \(f\) has enough derivatives near \(a\), then \(f\) can be approximated by a polynomial built from derivative data:

\[ f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k + R_n(x). \]

Under standard hypotheses, one form of the remainder is

\[ R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1} \]

for some \(\xi\) between \(a\) and \(x\).

Rigorous use of a Taylor polynomial therefore requires control of the remainder, not merely calculation of the polynomial.

Turning from Differentiation to Integration

Differentiation begins with local quotients. Riemann integration begins with finite sums over partitions and asks whether those sums stabilize as the partition becomes increasingly fine.

The key idea is approximation by rectangles, but the rigorous definition must guarantee that the final value does not depend on arbitrary choices of sample points.

Partitions of an Interval

A partition \(P\) of \([a,b]\) is a finite ordered set

\[ P=\{x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_n\} \]

satisfying

\[ a=x_0 The \(i\)-th subinterval is

\[ [x_{i-1},x_i], \]

with width

\[ \Delta x_i=x_i-x_{i-1}. \]

The Mesh of a Partition

The mesh, or norm, of a partition is

\[ \|P\| = \max_{1\le i\le n}\Delta x_i. \]

Saying

\[ \|P\|\to0 \]

means that even the largest subinterval is becoming small.

Riemann Sums

Choose a sample point

\[ t_i\in[x_{i-1},x_i] \]

from each subinterval. The corresponding Riemann sum is

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{i=1}^n f(t_i)\Delta x_i }. \]

A Riemann integral exists when these sums approach one common value as the partition becomes fine, regardless of the allowed sample-point choices.

Riemann Integrability

A bounded function \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is Riemann integrable if there exists \(I\in\mathbb{R}\) such that for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there is \(\delta>0\) with the property that

\[ \left| \sum_{i=1}^n f(t_i)\Delta x_i-I \right| <\varepsilon \]

whenever the tagged partition satisfies

\[ \|P\|<\delta. \]

In that case,

\[ I=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

Upper and Lower Darboux Sums

For a bounded function \(f\) and partition \(P\), define

\[ M_i = \sup_{x\in[x_{i-1},x_i]} f(x), \qquad m_i = \inf_{x\in[x_{i-1},x_i]} f(x). \]

The upper sum is

\[ U(f,P) = \sum_{i=1}^n M_i\Delta x_i, \]

and the lower sum is

\[ L(f,P) = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i\Delta x_i. \]

The Darboux Integrability Criterion

A bounded function \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is Riemann integrable if and only if for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists a partition \(P\) such that

\[ \boxed{ U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon }. \]

The condition says that the total uncertainty between upper and lower rectangle approximations can be made arbitrarily small.

Refinement Improves Bounds

If \(Q\) is a refinement of \(P\), then

\[ L(f,P)\le L(f,Q)\le U(f,Q)\le U(f,P). \]

Adding partition points can only raise lower sums and lower upper sums. The two families of approximations squeeze the possible integral value.

Continuous Functions Are Riemann Integrable

Every continuous function on a closed bounded interval \([a,b]\) is Riemann integrable.

The logic is structural:

  1. \([a,b]\) is compact.
  2. A continuous function on a compact set is uniformly continuous.
  3. Uniform continuity makes the oscillation of \(f\) small on sufficiently short subintervals.
  4. Therefore upper and lower sums can be made arbitrarily close.

Monotone Functions Are Riemann Integrable

Every bounded monotone function on \([a,b]\) is Riemann integrable, even if it has discontinuities.

Thus continuity is sufficient for Riemann integrability, but it is not necessary.

Finite Discontinuities Do Not Prevent Integrability

A bounded function with only finitely many discontinuities on \([a,b]\) is Riemann integrable.

The discontinuous points can be enclosed in intervals of very small total length, so their contribution to the difference between upper and lower sums can be controlled.

A Bounded Function That Is Not Riemann Integrable

Define the Dirichlet function

\[ f(x)= \begin{cases} 1, & x\in\mathbb{Q},\\ 0, & x\notin\mathbb{Q}. \end{cases} \]

Every nonempty interval contains both rational and irrational numbers. Therefore, on every subinterval,

\[ M_i=1, \qquad m_i=0. \]

Hence

\[ U(f,P)=b-a \]

and

\[ L(f,P)=0 \]

for every partition \(P\). The upper and lower sums never approach one another, so \(f\) is not Riemann integrable.

Basic Properties of the Riemann Integral

If \(f\) and \(g\) are Riemann integrable on \([a,b]\), then

\[ \int_a^b (f+g) = \int_a^b f+\int_a^b g, \] \[ \int_a^b cf = c\int_a^b f, \]

and for \(a \[ \int_a^b f = \int_a^c f+\int_c^b f. \]

Order and Comparison

If

\[ f(x)\le g(x) \]

for all \(x\in[a,b]\), then

\[ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \le \int_a^b g(x)\,dx }. \]

In particular, if \(f\ge0\), then

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\ge0. \]

An Integral Absolute-Value Bound

For an integrable function,

\[ \boxed{ \left| \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \right| \le \int_a^b |f(x)|\,dx }. \]

More generally, if \(|f(x)|\le M\), then

\[ \left| \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \right| \le M(b-a). \]

The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals

If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then there exists \(c\in[a,b]\) such that

\[ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = f(c)(b-a) }. \]

Therefore the average value

\[ \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]

is actually attained by \(f\) somewhere on the interval.

Accumulation Functions

Given an integrable function \(f\), define

\[ F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt. \]

This function records the accumulated signed contribution of \(f\) from the fixed starting point \(a\) to the variable endpoint \(x\).

Continuity of the Accumulation Function

If \(f\) is Riemann integrable on \([a,b]\), then the accumulation function

\[ F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt \]

is continuous.

If \(|f(t)|\le M\), then

\[ |F(x)-F(y)| = \left| \int_y^x f(t)\,dt \right| \le M|x-y|. \]

Thus \(F\) is actually Lipschitz.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part I

Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), and define

\[ F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt. \]

Then \(F\) is differentiable on \((a,b)\), and

\[ \boxed{F'(x)=f(x)}. \]

Integration followed by differentiation recovers the original continuous function.

Why Part I Works

Consider the difference quotient:

\[ \frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h} = \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,dt. \]

By the Mean Value Theorem for integrals, there is a point \(c_h\) between \(x\) and \(x+h\) such that

\[ \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,dt = f(c_h). \]

As \(h\to0\), we have \(c_h\to x\). Continuity gives

\[ f(c_h)\to f(x), \]

so \(F'(x)=f(x)\).

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part II

Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), and let \(F\) be any antiderivative of \(f\):

\[ F'(x)=f(x). \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) }. \]

This theorem converts an accumulation problem into endpoint evaluation of an antiderivative.

The Newton-Leibniz Connection

The Fundamental Theorem explains why differentiation and integration behave as inverse processes. The relationship is not merely computational; it is a theorem linking two independently defined limiting constructions.

\[ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx} \left( \int_a^x f(t)\,dt \right) = f(x) } \]

for continuous \(f\), while

\[ \boxed{ \int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) }. \]

Integration by Parts

The product rule implies

\[ (uv)'=u'v+uv'. \]

Integrating and applying the Fundamental Theorem gives

\[ \boxed{ \int_a^b u(x)v'(x)\,dx = [u(x)v(x)]_a^b - \int_a^b u'(x)v(x)\,dx }. \]

Thus integration by parts is the integrated form of the product rule.

Substitution as a Theorem

A rigorous substitution rule follows from the chain rule and the Fundamental Theorem. Under appropriate differentiability and continuity hypotheses,

\[ \int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du. \]

The validity of a substitution therefore depends on the regularity of the functions involved, not merely on a symbolic replacement of variables.

Uniform Convergence and Integration

Suppose \(f_n\to f\) uniformly on \([a,b]\), and each \(f_n\) is Riemann integrable. Then \(f\) is Riemann integrable and

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_a^b f_n(x)\,dx = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. \]

The proof uses

\[ \left| \int_a^b(f_n-f) \right| \le (b-a)\|f_n-f\|_\infty, \]

and uniform convergence drives the right side to zero.

Uniform Convergence and Differentiation

Differentiation is more delicate than integration.

Uniform convergence of \(f_n\) alone does not imply that

\[ f_n'\to f'. \]

A useful theorem is the following: if each \(f_n\) is differentiable, \(f_n(x_0)\) converges at one point \(x_0\), and \(f_n'\) converges uniformly to a function \(g\), then \(f_n\) converges uniformly to some differentiable function \(f\) and

\[ \boxed{f'=g}. \]

A Warning Example for Differentiation

Consider

\[ f_n(x)=\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}. \]

Since

\[ |f_n(x)|\le\frac1n, \]

we have

\[ f_n\to0 \]

uniformly. But

\[ f_n'(x)=\cos(nx), \]

which does not converge pointwise for many \(x\), much less uniformly. Uniform convergence of functions therefore does not control derivatives.

Differentiation Under the Integral Sign

Suppose a function depends on both a parameter \(x\) and an integration variable \(t\):

\[ F(x)=\int_a^b f(x,t)\,dt. \]

Under suitable regularity and uniformity conditions, one may differentiate through the integral:

\[ \boxed{ F'(x) = \int_a^b \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,t)\,dt }. \]

Later analysis courses develop broader versions of this principle using measure-theoretic convergence theorems.

Improper Integrals Are Different

A Riemann integral on a bounded closed interval is defined directly from partitions. An improper integral is instead defined by a limit of proper integrals.

For example,

\[ \int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx \]

means

\[ \lim_{R\to\infty} \int_1^R f(x)\,dx \]

when that limit exists.

The existence of each finite-interval integral does not by itself guarantee convergence of the improper integral.

Riemann Integration Has Limits

The Riemann integral handles continuous functions and many discontinuous functions very well, but it is not flexible enough for every limiting process used in modern analysis.

Important questions remain:

  • How should we integrate highly discontinuous functions?
  • When can pointwise limits of integrable functions remain integrable?
  • How should sets of negligible size be treated?
  • How do we build function spaces suited to convergence in average?

These questions motivate measure theory and the Lebesgue integral.

Common Error: Assuming Continuity Implies Differentiability

The implication

\[ \text{differentiable} \Longrightarrow \text{continuous} \]

is valid, but the reverse implication is not.

The standard counterexample is

\[ f(x)=|x| \]

at \(x=0\).

Common Error: Ignoring Theorem Hypotheses

Before applying Rolle's Theorem or the Mean Value Theorem, check both interval conditions:

  • continuity on the closed interval;
  • differentiability on the open interval.

A correct formula cannot compensate for a missing hypothesis.

Common Error: Treating Every Bounded Function as Integrable

Boundedness is necessary for the standard Riemann definition on \([a,b]\), but boundedness alone is not sufficient.

The Dirichlet function is bounded between \(0\) and \(1\), yet it is not Riemann integrable.

Common Error: Using One Riemann Sum as the Definition

A single left-endpoint, right-endpoint, or midpoint sum is only an approximation.

The integral is the limiting value that survives arbitrary sufficiently fine tagged partitions.

Common Error: Interchanging Limits Without Justification

Expressions such as

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_n \]

and

\[ \int\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n \]

are not automatically equal.

Likewise,

\[ \left(\lim f_n\right)' \]

need not equal

\[ \lim f_n'. \]

Interchanging analytic operations requires a theorem whose hypotheses have been verified.

A Reliable Procedure for Proving Differentiability

  1. Identify the point \(a\) and write the difference quotient.
  2. Simplify algebraically without substituting \(h=0\).
  3. Determine whether the limit exists and is finite.
  4. Check one-sided behavior when a corner, endpoint, or piecewise definition is involved.
  5. State the derivative only after the limit has been established.
  6. Use differentiability to infer continuity when useful.

A Reliable Procedure for Applying a Mean-Value Theorem

  1. Identify the closed interval \([a,b]\).
  2. Verify continuity on \([a,b]\).
  3. Verify differentiability on \((a,b)\).
  4. Compute the relevant endpoint quantity.
  5. State the theorem's conclusion precisely.
  6. Solve for or characterize the guaranteed point \(c\) when possible.

A Reliable Procedure for Proving Riemann Integrability

  1. Verify that the function is bounded on \([a,b]\).
  2. Look for a standard sufficient condition such as continuity or monotonicity.
  3. If needed, use upper and lower Darboux sums.
  4. Control the oscillation on each subinterval.
  5. Construct a partition making \(U(f,P)-L(f,P)\) smaller than a prescribed \(\varepsilon\).
  6. Conclude integrability from the Darboux criterion.

A Reliable Procedure for Interchanging a Limit and Integral

  1. Identify the sequence or family of functions.
  2. Determine the type of convergence available.
  3. Check whether the relevant theorem requires uniform convergence or another stronger condition.
  4. Verify integrability of the approximating functions.
  5. Apply the theorem only after its hypotheses are satisfied.
  6. State the exchanged limit explicitly.

Example: Mean Value Theorem Workflow

Let

\[ f(x)=x^2 \]

on \([1,4]\). Since polynomials are continuous and differentiable, the Mean Value Theorem applies.

The average slope is

\[ \frac{f(4)-f(1)}{4-1} = \frac{16-1}{3} = 5. \]

Since

\[ f'(x)=2x, \]

we solve

\[ 2c=5 \]

to obtain

\[ \boxed{c=\frac52}. \]

Example: Darboux Criterion for \(f(x)=x\)

Consider \(f(x)=x\) on \([0,1]\) and divide the interval into \(n\) equal subintervals of width \(1/n\).

Because \(f\) is increasing, the oscillation on each subinterval is

\[ \frac1n. \]

Therefore

\[ U(f,P_n)-L(f,P_n) = n\left(\frac1n\right)\left(\frac1n\right) = \frac1n. \]

Since

\[ \frac1n\to0, \]

the Darboux criterion confirms that \(f\) is Riemann integrable.

Example: Fundamental Theorem Workflow

Define

\[ F(x)=\int_0^x (1+t^2)\,dt. \]

The integrand is continuous, so the Fundamental Theorem gives

\[ \boxed{F'(x)=1+x^2}. \]

An antiderivative is

\[ x+\frac{x^3}{3}, \]

so

\[ F(x) = x+\frac{x^3}{3}. \]

Practice 2

The absolute-value function is continuous, so \(f\) is continuous at \(x=2\). For the derivative,

\[ \frac{|2+h-2|-0}{h} = \frac{|h|}{h}. \]

The right-hand limit is \(1\), while the left-hand limit is \(-1\). Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f'(2)\text{ does not exist}}. \]

Practice 3

The polynomial is continuous on \([1,3]\) and differentiable on \((1,3)\). Also,

\[ f(1)=0=f(3). \]

Since

\[ f'(x)=2x-4, \]

Rolle's Theorem guarantees a point satisfying

\[ 2c-4=0. \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{c=2}. \]

Practice 4

The average slope is

\[ \frac{2^3-0^3}{2-0}=4. \]

Since

\[ f'(x)=3x^2, \]

solve

\[ 3c^2=4. \]

Because \(c\in(0,2)\),

\[ \boxed{c=\frac{2}{\sqrt3}}. \]

Practice 5

For \(x\ne y\), the Mean Value Theorem gives a point \(c\) between \(x\) and \(y\) such that

\[ f(x)-f(y)=f'(c)(x-y). \]

Taking absolute values,

\[ |f(x)-f(y)| = |f'(c)|\,|x-y| \le 4|x-y|. \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{|f(x)-f(y)|\le4|x-y|}. \]

Practice 6

The interval \([2,5]\) is compact. A continuous function on a compact set is uniformly continuous. Therefore, for sufficiently short subintervals, the oscillation of the function is uniformly small. Choosing a fine enough partition makes

\[ U(f,P)-L(f,P) \]

arbitrarily small. By the Darboux criterion, \(f\) is Riemann integrable.

Practice 7

Every nonempty subinterval contains rational and irrational numbers. Therefore on every subinterval,

\[ M_i=2, \qquad m_i=-1. \]

Since the interval has total length \(1\),

\[ U(f,P)=2 \]

and

\[ L(f,P)=-1 \]

for every partition \(P\). Their difference is always \(3\), so the function is not Riemann integrable.

Practice 8

The integrand

\[ \sqrt{1+t^4} \]

is continuous. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus,

\[ \boxed{F'(x)=\sqrt{1+x^4}}. \]

Practice 9

We have

\[ \left| \int_0^3 f_n-\int_0^3 f \right| = \left| \int_0^3 (f_n-f) \right|. \]

Therefore,

\[ \left| \int_0^3 (f_n-f) \right| \le \int_0^3 |f_n-f| \le 3\|f_n-f\|_\infty. \]

Uniform convergence means

\[ \|f_n-f\|_\infty\to0, \]

so the integral difference tends to zero.

Practice 10

Uniform convergence of the functions alone gives no corresponding control of their derivatives. Additional hypotheses are needed, typically uniform convergence of the derivatives together with convergence at one point.

For example,

\[ f_n(x)=\frac{\sin(nx)}{n} \]

converges uniformly to \(0\), but

\[ f_n'(x)=\cos(nx) \]

does not converge in the required way.

What to Remember

Differentiability is defined by a limit:

\[ \boxed{ f'(a) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} }. \]

Differentiability implies continuity, but continuity alone does not imply differentiability.

The Mean Value Theorem converts derivative information into global control:

\[ f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \]

for some \(c\in(a,b)\), provided its hypotheses hold.

Riemann integration is defined through stable limiting behavior of partition sums. The Darboux criterion is

\[ \boxed{ U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon } \]

for suitably chosen partitions.

The Fundamental Theorem connects the two processes:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx} \left( \int_a^x f(t)\,dt \right) = f(x) } \]

for continuous \(f\), and

\[ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) } \]

when \(F'=f\).

The governing habit of rigorous analysis is to identify the theorem, verify every hypothesis, and only then use the conclusion.
