Introductory Functional Analysis

Functional analysis extends the structural ideas of linear algebra into infinite-dimensional spaces of functions and sequences. Norms provide size, completeness controls limits, inner products add geometry, dual spaces provide linear measurements, and bounded operators describe transformations. The major theorems of the subject show that these ingredients interact in surprisingly strong and useful ways.

The Central Idea

Functional analysis studies vector spaces that also carry analytical structure.

The central framework is

\[ \boxed{ \text{vector space} + \text{norm or inner product} + \text{completeness} + \text{operators} }. \]

This framework allows methods from linear algebra, topology, measure theory, and analysis to operate together.

From Linear Algebra to Infinite Dimension

In finite-dimensional linear algebra, vectors may be represented by finite coordinate lists such as

\[ (x_1,\ldots,x_n). \]

Functional analysis studies spaces whose vectors may instead be functions or infinite sequences:

\[ f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}, \qquad x=(x_1,x_2,\ldots). \]

Infinite dimension introduces new issues involving completeness, compactness, convergence, and continuity of linear maps.

Normed Spaces

A normed space is a vector space \(X\) equipped with a norm

\[ \|x\| \]

satisfying positivity, homogeneity, and the triangle inequality.

The norm induces the metric

\[ \boxed{ d(x,y)=\|x-y\| }. \]

Cauchy Sequences in Normed Spaces

A sequence \((x_n)\) is Cauchy if for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(N\) such that

\[ \|x_n-x_m\|<\varepsilon \]

whenever \(m,n\ge N\).

A Cauchy sequence is internally approaching a limit, even before that limit has been identified.

Banach Spaces

A Banach space is a complete normed vector space.

Thus

\[ \boxed{ \text{Banach} = \text{normed} + \text{complete} }. \]

Completeness guarantees that norm-Cauchy approximation procedures do not converge to objects outside the space.

Examples of Banach Spaces

Standard examples include

\[ \ell^p \qquad (1\le p\le\infty), \] \[ L^p(X,\mu) \qquad (1\le p\le\infty), \]

and

\[ C(K) \]

with the supremum norm when \(K\) is compact.

Inner-Product Spaces

An inner product adds angle and orthogonality to the vector-space structure. For a real inner-product space,

\[ \langle x,y\rangle \]

satisfies symmetry, linearity, and positive definiteness.

The induced norm is

\[ \boxed{ \|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle} }. \]

Hilbert Spaces

A Hilbert space is a complete inner-product space.

Thus

\[ \boxed{ \text{Hilbert} = \text{inner product} + \text{completeness} }. \]

Every Hilbert space is Banach under its induced norm.

Examples of Hilbert Spaces

Important examples include

\[ \ell^2 \]

with

\[ \langle x,y\rangle = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_ny_n, \]

and

\[ L^2(X,\mu) \]

with

\[ \langle f,g\rangle = \int_X f(x)g(x)\,d\mu \]

for real-valued functions.

Orthogonality

Vectors \(x\) and \(y\) are orthogonal when

\[ \boxed{ \langle x,y\rangle=0 }. \]

Orthogonality extends familiar perpendicular geometry to infinite-dimensional settings.

The Pythagorean Identity

If \(x\perp y\), then

\[ \boxed{ \|x+y\|^2 = \|x\|^2+\|y\|^2 }. \]

This identity supports projection, Fourier expansion, least squares, and orthogonal decomposition.

Orthogonal Projection

Let \(M\) be a closed subspace of a Hilbert space \(H\). For every \(x\in H\), there exists a unique element \(m\in M\) minimizing

\[ \|x-m\|. \]

The minimizing element is the orthogonal projection of \(x\) onto \(M\), often written

\[ \boxed{ P_Mx }. \]

The Projection Decomposition

Every \(x\in H\) can be written uniquely as

\[ \boxed{ x=P_Mx+(x-P_Mx) } \]

with

\[ P_Mx\in M \]

and

\[ x-P_Mx\in M^\perp. \]

Bounded Linear Operators

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be normed spaces. A linear operator

\[ T:X\to Y \]

is bounded if there exists \(C\ge0\) such that

\[ \boxed{ \|Tx\|_Y \le C\|x\|_X } \]

for every \(x\in X\).

Boundedness and Continuity

For linear maps between normed spaces,

\[ \boxed{ T\text{ bounded} \iff T\text{ continuous} }. \]

This equivalence allows analytic continuity questions to be reduced to norm estimates.

The Operator Norm

For a bounded linear operator,

\[ \boxed{ \|T\| = \sup_{\|x\|\le1}\|Tx\| }. \]

Equivalently, \(\|T\|\) is the smallest constant satisfying

\[ \|Tx\| \le \|T\|\|x\|. \]

Spaces of Bounded Operators

The bounded linear operators from \(X\) to \(Y\) form the space

\[ \mathcal{B}(X,Y). \]

If \(Y\) is Banach, then

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal{B}(X,Y) \text{ is Banach} }. \]

Linear Functionals

A bounded linear functional is a bounded linear map

\[ \varphi:X\to\mathbb{R} \]

or \(\mathbb{C}\).

Such functionals act as linear measurements of vectors.

The Dual Space

The set of all bounded linear functionals on \(X\) is the dual space

\[ \boxed{X^*}. \]

Its norm is

\[ \|\varphi\| = \sup_{\|x\|\le1}|\varphi(x)|. \]

The Dual Is Always Complete

Even if \(X\) itself is not Banach,

\[ \boxed{ X^* \text{ is Banach} }. \]

Completeness of the scalar field and uniform operator-norm control make the limit of a Cauchy sequence of bounded functionals bounded and linear.

Hahn-Banach Theorem

The Hahn-Banach theorem says, in one standard normed-space form, that a bounded linear functional defined on a subspace can be extended to the whole space without increasing its norm.

If

\[ \varphi:Y\to\mathbb{R} \]

is bounded and linear on \(Y\subseteq X\), then there exists

\[ \Phi:X\to\mathbb{R} \]

with

\[ \Phi|_Y=\varphi \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \|\Phi\|=\|\varphi\| }. \]

Why Hahn-Banach Is Foundational

Hahn-Banach guarantees that bounded linear functionals are plentiful enough to detect vectors.

In particular, if \(x\ne0\), there exists \(\varphi\in X^*\) such that

\[ \boxed{ \varphi(x)\ne0 }. \]

Thus the dual space can separate points of the original normed space.

Riesz Representation in Hilbert Spaces

If \(H\) is a Hilbert space and

\[ \varphi\in H^*, \]

then there exists a unique \(y\in H\) such that

\[ \boxed{ \varphi(x) = \langle x,y\rangle }. \]

Moreover,

\[ \boxed{ \|\varphi\|=\|y\| }. \]

Weak Convergence

A sequence \(x_n\) converges weakly to \(x\) if

\[ \boxed{ \varphi(x_n)\to\varphi(x) } \]

for every \(\varphi\in X^*\).

We write

\[ x_n\rightharpoonup x. \]

Norm Convergence Implies Weak Convergence

If

\[ \|x_n-x\|\to0, \]

then for every \(\varphi\in X^*\),

\[ |\varphi(x_n)-\varphi(x)| \le \|\varphi\|\|x_n-x\|\to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ x_n\to x\text{ in norm} \Longrightarrow x_n\rightharpoonup x }. \]

Weak Convergence Can Be Strictly Weaker

In infinite-dimensional spaces, a sequence may converge weakly without converging in norm.

This difference is one of the defining features of infinite-dimensional analysis.

Example: Standard Basis in \(\ell^2\)

Let

\[ e_n=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots) \]

be the \(n\)-th standard basis vector.

Then

\[ \|e_n\|_2=1 \]

for every \(n\), so \(e_n\) does not converge to \(0\) in norm.

But for every \(y\in\ell^2\),

\[ \langle e_n,y\rangle=y_n\to0. \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{ e_n\rightharpoonup0 }. \]

Weakly Convergent Sequences Are Bounded

If

\[ x_n\rightharpoonup x, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \sup_n\|x_n\|<\infty }. \]

One standard proof uses the Uniform Boundedness Principle applied to the evaluation maps on the dual.

The Uniform Boundedness Principle

Let \(X\) be Banach and let

\[ \{T_\alpha\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(X,Y). \]

If for every fixed \(x\in X\),

\[ \sup_\alpha\|T_\alpha x\|<\infty, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \sup_\alpha\|T_\alpha\|<\infty }. \]

Pointwise Control Becomes Uniform Control

Uniform Boundedness upgrades a family of separate pointwise estimates into one operator-norm estimate.

It is a powerful example of how completeness forces hidden uniformity.

The Open Mapping Theorem

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be Banach spaces. If

\[ T:X\to Y \]

is bounded, linear, and surjective, then

\[ \boxed{ T\text{ maps open sets to open sets} }. \]

Surjectivity plus completeness prevents a bounded linear map from collapsing neighborhoods too severely.

The Bounded Inverse Theorem

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are Banach spaces and

\[ T:X\to Y \]

is bounded, linear, and bijective, then

\[ \boxed{ T^{-1}:Y\to X \text{ is bounded} }. \]

Algebraic invertibility therefore becomes stable analytic invertibility.

The Closed Graph Theorem

Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are Banach spaces and

\[ T:X\to Y \]

is linear.

If the graph

\[ \{(x,Tx):x\in X\} \]

is closed in \(X\times Y\), then

\[ \boxed{ T\text{ is bounded} }. \]

Four Cornerstone Principles

At an introductory level, four theorems form a central structural cluster:

  • Hahn-Banach theorem;
  • Uniform Boundedness Principle;
  • Open Mapping Theorem;
  • Closed Graph Theorem.

Together they explain why completeness and duality impose strong regularity on infinite-dimensional linear systems.

Compact Operators

A bounded operator

\[ T:X\to Y \]

is compact if it maps bounded sets into relatively compact sets.

Equivalently, whenever \((x_n)\) is bounded, the sequence \((Tx_n)\) has a convergent subsequence.

Finite-Rank Operators Are Compact

If

\[ \dim(\operatorname{ran}T)<\infty \]

and \(T\) is bounded, then \(T\) is compact.

Compact operators can therefore be viewed as infinite-dimensional operators that retain important finite-dimensional behavior.

Compactness and Weak Convergence

If \(T:X\to Y\) is compact and

\[ x_n\rightharpoonup x, \]

then, under standard hypotheses,

\[ \boxed{ Tx_n\to Tx \text{ in norm} }. \]

Compact operators can convert weak convergence into strong convergence.

Adjoint Operators

If \(H\) and \(K\) are Hilbert spaces and

\[ T:H\to K \]

is bounded and linear, the adjoint \(T^*:K\to H\) is defined by

\[ \boxed{ \langle Tx,y\rangle_K = \langle x,T^*y\rangle_H }. \]

Self-Adjoint Operators

An operator \(T:H\to H\) is self-adjoint if

\[ \boxed{ T=T^* }. \]

Self-adjoint operators generalize symmetric matrices and are central to spectral theory.

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

A scalar \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(T\) if there exists \(x\ne0\) satisfying

\[ \boxed{ Tx=\lambda x }. \]

The vector \(x\) is an eigenvector.

The Spectrum

In finite dimensions, eigenvalues capture the main spectral information.

In infinite dimensions, the broader concept is the spectrum. For a bounded operator \(T:X\to X\),

\[ \boxed{ \sigma(T) = \{\lambda: T-\lambda I \text{ is not boundedly invertible}\} }. \]

Why Spectrum Is Broader Than Eigenvalues

In infinite-dimensional spaces, \(T-\lambda I\) may fail to have a bounded inverse even when

\[ Tx=\lambda x \]

has no nonzero solution.

Thus the spectrum may contain points that are not eigenvalues.

Spectral Behavior of Compact Operators

Compact operators on infinite-dimensional spaces retain much of the eigenvalue structure of finite-dimensional matrices.

Nonzero spectral values of a compact operator are eigenvalues with finite multiplicity, and the only possible accumulation point of nonzero eigenvalues is \(0\).

Compact Self-Adjoint Operators

A compact self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space admits an orthonormal eigenvector decomposition analogous to diagonalization of a symmetric matrix.

This is one of the clearest bridges from linear algebra to infinite-dimensional spectral theory.

Orthonormal Sets

A collection \(\{e_\alpha\}\) is orthonormal if

\[ \langle e_\alpha,e_\beta\rangle = 0 \quad \text{for }\alpha\ne\beta \]

and

\[ \|e_\alpha\|=1. \]

Orthonormal Bases

An orthonormal basis of a Hilbert space is an orthonormal set whose closed linear span is the entire space.

In a separable Hilbert space, one may work with a countable orthonormal basis

\[ e_1,e_2,\ldots. \]

Fourier Coefficients in Hilbert Space

Relative to an orthonormal basis \((e_n)\), define coefficients

\[ c_n=\langle x,e_n\rangle. \]

These are the infinite-dimensional analogue of vector coordinates.

Parseval's Identity

If \((e_n)\) is an orthonormal basis of \(H\), then

\[ \boxed{ \|x\|^2 = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |\langle x,e_n\rangle|^2 }. \]

Parseval's identity expresses conservation of squared norm across orthogonal coordinates.

Bessel's Inequality

For any orthonormal sequence \((e_n)\),

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |\langle x,e_n\rangle|^2 \le \|x\|^2 }. \]

Equality for every \(x\) occurs when the orthonormal system is complete.

Weak Compactness

Norm compactness is often too strong in infinite-dimensional spaces.

Weak compactness provides a more flexible replacement. Important bounded sequences may fail to have norm-convergent subsequences while still possessing weakly convergent subsequences under suitable hypotheses.

Reflexive Spaces

Every normed space has a natural map into its double dual:

\[ J:X\to X^{**}, \]

defined by

\[ J(x)(\varphi)=\varphi(x). \]

The space \(X\) is reflexive if this canonical map is onto.

Examples of Reflexive Spaces

Every Hilbert space is reflexive.

More generally,

\[ L^p \quad\text{and}\quad \ell^p \]

are reflexive when

\[ 1 By contrast, \(L^1\), \(\ell^1\), and \(L^\infty\) are not generally reflexive.

Why Reflexivity Matters

Reflexivity provides useful weak compactness properties.

In particular, bounded sequences in many reflexive Banach spaces admit weakly convergent subsequences.

This is especially valuable in optimization, partial differential equations, and the calculus of variations.

Functional Analysis in Optimization

Optimization over function spaces often uses weak convergence because bounded minimizing sequences may fail to converge strongly.

Weak compactness can provide a candidate limit, while lower-semicontinuity can preserve the inequality needed to prove existence of a minimizer.

Functional Analysis in Differential Equations

Differential operators, weak derivatives, and Sobolev-type spaces transform differential equations into operator equations such as

\[ Au=f. \]

Existence and stability may then be studied through boundedness, coercivity, compactness, duality, and weak convergence.

Functional Analysis in Fourier and Spectral Methods

Fourier series become orthogonal expansions in Hilbert space.

Differential and integral operators become objects whose spectral properties can be analyzed through eigenvalues, eigenfunctions, and operator theory.

Functional Analysis in Probability

Random variables with finite moments belong to \(L^p\) spaces.

Conditional expectation may be interpreted as a projection in \(L^2\), while convergence of random variables can be related to norm, weak, almost-everywhere, and measure-theoretic convergence.

Functional Analysis in Generalized Functions

Distribution spaces are dual spaces of test-function spaces.

The distributional viewpoint is therefore a direct application of the central functional-analytic idea:

\[ \boxed{ \text{understand an object through its action on a space of probes} }. \]

Finite-Dimensional Intuition That Survives

Many ideas transfer cleanly:

  • linearity;
  • kernels and ranges;
  • inner products;
  • orthogonality;
  • projection;
  • eigenvalue equations;
  • coordinate expansions.

Finite-Dimensional Intuition That Fails

Several familiar facts do not generalize automatically:

  • closed and bounded need not imply compact;
  • linear need not imply bounded;
  • weak convergence need not imply norm convergence;
  • spectral points need not be eigenvalues;
  • all norms need not be equivalent.

Common Error: Assuming Every Normed Space Is Complete

A norm defines distance, but it does not guarantee that every Cauchy sequence converges inside the space.

Completeness must be checked separately.

Common Error: Assuming Every Banach Space Is Hilbert

Banach spaces require completeness of a norm.

Hilbert spaces require that the norm arise from an inner product.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Hilbert} \Longrightarrow \text{Banach}, } \]

but not conversely.

Common Error: Treating Weak Convergence as Pointwise Convergence

Weak convergence is defined through all bounded linear functionals:

\[ \varphi(x_n)\to\varphi(x). \]

It is not the same as coordinatewise convergence or pointwise convergence of functions unless a specific theorem identifies those notions.

Common Error: Assuming Closed and Bounded Means Compact

The Heine-Borel theorem is a finite-dimensional result.

Closed bounded sets in infinite-dimensional Banach spaces are generally not compact in the norm topology.

Common Error: Forgetting Completeness Hypotheses

Uniform Boundedness, Open Mapping, Bounded Inverse, and Closed Graph theorems depend on Banach-space completeness.

Their conclusions should not be applied after silently dropping those hypotheses.

Common Error: Confusing Spectrum with Eigenvalues

Every eigenvalue belongs to the spectrum, but the spectrum can be larger.

Infinite-dimensional operators may have spectral values that do not admit nonzero eigenvectors.

A Reliable Procedure for Analyzing a Function Space

  1. Identify the underlying vector space.
  2. Identify the norm or inner product.
  3. Determine the induced notion of convergence.
  4. Check whether the space is complete.
  5. Identify useful dense or closed subspaces.
  6. Determine whether Hilbert-space geometry is available.

A Reliable Procedure for Analyzing an Operator

  1. State the domain and codomain.
  2. Verify linearity.
  3. Estimate \(\|Tx\|\) in terms of \(\|x\|\).
  4. Determine boundedness and the operator norm.
  5. Find the kernel and range when relevant.
  6. Check invertibility, compactness, adjoint structure, or spectral behavior as needed.

A Reliable Procedure for Using a Major Banach-Space Theorem

  1. Name the theorem explicitly.
  2. Identify every required space.
  3. Verify completeness where required.
  4. Verify linearity and boundedness hypotheses.
  5. Check surjectivity, closed graph, or pointwise boundedness when relevant.
  6. Only then state the theorem's conclusion.

A Reliable Procedure for Testing Weak Convergence

  1. Identify the dual space or a sufficiently rich class of bounded functionals.
  2. Fix an arbitrary functional \(\varphi\).
  3. Compute or estimate \(\varphi(x_n)\).
  4. Show \(\varphi(x_n)\to\varphi(x)\).
  5. Repeat conceptually for every bounded functional.
  6. Do not infer norm convergence unless an additional theorem permits it.

Example: Projection in \(L^2\)

Let \(M\) be the subspace of constant functions in \(L^2([0,1])\).

The projection of \(f\) onto \(M\) is the constant function

\[ \boxed{ P_Mf = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx }. \]

The residual

\[ f-P_Mf \]

is orthogonal to every constant function.

Example: Weak but Not Norm Convergence

In \(\ell^2\), the standard basis vectors satisfy

\[ e_n\rightharpoonup0 \]

but

\[ \|e_n\|_2=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ e_n\not\to0 \text{ in norm} }. \]

Example: Bounded Inverse Theorem

Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are Banach spaces and \(T:X\to Y\) is bounded, linear, and bijective.

Algebra alone provides an inverse map \(T^{-1}\).

Functional analysis adds the stronger conclusion

\[ \boxed{ \|T^{-1}y\| \le C\|y\| } \]

for some \(C\), so solving \(Tx=y\) depends continuously on the data \(y\).

The space \(\ell^2\) is both Banach and Hilbert.

The space \(C([0,1])\) with the supremum norm is Banach but not Hilbert under that norm.

Practice 2

Since \(0\le x\le1\),

\[ \|Tf\|_\infty = \sup_{x\in[0,1]}|x f(x)| \le \|f\|_\infty. \]

Thus \(\|T\|\le1\). Taking \(f\equiv1\) gives

\[ \|Tf\|_\infty=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\|T\|=1}. \]

We have

\[ |\varphi(f)| = |f(1/2)| \le \|f\|_\infty. \]

Thus \(\varphi\) is bounded and \(\|\varphi\|\le1\). Taking \(f\equiv1\) gives equality, so

\[ \boxed{\|\varphi\|=1}. \]

Hence \(x_n\rightharpoonup x\).

the sequence does not converge to zero in norm.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ e_n\rightharpoonup0 }. \]

The theorem guarantees that

exactly when

Boundedness alone does not imply norm sequential compactness in an infinite-dimensional Banach space.

For example, the standard basis \((e_n)\) in \(\ell^2\) is bounded but has no norm-convergent subsequence because

\[ \|e_n-e_m\|_2=\sqrt2 \]

whenever \(n\ne m\).

What to Remember

A Banach space is a complete normed space:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Banach} = \text{normed} + \text{complete} }. \]

A Hilbert space is a complete inner-product space:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Hilbert} = \text{inner product} + \text{complete} }. \]

Bounded linear operators satisfy

\[ \boxed{ \|Tx\| \le \|T\|\|x\| }. \]

The dual space

\[ \boxed{X^*} \]

contains all bounded linear functionals, and weak convergence is detected by those functionals:

\[ \boxed{ x_n\rightharpoonup x \iff \varphi(x_n)\to\varphi(x) \text{ for every }\varphi\in X^* }. \]

Hahn-Banach, Uniform Boundedness, Open Mapping, and Closed Graph are major structural theorems showing how completeness and duality control linear operators.

Compact and self-adjoint operators recover important finite-dimensional spectral behavior inside infinite-dimensional spaces.

The governing habit of functional analysis is: identify the space, topology or norm, completeness assumptions, operator, and convergence mode before transferring finite-dimensional intuition.

Section Perspective

The lessons in Real, Functional, and Generalized Analysis form a continuous chain of ideas:

\[ \text{real-number completeness} \longrightarrow \text{convergence} \longrightarrow \text{metric spaces} \longrightarrow \text{normed and inner-product spaces} \] \[ \longrightarrow \text{compactness} \longrightarrow \text{rigorous calculus} \longrightarrow \text{measure} \longrightarrow \text{Lebesgue integration} \] \[ \longrightarrow \text{function spaces} \longrightarrow \text{operators and duality} \longrightarrow \text{distributions} \longrightarrow \text{functional analysis}. \]

Each stage enlarges the class of objects that can be analyzed while preserving control through definitions, norms, limits, and structural theorems.

Where We Go Next

Functional analysis is a meeting point rather than an endpoint. Its methods support advanced study of partial differential equations, spectral theory, operator theory, harmonic analysis, optimization, probability, numerical analysis, and mathematical physics.

At this stage, the central objective is not to memorize every theorem in its most general form, but to recognize the recurring architecture: choose the right space, choose the right notion of convergence, identify the operator, verify the hypotheses, and use structure to control infinite-dimensional behavior.
