Probability Distributions in Models

When uncertainty cannot be described adequately by a single range, a probability distribution provides a richer model of what values may occur and how plausible those values are. Distributions allow uncertainty to be represented directly inside mathematical models, making it possible to compare typical outcomes, rare events, spread, risk, and the likelihood of crossing important thresholds.

The Central Idea

A model often contains quantities whose exact values are not known in advance. Instead of assigning one fixed value, we can describe an uncertain quantity by a probability distribution.

If an uncertain model input is represented by a random variable \(X\), then the model may take the form

\[ Y=f(X), \]

or, with uncertain parameters,

\[ Y=f(X;\Theta). \]

The output \(Y\) is then uncertain as well. Rather than producing only one prediction, the model can produce a distribution of possible predictions.

This shift is fundamental: uncertainty is no longer represented merely as a possible interval around a value. It is represented as a mathematical pattern of relative plausibility across many possible values.

From Ranges to Distributions

Suppose a parameter is believed to lie between \(8\) and \(12\). A simple interval representation is

\[ 8\leq\theta\leq12. \]

This tells us which values are being treated as plausible, but it does not tell us whether all values are equally plausible or whether values near \(10\) are more likely than values near the endpoints.

A probability distribution adds that structure. It can represent, for example, a strong concentration around \(10\), an equal weighting across the interval, or a skew toward one side.

The same range can therefore support very different models depending on how probability is distributed inside that range.

Random Variables

A random variable is a numerical quantity whose realized value is associated with the outcome of an uncertain process.

Random variables are commonly written with capital letters such as \(X\), \(Y\), or \(T\). A particular realized value may be written with a lowercase letter such as \(x\), \(y\), or \(t\).

Examples include

  • the number of customers arriving during an hour;
  • the lifetime of a manufactured component;
  • tomorrow's demand for a product;
  • measurement error in a sensor;
  • the return on an investment over a fixed period;
  • the amount of rainfall during a storm.

Calling a quantity random does not necessarily mean that the underlying system is fundamentally unpredictable. In modeling, randomness often serves as a mathematical representation of incomplete information, natural variability, or both.

Discrete and Continuous Random Variables

Random variables are often divided into two broad types.

Discrete Random Variables

A discrete random variable takes values from a finite or countable set.

For example, if \(X\) is the number of defective parts in a shipment, then \(X\) may take values

\[ 0,1,2,3,\ldots \]

Continuous Random Variables

A continuous random variable can take values across an interval or collection of intervals.

Examples include time, temperature, length, mass, and many continuously measured physical quantities.

This distinction affects how probabilities are represented and calculated.

Probability Mass Functions

For a discrete random variable, probabilities can be assigned directly to individual values.

The probability mass function, often abbreviated PMF, is written as

\[ p(x)=P(X=x). \]

The probabilities must satisfy

\[ p(x)\geq0 \]

for every possible value of \(x\), and

\[ \sum_x p(x)=1. \]

Thus, the entire distribution allocates a total probability of \(1\) across all possible outcomes.

A Discrete Model Example

Suppose a small service desk models the number of urgent requests arriving during a 30-minute period as follows:

Requests \(x\) Probability \(P(X=x)\)
00.20
10.35
20.30
30.15

The probabilities sum to

\[ 0.20+0.35+0.30+0.15=1. \]

The distribution communicates more than the possible counts. It also shows that one or two requests are more typical than zero or three requests.

Probability Density Functions

Continuous random variables are commonly represented by a probability density function, or PDF, written as \(f(x)\).

For a valid density,

\[ f(x)\geq0 \]

and

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1. \]

Probability over an interval is found from area under the density curve:

\[ P(a\leq X\leq b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

For a continuous variable, the probability of one exact isolated value is zero:

\[ P(X=x)=0. \]

What matters is probability over intervals rather than probability attached to individual points.

Cumulative Distribution Functions

The cumulative distribution function, or CDF, gives the probability that a random variable is less than or equal to a particular value:

\[ F(x)=P(X\leq x). \]

A CDF applies to both discrete and continuous random variables. It is especially useful when a model asks threshold questions such as

  • What is the probability that demand stays below capacity?
  • What is the probability that a project finishes before a deadline?
  • What is the probability that a measured quantity exceeds a safety limit?

Threshold questions are often more decision-relevant than the probability of any one exact outcome.

Parameters of a Distribution

Many probability distributions belong to families whose shapes are controlled by one or more parameters.

For example, a distribution may be written in the general form

\[ X\sim D(\theta_1,\theta_2,\ldots), \]

where the parameters determine features such as location, spread, shape, rate, or tail behavior.

These probability-distribution parameters are themselves model parameters. If they are estimated from limited data, then the distribution used to represent uncertainty may also contain parameter uncertainty.

Expected Value

The expected value is a probability-weighted average of the values a random variable can take. It is commonly written as

\[ E[X] \]

or

\[ \mu. \]

For a discrete random variable,

\[ E[X]=\sum_x xP(X=x). \]

For a continuous random variable with density \(f(x)\),

\[ E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f(x)\,dx, \]

when the integral exists.

Expected value is often interpreted as a long-run average or central tendency under the model. It should not automatically be interpreted as the most likely individual outcome.

Expected Value Example

Using the service-desk distribution,

\[ E[X] =0(0.20)+1(0.35)+2(0.30)+3(0.15). \]

Therefore,

\[ E[X]=0+0.35+0.60+0.45=1.40. \]

The model predicts an average of \(1.4\) urgent requests per 30-minute period over many comparable periods.

The value \(1.4\) need not be an observable count in any one period. It is a distribution-level summary.

Variance and Standard Deviation

A central value alone does not describe how widely outcomes can vary.

The variance of \(X\) is

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X)=E\left[(X-E[X])^2\right]. \]

It measures average squared deviation from the expected value.

The standard deviation is

\[ \sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}. \]

Standard deviation is often easier to interpret because it has the same units as the original random variable.

In modeling, spread matters because two systems with the same expected value can produce very different operational or decision risks.

Location Is Not Enough

Consider two demand models with the same expected demand:

\[ E[X_1]=E[X_2]=100. \]

If \(X_1\) is concentrated tightly near \(100\) while \(X_2\) frequently ranges from \(50\) to \(150\), the two models have very different planning consequences.

Capacity planning, inventory decisions, staffing, reserves, and safety margins depend on the distribution's spread and tails, not only on its average.

Shape of a Distribution

Probability distributions can differ in several qualitative ways.

  • Center: where values tend to cluster.
  • Spread: how widely values vary.
  • Symmetry: whether the two sides have similar shape.
  • Skewness: whether one tail extends farther than the other.
  • Tails: how much probability lies far from the center.
  • Modes: locations where probability is especially concentrated.

Distribution shape should reflect the mechanism being modeled rather than being chosen only because a familiar formula is convenient.

Uniform Distributions

A uniform distribution assigns equal density across a specified continuous interval.

If

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(a,b), \]

then the density is

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{b-a} \]

for \(a\leq x\leq b\), and \(0\) elsewhere.

A uniform model can be appropriate when values within a bounded interval are intentionally treated as equally plausible. It should not be used merely because only the minimum and maximum are known unless that equal-plausibility assumption is defensible.

Normal Distributions

The normal distribution is a symmetric, bell-shaped distribution widely used to model quantities formed by many small additive influences.

It is commonly written

\[ X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2), \]

where \(\mu\) is the mean and \(\sigma^2\) is the variance.

The density is

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}. \]

Normal distributions arise in measurement-error models and many statistical approximations, but not every uncertain quantity is normally distributed. Bounded, highly skewed, discrete, or heavy-tailed quantities may require other representations.

Bernoulli and Binomial Models

A Bernoulli random variable represents an event with two modeled outcomes, often coded as \(0\) and \(1\).

If

\[ P(X=1)=p, \]

then

\[ P(X=0)=1-p. \]

Repeated independent Bernoulli trials with the same success probability lead to the binomial distribution. If \(X\) counts the number of successes in \(n\) trials, then

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p). \]

Such models are useful for quantities such as defect counts, response counts, pass-fail outcomes, and other finite repeated-event systems when the model assumptions are reasonable.

Poisson Models

The Poisson distribution is commonly used for counts of events occurring within a fixed interval of time, space, area, or exposure.

It is written

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda), \]

where \(\lambda>0\) represents the modeled average event count over the interval.

The probability mass function is

\[ P(X=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots \]

A Poisson model can be useful for arrivals, calls, failures, incidents, or detections when its assumptions match the system being studied.

Empirical Distributions

A model does not always need to assume a named theoretical distribution. Observed data can be used directly to form an empirical distribution.

If observations are

\[ x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n, \]

the empirical distribution treats the observed sample as a representation of the values that have occurred under the observed conditions.

Empirical distributions are especially useful when the data show irregular shapes, multiple modes, or tail behavior that a simple named distribution would hide.

Their limitation is that they reflect the available sample. Sparse or biased data can lead to a misleading representation of uncertainty.

Choosing a Distribution

A probability distribution is a modeling assumption. Its choice should be justified by the structure of the problem, data, physical constraints, or a combination of these.

Useful questions include

  • Is the quantity discrete or continuous?
  • Can it be negative, or is it constrained to be nonnegative?
  • Is it bounded above or below?
  • Is the distribution approximately symmetric or strongly skewed?
  • Are extreme values operationally important?
  • Does a known mechanism suggest a particular distribution family?
  • Is there enough data to estimate the distribution reliably?

A mathematically convenient distribution is not automatically a good model.

Probabilistic Model Output

Once uncertain inputs are represented by distributions, a deterministic formula can produce a probabilistic output.

Suppose

\[ Y=3X+2. \]

If \(X\) is random, then \(Y\) is random. The formula determines how the distribution of \(X\) is transformed into a distribution for \(Y\).

Expected value transforms particularly simply in this linear model:

\[ E[Y]=E[3X+2]=3E[X]+2. \]

Variance transforms as

\[ \operatorname{Var}(Y)=9\operatorname{Var}(X). \]

Thus, even a familiar algebraic relationship acquires a richer interpretation when its inputs are uncertain.

Nonlinear Models and Distribution Shape

Nonlinear functions can change not only the center and spread of a distribution but also its shape.

For example, if

\[ Y=X^2, \]

then negative and positive values of \(X\) can map to the same output value, and the distribution of \(Y\) may become asymmetric even when the distribution of \(X\) is symmetric.

This is one reason uncertainty propagation through nonlinear models often requires simulation, approximation, or specialized transformation methods.

Model Prediction Versus Observation

A probability distribution should not be interpreted as a claim that every outcome will look typical. Low-probability outcomes remain possible under the model.

If an observation lies far in a modeled tail, several explanations are possible:

  • a rare event occurred under an adequate model;
  • the distribution parameters were estimated poorly;
  • the assumed distribution shape was inappropriate;
  • the system changed;
  • measurement or data-quality problems occurred.

A surprising observation is therefore evidence to investigate, not automatic proof that the model has failed.

Probability Is Model Dependent

Statements such as

\[ P(X>100)=0.05 \]

are always interpreted relative to a probability model, its assumptions, its parameter estimates, and the information used to construct it.

The numerical probability is not detached from those assumptions. If the model changes, the probability may change as well.

Responsible probabilistic modeling therefore requires attention to both the calculations and the assumptions that produce them.

Distributions and Decision Thresholds

Many practical decisions depend on whether a model output crosses a critical threshold.

Suppose a process is acceptable only when \(Y\leq50\). A deterministic model may report the nominal prediction

\[ Y=47. \]

A probabilistic model can ask the more informative question

\[ P(Y>50)=? \]

That probability can be more relevant for engineering, finance, operations, safety, and policy decisions than the nominal value alone.

Common Modeling Errors

  • Treating every uncertain quantity as normal. Distribution shape should be justified rather than assumed automatically.
  • Ignoring physical bounds. A model should not assign substantial probability to impossible values without justification.
  • Confusing density with probability. For continuous variables, probability is area under the density over an interval.
  • Using only the mean. Expected value can hide important spread, skewness, and tail risk.
  • Treating estimated distribution parameters as exact. Parameters inferred from data may themselves be uncertain.
  • Ignoring dependence. Two uncertain quantities may not vary independently; later lessons will treat this directly.

A Practical Modeling Workflow

  1. Identify which quantities are uncertain.
  2. Determine whether each quantity is discrete or continuous.
  3. Identify physical, logical, or operational bounds.
  4. Use data or domain structure to select a distributional representation.
  5. Estimate or specify the distribution parameters.
  6. Propagate the uncertain inputs through the model.
  7. Summarize the resulting output distribution.
  8. Evaluate probabilities tied to the actual decision or threshold.
  9. Check whether conclusions are sensitive to the distributional assumptions.

This workflow connects uncertainty representation to the larger modeling cycle of assumptions, computation, validation, interpretation, and decision making.

\[ 0.25+0.50+0.25=1. \]

What to Remember

  • A probability distribution describes how plausibility is allocated across possible values.
  • Random variables provide numerical representations of uncertain quantities.
  • Discrete distributions assign probability mass to individual values.
  • Continuous distributions assign probability through area under a density curve.
  • The CDF gives the probability that a random variable is at or below a threshold.
  • Expected value summarizes probability-weighted central tendency.
  • Variance and standard deviation summarize spread.
  • Distribution shape, bounds, skewness, and tails can materially affect model decisions.
  • Named distributions are assumptions that should be justified by data or mechanism.
  • Uncertain inputs create distributions of model outputs, not merely single predictions.

Probability distributions make uncertainty operational. They allow a model to answer not only what might happen, but how likely different regions of the outcome space are under the assumptions being used.
