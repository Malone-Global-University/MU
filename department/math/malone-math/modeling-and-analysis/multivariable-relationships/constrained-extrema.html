Constrained Extrema

Many optimization problems do not allow the input variables to vary freely. Instead, the feasible inputs must satisfy equations or inequalities such as budgets, geometric conditions, resource limits, or physical laws. Constrained optimization finds maxima and minima while respecting those restrictions. The method of Lagrange multipliers provides one of the central tools for equality-constrained multivariable optimization.

The Central Idea

Suppose we want to optimize

\[ f(x,y) \]

but the variables must satisfy

\[ g(x,y)=c. \]

We are not allowed to search the entire plane.

We must search only the points lying on the constraint set

\[ \boxed{ g(x,y)=c }. \]

Unconstrained versus Constrained Optimization

In unconstrained optimization, candidate interior extrema often satisfy

\[ \nabla f=\mathbf{0}. \]

Under a constraint, the optimum may occur at a point where

\[ \nabla f\neq\mathbf{0}. \]

The constraint prevents movement in arbitrary directions, so the ordinary critical-point condition is no longer sufficient.

The Feasible Set

The set of all inputs satisfying the constraints is called the feasible set.

For example,

\[ x^2+y^2=25 \]

restricts feasible points to a circle.

The optimization problem is therefore one-dimensional even though the objective function depends on two variables.

A Simple Constrained Example

Maximize and minimize

\[ f(x,y)=x+y \]

subject to

\[ x^2+y^2=1. \]

Geometrically, we are asking where the level lines

\[ x+y=k \]

first and last touch the unit circle.

Geometry of a Constrained Extremum

At a smooth constrained extremum, the objective level curve is often tangent to the constraint curve.

Since gradients are normal to level curves, their normals must be parallel.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f \parallel \nabla g }. \]

The Lagrange Multiplier Condition

Parallel vectors differ by a scalar multiple.

Therefore, at a regular constrained extremum,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g }. \]

The scalar

\[ \lambda \]

is called a Lagrange multiplier.

The Full Lagrange System

To optimize

\[ f(x,y) \]

subject to

\[ g(x,y)=c, \]

solve

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g } \]

together with

\[ \boxed{ g(x,y)=c }. \]

Coordinate Form

In two variables, the system becomes

\[ \boxed{ f_x=\lambda g_x } \] \[ \boxed{ f_y=\lambda g_y } \]

together with

\[ \boxed{ g(x,y)=c }. \]

Example: Linear Objective on a Circle

Let

\[ f(x,y)=x+y \]

with constraint

\[ g(x,y)=x^2+y^2=1. \]

The gradients are

\[ \nabla f=\langle1,1\rangle \]

and

\[ \nabla g=\langle2x,2y\rangle. \]

Set Up the Lagrange Equations

We require

\[ 1=2\lambda x \]

and

\[ 1=2\lambda y. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=y. \]

Apply the Constraint

Since

\[ x=y, \]

the constraint gives

\[ x^2+x^2=1. \]

Thus,

\[ 2x^2=1, \]

so

\[ x=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt2}. \]

Hence the candidate points are

\[ \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \frac{1}{\sqrt2} \right) \]

and

\[ \left( -\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, -\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \right). \]

Classify by Comparing Objective Values

Evaluate:

\[ f\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \frac{1}{\sqrt2} \right) = \sqrt2 \]

and

\[ f\left( -\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, -\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \right) = -\sqrt2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ f_{\max}=\sqrt2 } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ f_{\min}=-\sqrt2 }. \]

Why Comparing Values Matters

The Lagrange equations generate candidate points.

They do not automatically tell us which candidate is a maximum or minimum.

A reliable final step is to evaluate the objective at every feasible candidate and compare the resulting values.

Compactness and Guaranteed Extrema

If a continuous objective function is restricted to a nonempty compact feasible set, then it attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum.

In elementary multivariable problems, a closed and bounded constraint set often provides this guarantee.

Example: Product on a Circle

Find the extrema of

\[ f(x,y)=xy \]

subject to

\[ x^2+y^2=1. \]

We have

\[ \nabla f=\langle y,x\rangle \]

and

\[ \nabla g=\langle2x,2y\rangle. \]

Lagrange Equations for the Product

Solve

\[ y=2\lambda x \] \[ x=2\lambda y \]

together with

\[ x^2+y^2=1. \]

Multiplying the first equation by \(y\) and the second by \(x\) gives

\[ y^2=2\lambda xy \]

and

\[ x^2=2\lambda xy. \]

Hence,

\[ x^2=y^2. \]

Candidate Points for the Product

Since

\[ x^2=y^2, \]

we have

\[ y=\pm x. \]

Using

\[ x^2+y^2=1, \]

gives

\[ x^2=\frac12. \]

Therefore the four candidates are

\[ \left( \pm\frac1{\sqrt2}, \pm\frac1{\sqrt2} \right) \]

with independent sign patterns consistent with \(y=\pm x\).

Classifying the Product Example

When the signs agree,

\[ xy=\frac12. \]

When the signs differ,

\[ xy=-\frac12. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ f_{\max}=\frac12 } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ f_{\min}=-\frac12 }. \]

Elimination as an Alternative

Not every constrained problem requires Lagrange multipliers.

If the constraint can be solved conveniently for one variable, substitute that expression into the objective and reduce the problem to one-variable calculus.

Lagrange multipliers are especially valuable when direct elimination is inconvenient or obscures symmetry.

Example: Solve the Constraint Directly

Suppose

\[ x+y=10. \]

Then

\[ y=10-x. \]

If the objective is

\[ f(x,y)=xy, \]

substitute:

\[ f(x,10-x)=x(10-x). \]

The problem becomes an ordinary one-variable quadratic optimization.

Example: Maximum Product with Fixed Sum

Differentiate

\[ q(x)=10x-x^2. \]

Then

\[ q'(x)=10-2x. \]

Set

\[ q'(x)=0, \]

giving

\[ x=5. \]

Therefore,

\[ y=5, \]

and the maximum product is

\[ \boxed{25}. \]

Geometric Meaning of the Multiplier

At a constrained optimum,

\[ \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g. \]

The multiplier tells how strongly the objective gradient aligns with the constraint normal.

In many parameterized optimization problems, \(\lambda\) also has a sensitivity interpretation.

Multiplier as Sensitivity

Suppose the constraint is

\[ g(x,y)=c \]

and the optimized value depends on \(c\).

Under suitable regularity conditions, the multiplier is related to the rate at which the optimal objective value changes as the constraint level \(c\) changes.

This interpretation is important in economics, engineering, and resource allocation.

Three Variables with One Constraint

To optimize

\[ f(x,y,z) \]

subject to

\[ g(x,y,z)=c, \]

solve

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g } \]

together with the constraint equation.

Coordinate Form in Three Variables

The system is

\[ f_x=\lambda g_x, \] \[ f_y=\lambda g_y, \] \[ f_z=\lambda g_z, \]

together with

\[ g(x,y,z)=c. \]

Example: Linear Objective on a Sphere

Optimize

\[ f(x,y,z)=x+2y+2z \]

subject to

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2=9. \]

The gradients are

\[ \nabla f=\langle1,2,2\rangle \]

and

\[ \nabla g=\langle2x,2y,2z\rangle. \]

Set Up the Sphere Equations

We require

\[ 1=2\lambda x, \] \[ 2=2\lambda y, \] \[ 2=2\lambda z. \]

Therefore,

\[ x:y:z=1:2:2. \]

Apply the Sphere Constraint

Write

\[ (x,y,z)=t(1,2,2). \]

Then

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2 = t^2(1+4+4) = 9t^2. \]

Since the constraint requires \(9\),

\[ 9t^2=9, \]

so

\[ t=\pm1. \]

Classify the Sphere Example

The candidate points are

\[ (1,2,2) \]

and

\[ (-1,-2,-2). \]

Evaluate:

\[ f(1,2,2)=9 \]

and

\[ f(-1,-2,-2)=-9. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ f_{\max}=9, \qquad f_{\min}=-9 }. \]

Connection to the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

The previous example also follows from

\[ |\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}| \le \|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|. \]

With

\[ \mathbf{a}=\langle1,2,2\rangle \]

and

\[ \|\mathbf{x}\|=3, \]

we obtain

\[ |f| \le 3\cdot3 = 9. \]

Lagrange multipliers recover the same geometric alignment condition.

Multiple Equality Constraints

Suppose we want to optimize

\[ f(x,y,z) \]

subject to two constraints

\[ g(x,y,z)=c \]

and

\[ h(x,y,z)=d. \]

Under suitable regularity conditions, the constrained-extremum condition is

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g + \mu\nabla h }. \]

Why Two Multipliers Appear

With two independent constraints, the feasible directions lie in the intersection of two tangent conditions.

The objective gradient must lie in the span of the two constraint normals.

The scalars

\[ \lambda \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mu \]

describe that linear combination.

Regularity of the Constraint

The standard one-constraint Lagrange condition assumes the constraint is smooth and

\[ \nabla g\neq\mathbf{0} \]

at the point.

If

\[ \nabla g=\mathbf{0}, \]

the usual geometric normal direction is not available, and the point requires separate attention.

Singular Constraint Points

A singular point of the constraint can still contain a constrained extremum.

Therefore, a complete analysis should not discard feasible points merely because the standard Lagrange equations fail there.

Singular feasible points should be checked separately.

Equality Constraints versus Inequality Constraints

A constraint such as

\[ g(x,y)=c \]

restricts the search to a boundary curve or surface.

An inequality such as

\[ g(x,y)\le c \]

defines a region that may contain interior points as well as a boundary.

Optimization over a Closed Region

Suppose we optimize \(f(x,y)\) over a closed bounded region \(R\).

A complete search typically includes:

  1. interior critical points where \(\nabla f=\mathbf{0}\);
  2. boundary extrema along each boundary component;
  3. corner or endpoint values;
  4. singular boundary points when present.

Why Boundary Checks Matter

Absolute extrema on a closed region often occur on the boundary rather than at interior critical points.

Ignoring the boundary can therefore miss the true maximum or minimum.

Example: Optimization on a Disk

Suppose

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+y \]

on the closed disk

\[ x^2+y^2\le1. \]

We must check both the interior and the boundary circle.

Interior Candidates on the Disk

The gradient is

\[ \nabla f = \langle2x,1\rangle. \]

Since

\[ \nabla f=\mathbf{0} \]

would require both

\[ 2x=0 \]

and

\[ 1=0, \]

there are no interior critical points.

Boundary Candidates on the Disk

On

\[ x^2+y^2=1, \]

use Lagrange multipliers:

\[ \langle2x,1\rangle = \lambda\langle2x,2y\rangle. \]

These equations, together with the circle constraint, produce the boundary candidates.

The absolute extrema are determined by comparing all feasible candidate values.

Boundary Parameterization as an Alternative

Instead of Lagrange multipliers, a boundary can sometimes be parameterized.

For the unit circle,

\[ x=\cos t, \qquad y=\sin t. \]

Then the constrained objective becomes a one-variable function

\[ F(t)=f(\cos t,\sin t). \]

Ordinary one-variable optimization can then be used.

Choosing Between Methods

Useful constrained-optimization methods include:

  • direct substitution;
  • parameterization;
  • Lagrange multipliers;
  • symmetry or inequalities;
  • numerical optimization when symbolic methods are impractical.

The best method depends on the structure of the problem.

Economic Interpretation

Suppose a firm wants to maximize

\[ P(x,y) \]

subject to a resource constraint

\[ C(x,y)=B. \]

Then the Lagrange condition

\[ \nabla P = \lambda\nabla C \]

says that at the constrained optimum, the direction of greatest profit increase is aligned with the normal to the budget boundary.

Resource-Allocation Interpretation

If two inputs consume a fixed resource, an optimal allocation occurs where the marginal benefit of reallocating resources can no longer improve the objective without violating the constraint.

Lagrange multipliers formalize this balance.

Geometric Design Example

Suppose a rectangle has fixed perimeter

\[ 2x+2y=P \]

and area

\[ A=xy. \]

Maximizing area subject to fixed perimeter is a constrained-extrema problem.

Fixed-Perimeter Rectangle with Lagrange Multipliers

Let

\[ f(x,y)=xy \]

and

\[ g(x,y)=2x+2y=P. \]

Then

\[ \nabla f=\langle y,x\rangle \]

and

\[ \nabla g=\langle2,2\rangle. \]

Thus,

\[ y=2\lambda, \qquad x=2\lambda. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=y. \]

Conclusion of the Rectangle Example

Since

\[ x=y, \]

the perimeter condition gives

\[ 4x=P. \]

Hence,

\[ x=y=\frac{P}{4}. \]

So among rectangles with fixed perimeter, the square has maximum area.

Distance Problems as Constrained Optimization

The distance from a point \((x,y)\) to the origin is

\[ d=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}. \]

Because the square root is increasing, minimizing distance is equivalent to minimizing

\[ d^2=x^2+y^2. \]

This often simplifies constrained distance problems.

Example: Closest Point on a Line

Find the point on

\[ x+y=4 \]

closest to the origin.

Minimize

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+y^2 \]

subject to

\[ g(x,y)=x+y=4. \]

Solve the Closest-Point Problem

The gradients are

\[ \nabla f=\langle2x,2y\rangle \]

and

\[ \nabla g=\langle1,1\rangle. \]

Thus,

\[ 2x=\lambda, \qquad 2y=\lambda. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=y. \]

Using

\[ x+y=4, \]

gives

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)=(2,2) }. \]

Second-Order Classification

For many elementary constrained problems on compact sets, direct comparison of candidate values is sufficient.

More advanced methods use second-order information restricted to feasible directions.

These methods involve the Hessian, bordered Hessians, or second variations.

The Lagrangian Function

Another common formulation defines

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal{L}(x,y,\lambda) = f(x,y) - \lambda\bigl(g(x,y)-c\bigr) }. \]

The stationarity conditions are

\[ \mathcal{L}_x=0, \qquad \mathcal{L}_y=0, \qquad \mathcal{L}_\lambda=0. \]

Equivalence of the Lagrangian Form

The equations

\[ \mathcal{L}_x=0 \]

and

\[ \mathcal{L}_y=0 \]

give

\[ \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g. \]

The equation

\[ \mathcal{L}_\lambda=0 \]

recovers the constraint

\[ g(x,y)=c. \]

Sign Convention for the Lagrangian

Some authors define

\[ \mathcal{L} = f+\lambda(g-c) \]

instead of

\[ f-\lambda(g-c). \]

Either convention is valid.

The multiplier changes sign, but the feasible optimizer does not.

Common Error: Forgetting the Constraint Equation

Solving only

\[ \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g \]

is incomplete.

The candidate must also satisfy

\[ \boxed{ g(x,y)=c }. \]

Common Error: Setting \(\nabla f=\mathbf{0}\)

In a constrained problem, the optimum may occur where

\[ \nabla f\neq\mathbf{0}. \]

The correct regularity condition is usually

\[ \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g, \]

not

\[ \nabla f=\mathbf{0}. \]

Common Error: Ignoring Feasibility

A point found algebraically is not a valid constrained candidate unless it satisfies every constraint.

Always substitute candidate points back into the original constraint equations.

Common Error: Dividing by a Variable Too Early

Lagrange systems often contain products such as

\[ x(1-2\lambda)=0. \]

Dividing by \(x\) would lose the possibility

\[ x=0. \]

Separate cases before dividing by expressions that may vanish.

Common Error: Assuming Every Lagrange Candidate Is an Extremum

The method produces necessary-condition candidates under appropriate hypotheses.

Candidates should be evaluated, compared, and interpreted in the context of the feasible set.

Common Error: Missing Singular Constraint Points

If

\[ \nabla g=\mathbf{0} \]

at a feasible point, the standard Lagrange condition may not capture that point.

Such singular feasible points require separate checking.

Common Error: Ignoring Corners and Endpoints

For inequality-constrained or piecewise boundaries, extrema can occur at corners, endpoints, or intersections of boundary pieces.

These should be included in any absolute-extrema comparison.

A Reliable One-Constraint Lagrange Procedure

  1. Identify the objective \(f\).
  2. Write the equality constraint as \(g=c\).
  3. Compute \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\).
  4. Set \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\).
  5. Include the original constraint equation.
  6. Solve the full system without discarding zero cases prematurely.
  7. Check that every candidate is feasible.
  8. Check singular feasible points separately if necessary.
  9. Evaluate \(f\) at every candidate.
  10. Compare values to identify maxima and minima.

A Reliable Closed-Region Procedure

  1. Find interior critical points.
  2. Analyze each smooth boundary component.
  3. Use substitution, parameterization, or Lagrange multipliers on the boundary.
  4. Check corners, endpoints, and singular boundary points.
  5. Evaluate the objective at every feasible candidate.
  6. Compare all values to determine absolute extrema.

Example: Full Lagrange Workflow

Maximize and minimize

\[ f(x,y)=3x+4y \]

subject to

\[ x^2+y^2=25. \]

The gradients are

\[ \nabla f=\langle3,4\rangle \]

and

\[ \nabla g=\langle2x,2y\rangle. \]

Set Up the Full Workflow

The equations are

\[ 3=2\lambda x, \] \[ 4=2\lambda y, \]

together with

\[ x^2+y^2=25. \]

The first two equations imply

\[ x:y=3:4. \]

Complete the Full Workflow

Write

\[ (x,y)=t(3,4). \]

Then

\[ x^2+y^2 = 25t^2. \]

Since the constraint requires \(25\),

\[ t=\pm1. \]

The candidate points are

\[ (3,4) \]

and

\[ (-3,-4). \]

Compare the Full Workflow Values

Evaluate:

\[ f(3,4) = 9+16 = 25 \]

and

\[ f(-3,-4) = -25. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ f_{\max}=25 } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ f_{\min}=-25 }. \]

What to Remember

Constrained optimization searches for extrema only among feasible inputs.

  • Equality constraints define curves, surfaces, or higher-dimensional feasible sets.
  • At a regular equality-constrained extremum, \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\).
  • The original constraint equation must remain part of the system.
  • Lagrange equations produce candidates, not automatic classifications.
  • Compare objective values at all feasible candidates.
  • Check singular constraint points separately when necessary.
  • For closed regions, include interior, boundary, corner, and endpoint analysis.
  • Multiple independent equality constraints introduce multiple multipliers.

The central geometric condition is

\[ \boxed{ \text{objective level set tangent to constraint} \Longrightarrow \nabla f \parallel \nabla g }. \]
