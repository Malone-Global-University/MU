Gradients

A gradient gathers the first partial derivatives of a scalar-valued multivariable function into a single vector. That vector summarizes local change: it points in the direction of greatest increase, its opposite points toward greatest decrease, its magnitude gives the maximum directional rate, and it is perpendicular to smooth level sets. The gradient is one of the central organizing tools of multivariable analysis.

The Central Idea

Suppose

\[ z=f(x,y). \]

The partial derivatives

\[ f_x \qquad\text{and}\qquad f_y \]

give the local rates of change along the coordinate directions.

The gradient packages those coordinate rates into the vector

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f(x,y) = \langle f_x(x,y),f_y(x,y)\rangle }. \]

The Gradient Symbol

The symbol

\[ \nabla \]

is called nabla or del.

Thus

\[ \nabla f \]

is read “the gradient of \(f\).”

Gradient in Two Variables

For

\[ f(x,y), \]

the gradient is

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \left\langle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right\rangle }. \]

It is a vector-valued function built from a scalar-valued function.

Gradient in Three Variables

For

\[ f(x,y,z), \]

the gradient is

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \left\langle f_x,f_y,f_z \right\rangle }. \]

The idea extends naturally to even more input variables.

The Gradient Is a Vector Field

Because

\[ \nabla f(x,y) \]

assigns a vector to each point in the domain, the gradient defines a vector field.

Each vector records local information about how the scalar function changes near its base point.

Example: Basic Gradient

Let

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+3xy+y^2. \]

Then

\[ f_x=2x+3y \]

and

\[ f_y=3x+2y. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f(x,y) = \langle2x+3y,\ 3x+2y\rangle }. \]

Evaluating a Gradient at a Point

For

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+3xy+y^2, \]

evaluate the gradient at \((1,2)\):

\[ \nabla f(1,2) = \langle2(1)+3(2),\ 3(1)+2(2)\rangle. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f(1,2)=\langle8,7\rangle }. \]

What the Components Mean

If

\[ \nabla f(a,b)=\langle A,B\rangle, \]

then

\[ A=f_x(a,b) \]

is the coordinate rate in the \(x\)-direction, and

\[ B=f_y(a,b) \]

is the coordinate rate in the \(y\)-direction.

The gradient combines them into one geometric object.

Connection to Directional Derivatives

In the previous lesson, we found that for a differentiable function,

\[ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = f_xu_1+f_yu_2. \]

Since

\[ \nabla f = \langle f_x,f_y\rangle, \]

we can write

\[ \boxed{ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u} }. \]

The Dot-Product Form

If \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\nabla f\) and the unit direction \(\mathbf{u}\), then

\[ \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u} = \|\nabla f\|\|\mathbf{u}\|\cos\theta. \]

Because \(\|\mathbf{u}\|=1\),

\[ \boxed{ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta }. \]

Why the Gradient Gives the Greatest Increase

Since

\[ -1\le\cos\theta\le1, \]

the directional derivative is largest when

\[ \cos\theta=1. \]

This occurs when

\[ \theta=0, \]

meaning the direction of travel is the same as the direction of \(\nabla f\).

Direction of Steepest Ascent

If

\[ \nabla f(a,b)\neq\mathbf{0}, \]

then the direction of greatest increase is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\nabla f(a,b)} {\|\nabla f(a,b)\|} }. \]

The gradient itself points in the steepest-ascent direction.

Maximum Directional Derivative

The largest possible directional derivative at a point is

\[ \boxed{ \|\nabla f(a,b)\| }. \]

Thus the magnitude of the gradient gives the maximum instantaneous rate of increase per unit distance in input space.

Direction of Steepest Descent

The smallest directional derivative occurs when

\[ \cos\theta=-1. \]

This means the direction of travel is opposite the gradient.

Therefore, the direction of steepest descent is

\[ \boxed{ -\frac{\nabla f(a,b)} {\|\nabla f(a,b)\|} }. \]

Minimum Directional Derivative

The most negative directional derivative is

\[ \boxed{ -\|\nabla f(a,b)\| }. \]

So the gradient magnitude controls both the steepest increase and the steepest decrease.

Example: Steepest Ascent

Let

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+4y^2. \]

Then

\[ \nabla f = \langle2x,8y\rangle. \]

At \((1,1)\),

\[ \nabla f(1,1)=\langle2,8\rangle. \]

Therefore the direction of steepest ascent is

\[ \frac{\langle2,8\rangle}{\sqrt{2^2+8^2}} = \frac{\langle2,8\rangle}{\sqrt{68}}. \]

Simplifying,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}_{\max} = \left\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt{17}}, \frac{4}{\sqrt{17}} \right\rangle }. \]

Maximum Rate in the Previous Example

The gradient magnitude is

\[ \|\nabla f(1,1)\| = \sqrt{2^2+8^2} = \sqrt{68} = 2\sqrt{17}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \max D_{\mathbf{u}}f(1,1) = 2\sqrt{17} }. \]

Level Curves Revisited

A level curve is defined by

\[ f(x,y)=c. \]

Along a level curve, the output remains constant.

Therefore, motion tangent to the level curve produces no first-order change in \(f\).

The Gradient Is Normal to Level Curves

If \(\mathbf{t}\) is a tangent direction to a smooth level curve, then

\[ D_{\mathbf{t}}f=0. \]

But

\[ D_{\mathbf{t}}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{t}. \]

Hence,

\[ \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{t}=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f \perp \text{the level curve} }. \]

Geometric Meaning of Orthogonality

If a contour map represents elevation, the contour itself traces points of equal height.

Walking along the contour produces zero first-order height change.

The gradient points directly across the contour in the locally steepest uphill direction.

Example: Circular Level Curves

Consider

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+y^2. \]

Its level curves are

\[ x^2+y^2=c, \]

which are circles.

The gradient is

\[ \nabla f=\langle2x,2y\rangle. \]

This vector points radially outward, exactly perpendicular to each circle.

Example: Elliptical Level Curves

Let

\[ f(x,y)=4x^2+y^2. \]

Its level curves satisfy

\[ 4x^2+y^2=c. \]

The gradient is

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f=\langle8x,2y\rangle }. \]

At every noncritical point, the gradient is normal to the corresponding ellipse.

Level Surfaces in Three Variables

For

\[ f(x,y,z)=c, \]

the level set is typically a surface.

The gradient

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \langle f_x,f_y,f_z\rangle } \]

is normal to that surface wherever the gradient is nonzero.

Example: Spherical Level Surfaces

Let

\[ f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2. \]

Then

\[ \nabla f = \langle2x,2y,2z\rangle. \]

The level surfaces

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2=c \]

are spheres, and the gradient points radially outward, normal to each sphere.

Gradient of a Linear Function

Consider

\[ f(x,y)=ax+by+c. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f=\langle a,b\rangle }. \]

The gradient is constant everywhere because the surface is a plane with constant slopes.

Gradient of a Constant Function

If

\[ f(x,y)=C, \]

then

\[ f_x=0, \qquad f_y=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f=\mathbf{0} }. \]

A constant function has no local first-order change in any direction.

Zero Gradient

If

\[ \nabla f(a,b)=\mathbf{0}, \]

then all coordinate partial derivatives vanish at the point.

Consequently,

\[ D_{\mathbf{u}}f(a,b)=0 \]

for every unit direction \(\mathbf{u}\), provided the usual differentiability conditions hold.

Critical Points

Points where

\[ \nabla f=\mathbf{0} \]

are important candidates for local maxima, local minima, and saddle points.

A zero gradient does not by itself determine which kind of point occurs. Later optimization methods provide the classification tools.

Example: Finding Critical Points

Let

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+y^2-4x+6y. \]

Then

\[ \nabla f = \langle2x-4,2y+6\rangle. \]

Set the gradient equal to zero:

\[ 2x-4=0, \qquad 2y+6=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ (x,y)=(2,-3) }. \]

Gradient Magnitude

In two variables,

\[ \boxed{ \|\nabla f\| = \sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2} }. \]

In three variables,

\[ \boxed{ \|\nabla f\| = \sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2+f_z^2} }. \]

Gradient Magnitude as Sensitivity

A large value of

\[ \|\nabla f\| \]

means the output can change rapidly in some direction near the point.

A small value means all directional rates are relatively limited.

A zero value means every first-order directional rate is zero.

Contour Spacing and Gradient Magnitude

On a contour map with equal changes in \(f\) between successive contours:

  • closely spaced contours suggest a larger gradient magnitude;
  • widely spaced contours suggest a smaller gradient magnitude.

The gradient points approximately across the contours in the direction of increasing contour values.

Example: Temperature Field

Suppose

\[ T(x,y) \]

gives temperature across a region.

Then

\[ \nabla T(x,y) \]

points in the direction of the fastest local temperature increase.

Its magnitude gives the maximum temperature increase per unit distance.

Example: Terrain Elevation

If

\[ h(x,y) \]

is elevation, then

\[ \nabla h \]

points directly uphill in the steepest local direction.

The vector

\[ -\nabla h \]

points directly downhill.

Example: Cost Function

Suppose

\[ C(x,y) \]

is a cost function.

Then

\[ \nabla C = \langle C_x,C_y\rangle \]

summarizes first-order cost sensitivity to both inputs.

The negative gradient points in the local direction of fastest cost reduction.

Gradient Descent

The observation that

\[ -\nabla f \]

points toward steepest local decrease motivates gradient descent.

A basic iteration has the form

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - \alpha_k\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k) }. \]

The step size \(\alpha_k>0\) controls how far the method moves at each iteration.

Gradient Ascent

To increase a function instead of decrease it, move in the gradient direction:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + \alpha_k\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k) }. \]

This is the corresponding idea of gradient ascent.

The Gradient and Local Linear Approximation

Near \((a,b)\), a differentiable function satisfies

\[ f(a+\Delta x,b+\Delta y) \approx f(a,b) + f_x(a,b)\Delta x + f_y(a,b)\Delta y. \]

In vector form,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta f \approx \nabla f(a,b) \cdot \langle\Delta x,\Delta y\rangle }. \]

This is the bridge to tangent planes and linear approximation.

Gradient and the Differential

The total differential is

\[ df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy. \]

Using the gradient,

\[ \boxed{ df = \nabla f\cdot \langle dx,dy\rangle }. \]

The gradient therefore acts as the coefficient vector of the local linear change.

Example: Linear Change Estimate

Let

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+xy. \]

Then

\[ \nabla f = \langle2x+y,x\rangle. \]

At \((2,1)\),

\[ \nabla f(2,1) = \langle5,2\rangle. \]

For a small change

\[ \langle\Delta x,\Delta y\rangle = \langle0.01,-0.02\rangle, \]

the estimated output change is

\[ \Delta f \approx 5(0.01)+2(-0.02)=0.01. \]

Gradient and Normal Vectors

For a level curve

\[ f(x,y)=c, \]

the gradient provides a normal vector.

For a level surface

\[ f(x,y,z)=c, \]

the gradient likewise provides a normal vector to the surface.

This fact will be used directly in tangent-plane equations.

Example: Normal to a Level Curve

Consider the level curve

\[ x^2+2y^2=6. \]

Define

\[ F(x,y)=x^2+2y^2. \]

Then

\[ \nabla F = \langle2x,4y\rangle. \]

At \((2,1)\),

\[ \boxed{ \nabla F(2,1)=\langle4,4\rangle }. \]

This vector is normal to the curve at that point.

Example: Tangent Direction from the Gradient

If

\[ \nabla F(2,1)=\langle4,4\rangle, \]

then a tangent vector

\[ \mathbf{t}=\langle a,b\rangle \]

must satisfy

\[ \langle4,4\rangle\cdot\langle a,b\rangle=0. \]

One choice is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{t}=\langle1,-1\rangle }. \]

Gradient of a Composite Expression

Consider

\[ f(x,y)=e^{x^2+y^2}. \]

By the chain rule,

\[ f_x=2xe^{x^2+y^2} \]

and

\[ f_y=2ye^{x^2+y^2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = e^{x^2+y^2}\langle2x,2y\rangle }. \]

Gradient with Logarithms

Let

\[ f(x,y)=\ln(x^2+y^2). \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \left\langle \frac{2x}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{2y}{x^2+y^2} \right\rangle } \]

wherever \(x^2+y^2>0\).

Gradient with Trigonometric Functions

Consider

\[ f(x,y)=\sin(xy)+\cos x. \]

Then

\[ f_x=y\cos(xy)-\sin x \]

and

\[ f_y=x\cos(xy). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \langle y\cos(xy)-\sin x,\, x\cos(xy) \rangle }. \]

Units of the Gradient

Each gradient component inherits the units of a partial derivative.

If \(f\) is measured in dollars and both input coordinates are measured in meters, then

\[ \nabla f \]

has components measured in dollars per meter.

When the input variables have different units, each component retains its own corresponding output-per-input unit.

Gradient Components Can Have Different Units

Suppose

\[ C(q,t) \]

is cost in dollars, where \(q\) is units produced and \(t\) is hours.

Then

\[ C_q \]

has units dollars per unit produced, while

\[ C_t \]

has units dollars per hour.

This matters when interpreting gradient magnitude or geometric direction: coordinate scaling and units must be handled thoughtfully.

Common Error: Forgetting That the Gradient Is a Vector

The gradient is not

\[ f_x+f_y. \]

It is the ordered vector

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f=\langle f_x,f_y\rangle }. \]

The distinction preserves directional information.

Common Error: Evaluating Before Differentiating

A reliable workflow is to first compute

\[ \nabla f(x,y), \]

then substitute the requested point.

Substituting too early can erase variables needed for differentiation.

Common Error: Confusing the Gradient with a Unit Direction

The gradient

\[ \nabla f \]

generally does not have length \(1\).

Its direction gives steepest ascent, while its magnitude gives the maximum rate.

The corresponding unit direction is

\[ \frac{\nabla f}{\|\nabla f\|}. \]

Common Error: Saying the Gradient Is Tangent to a Level Curve

At a regular point where

\[ \nabla f\neq\mathbf{0}, \]

the gradient is normal, not tangent, to the level curve.

Common Error: Assuming Zero Gradient Guarantees an Extremum

A zero gradient identifies a critical point, but the point may be:

  • a local maximum;
  • a local minimum;
  • a saddle point;
  • or another degenerate critical configuration.

Additional analysis is required.

Common Error: Ignoring the Domain

The gradient is meaningful only where the relevant partial derivatives exist.

For example,

\[ f(x,y)=\ln(x^2+y^2) \]

is not defined at \((0,0)\), so no gradient exists there.

A Reliable Procedure for Computing a Gradient

  1. Write the scalar function clearly.
  2. Compute the partial derivative with respect to each input variable.
  3. Place those partial derivatives in coordinate order.
  4. Write the resulting vector as \(\nabla f\).
  5. Evaluate at the requested point if needed.
  6. Check the domain and differentiability assumptions.

A Reliable Procedure for Steepest Ascent

  1. Compute \(\nabla f\).
  2. Evaluate it at the point.
  3. If the gradient is nonzero, normalize it.
  4. The normalized gradient gives the direction of steepest ascent.
  5. The gradient magnitude gives the maximum directional derivative.

A Reliable Procedure for Steepest Descent

  1. Compute and evaluate \(\nabla f\).
  2. Reverse its direction.
  3. Normalize if a unit direction is requested.
  4. The minimum directional derivative is \(-\|\nabla f\|\).

Example: Full Gradient Workflow

Let

\[ f(x,y)=x^2y+3xy^2. \]

First compute

\[ f_x=2xy+3y^2, \] \[ f_y=x^2+6xy. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \langle2xy+3y^2,\ x^2+6xy\rangle }. \]

Evaluate the Full Workflow

At \((1,2)\),

\[ \nabla f(1,2) = \langle 2(1)(2)+3(2^2),\, 1^2+6(1)(2) \rangle. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f(1,2) = \langle16,13\rangle }. \]

Steepest Ascent in the Full Workflow

The gradient magnitude is

\[ \|\nabla f(1,2)\| = \sqrt{16^2+13^2} = \sqrt{425} = 5\sqrt{17}. \]

Therefore the unit steepest-ascent direction is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}_{\max} = \frac{1}{5\sqrt{17}} \langle16,13\rangle }. \]

The maximum directional derivative is

\[ \boxed{ 5\sqrt{17} }. \]

What to Remember

The gradient is the central vector of local scalar change.

  • For \(f(x,y)\), \(\nabla f=\langle f_x,f_y\rangle\).
  • For \(f(x,y,z)\), \(\nabla f=\langle f_x,f_y,f_z\rangle\).
  • Directional derivatives satisfy \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u}\).
  • The gradient points in the direction of greatest increase.
  • The negative gradient points in the direction of greatest decrease.
  • The gradient magnitude is the maximum directional derivative.
  • The gradient is normal to smooth level curves and level surfaces.
  • A zero gradient identifies an important critical point but does not by itself classify it.

The core geometric relationship is

\[ \boxed{ \text{gradient} = \text{steepest-ascent direction} + \text{maximum local rate information} }. \]

Where We Go Next

The gradient organizes the local rates of change and provides a normal direction to level sets. Those same first derivatives also determine the best local linear model of a multivariable function.
