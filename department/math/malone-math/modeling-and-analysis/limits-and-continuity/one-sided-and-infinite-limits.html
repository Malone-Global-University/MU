One-Sided and Infinite Limits

A two-sided limit asks what a function approaches from both directions at once. One-sided limits separate those directions. Infinite limits describe a different kind of behavior: instead of approaching a finite output, the function grows without bound. Together, these ideas let us analyze jumps, vertical asymptotes, and other behaviors that ordinary substitution alone cannot describe.

The Central Idea

To understand what happens near \(x=a\), we can approach \(a\) from two separate directions.

From the left:

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)} \]

From the right:

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)}. \]

A finite two-sided limit exists only when the left-hand and right-hand limits agree.

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L \quad\text{when}\quad \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)= \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=L }. \]

What the Minus and Plus Signs Mean

In

\[ x\to a^-, \]

the superscript minus means that \(x\) approaches \(a\) through values less than \(a\).

In

\[ x\to a^+, \]

the superscript plus means that \(x\) approaches \(a\) through values greater than \(a\).

These signs describe the direction of approach. They do not mean that \(a\) itself is negative or positive.

Approaching from the Left

To estimate

\[ \lim_{x\to3^-}f(x), \]

we might use inputs such as

\[ 2.9,\quad2.99,\quad2.999. \]

Every input is less than \(3\), but the values move progressively closer to \(3\).

Approaching from the Right

To estimate

\[ \lim_{x\to3^+}f(x), \]

we might use

\[ 3.1,\quad3.01,\quad3.001. \]

Every input is greater than \(3\), but again the values move progressively closer to \(3\).

A Simple One-Sided Example

Consider

\[ f(x)=x^2. \]

As \(x\) approaches \(2\) from the left,

\[ f(x)\to4. \]

As \(x\) approaches \(2\) from the right,

\[ f(x)\to4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \lim_{x\to2^-}x^2=4, \qquad \lim_{x\to2^+}x^2=4, \]

and hence

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to2}x^2=4}. \]

When the Two Sides Disagree

Suppose

\[ \lim_{x\to4^-}f(x)=2 \]

while

\[ \lim_{x\to4^+}f(x)=7. \]

The function approaches different values from the two directions.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to4}f(x)\text{ does not exist}}. \]

The Two-Sided Limit Test

For a finite limit, the rule is:

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L \iff \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=L \text{ and } \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=L }. \]

The symbol \(\iff\) means “if and only if.”

This criterion is one of the most useful tests in elementary limit analysis.

A Piecewise Example

Suppose

\[ f(x)= \begin{cases} x+1, & x<2,\\ 5-x, & x\ge2. \end{cases} \]

For the left-hand limit, use the formula valid when \(x<2\):

\[ \lim_{x\to2^-}f(x) = \lim_{x\to2^-}(x+1) = 3. \]

For the right-hand limit, use the formula valid when \(x\ge2\):

\[ \lim_{x\to2^+}f(x) = \lim_{x\to2^+}(5-x) = 3. \]

Since the two sides agree,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=3}. \]

A Piecewise Jump

Now consider

\[ g(x)= \begin{cases} x+1, & x<2,\\ x+4, & x\ge2. \end{cases} \]

From the left,

\[ \lim_{x\to2^-}g(x)=3. \]

From the right,

\[ \lim_{x\to2^+}g(x)=6. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to2}g(x)\text{ does not exist}}. \]

This is the basic pattern of a jump discontinuity.

Graphical One-Sided Limits

On a graph, a left-hand limit is found by tracing the graph toward the target \(x\)-value from smaller \(x\)-values.

A right-hand limit is found by tracing toward the same point from larger \(x\)-values.

The actual filled point at the target still does not determine either one-sided limit by itself.

Endpoints Naturally Use One-Sided Limits

Suppose a function is defined only for

\[ x\ge0. \]

At the endpoint \(x=0\), there are no domain values immediately to the left.

The relevant local behavior is therefore

\[ \lim_{x\to0^+}f(x). \]

One-sided limits are especially important at endpoints of restricted domains.

Example with a Square Root

Consider

\[ f(x)=\sqrt{x}. \]

Over the real numbers, the domain is

\[ x\ge0. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to0^+}\sqrt{x}=0}. \]

A real left-hand limit at \(0\) is not available because the function is not defined for negative real inputs near \(0\).

Infinite Limits

Sometimes function values do not approach a finite number. Instead, their magnitude grows without bound.

We write

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=+\infty} \]

when \(f(x)\) becomes arbitrarily large and positive as \(x\) approaches \(a\).

Similarly,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=-\infty} \]

means the outputs decrease without bound.

Infinity Is Not an Ordinary Real Number

The symbols

\[ +\infty \qquad\text{and}\qquad -\infty \]

describe unbounded behavior.

They are not ordinary real-number outputs that a function simply reaches.

An infinite limit tells us that the function can exceed every positive bound, or fall below every negative bound, sufficiently close to the target.

The Basic Reciprocal Model

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x}. \]

As \(x\) approaches \(0\) from the right, \(x\) is a small positive number.

Its reciprocal becomes very large and positive:

\[ \frac{1}{0.1}=10, \qquad \frac{1}{0.01}=100, \qquad \frac{1}{0.001}=1000. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{1}{x}=+\infty}. \]

The Reciprocal from the Left

When \(x\) approaches \(0\) from the left, \(x\) is a small negative number.

\[ \frac{1}{-0.1}=-10, \qquad \frac{1}{-0.01}=-100, \qquad \frac{1}{-0.001}=-1000. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to0^-}\frac{1}{x}=-\infty}. \]

Opposite Infinite Behavior

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x}, \]

we have

\[ \lim_{x\to0^-}\frac{1}{x}=-\infty \]

and

\[ \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{1}{x}=+\infty. \]

The two sides do not agree.

Thus there is no single two-sided limit at \(x=0\).

Same-Sign Infinite Behavior

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x^2}. \]

Whether \(x\) approaches \(0\) from the left or the right,

\[ x^2 \]

is a small positive number.

Therefore,

\[ \lim_{x\to0^-}\frac{1}{x^2}=+\infty \]

and

\[ \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{1}{x^2}=+\infty. \]

We summarize this behavior as

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1}{x^2}=+\infty}. \]

Vertical Asymptotes

If a function grows without bound as \(x\) approaches \(a\) from at least one side, the vertical line

\[ \boxed{x=a} \]

is a vertical asymptote of the graph.

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x}, \]

the line

\[ x=0 \]

is a vertical asymptote.

Vertical Asymptotes and Denominators

Rational functions often have vertical asymptotes where a denominator approaches zero while the numerator remains nonzero.

For example,

\[ f(x)=\frac{3}{x-2} \]

has a denominator approaching zero when

\[ x\to2. \]

The behavior on each side depends on the sign of \(x-2\).

Analyzing \(\frac{3}{x-2}\) from the Left

If \(x<2\) but close to \(2\), then

\[ x-2 \]

is a small negative number.

Therefore,

\[ \frac{3}{x-2} \]

is a large negative number.

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to2^-}\frac{3}{x-2}=-\infty}. \]

Analyzing \(\frac{3}{x-2}\) from the Right

If \(x>2\) but close to \(2\), then

\[ x-2 \]

is a small positive number.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to2^+}\frac{3}{x-2}=+\infty}. \]

Sign Analysis Is Often the Fastest Method

For an expression such as

\[ \frac{5}{x+4}, \]

the denominator changes sign at

\[ x=-4. \]

Immediately to the left of \(-4\), \(x+4<0\).

Immediately to the right, \(x+4>0\).

Since the numerator \(5\) is positive,

\[ \lim_{x\to-4^-}\frac{5}{x+4}=-\infty, \qquad \lim_{x\to-4^+}\frac{5}{x+4}=+\infty. \]

Squared Denominators Behave Differently

Consider

\[ \frac{5}{(x+4)^2}. \]

The squared denominator is positive on both sides of \(-4\).

As \(x\to-4\), the denominator approaches zero through positive values.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to-4^-}\frac{5}{(x+4)^2} = \lim_{x\to-4^+}\frac{5}{(x+4)^2} = +\infty }. \]

Odd and Even Powers Near a Vertical Asymptote

The parity of a factor can help predict sign behavior.

For

\[ \frac{1}{(x-a)^n}, \]
  • if \(n\) is even, the denominator has the same positive sign on both sides;
  • if \(n\) is odd, the denominator changes sign across \(x=a\).

This provides a quick structural shortcut for many rational limits.

A Numerator Can Reverse the Sign

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{-2}{(x-1)^2}. \]

The denominator is positive and approaches \(0\) from above on both sides.

The numerator is negative.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to1^-}f(x) = \lim_{x\to1^+}f(x) = -\infty }. \]

Not Every Zero Denominator Creates a Vertical Asymptote

Consider

\[ \frac{x^2-4}{x-2}. \]

Both numerator and denominator vanish at \(x=2\).

Factoring gives

\[ \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=x+2 \qquad(x\neq2). \]

Thus,

\[ \lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}=4. \]

The graph has a removable hole, not a vertical asymptote.

Hole versus Vertical Asymptote

A useful first distinction is:

  • a factor that cancels completely often produces a removable hole;
  • a denominator factor that remains after simplification can produce a vertical asymptote.

Algebraic simplification should therefore come before declaring a vertical asymptote.

Example: Simplify Before Classifying

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{(x-3)(x+1)}{(x-3)(x-5)}. \]

For \(x\neq3\),

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-5}. \]

The factor \(x-3\) cancels, so \(x=3\) corresponds to a removable hole.

The factor \(x-5\) remains in the denominator, so \(x=5\) is a vertical asymptote.

One-Sided Limits at the Vertical Asymptote

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-5}, \]

near \(x=5\), the numerator is close to \(6\), which is positive.

The denominator is negative immediately to the left and positive immediately to the right.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to5^-}\frac{x+1}{x-5}=-\infty } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to5^+}\frac{x+1}{x-5}=+\infty }. \]

Numerical Evidence for an Infinite Limit

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{(x-2)^2}. \]
\(x\) \(f(x)\)
\(1.9\) \(100\)
\(1.99\) \(10{,}000\)
\(1.999\) \(1{,}000{,}000\)
\(2.001\) \(1{,}000{,}000\)
\(2.01\) \(10{,}000\)
\(2.1\) \(100\)

The outputs grow rapidly without bound from both sides.

Graphical Appearance of Infinite Limits

Near a vertical asymptote, the graph typically rises or falls sharply as it approaches the asymptote.

A branch may rise toward

\[ +\infty \]

or fall toward

\[ -\infty. \]

The direction can differ on the two sides.

Infinite Limits Are Still Local

The statement

\[ \lim_{x\to2^+}f(x)=+\infty \]

concerns values of \(f(x)\) for \(x\) close to \(2\) on the right.

It says nothing by itself about what happens far from \(x=2\).

Infinite limits at finite inputs are therefore still local descriptions.

Do Not Confuse Infinite Limits with Limits at Infinity

These two ideas use the word “infinity” differently.

An infinite limit has a finite input target but unbounded output:

\[ \lim_{x\to2}f(x)=+\infty. \]

A limit at infinity asks what happens as the input itself grows without bound:

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x). \]

The next lesson develops the second idea.

A Reliable Procedure for One-Sided Limits

  1. Identify the target input \(a\).
  2. Determine whether the approach is from the left or right.
  3. Use only values from the requested side.
  4. For piecewise functions, select the formula valid on that side.
  5. Evaluate the behavior as \(x\) moves toward \(a\).
  6. Keep the one-sided answer separate from the two-sided conclusion.

A Reliable Procedure for Infinite Limits

  1. Check whether direct substitution produces a zero denominator.
  2. Simplify first to identify removable factors.
  3. Locate any denominator factor that remains zero at the target.
  4. Determine the sign of the numerator near the target.
  5. Determine the sign of the denominator on each side.
  6. Combine the signs to decide between \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\).
  7. Confirm with a table or graph when useful.

Example: Full Sign-Analysis Workflow

Evaluate the one-sided limits of

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+2}{x-1} \]

at \(x=1\).

Near \(x=1\), the numerator \(x+2\) is close to \(3\), so it is positive.

From the left,

\[ x-1<0, \]

so

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to1^-}\frac{x+2}{x-1}=-\infty}. \]

From the right,

\[ x-1>0, \]

so

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to1^+}\frac{x+2}{x-1}=+\infty}. \]

Example: Even-Power Denominator

Evaluate

\[ \lim_{x\to3^\pm}\frac{4}{(x-3)^2}. \]

Because

\[ (x-3)^2>0 \]

on both sides of \(3\), and the denominator approaches \(0\),

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to3^-}\frac{4}{(x-3)^2}=+\infty } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to3^+}\frac{4}{(x-3)^2}=+\infty }. \]

Common Error: Treating \(a^-\) as a Negative Number

In

\[ x\to5^-, \]

the minus sign does not mean \(-5\).

It means approaching \(5\) through values less than \(5\).

Common Error: Ignoring the Other Side

Knowing

\[ \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=L \]

does not by itself establish

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L. \]

The right-hand limit must also exist and equal \(L\).

Common Error: Calling Every Undefined Point a Vertical Asymptote

An undefined point may be a removable hole instead.

Always simplify a rational expression before classifying the discontinuity.

A canceled factor can create a hole; a remaining denominator factor can create a vertical asymptote.

Common Error: Saying Infinity Is a Function Value

When we write

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=+\infty, \]

we are describing unbounded growth.

We are not saying that \(f(a)\) equals an ordinary real number called infinity.

Common Error: Missing the Sign

The expressions

\[ +\infty \qquad\text{and}\qquad -\infty \]

describe different directions of unbounded behavior.

Sign analysis is therefore essential near vertical asymptotes.

What to Remember

One-sided limits separate the two directions of approach:

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \boxed{\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)}. \]
  • A finite two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits agree.
  • Piecewise functions often require separate left-hand and right-hand analysis.
  • Infinite limits describe unbounded output near a finite input.
  • \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\) describe different directions of unbounded behavior.
  • Vertical asymptotes are associated with unbounded behavior near a finite input.
  • Always simplify rational expressions before declaring a vertical asymptote.
  • Sign analysis is one of the fastest tools for determining one-sided infinite behavior.

The most important checkpoint is: analyze the left and right sides separately before combining them into a two-sided conclusion.
