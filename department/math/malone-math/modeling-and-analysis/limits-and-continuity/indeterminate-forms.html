Indeterminate Forms

Some limit problems produce symbolic forms such as \(0/0\), \(\infty/\infty\), or \(\infty-\infty\). These forms do not tell us the value of the limit. Instead, they signal that competing parts of the expression are changing at comparable rates and that the expression must be analyzed more carefully. Algebraic rewriting often reveals the behavior hidden by the initial form.

The Central Idea

An indeterminate form is a symbolic pattern that can arise during limit evaluation but does not, by itself, determine the value of the limit.

For example,

\[ \boxed{\frac{0}{0}} \]

is not a numerical answer.

It tells us that both numerator and denominator approach zero, but their relative rates of approach still need to be understood.

Why “Indeterminate” Means Undetermined

The word indeterminate means that the visible form does not settle the result.

Different functions can produce the same apparent form but have different limits.

Therefore, the form is a signal to continue the analysis.

The Form \(0/0\)

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}. \]

Direct substitution gives

\[ \frac{0}{0}. \]

This does not mean the limit is \(0\), \(1\), undefined, or infinite.

It means more work is required.

Factoring Can Resolve \(0/0\)

Factor the numerator:

\[ x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2). \]

For \(x\neq2\),

\[ \frac{x^2-4}{x-2} = \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x+2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}=4}. \]

The Same Form Can Produce a Different Limit

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{3x}{x}. \]

Direct substitution gives \(0/0\).

But for \(x\neq0\),

\[ \frac{3x}{x}=3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to0}\frac{3x}{x}=3}. \]

The Same Form Can Produce Zero

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x^2}{x}. \]

Again, direct substitution gives \(0/0\).

For \(x\neq0\),

\[ \frac{x^2}{x}=x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x^2}{x}=0}. \]

The Same Form Can Produce an Infinite Limit

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{x}{x^2}. \]

Direct substitution suggests \(0/0\).

But for \(x\neq0\),

\[ \frac{x}{x^2}=\frac{1}{x}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{x}{x^2}=+\infty}. \]

One Form, Many Possible Outcomes

The form \(0/0\) can lead to:

  • a finite nonzero limit;
  • a zero limit;
  • an infinite limit;
  • no limit at all.

That is exactly why the form is called indeterminate.

The Form \(\infty/\infty\)

Another common indeterminate form appears in limits at infinity.

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{x}{x}. \]

Both numerator and denominator grow without bound, producing the informal form

\[ \frac{\infty}{\infty}. \]

But the quotient simplifies to

\[ 1. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{x}{x}=1}. \]

The Same \(\infty/\infty\) Form Can Give Zero

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{x}{x^2}. \]

Both numerator and denominator become unbounded, but

\[ \frac{x}{x^2}=\frac{1}{x}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{0}. \]

The Same \(\infty/\infty\) Form Can Grow Without Bound

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{x^2}{x}. \]

Both numerator and denominator grow without bound, but

\[ \frac{x^2}{x}=x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{+\infty}. \]

Rates of Growth Control \(\infty/\infty\)

In an \(\infty/\infty\) form, the key question is not simply whether both parts grow.

The important question is:

How fast does the numerator grow compared with the denominator?

Degree comparison, dominant terms, and later derivative-based methods can answer that question.

Dominant Terms Resolve Many Rational Forms

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty} \frac{4x^3+2x}{2x^3-5}. \]

The visible form is \(\infty/\infty\).

Divide by \(x^3\):

\[ \frac{4+\frac{2}{x^2}} {2-\frac{5}{x^3}}. \]

As \(x\to+\infty\), the reciprocal powers vanish.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

The Form \(\infty-\infty\)

A difference of two unbounded quantities is also indeterminate.

For example,

\[ \sqrt{x^2+x}-x \]

as \(x\to+\infty\) appears to have the form

\[ \infty-\infty. \]

The difference between two large quantities may approach a finite number, zero, infinity, or fail to settle.

Rationalization Can Resolve \(\infty-\infty\)

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty} \left(\sqrt{x^2+x}-x\right). \]

Multiply by the conjugate:

\[ \frac{\sqrt{x^2+x}-x}{1} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x^2+x}+x} {\sqrt{x^2+x}+x}. \]

The numerator becomes

\[ x^2+x-x^2=x. \]

So

\[ \sqrt{x^2+x}-x = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+x}+x}. \]

Finish the Rationalized Limit

Divide numerator and denominator by \(x\):

\[ \frac{1} {\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{x}}+1}. \]

As \(x\to+\infty\),

\[ \frac{1}{x}\to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to+\infty} \left(\sqrt{x^2+x}-x\right) = \frac{1}{2} }. \]

The Form \(0\cdot\infty\)

A product can also be indeterminate when one factor approaches \(0\) and another grows without bound.

For example,

\[ x\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \]

as \(x\to+\infty\) has the informal form

\[ \infty\cdot0. \]

But the product is exactly

\[ 1. \]

Rewrite Products as Quotients

A product of the form

\[ 0\cdot\infty \]

can often be rewritten as a quotient.

If

\[ f(x)g(x) \]

has one factor approaching zero, rewrite it as

\[ \frac{f(x)}{1/g(x)} \]

or

\[ \frac{g(x)}{1/f(x)}. \]

This can convert the product into a \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\) form that is easier to analyze.

Example: A Product with Competing Behavior

Consider

\[ x\cdot\frac{1}{x^2} \]

as \(x\to+\infty\).

One factor grows without bound and the other approaches zero.

But

\[ x\cdot\frac{1}{x^2} = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{0}. \]

The Form \(1^\infty\)

Exponential expressions can produce indeterminate forms too.

A familiar example is

\[ \left(1+\frac{1}{x}\right)^x \]

as \(x\to+\infty\).

The base approaches \(1\), while the exponent grows without bound.

The expression approaches the important constant

\[ \boxed{e}. \]

Why \(1^\infty\) Is Indeterminate

If a base approaches \(1\), it may seem natural to expect the whole expression to approach \(1\).

But a very large exponent can magnify tiny departures from \(1\).

The competition between the base and exponent determines the limit.

The Forms \(0^0\) and \(\infty^0\)

Two additional exponential indeterminate forms are

\[ \boxed{0^0} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\infty^0}. \]

In limit problems, these forms indicate that both the base and exponent are changing in ways that require further analysis.

Later logarithmic and derivative-based methods provide systematic tools for these cases.

The Standard Indeterminate Forms

The commonly encountered indeterminate forms are:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{0}{0}, \quad \frac{\infty}{\infty}, \quad \infty-\infty, \quad 0\cdot\infty, \quad 1^\infty, \quad 0^0, \quad \infty^0 }. \]

Each form is a diagnostic signal, not a final answer.

Not Every Undefined-Looking Form Is Indeterminate

Consider

\[ \frac{5}{0^+}. \]

A positive nonzero numerator divided by a positive quantity approaching zero grows toward

\[ +\infty. \]

This is not the same kind of ambiguity as \(0/0\).

Zero Divided by a Nonzero Number Is Determinate

If

\[ f(x)\to0 \]

and

\[ g(x)\to c\neq0, \]

then

\[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\to0. \]

The form

\[ \frac{0}{c} \]

is determinate.

Nonzero Divided by Zero Is Not \(0/0\)

If

\[ f(x)\to c\neq0 \]

and

\[ g(x)\to0, \]

then the quotient may grow without bound or may have different one-sided behavior.

Sign analysis is usually the next step.

Direct Substitution Is Still the First Check

When evaluating a limit, start by substituting the target whenever the expression permits.

If substitution gives an ordinary finite number, the limit is often settled immediately.

If it gives an indeterminate form, identify the form and choose an appropriate rewriting strategy.

Strategy 1: Factor and Cancel

Factoring is especially useful for polynomial quotients producing \(0/0\).

Example:

\[ \lim_{x\to3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}. \]

Factor:

\[ \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3}=x+3 \qquad(x\neq3). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{6}. \]

Strategy 2: Combine Fractions

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to0} \left( \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{x+1} \right). \]

Combine the fractions:

\[ \frac{x+1-x}{x(x+1)} = \frac{1}{x(x+1)}. \]

The rewritten expression makes the behavior clearer than the original difference.

Strategy 3: Rationalize

Radical expressions producing \(0/0\) or \(\infty-\infty\) often respond well to conjugates.

For

\[ \lim_{x\to4} \frac{\sqrt{x}-2}{x-4}, \]

multiply by

\[ \frac{\sqrt{x}+2}{\sqrt{x}+2}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{\sqrt{x}-2}{x-4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+2} \qquad(x\neq4), \]

so

\[ \boxed{\frac14}. \]

Strategy 4: Divide by the Dominant Power

For rational limits at infinity, divide numerator and denominator by the highest relevant power of \(x\).

Example:

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty} \frac{3x^2+1}{5x^2-4x}. \]

Divide by \(x^2\):

\[ \frac{3+\frac{1}{x^2}} {5-\frac{4}{x}}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\frac35}. \]

Strategy 5: Rewrite Products

Products involving \(0\cdot\infty\) are often easier after conversion to a quotient.

For example,

\[ x\left(\frac{1}{x^2}\right) = \frac{x}{x^2} = \frac{1}{x}. \]

The apparent competition is now easy to analyze.

Strategy 6: Use Known Limit Structures

Some indeterminate forms occur in standard patterns.

One important example is

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to+\infty} \left(1+\frac{1}{x}\right)^x=e }. \]

Recognizing established limit forms can save substantial algebra.

Indeterminate Forms Are About Rates

At a deeper level, indeterminate forms appear because two or more parts of an expression are approaching critical values at competing rates.

For example, in

\[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \]

with both \(f(x)\to0\) and \(g(x)\to0\), the answer depends on which quantity approaches zero faster.

Example: Compare Orders of Vanishing

Compare

\[ \frac{x}{x}, \qquad \frac{x^2}{x}, \qquad \frac{x}{x^2} \]

as \(x\to0^+\).

All begin with the form \(0/0\), but simplify to

\[ 1, \qquad x, \qquad \frac{1}{x}. \]

Their limits are

\[ 1, \qquad 0, \qquad +\infty. \]

The relative powers determine the outcome.

Example: Compare Orders of Growth

Compare

\[ \frac{x}{x}, \qquad \frac{x}{x^2}, \qquad \frac{x^2}{x} \]

as \(x\to+\infty\).

All have the form \(\infty/\infty\), but their limits are

\[ 1, \qquad 0, \qquad +\infty. \]

Again, the relative rates determine the result.

Numerical Investigation Can Reveal the Trend

If an algebraic path is not immediately obvious, a table can help reveal what the expression appears to be doing.

For example, near \(x=2\),

\[ \frac{x^2-4}{x-2} \]

produces values close to

\[ 4. \]

Numerical evidence does not replace exact reasoning, but it can guide it.

Graphs Can Distinguish Possible Behaviors

A graph can reveal whether an indeterminate expression is approaching:

  • a finite value;
  • zero;
  • a vertical asymptote;
  • different values from opposite sides;
  • an oscillating pattern.

Graphical evidence is especially useful when choosing the next algebraic strategy.

Common Error: Treating \(0/0\) as Zero

The expression

\[ \frac{0}{0} \]

is not equal to \(0\).

In a limit problem, it means the numerator and denominator are both vanishing and their relative behavior must be examined.

Common Error: Treating \(0/0\) as One

It may seem tempting to reason that “the same zero is divided by itself.”

But zeros arising in limits come from changing quantities, not one fixed common number.

Different rates of approach can produce different outcomes.

Common Error: Treating \(\infty/\infty\) as One

Two quantities can both grow without bound while growing at very different rates.

For example,

\[ \frac{x}{x^2}\to0 \]

while

\[ \frac{x^2}{x}\to+\infty. \]

So \(\infty/\infty\) cannot be simplified to \(1\).

Common Error: Cancelling Terms Instead of Factors

In

\[ \frac{x^2-4}{x-2}, \]

cancellation is possible only after factoring:

\[ \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}. \]

Terms separated by addition or subtraction cannot be cancelled directly.

Common Error: Stopping After Naming the Form

Writing

\[ \frac{0}{0} \]

or

\[ \frac{\infty}{\infty} \]

identifies the problem but does not solve it.

The next step must be algebraic, numerical, graphical, or later derivative-based analysis.

A Reliable Procedure

  1. Try direct substitution or direct end-behavior analysis.
  2. Identify the resulting form.
  3. Decide whether the form is determinate or indeterminate.
  4. If indeterminate, choose a rewriting strategy.
  5. Factor and cancel when polynomial structure permits.
  6. Rationalize radical differences when appropriate.
  7. Combine fractions if separate terms are competing.
  8. Divide by dominant powers for rational limits at infinity.
  9. Rewrite products as quotients when useful.
  10. Reevaluate the simplified expression.

Example: Full \(0/0\) Workflow

Evaluate

\[ \lim_{x\to5} \frac{x^2-25}{x-5}. \]

Direct substitution gives

\[ \frac{0}{0}. \]

Factor:

\[ x^2-25=(x-5)(x+5). \]

Cancel the common factor for \(x\neq5\):

\[ \frac{x^2-25}{x-5}=x+5. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{10}. \]

Example: Full \(\infty/\infty\) Workflow

Evaluate

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty} \frac{7x^4-2x}{3x^4+x^2+1}. \]

Both numerator and denominator grow without bound.

Divide by \(x^4\):

\[ \frac{7-\frac{2}{x^3}} {3+\frac{1}{x^2}+\frac{1}{x^4}}. \]

The reciprocal terms vanish, leaving

\[ \boxed{\frac73}. \]

Example: Full Radical Workflow

Evaluate

\[ \lim_{x\to9} \frac{\sqrt{x}-3}{x-9}. \]

Direct substitution gives \(0/0\).

Rationalize:

\[ \frac{\sqrt{x}-3}{x-9} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x}+3}{\sqrt{x}+3}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{x-9}{(x-9)(\sqrt{x}+3)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+3} \qquad(x\neq9). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\frac16}. \]

What to Remember

Indeterminate forms are warning signals:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{0}{0}, \quad \frac{\infty}{\infty}, \quad \infty-\infty, \quad 0\cdot\infty, \quad 1^\infty, \quad 0^0, \quad \infty^0 }. \]
  • An indeterminate form is not a final answer.
  • The same form can lead to different limits.
  • Factoring often resolves \(0/0\) polynomial quotients.
  • Rationalization is useful with radical differences.
  • Degree comparison resolves many \(\infty/\infty\) rational limits.
  • Products can often be rewritten as quotients.
  • Indeterminate forms are fundamentally about competing rates of change.

The central strategic habit is: identify the form, then rewrite the expression until the actual behavior becomes visible.
