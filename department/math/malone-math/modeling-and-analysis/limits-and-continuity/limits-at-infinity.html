Limits at Infinity

A limit at infinity describes the long-run behavior of a function as its input grows without bound in the positive or negative direction. Instead of asking what happens near one finite input, we ask what the function does farther and farther to the right or left. This viewpoint reveals end behavior, horizontal asymptotes, dominant terms, and the large-scale structure of many functions.

The Central Idea

A limit at infinity asks what happens to \(f(x)\) as \(x\) becomes arbitrarily large in magnitude.

To study the right end of a graph, we write

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)}. \]

To study the left end, we write

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)}. \]

These are statements about long-run behavior rather than behavior near one finite input.

Infinity Describes Direction, Not an Input Value

In

\[ x\to+\infty, \]

the variable \(x\) does not eventually arrive at a real number called infinity.

Instead, \(x\) is allowed to become larger than any chosen positive bound.

Likewise,

\[ x\to-\infty \]

means that \(x\) becomes more and more negative without bound.

Limits at Infinity Are About End Behavior

The phrase end behavior describes what a graph does far to the right and far to the left.

A function may:

  • approach a finite number;
  • grow toward \(+\infty\);
  • decrease toward \(-\infty\);
  • oscillate without settling;
  • behave differently in the two directions.

A First Example

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x}. \]

As \(x\) becomes very large and positive,

\[ \frac{1}{x} \]

becomes very small and positive.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{1}{x}=0}. \]

The Same Function to the Left

As \(x\) becomes very large in magnitude and negative,

\[ \frac{1}{x} \]

becomes very small and negative.

It still approaches \(0\).

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{1}{x}=0}. \]

Numerical Evidence

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x}, \]

a table illustrates the trend:

\(x\) \(\frac{1}{x}\)
\(10\) \(0.1\)
\(100\) \(0.01\)
\(1000\) \(0.001\)
\(-10\) \(-0.1\)
\(-100\) \(-0.01\)
\(-1000\) \(-0.001\)

The outputs approach \(0\) in both directions.

Horizontal Asymptotes

If

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)=L \]

or

\[ \lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=L, \]

then the horizontal line

\[ \boxed{y=L} \]

is a horizontal asymptote of the function in that direction.

A Function Can Cross a Horizontal Asymptote

A horizontal asymptote describes end behavior.

It does not create a barrier that the graph is forbidden to cross.

A function may cross the line \(y=L\) many times and still satisfy

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)=L. \]

Do Not Confuse Horizontal and Vertical Asymptotes

A vertical asymptote describes behavior near a finite input:

\[ x=a. \]

A horizontal asymptote describes behavior as the input moves without bound:

\[ y=L. \]

These are different geometric features produced by different kinds of limits.

Reciprocal Powers

For any positive integer \(n\),

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{1}{x^n}=0}. \]

The same is true as \(x\to-\infty\):

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{1}{x^n}=0}. \]

The sign may differ while approaching \(0\), but the limit itself is still \(0\).

Why Reciprocal Powers Go to Zero

As \(|x|\) becomes very large,

\[ |x|^n \]

becomes very large.

Therefore,

\[ \frac{1}{|x|^n} \]

becomes arbitrarily small.

This principle is one of the main tools for rational limits at infinity.

Polynomial End Behavior

For a polynomial, the highest-degree term eventually dominates the lower degree terms.

Consider

\[ f(x)=3x^4-7x^2+2x-10. \]

For very large \(|x|\), the term

\[ 3x^4 \]

has the greatest effect on the overall value.

Dominant-Term Reasoning

If

\[ f(x)=a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0, \]

then for large \(|x|\),

\[ f(x) \]

behaves approximately like

\[ a_nx^n. \]

This is why polynomial end behavior can usually be predicted from the leading term alone.

Even-Degree Polynomials

Suppose the leading term is

\[ 2x^4. \]

Since \(x^4\) is positive for both large positive and large negative \(x\),

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}2x^4=+\infty} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}2x^4=+\infty}. \]

Negative Even-Degree Leading Terms

For

\[ -5x^6, \]

the even power is positive but the coefficient is negative.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}-5x^6=-\infty} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}-5x^6=-\infty}. \]

Odd-Degree Polynomials

For the leading term

\[ 3x^5, \]

the sign changes between the two ends.

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}3x^5=+\infty} \]

while

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}3x^5=-\infty}. \]

Negative Odd-Degree Leading Terms

For

\[ -2x^3, \]

the directions reverse:

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}-2x^3=-\infty} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}-2x^3=+\infty}. \]

Rational Functions

A rational function has the form

\[ R(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}, \]

where \(P(x)\) and \(Q(x)\) are polynomials.

At infinity, the most important comparison is usually between the degrees of the numerator and denominator.

Case 1: Numerator Degree Less Than Denominator Degree

Consider

\[ \frac{3x+1}{x^2+5}. \]

The numerator has degree \(1\), while the denominator has degree \(2\).

The denominator grows faster.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{3x+1}{x^2+5}=0}. \]

Why the Larger Denominator Degree Wins

Divide the numerator and denominator by \(x^2\):

\[ \frac{3x+1}{x^2+5} = \frac{\frac{3}{x}+\frac{1}{x^2}} {1+\frac{5}{x^2}}. \]

As \(x\to\pm\infty\),

\[ \frac{1}{x}\to0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{1}{x^2}\to0. \]

Thus,

\[ \frac{0+0}{1+0}=0. \]

Case 2: Equal Degrees

Consider

\[ \frac{4x^2-3x+1}{2x^2+5x-7}. \]

Both numerator and denominator have degree \(2\).

For large \(|x|\), the function behaves like

\[ \frac{4x^2}{2x^2}=2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \frac{4x^2-3x+1}{2x^2+5x-7} =2 }. \]

Equal Degrees: Leading-Coefficient Rule

If the numerator and denominator have the same degree \(n\),

\[ \frac{a_nx^n+\cdots}{b_nx^n+\cdots}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \frac{a_nx^n+\cdots}{b_nx^n+\cdots} = \frac{a_n}{b_n} }. \]

The horizontal asymptote is

\[ \boxed{y=\frac{a_n}{b_n}}. \]

Case 3: Numerator Degree Greater Than Denominator Degree

Consider

\[ \frac{x^3+1}{x^2+4}. \]

The numerator grows faster than the denominator.

For large \(|x|\), the function behaves roughly like

\[ \frac{x^3}{x^2}=x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{x^3+1}{x^2+4}=+\infty} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{x^3+1}{x^2+4}=-\infty}. \]

No Finite Horizontal Asymptote in the Higher-Numerator Case

If the numerator degree exceeds the denominator degree, the rational function does not approach a finite constant simply because of degree balance.

It may instead grow without bound or approach a nonhorizontal asymptotic shape.

Polynomial division can reveal that larger-scale structure.

The Degree Comparison Shortcut

For

\[ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}, \]

compare

\[ \deg P \qquad\text{and}\qquad \deg Q. \]
  • If \(\deg P<\deg Q\), the limit is \(0\).
  • If \(\deg P=\deg Q\), use the ratio of leading coefficients.
  • If \(\deg P>\deg Q\), inspect the dominant quotient or divide the polynomials.

Why Dividing by the Highest Power Works

Consider

\[ \frac{5x^3-2x}{2x^3+x^2+1}. \]

Divide every term by \(x^3\):

\[ \frac{5-\frac{2}{x^2}} {2+\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{x^3}}. \]

As \(x\to\pm\infty\), every reciprocal power tends to \(0\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \frac{5x^3-2x}{2x^3+x^2+1} = \frac{5}{2} }. \]

Example: Lower Numerator Degree

Evaluate

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{7x^2+3}{2x^5-x}. \]

Divide by \(x^5\):

\[ \frac{\frac{7}{x^3}+\frac{3}{x^5}} {2-\frac{1}{x^4}}. \]

Every reciprocal power approaches \(0\), so

\[ \boxed{0}. \]

Example: Equal Degrees with Negative Coefficients

Evaluate

\[ \lim_{x\to-\infty} \frac{-6x^4+2x}{3x^4-x^2+8}. \]

The degrees are equal, so use the leading coefficients:

\[ \boxed{\frac{-6}{3}=-2}. \]

Example: One Degree Higher in the Numerator

Consider

\[ \frac{2x^2+1}{x-3}. \]

The dominant quotient is

\[ \frac{2x^2}{x}=2x. \]

Thus,

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{2x^2+1}{x-3}=+\infty \]

and

\[ \lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{2x^2+1}{x-3}=-\infty. \]

Polynomial Division Can Reveal Slant Behavior

For the function

\[ \frac{x^2+1}{x}, \]

division gives

\[ x+\frac{1}{x}. \]

Since

\[ \frac{1}{x}\to0, \]

the graph becomes increasingly close to the line

\[ y=x. \]

This is an example of nonhorizontal asymptotic behavior.

Radical Functions at Infinity

Radicals require attention to both dominant terms and signs.

Consider

\[ \sqrt{x^2+1}. \]

Factor \(x^2\) inside the radical:

\[ \sqrt{x^2\left(1+\frac{1}{x^2}\right)} = |x|\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{x^2}}. \]

The absolute value is essential because

\[ \sqrt{x^2}=|x|. \]

Right-End Radical Behavior

As \(x\to+\infty\),

\[ |x|=x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \sqrt{x^2+1} = x\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{x^2}}. \]

Since the square-root factor approaches \(1\),

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}\sqrt{x^2+1}=+\infty}. \]

Left-End Radical Behavior

As \(x\to-\infty\),

\[ |x|=-x. \]

Thus,

\[ \sqrt{x^2+1} = -x\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{x^2}}. \]

Because \(-x\to+\infty\),

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}\sqrt{x^2+1}=+\infty}. \]

A Useful Radical Difference

Consider

\[ \sqrt{x^2+1}-x \]

as \(x\to+\infty\).

Direct comparison gives an expression of the form

\[ \infty-\infty, \]

which does not determine the answer.

Rationalize:

\[ \sqrt{x^2+1}-x = \frac{1} {\sqrt{x^2+1}+x}. \]

The denominator grows without bound, so

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to+\infty} \left(\sqrt{x^2+1}-x\right) =0 }. \]

Exponential Growth at Infinity

For a base

\[ b>1, \]

exponential functions grow without bound:

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}b^x=+\infty}. \]

In the opposite direction,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}b^x=0}. \]

Exponential Decay

If

\[ 0 then the directions reverse:

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}b^x=0} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}b^x=+\infty}. \]

Logarithmic Growth

For the natural logarithm,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}\ln x=+\infty}. \]

The growth is unbounded but much slower than polynomial and exponential growth.

The real logarithm is defined only for

\[ x>0, \]

so there is no corresponding real limit as \(x\to-\infty\).

Growth Rates Matter

Many limits at infinity are governed by which part of an expression grows faster.

A useful long-run hierarchy is:

\[ \text{logarithmic} \;<\; \text{power} \;<\; \text{exponential} \]

in the sense that, under suitable positive-domain conditions, exponentials eventually outgrow powers, and powers eventually outgrow logarithms.

Example: Exponential Dominates Polynomial

Consider

\[ \frac{x^3}{2^x}. \]

The exponential denominator eventually grows much faster than the polynomial numerator.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{x^3}{2^x}=0}. \]

Later analysis methods will provide systematic proofs of limits like this.

Graphical Interpretation

To estimate a limit at infinity from a graph, follow the curve farther and farther to the right or left.

Ask whether the graph:

  • levels off toward a horizontal line;
  • rises without bound;
  • falls without bound;
  • approaches a slanted or curved path;
  • continues oscillating.

Numerical Tables for End Behavior

A useful numerical table should use increasingly large magnitudes.

For \(x\to+\infty\), sample values such as

\[ 10,\quad100,\quad1000,\quad10{,}000. \]

For \(x\to-\infty\), use

\[ -10,\quad-100,\quad-1000,\quad-10{,}000. \]

Random nearby values are less useful for long-run analysis.

A Function Can Have Different End Limits

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}. \]

For large positive \(x\),

\[ f(x)\to1. \]

For large negative \(x\),

\[ f(x)\to-1. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}=1} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}=-1}. \]

Why the Absolute Value Matters Again

Rewrite

\[ \sqrt{x^2+1} = |x|\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{x^2}}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}} = \frac{x}{|x|} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{x^2}}}. \]

The factor

\[ \frac{x}{|x|} \]

equals \(1\) for positive \(x\) and \(-1\) for negative \(x\).

That sign difference controls the two end limits.

Some Functions Have No Limit at Infinity

Consider

\[ f(x)=\sin x. \]

As \(x\to+\infty\), the function continues oscillating between

\[ -1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 1. \]

It does not settle toward one finite value or grow consistently toward one infinite direction.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}\sin x\text{ does not exist}}. \]

Bounded Does Not Automatically Mean Convergent

The function

\[ \sin x \]

remains bounded:

\[ -1\le\sin x\le1. \]

But boundedness alone does not force a limit to exist.

A limit requires the function values to approach one common target.

A Reliable Procedure for Rational Limits at Infinity

  1. Identify the highest degree in the numerator.
  2. Identify the highest degree in the denominator.
  3. Compare the degrees.
  4. If helpful, divide every term by the highest relevant power of \(x\).
  5. Use \(\frac{1}{x^n}\to0\) for positive integers \(n\).
  6. Track signs separately for \(x\to+\infty\) and \(x\to-\infty\).
  7. Interpret any finite constant as a horizontal asymptote.

A Reliable Procedure for General Limits at Infinity

  1. Identify the direction: \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\).
  2. Look for the dominant part of the expression.
  3. Factor or divide by the dominant power when appropriate.
  4. Use reciprocal-power limits to eliminate vanishing terms.
  5. Check for absolute values when square roots of even powers appear.
  6. Use known growth-rate comparisons when exponentials or logarithms appear.
  7. Confirm the result graphically or numerically when useful.

Example: Full Rational Workflow

Evaluate

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty} \frac{6x^3-4x+1}{3x^3+2x^2-5}. \]

The numerator and denominator both have degree \(3\).

Divide by \(x^3\):

\[ \frac{6-\frac{4}{x^2}+\frac{1}{x^3}} {3+\frac{2}{x}-\frac{5}{x^3}}. \]

Every reciprocal-power term approaches \(0\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

The graph has the horizontal asymptote

\[ \boxed{y=2}. \]

Example: Different Degrees

Evaluate

\[ \lim_{x\to-\infty} \frac{5x^2+1}{x^4+3}. \]

The denominator degree is larger.

Divide by \(x^4\):

\[ \frac{\frac{5}{x^2}+\frac{1}{x^4}} {1+\frac{3}{x^4}}. \]

Every reciprocal-power term tends to \(0\), so

\[ \boxed{0}. \]

Example: Polynomial End Behavior

Evaluate

\[ \lim_{x\to-\infty} \left(-4x^5+2x^2-7\right). \]

The dominant term is

\[ -4x^5. \]

As \(x\to-\infty\),

\[ x^5\to-\infty, \]

so multiplying by \(-4\) gives

\[ \boxed{+\infty}. \]

Example: Radical Ratio

Evaluate

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty} \frac{3x}{\sqrt{9x^2+4}}. \]

Factor \(x^2\) inside the radical:

\[ \sqrt{9x^2+4} = x\sqrt{9+\frac{4}{x^2}} \]

because \(x>0\) for sufficiently large positive \(x\).

Therefore,

\[ \frac{3x}{\sqrt{9x^2+4}} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{9+\frac{4}{x^2}}} \to \frac{3}{3} = \boxed{1}. \]

Common Error: Substituting Infinity as a Number

Expressions such as

\[ \frac{1}{\infty} \]

are informal shorthand, not ordinary arithmetic.

The precise statement is

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{1}{x}=0. \]

Use limit reasoning rather than treating infinity as a real input.

Common Error: Comparing Coefficients Before Degrees

In

\[ \frac{1000x}{x^2}, \]

the large coefficient \(1000\) does not control the end behavior.

The denominator has the larger degree:

\[ \frac{1000x}{x^2}=\frac{1000}{x}\to0. \]

Degree growth eventually dominates fixed coefficients.

Common Error: Using the Leading-Coefficient Ratio for Unequal Degrees

The ratio of leading coefficients applies directly only when the numerator and denominator have the same degree.

For

\[ \frac{3x^2}{5x^4}, \]

the answer is not

\[ \frac{3}{5}. \]

Instead,

\[ \frac{3x^2}{5x^4} = \frac{3}{5x^2} \to0. \]

Common Error: Forgetting the Left-End Sign

Odd powers change sign when \(x\) becomes negative.

For example,

\[ x^3\to-\infty \]

as

\[ x\to-\infty. \]

The left end of a graph must therefore be analyzed separately from the right when odd powers are involved.

Common Error: Replacing \(\sqrt{x^2}\) with \(x\)

The correct identity is

\[ \boxed{\sqrt{x^2}=|x|}. \]

When \(x\to-\infty\),

\[ |x|=-x. \]

Forgetting the absolute value can reverse the sign of a left-end limit.

Common Error: Assuming Bounded Oscillation Has a Limit

A function can remain within a fixed range and still fail to approach one value.

The standard example is

\[ \sin x. \]

Boundedness and convergence are different properties.

What to Remember

Limits at infinity describe long-run behavior:

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)}. \]
  • Reciprocal powers \(\frac{1}{x^n}\) approach \(0\).
  • Polynomial end behavior is governed by the leading term.
  • For rational functions, compare numerator and denominator degrees.
  • Equal rational degrees produce the ratio of leading coefficients.
  • A finite limit at infinity produces a horizontal asymptote.
  • Square roots of \(x^2\) require \(|x|\), especially as \(x\to-\infty\).
  • Bounded oscillation does not guarantee a limit.
  • Different ends of the same function may have different limits.

The central strategic question is: which part of the expression controls the function when \(|x|\) becomes very large?

Where We Go Next
