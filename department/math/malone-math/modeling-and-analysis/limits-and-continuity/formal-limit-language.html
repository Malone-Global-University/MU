Formal Limit Language

Informally, a limit says that function values can be made as close as we want to a target value by taking the input sufficiently close to a target input. Formal limit language turns that idea into a precise mathematical statement. The epsilon-delta definition does not replace numerical, graphical, or symbolic intuition; it explains exactly what those ideas mean and provides a rigorous foundation for later analysis.

The Central Idea

The statement

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L \]

means that \(f(x)\) can be forced arbitrarily close to \(L\) by requiring \(x\) to be sufficiently close to \(a\), without requiring \(x=a\).

The formal definition measures these two kinds of closeness with two positive numbers:

  • \(\varepsilon\) measures how close \(f(x)\) must be to \(L\);
  • \(\delta\) measures how close \(x\) must be to \(a\).

Distance on the Number Line

The distance between two real numbers \(x\) and \(a\) is

\[ |x-a|. \]

Therefore,

\[ |x-a|<\delta \]

means that \(x\) lies within a distance \(\delta\) of \(a\).

Output Distance

Likewise,

\[ |f(x)-L| \]

measures the distance between the function value \(f(x)\) and the proposed limit \(L\).

The inequality

\[ |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon \]

means that the output lies within \(\varepsilon\) of \(L\).

The Formal Epsilon-Delta Definition

We say

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L} \]

if, for every

\[ \varepsilon>0, \]

there exists a

\[ \delta>0 \]

such that whenever

\[ 0<|x-a|<\delta, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon}. \]

Read the Definition in Words

The definition can be read as:

For every desired output tolerance around \(L\), we can find an input tolerance around \(a\) that guarantees the output stays inside the desired range.

Why \(\varepsilon\) Comes First

The definition begins with

\[ \text{for every }\varepsilon>0. \]

This means the output requirement may be arbitrarily strict.

Someone can demand

\[ \varepsilon=1, \qquad 0.1, \qquad 0.001, \]

or any other positive tolerance.

We must then produce an appropriate \(\delta\).

Why \(\delta\) Depends on \(\varepsilon\)

A smaller allowed output error usually requires the input to be closer to the target.

Therefore, \(\delta\) is often chosen as a function of \(\varepsilon\).

The logical order is:

\[ \varepsilon \longrightarrow \delta \longrightarrow \text{guaranteed output closeness}. \]

The Meaning of \(0<|x-a|\)

The condition

\[ 0<|x-a| \]

excludes the point

\[ x=a. \]

This reflects the fact that a limit concerns nearby behavior and does not require the function to be defined at the target itself.

Punctured Neighborhoods

The condition

\[ 0<|x-a|<\delta \]

describes a punctured neighborhood around \(a\).

It includes all points sufficiently close to \(a\) while excluding \(a\) itself.

The inequality

\[ |x-a|<\delta \]

is equivalent to

\[ 0<|x-a|<\delta \]

corresponds to

\[ (a-\delta,a)\cup(a,a+\delta). \]

The inequality

\[ |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon \]

\[ L-\varepsilon So the formal definition says that sufficiently nearby inputs must produce outputs inside any requested band around \(L\).

A Simple Linear Example

Prove that

\[ \lim_{x\to2}(3x+1)=7. \]

We want to make

\[ |(3x+1)-7| \]

smaller than \(\varepsilon\).

Simplify the Output Distance

We have

\[ |(3x+1)-7| = |3x-6| = 3|x-2|. \]

Therefore, to guarantee

\[ 3|x-2|<\varepsilon, \]

it is enough to require

\[ |x-2|<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}. \]

Choose \(\delta\)

Choose

\[ \boxed{\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}}. \]

Then

\[ 0<|x-2|<\delta \]

implies

\[ |(3x+1)-7| = 3|x-2| < 3\delta = \varepsilon. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to2}(3x+1)=7}. \]

The Proof Has Two Phases

Epsilon-delta proofs are often easiest to understand as two stages.

  1. Discovery: work backward to determine a useful choice of \(\delta\).
  2. Verification: start from \(0<|x-a|<\delta\) and prove \(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\).

Discovery versus Presentation

During discovery, it is natural to start with

\[ |f(x)-L| \]

and manipulate it until it is controlled by

\[ |x-a|. \]

In the final proof, however, we usually state the chosen \(\delta\) first and then verify that it works.

Another Linear Example

Prove

\[ \lim_{x\to4}(2x-5)=3. \]

Compute

\[ |(2x-5)-3| = |2x-8| = 2|x-4|. \]

Therefore, choosing

\[ \boxed{\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}} \]

is sufficient.

Constant Functions

Consider

\[ f(x)=c. \]

To prove

\[ \lim_{x\to a}c=c, \]

observe that

\[ |f(x)-c| = |c-c| = 0. \]

Since

\[ 0<\varepsilon \]

for every \(\varepsilon>0\), any positive \(\delta\) works.

Why the Minimum Function Appears

A proof may require several conditions on \(\delta\).

Choosing

\[ \delta=\min\{\delta_1,\delta_2\} \]

guarantees both

\[ |x-a|<\delta_1 \]

and

\[ |x-a|<\delta_2. \]

This is a standard technique in rigorous analysis.

The Choice of \(\delta\) Is Usually Not Unique

A valid epsilon-delta proof does not require the largest possible \(\delta\).

Any positive choice that guarantees the desired implication is acceptable.

Simpler choices are often preferable to optimal ones.

The Logical Structure Matters

The formal statement has the structure

\[ \boxed{ \forall\varepsilon>0, \ \exists\delta>0 \text{ such that } 0<|x-a|<\delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon }. \]

The order of

\[ \forall \qquad\text{and}\qquad \exists \]

is essential.

Quantifiers

The symbol

\[ \forall \]

means “for every.”

The symbol

\[ \exists \]

means “there exists.”

Therefore,

\[ \forall\varepsilon>0,\exists\delta>0 \]

means: for every positive epsilon, there exists a positive delta.

Why the Quantifier Order Cannot Be Reversed

The statement

\[ \forall\varepsilon>0,\exists\delta>0 \]

allows \(\delta\) to depend on \(\varepsilon\).

Reversing the order to

\[ \exists\delta>0,\forall\varepsilon>0 \]

would demand one fixed \(\delta\) that works for every possible output tolerance, which is generally far too strong.

Implication Language

The symbol

\[ \Rightarrow \]

means “implies.”

In a limit proof,

\[ 0<|x-a|<\delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon \]

says that input closeness guarantees output closeness.

The Definition Does Not Say \(x\) Equals \(a\)

The condition

\[ 0<|x-a|<\delta \]

explicitly requires

\[ x\neq a. \]

This is one of the most important distinctions between a limit and a function value.

The Definition Does Not Require \(f(a)=L\)

A limit can exist even if

\[ f(a) \]

is undefined or has a different value.

The formal definition only controls

\[ f(x) \]

for nearby inputs with \(x\neq a\).

Formal Language for Continuity

Continuity at \(x=a\) requires

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a). \]

In epsilon-delta language, this means: for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that

\[ |x-a|<\delta \]

implies

\[ |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon. \]

Because continuity includes the point itself, the puncture is no longer essential in the same way.

Two-Sided Limits from One-Sided Limits

Formally, the two-sided limit

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L \]

holds if and only if

\[ \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=L \]

and

\[ \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=L. \]

Formal Language for Infinite Limits

To say

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=+\infty \]

means that \(f(x)\) can be made larger than any chosen positive bound by taking \(x\) sufficiently close to \(a\).

More formally, for every

\[ M>0, \]

there exists

\[ \delta>0 \]

such that

\[ 0<|x-a|<\delta \]

implies

\[ \boxed{f(x)>M}. \]

Formal Language for Negative Infinite Limits

To say

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=-\infty \]

means that for every

\[ M>0, \]

there exists

\[ \delta>0 \]

such that

\[ 0<|x-a|<\delta \]

implies

\[ \boxed{f(x)<-M}. \]

Formal Language for Limits at Infinity

To say

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)=L \]

means that outputs can be made arbitrarily close to \(L\) by taking \(x\) sufficiently large.

Formally, for every

\[ \varepsilon>0, \]

there exists

\[ N>0 \]

such that

\[ x>N \]

implies

\[ \boxed{|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon}. \]

Limits as \(x\to-\infty\)

To say

\[ \lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=L \]

means that for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(N>0\) such that

\[ x<-N \]

implies

\[ |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon. \]

A Formal Example at Infinity

Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac1x=0. \]

We want

\[ \left|\frac1x-0\right| = \frac1x < \varepsilon. \]

This holds whenever

\[ x>\frac1\varepsilon. \]

Therefore, choose

\[ \boxed{N=\frac1\varepsilon}. \]

Formal Proof versus Computation

A formal proof is different from simply computing a limit.

Computation asks:

What is the limit?

A formal proof asks:

Why does the function satisfy the exact definition of that limit?

Formal Proofs Validate the Informal Picture

Tables and graphs suggest behavior.

Algebra often computes the candidate limit.

Epsilon-delta reasoning verifies that the candidate satisfies the rigorous definition.

A Reliable Procedure for Linear Epsilon-Delta Proofs

  1. Start with \(|f(x)-L|\).
  2. Simplify until it becomes a constant multiple of \(|x-a|\).
  3. Solve the inequality for \(|x-a|\).
  4. Choose \(\delta\) in terms of \(\varepsilon\).
  5. Begin the formal proof with an arbitrary \(\varepsilon>0\).
  6. State the chosen \(\delta\).
  7. Assume \(0<|x-a|<\delta\).
  8. Verify that \(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\).

A Reliable Procedure for Nonlinear Proofs

  1. Factor or otherwise rewrite \(|f(x)-L|\).
  2. Identify any extra factors not directly controlled by \(|x-a|\).
  3. Impose a simple preliminary restriction such as \(|x-a|<1\).
  4. Use that restriction to bound the extra factors.
  5. Determine the epsilon-dependent restriction.
  6. Choose \(\delta\) as the minimum of all required bounds.
  7. Verify the chain of inequalities carefully.

Common Error: Choosing \(\varepsilon\) Yourself

In a formal proof, \(\varepsilon\) is arbitrary.

You do not get to choose a convenient value such as

\[ \varepsilon=0.1. \]

The proof must work for every positive \(\varepsilon\).

Common Error: Letting \(\delta\) Depend on \(x\)

The choice of \(\delta\) may depend on \(\varepsilon\), \(a\), and fixed features of the function.

It should not depend on the particular nearby \(x\) chosen after the \(\delta\)-neighborhood is established.

Common Error: Forgetting the Puncture

For an ordinary limit,

\[ 0<|x-a|<\delta \]

excludes \(x=a\).

Omitting the initial \(0<\) can blur the distinction between limits and continuity.

Common Error: Reversing the Quantifiers

The correct order is

\[ \forall\varepsilon>0,\exists\delta>0. \]

The demanded output tolerance comes first; the input tolerance is chosen in response.

Common Error: Showing Only a Candidate \(\delta\)

Finding a plausible formula for \(\delta\) is only the discovery stage.

A proof must also verify that the choice actually implies

\[ |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon. \]

Common Error: Treating the Definition as a Formula to Memorize

The epsilon-delta definition is a logical guarantee, not merely a string of symbols.

Its meaning is: every desired output precision can be achieved through sufficient input precision.

What to Remember

The formal definition of

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L \]

is

\[ \boxed{ \forall\varepsilon>0, \ \exists\delta>0 \text{ such that } 0<|x-a|<\delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon }. \]
  • \(\varepsilon\) measures output tolerance.
  • \(\delta\) measures input tolerance.
  • The target input itself is excluded in an ordinary limit.
  • \(\delta\) may depend on \(\varepsilon\).
  • The order of the quantifiers matters.
  • Linear proofs usually give \(\delta\) directly.
  • Nonlinear proofs often require auxiliary bounds and a minimum choice.
  • Formal definitions extend naturally to one-sided, infinite, and infinite-input limits.

The central meaning is: every desired level of output accuracy can be guaranteed by taking the input sufficiently close to the target.
