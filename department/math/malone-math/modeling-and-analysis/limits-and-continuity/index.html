5.3 Limits and Continuity

Limits describe what a function approaches, while continuity asks whether that approaching behavior agrees with the function itself. This section develops the idea from numerical, graphical, symbolic, asymptotic, formal, and applied perspectives, building the foundation needed for derivatives and the broader study of continuous change.

Section Overview

Limits are one of the central organizing ideas of mathematical analysis. They let us describe what happens near a point even when a function is undefined there, compare behavior from different directions, analyze unbounded growth, understand long-run behavior, and define continuity precisely.

The lessons in this section move from intuitive approaches to increasingly rigorous ones. Numerical tables and graphs come first, followed by one-sided and infinite limits, limit laws, comparison methods, asymptotic reasoning, formal epsilon-delta language, and applications.

Core Relationship

The central limit statement is

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L. \]

It says that the values of \(f(x)\) approach \(L\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\).

Continuity connects that limiting behavior to the actual function value:

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)}. \]

Much of this section develops the tools needed to interpret, evaluate, and apply these two statements.

Lessons

  1. Numerical, Graphical, and Symbolic Limits

    Introduces limits through tables, graphs, formulas, direct substitution, removable holes, and the idea that a limit describes nearby behavior rather than necessarily the value at the target.

  2. One-Sided and Infinite Limits

    Separates left-hand and right-hand behavior, explains when a two-sided limit exists, and develops infinite limits and vertical asymptotes.

  3. Limits at Infinity

    Studies end behavior as \(x\to+\infty\) or \(x\to-\infty\), including dominant terms, rational functions, horizontal asymptotes, radicals, exponentials, logarithms, and oscillation.

  4. Indeterminate Forms

    Explains why forms such as \(0/0\), \(\infty/\infty\), and \(\infty-\infty\) are signals for further analysis rather than final answers.

  5. Continuity at a Point and on an Interval

    Develops the three conditions for continuity, continuity on open and closed intervals, endpoint behavior, piecewise continuity, and continuous extensions.

  6. Types of Discontinuity

    Classifies removable, jump, infinite, and oscillatory discontinuities using one-sided limits, function values, algebraic structure, and graph behavior.

  7. Limit Laws

    Builds a systematic algebra of limits for constants, sums, differences, constant multiples, products, quotients, powers, roots, absolute values, and compositions.

  8. The Squeeze Theorem

    Shows how difficult or oscillatory functions can be trapped between simpler bounds that approach the same value.

  9. Asymptotic Behavior

    Develops dominant-term reasoning, horizontal and vertical asymptotes, slant asymptotes, relative growth, asymptotic equivalence, and approximation.

  10. Formal Limit Language

    Introduces neighborhoods, punctured neighborhoods, quantifiers, epsilon-delta reasoning, formal one-sided limits, infinite limits, and limits at infinity.

  11. Continuity in Applications

    Connects continuity to physical systems, engineering, measurement, thresholds, economics, biology, interpolation, numerical methods, modeling assumptions, and existence arguments.

Learning Path

The section is designed to be read in order.

The first lessons establish the basic meaning of approach and distinguish finite, one-sided, infinite, and long-run behavior. The middle lessons organize limit calculations and continuity failures. The final lessons move toward comparison, asymptotic reasoning, formal proof language, and modeling.

A useful conceptual progression is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{approach} \rightarrow \text{one-sided behavior} \rightarrow \text{limit calculation} \rightarrow \text{continuity} \rightarrow \text{asymptotics} \rightarrow \text{formal reasoning} }. \]

Key Questions

By the end of this section, you should be able to answer questions such as:

  • What value does a function approach near a given input?
  • Do the left-hand and right-hand behaviors agree?
  • Does a function grow without bound near a finite point?
  • What happens to a function as the input becomes very large?
  • When can direct substitution be used?
  • What does an indeterminate form tell us?
  • Is a function continuous at a point or throughout an interval?
  • What kind of discontinuity occurs when continuity fails?
  • Which algebraic laws preserve limits?
  • Can comparison or squeezing determine a difficult limit?
  • Which terms dominate asymptotic behavior?
  • What does a limit statement mean formally?
  • When is continuity an appropriate assumption in an applied model?

What to Remember

Limits provide a language for describing behavior near points and at large scales. Continuity uses limits to determine whether the function fits together without a break.

The central relationship is

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)} \]

whenever \(f\) is continuous at \(a\).

The tools developed throughout this section—one-sided limits, infinite limits, limit laws, the Squeeze Theorem, asymptotic reasoning, and formal limit language—form the foundation for the analysis of rates of change.

Where We Go Next

Limits describe what happens as inputs approach a target. The next stage of analysis uses limits to define instantaneous change.

This leads naturally to derivatives, local rates of change, tangent lines, and differential reasoning.
