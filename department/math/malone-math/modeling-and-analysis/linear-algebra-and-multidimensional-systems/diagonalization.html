Diagonalization

Diagonalization rewrites a linear transformation in a basis made from its eigenvectors. In that special coordinate system, the transformation acts by independent scaling along coordinate directions, so the matrix becomes diagonal. This converts a complicated matrix into a simpler form while preserving the underlying transformation and makes repeated matrix operations dramatically easier.

The Central Idea

A square matrix \(A\) is diagonalizable when there exists an invertible matrix \(P\) and a diagonal matrix \(D\) such that

\[ \boxed{A=PDP^{-1}}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{P^{-1}AP=D}. \]

The columns of \(P\) are eigenvectors of \(A\), and the diagonal entries of \(D\) are the corresponding eigenvalues.

Why Diagonal Matrices Are Special

A diagonal matrix has the form

\[ D= \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1&0&\cdots&0\\ 0&\lambda_2&\cdots&0\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ 0&0&\cdots&\lambda_n \end{bmatrix}. \]

It scales each coordinate direction independently:

\[ D \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ \vdots\\ x_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1x_1\\ \vdots\\ \lambda_nx_n \end{bmatrix}. \]

There is no mixing between coordinates.

Diagonalization Is a Change of Basis

The matrix \(A\) may look complicated in the standard basis.

If the space has a basis of eigenvectors, then using that eigenbasis as the coordinate system changes the matrix representation into a diagonal matrix.

Thus diagonalization is fundamentally a basis-selection problem.

Eigenvectors Become Coordinate Axes

Suppose

\[ Av_i=\lambda_i v_i. \]

If the eigenvectors

\[ v_1,\ldots,v_n \]

form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\), then every vector can be written uniquely as

\[ x=c_1v_1+\cdots+c_nv_n. \]

Applying \(A\) gives

\[ Ax=c_1\lambda_1v_1+\cdots+c_n\lambda_nv_n. \]

In the eigenbasis, the transformation simply scales each coordinate.

Constructing the Matrix \(P\)

Let

\[ v_1,\ldots,v_n \]

be linearly independent eigenvectors of \(A\).

Form

\[ \boxed{ P= \begin{bmatrix} \vert&&\vert\\ v_1&\cdots&v_n\\ \vert&&\vert \end{bmatrix}}. \]

Because the eigenvectors are independent, \(P\) is invertible.

Constructing the Matrix \(D\)

Place the eigenvalues in the same order as their corresponding eigenvectors:

\[ \boxed{ D= \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1&0&\cdots&0\\ 0&\lambda_2&\cdots&0\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ 0&0&\cdots&\lambda_n \end{bmatrix}}. \]

The ordering matters.

If \(v_j\) is column \(j\) of \(P\), then \(\lambda_j\) must occupy position \(j\) on the diagonal of \(D\).

Why \(AP=PD\)

Multiplying \(A\) by \(P\) applies \(A\) to every column:

\[ AP= \begin{bmatrix} Av_1&\cdots&Av_n \end{bmatrix}. \]

Since each \(v_i\) is an eigenvector,

\[ AP= \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1v_1&\cdots&\lambda_nv_n \end{bmatrix}. \]

But right-multiplying \(P\) by \(D\) scales column \(i\) by \(\lambda_i\), so

\[ \boxed{AP=PD}. \]

Deriving \(A=PDP^{-1}\)

Starting from

\[ AP=PD, \]

multiply on the right by \(P^{-1}\):

\[ APP^{-1}=PDP^{-1}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A=PDP^{-1}}. \]

Equivalent Form \(P^{-1}AP=D\)

Starting from

\[ A=PDP^{-1}, \]

multiply on the left by \(P^{-1}\) and on the right by \(P\):

\[ \boxed{P^{-1}AP=D}. \]

This shows directly that \(D\) is the matrix representation of the same transformation in the eigenvector basis.

Example: A Simple Diagonalizable Matrix

Consider

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} 2&1\\ 0&3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Its eigenvalues are

\[ \lambda_1=2, \qquad \lambda_2=3. \]

Corresponding eigenvectors may be chosen as

\[ v_1= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad v_2= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Build \(P\) and \(D\)

Place the eigenvectors into \(P\):

\[ P= \begin{bmatrix} 1&1\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Place the corresponding eigenvalues into \(D\):

\[ D= \begin{bmatrix} 2&0\\ 0&3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{A=PDP^{-1}}. \]

When Is a Matrix Diagonalizable?

An \(n\times n\) matrix is diagonalizable exactly when it has

\[ \boxed{n\text{ linearly independent eigenvectors}}. \]

Equivalently, the vector space must possess a basis consisting entirely of eigenvectors of \(A\).

Distinct Eigenvalues Give Independence

Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent.

Therefore, if an \(n\times n\) matrix has \(n\) distinct eigenvalues, then it automatically has \(n\) independent eigenvectors.

Hence

\[ \boxed{ n\text{ distinct eigenvalues} \Longrightarrow A\text{ is diagonalizable}}. \]

Distinct Eigenvalues Are Sufficient, Not Necessary

A matrix can still be diagonalizable when some eigenvalues repeat.

The real issue is not whether eigenvalues repeat, but whether enough independent eigenvectors exist.

Repeated eigenvalues require closer inspection of their eigenspaces.

Algebraic Multiplicity

The algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue is its multiplicity as a root of the characteristic polynomial

\[ \det(A-\lambda I)=0. \]

For example, if

\[ (\lambda-2)^3(\lambda+1)=0, \]

then \(\lambda=2\) has algebraic multiplicity \(3\).

Geometric Multiplicity

The geometric multiplicity of an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is the dimension of its eigenspace:

\[ \boxed{ \dim\ker(A-\lambda I)}. \]

It counts how many linearly independent eigenvectors are available for that eigenvalue.

Multiplicity Bounds

For every eigenvalue,

\[ \boxed{ 1 \le \text{geometric multiplicity} \le \text{algebraic multiplicity}}. \]

A repeated eigenvalue may therefore contribute one independent eigenvector or several.

Diagonalizability with Repeated Eigenvalues

A matrix is diagonalizable when the total number of independent eigenvectors supplied by all eigenspaces equals \(n\).

Equivalently, for each eigenvalue, its eigenspace must be large enough so that the geometric multiplicities collectively sum to \(n\).

Example: Repeated but Diagonalizable

Consider

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} 2&0\\ 0&2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The only eigenvalue is

\[ \lambda=2, \]

with algebraic multiplicity \(2\).

But every nonzero vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is an eigenvector, so the eigenspace has dimension \(2\).

Therefore \(A\) is diagonalizable.

Example: Repeated and Not Diagonalizable

Consider

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} 2&1\\ 0&2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The only eigenvalue is

\[ \lambda=2 \]

with algebraic multiplicity \(2\).

However,

\[ A-2I= \begin{bmatrix} 0&1\\ 0&0 \end{bmatrix}, \]

whose null space is one-dimensional.

Only one independent eigenvector exists, so the matrix is not diagonalizable.

Defective Matrices

A square matrix that does not have enough independent eigenvectors to form a basis is sometimes called defective.

A defective matrix cannot be diagonalized.

More advanced methods replace diagonal form with structures such as Jordan form.

Diagonal Matrices Are Already Diagonalized

If \(A\) is already diagonal, we may simply choose

\[ P=I \]

and

\[ D=A. \]

Then

\[ A=IDI^{-1}=D. \]

Scalar Matrices

A scalar matrix

\[ A=\lambda I \]

acts by scaling every vector by the same factor.

Every nonzero vector is an eigenvector, so every basis is an eigenbasis.

Such a matrix is diagonalizable in infinitely many ways.

Diagonalization Is Not Unique

The factorization

\[ A=PDP^{-1} \]

is generally not unique.

Eigenvectors may be rescaled, eigenvectors within an eigenspace may be replaced by another basis, and the ordering of eigenvectors may be changed if the diagonal entries of \(D\) are reordered consistently.

Rescaling Eigenvectors

If \(v\) is an eigenvector, then any nonzero scalar multiple

\[ cv \]

is also an eigenvector for the same eigenvalue.

Therefore rescaling a column of \(P\) does not destroy a valid diagonalization as long as the matrix remains invertible.

Reordering Eigenvectors

Suppose

\[ P= \begin{bmatrix} v_1&v_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D= \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1&0\\ 0&\lambda_2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

If the columns of \(P\) are swapped, the diagonal entries of \(D\) must be swapped as well.

The pairing between each eigenvector and its eigenvalue must remain intact.

Diagonalization and Coordinates

Let the eigenbasis be

\[ B=(v_1,\ldots,v_n). \]

Then \(P\) converts \(B\)-coordinates into standard coordinates:

\[ x=P[x]_B. \]

Therefore

\[ [x]_B=P^{-1}x. \]

The Transformation in Eigenbasis Coordinates

Start with standard coordinates \(x\).

Convert to eigenbasis coordinates:

\[ P^{-1}x. \]

Apply the diagonal scaling:

\[ D(P^{-1}x). \]

Convert back:

\[ P(DP^{-1}x). \]

Hence

\[ Ax=PDP^{-1}x. \]

Why Matrix Powers Become Easy

Suppose

\[ A=PDP^{-1}. \]

Then

\[ A^2 = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1}). \]

Since

\[ P^{-1}P=I, \]

we obtain

\[ A^2=PD^2P^{-1}. \]

General Power Formula

Repeating the cancellation gives

\[ \boxed{A^k=PD^kP^{-1}}. \]

Because \(D\) is diagonal,

\[ D^k= \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1^k&0&\cdots&0\\ 0&\lambda_2^k&\cdots&0\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ 0&0&\cdots&\lambda_n^k \end{bmatrix}. \]

This is far easier than multiplying \(A\) by itself repeatedly.

Example: Powers of a Diagonalizable Matrix

Suppose

\[ A=P \begin{bmatrix} 2&0\\ 0&3 \end{bmatrix} P^{-1}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ A^{10} = P \begin{bmatrix} 2^{10}&0\\ 0&3^{10} \end{bmatrix} P^{-1}}. \]

Only the eigenvalues need to be raised to the tenth power.

Negative Powers

If \(A\) is invertible and diagonalizable, then no diagonal entry of \(D\) is zero.

Therefore,

\[ A^{-1}=PD^{-1}P^{-1}, \]

where

\[ D^{-1} = \operatorname{diag} \left( \frac1{\lambda_1}, \ldots, \frac1{\lambda_n} \right). \]

Functions of a Diagonalizable Matrix

Diagonalization also simplifies many matrix functions.

If a suitable scalar function \(f\) is applied through a matrix functional calculus, then

\[ \boxed{ f(A)=Pf(D)P^{-1}}, \]

where

\[ f(D)= \operatorname{diag} \bigl(f(\lambda_1),\ldots,f(\lambda_n)\bigr). \]

Matrix Exponentials

One especially important example is the matrix exponential.

If

\[ A=PDP^{-1}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ e^{At} = Pe^{Dt}P^{-1}}, \]

where

\[ e^{Dt} = \operatorname{diag} \left( e^{\lambda_1t}, \ldots, e^{\lambda_nt} \right). \]

This is central to systems of differential equations.

Discrete Dynamical Systems

Consider

\[ x_{k+1}=Ax_k. \]

After \(k\) steps,

\[ x_k=A^kx_0. \]

If \(A\) is diagonalizable,

\[ x_k=PD^kP^{-1}x_0. \]

The long-term behavior is therefore governed by powers of the eigenvalues.

Dominant Eigenvalues

In repeated applications of a diagonalizable matrix, eigenvalues with larger absolute value tend to dominate the long-term behavior.

If

\[ |\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|, \]

then the component along the first eigendirection may eventually dominate, provided the initial vector has a nonzero component in that direction.

Decay, Stability, and Growth

In a discrete system:

  • if \(|\lambda|<1\), that eigendirection decays;
  • if \(|\lambda|>1\), that eigendirection grows in magnitude;
  • if \(|\lambda|=1\), its magnitude may persist;
  • negative eigenvalues introduce sign reversal between iterations.

Continuous Systems

For a differential system

\[ x'(t)=Ax(t), \]

diagonalization reduces the system into independent scalar equations in eigenbasis coordinates.

Each coordinate evolves according to

\[ y_i'(t)=\lambda_i y_i(t). \]

Hence

\[ y_i(t)=c_ie^{\lambda_it}. \]

Diagonalization Decouples a System

A matrix with off-diagonal entries mixes coordinates.

A diagonal matrix does not.

Thus changing into an eigenbasis converts a coupled linear system into independent one-dimensional scaling laws.

This process is often called decoupling.

Symmetric Matrices

Real symmetric matrices have an especially strong diagonalization property.

Every real symmetric matrix can be diagonalized using an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

This leads to the spectral theorem, developed more fully alongside inner products and orthogonality.

Orthogonal Diagonalization

If the eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormally, form

\[ Q= \begin{bmatrix} \vert&&\vert\\ q_1&\cdots&q_n\\ \vert&&\vert \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ Q^{-1}=Q^T. \]

The diagonalization becomes

\[ \boxed{A=QDQ^T}. \]

Similarity

Two matrices \(A\) and \(B\) are similar if

\[ \boxed{B=P^{-1}AP} \]

for some invertible matrix \(P\).

Similar matrices represent the same linear transformation relative to different bases.

Diagonalization as Similarity

A matrix is diagonalizable exactly when it is similar to a diagonal matrix.

That is,

\[ \boxed{ A\text{ is diagonalizable} \iff A\sim D \text{ for some diagonal }D}. \]

Quantities Preserved by Similarity

Similar matrices share important structural quantities, including:

  • the same characteristic polynomial;
  • the same eigenvalues with algebraic multiplicities;
  • the same determinant;
  • the same trace;
  • the same rank.

They may look different numerically because the coordinate system has changed.

Trace and Diagonalization

If

\[ A=PDP^{-1}, \]

then

\[ \operatorname{tr}(A)=\operatorname{tr}(D). \]

Since \(D\) contains the eigenvalues on its diagonal,

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{tr}(A) = \lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_n} \]

when eigenvalues are counted with algebraic multiplicity.

Determinant and Diagonalization

Similar matrices have equal determinants.

Therefore,

\[ \det(A)=\det(D). \]

Since the determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal entries,

\[ \boxed{ \det(A)=\lambda_1\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_n}. \]

Zero Eigenvalues and Invertibility

If one diagonal entry of \(D\) is zero, then

\[ \det(D)=0. \]

Therefore \(A\) is singular.

Thus an invertible diagonalizable matrix has no zero eigenvalues.

A Reliable Procedure for Diagonalizing a Matrix

  1. Find the eigenvalues of \(A\).
  2. For each eigenvalue \(\lambda\), solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\).
  3. Find a basis for each eigenspace.
  4. Count the total number of independent eigenvectors.
  5. If fewer than \(n\) exist, the matrix is not diagonalizable.
  6. If \(n\) independent eigenvectors exist, place them into the columns of \(P\).
  7. Place the corresponding eigenvalues into the diagonal of \(D\) in matching order.
  8. Verify \(AP=PD\) or \(P^{-1}AP=D\).

A Reliable Procedure with Distinct Eigenvalues

  1. Compute the characteristic polynomial.
  2. Find all \(n\) distinct eigenvalues.
  3. Find one eigenvector for each eigenvalue.
  4. Place those eigenvectors into \(P\).
  5. Place the matching eigenvalues into \(D\).

Because eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are automatically independent, no additional independence test is necessary.

A Reliable Procedure with Repeated Eigenvalues

  1. Record each eigenvalue's algebraic multiplicity.
  2. Find the eigenspace \(\ker(A-\lambda I)\).
  3. Determine its dimension, the geometric multiplicity.
  4. Collect a basis of eigenvectors from each eigenspace.
  5. Add the geometric multiplicities.
  6. If the total is \(n\), the matrix is diagonalizable.
  7. If the total is less than \(n\), it is not diagonalizable.

Common Error: Putting Eigenvectors in \(D\)

The diagonal matrix \(D\) contains eigenvalues, not eigenvectors.

Eigenvectors form the columns of \(P\).

The correct structure is

\[ \boxed{ P=[v_1\ \cdots\ v_n], \qquad D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)}. \]

Common Error: Mismatching Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues

If column \(j\) of \(P\) is an eigenvector for \(\lambda_j\), then position \(j\) on the diagonal of \(D\) must contain \(\lambda_j\).

Reordering is allowed only when both matrices are reordered consistently.

Common Error: Assuming Every Square Matrix Is Diagonalizable

Squareness is necessary for ordinary diagonalization, but it is not sufficient.

The matrix must possess enough independent eigenvectors to form a basis.

Common Error: Assuming Repeated Eigenvalues Prevent Diagonalization

Repeated eigenvalues do not automatically cause failure.

A repeated eigenvalue is acceptable if its eigenspace supplies enough independent eigenvectors.

Common Error: Counting Eigenvalues Instead of Eigenvectors

A matrix may have an eigenvalue of algebraic multiplicity \(3\) but only one independent eigenvector associated with it.

Diagonalizability depends on the total number of independent eigenvectors, not just on root multiplicities.

Common Error: Writing \(A=P^{-1}DP\)

The standard diagonalization identity is

\[ \boxed{A=PDP^{-1}}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{D=P^{-1}AP}. \]

Reversing the order changes the transformation.

Common Error: Assuming \(P\) Is Orthogonal

In a general diagonalization,

\[ P^{-1} \]

is not necessarily equal to

\[ P^T. \]

That simplification occurs when \(P\) is orthogonal, as in orthogonal diagonalization of real symmetric matrices.

Common Error: Computing \(A^k\) Directly After Diagonalizing

Once

\[ A=PDP^{-1} \]

is known, use

\[ \boxed{A^k=PD^kP^{-1}}. \]

Repeated multiplication of \(A\) defeats one of the main computational advantages of diagonalization.

Example: Full Diagonalization Workflow

Consider

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} 4&1\\ 2&3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Its characteristic polynomial is

\[ \det(A-\lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 4-\lambda&1\\ 2&3-\lambda \end{vmatrix}. \]

Therefore,

\[ (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-2 = \lambda^2-7\lambda+10. \]

Factor:

\[ (\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)=0. \]

So the eigenvalues are

\[ \lambda_1=5, \qquad \lambda_2=2. \]

Find the Eigenvector for \(\lambda=5\)

Solve

\[ (A-5I)v=0. \]

We have

\[ A-5I= \begin{bmatrix} -1&1\\ 2&-2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Thus

\[ -x+y=0, \]

so one eigenvector is

\[ v_1= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Find the Eigenvector for \(\lambda=2\)

Solve

\[ (A-2I)v=0. \]

Then

\[ A-2I= \begin{bmatrix} 2&1\\ 2&1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Thus

\[ 2x+y=0. \]

One eigenvector is

\[ v_2= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ -2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Assemble the Diagonalization

Place the eigenvectors into

\[ P= \begin{bmatrix} 1&1\\ 1&-2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Place the matching eigenvalues into

\[ D= \begin{bmatrix} 5&0\\ 0&2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A=PDP^{-1}}. \]

Use the Diagonalization to Compute Powers

For the matrix above,

\[ A^k = P \begin{bmatrix} 5^k&0\\ 0&2^k \end{bmatrix} P^{-1}. \]

The complicated repeated action of \(A\) has been reduced to raising two scalars to powers.

Why Diagonalization Matters in Multidimensional Systems

A coupled multidimensional system may mix several variables at every step.

Diagonalization identifies special directions in which the transformation acts independently.

In that coordinate system, repeated transformations, differential systems, long-term growth, stability, and modal behavior become much easier to analyze.

The method underlies applications in dynamics, vibration analysis, population models, economics, control systems, quantum mechanics, data analysis, and numerical simulation.

What to Remember

Diagonalization rewrites a square matrix as

\[ \boxed{A=PDP^{-1}}. \]
  • The columns of \(P\) are linearly independent eigenvectors.
  • The diagonal entries of \(D\) are the corresponding eigenvalues.
  • A matrix is diagonalizable exactly when it has a basis of eigenvectors.
  • \(n\) distinct eigenvalues guarantee diagonalizability for an \(n\times n\) matrix.
  • Repeated eigenvalues require enough independent eigenvectors in their eigenspaces.
  • \(A^k=PD^kP^{-1}\).
  • Diagonalization decouples a multidimensional linear transformation into independent scalar actions.
  • Similar matrices represent the same transformation in different bases.
  • Real symmetric matrices admit orthogonal diagonalization.

The structural idea is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{find a basis in which the transformation acts by independent scaling}}. \]
