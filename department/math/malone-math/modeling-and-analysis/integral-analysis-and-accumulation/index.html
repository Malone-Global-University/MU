Integral Analysis and Accumulation

Integration is the mathematics of accumulation. It begins with the problem of reversing derivatives, develops through sums and limits, and grows into a general framework for measuring total change, area, volume, work, average values, and continuously accumulated quantities.

This section develops integration in a deliberate sequence: first the algebraic idea of antiderivatives, then the limiting idea of Riemann sums, then the definite integral and Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, followed by major integration techniques and applications.

Section Overview

Differential analysis asks how rapidly a quantity is changing. Integral analysis asks how those changes accumulate.

The central relationship is

\[ \boxed{ \text{rate of change} \longleftrightarrow \text{accumulated change} }. \]

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus makes this relationship precise by connecting derivatives and definite integrals through antiderivatives.

The lessons in this section move from foundational meaning to technique, then from technique to interpretation and application.

Learning Path

  1. Reverse differentiation with antiderivatives.
  2. Approximate continuous totals with Riemann sums.
  3. Define exact accumulation with definite integrals.
  4. Connect derivatives and integrals through the Fundamental Theorem.
  5. Develop major techniques for finding antiderivatives.
  6. Extend integration to infinite intervals and singular behavior.
  7. Approximate difficult integrals numerically.
  8. Apply integration to geometry and physical work.
  9. Use integrals to compute continuous averages.
  10. Treat accumulated totals themselves as functions.

Reverse the product rule. Integration by parts is especially useful when an integrand contains a product whose pieces become simpler when one is differentiated and the other is integrated.

Core Formulas

Antiderivative

\[ F'(x)=f(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C. \]

Definite Integral

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{\|P\|\to0} \sum f(x_i^*)\Delta x_i. \]

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

\[ \frac{d}{dx} \int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x), \] \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a). \]

Average Value

\[ f_{\text{avg}} = \frac1{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

Net Change

\[ Q(b)-Q(a) = \int_a^b Q'(x)\,dx. \]

Major Integration Techniques

Different integrands suggest different methods.

Structure Typical Method
Directly recognizable derivative reversal Basic antiderivative rules
Composition with an inner derivative Substitution
Product of useful factors Integration by parts
Proper rational function with factorable denominator Partial fractions
No convenient symbolic antiderivative Numerical integration
Infinite interval or unbounded integrand Improper integral and limits

The General Accumulation Pattern

Many applications can be summarized by

\[ \boxed{ \text{total} = \int (\text{local rate or density}) (\text{small change}) }. \]

Examples include:

  • area from height times width;
  • volume from cross-sectional area times thickness;
  • mass from density times length or volume;
  • work from force times displacement;
  • displacement from velocity times time;
  • change in cost from marginal cost times quantity;
  • probability from density times input width.

Conceptual Map

The section can be viewed as four connected layers.

Layer 1: Meaning

Riemann sums and definite integrals explain what continuous accumulation means.

Layer 2: Connection

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connects accumulation with antiderivatives.

Layer 3: Technique

Substitution, integration by parts, and partial fractions expand the class of integrals that can be evaluated symbolically.

Layer 4: Application

Improper integrals, numerical integration, geometric applications, average values, and accumulation functions extend integration to realistic mathematical models.

What You Should Leave This Section Knowing

By the end of Integral Analysis and Accumulation, you should be able to:

  • interpret an antiderivative as a reverse derivative;
  • construct and interpret Riemann sums;
  • explain a definite integral as exact signed accumulation;
  • use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in both directions;
  • choose among major integration techniques;
  • test and evaluate basic improper integrals;
  • approximate integrals numerically from formulas or data;
  • set up integration models for area, volume, length, and work;
  • compute and interpret average values;
  • analyze accumulation functions from the behavior of their rates.

From Change to Total

Differential analysis studies what is happening locally. Integral analysis reconstructs the larger total from those local changes.

Together, differentiation and integration provide a language for moving between instantaneous behavior and accumulated behavior:

\[ \boxed{ \text{local change} \longleftrightarrow \text{global accumulation} }. \]

That relationship is one of the central organizing ideas of continuous mathematics and mathematical modeling.
