Accumulation Functions

A definite integral usually returns one number. An accumulation function lets one endpoint vary, so the accumulated total itself becomes a function. This viewpoint unifies integration, derivatives, signed area, net change, and applications: the value of the function tells us how much has accumulated so far, while its derivative tells us the current rate of accumulation.

The Central Idea

Fix a starting point \(a\), and define

\[ \boxed{ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt }. \]

For each value of \(x\), the function \(A(x)\) records the net accumulation of \(f\) from \(a\) to \(x\).

The variable \(x\) tells us how far the accumulation process has gone.

From a Number to a Function

A definite integral such as

\[ \int_1^5 f(t)\,dt \]

is one number.

But

\[ A(x)=\int_1^x f(t)\,dt \]

changes as \(x\) changes.

Therefore \(A\) is a function, not merely a single accumulated total.

Why the Integration Variable Is Usually \(t\)

In

\[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt, \]

\(x\) is already being used as the input to the accumulation function.

The symbol \(t\) is a local variable used only inside the integral.

We could equally write

\[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(u)\,du. \]

The value is unchanged.

The Starting Value

Since the upper and lower limits are equal when \(x=a\),

\[ A(a)=\int_a^a f(t)\,dt. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A(a)=0}. \]

A basic accumulation function starts with zero accumulated change at its reference point.

The Fundamental Theorem Connection

If \(f\) is continuous and

\[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt, \]

then the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives

\[ \boxed{A'(x)=f(x)}. \]

This is the key fact governing accumulation functions.

The Rate of Accumulation

The value \(A(x)\) tells us the total accumulated amount.

The derivative \(A'(x)\) tells us how fast that total is changing.

Since

\[ A'(x)=f(x), \]

we can say

\[ \boxed{ \text{current rate of accumulation} = \text{integrand} }. \]

Why the Derivative Equals the Integrand

Suppose \(x\) increases by a small amount \(\Delta x\).

Then the additional accumulation is approximately

\[ \Delta A\approx f(x)\Delta x. \]

Dividing by \(\Delta x\),

\[ \frac{\Delta A}{\Delta x}\approx f(x). \]

As \(\Delta x\to0\),

\[ \boxed{A'(x)=f(x)}. \]

Example: A Polynomial Accumulation Function

Let

\[ A(x)=\int_0^x t^2\,dt. \]

By the Fundamental Theorem,

\[ A'(x)=x^2. \]

We can also evaluate the integral explicitly:

\[ A(x)= \left[ \frac{t^3}{3} \right]_0^x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ A(x)=\frac{x^3}{3} }. \]

Checking the Example

Differentiate

\[ A(x)=\frac{x^3}{3}. \]

Then

\[ A'(x)=x^2. \]

This is exactly the original integrand.

The accumulation function is therefore an antiderivative selected by the condition

\[ A(0)=0. \]

Accumulation Is Signed

If \(f(t)>0\), then moving to the right adds positive accumulation.

If \(f(t)<0\), then moving to the right adds negative accumulation.

Therefore \(A(x)\) tracks

\[ \boxed{ \text{positive contribution} - \text{negative contribution} }. \]

Reading \(A(x)\) from the Graph of \(f\)

If

\[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt, \]

then \(A(x)\) equals the signed area accumulated between \(a\) and \(x\).

To compute \(A(x)\) geometrically:

  1. identify regions between \(a\) and \(x\);
  2. find their geometric areas;
  3. assign positive signs above the axis;
  4. assign negative signs below the axis;
  5. add the signed contributions.

Example: Accumulated Signed Area

Suppose \(A(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt\).

Assume the graph of \(f\) contributes area \(5\) above the axis from \(0\) to \(2\), then area \(3\) below the axis from \(2\) to \(4\).

Then

\[ A(2)=5. \]

At \(x=4\),

\[ A(4)=5-3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A(4)=2}. \]

When Is an Accumulation Function Increasing?

Since

\[ A'(x)=f(x), \]

if

\[ f(x)>0, \]

then

\[ A'(x)>0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ f>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A\text{ is increasing} }. \]

When Is an Accumulation Function Decreasing?

If

\[ f(x)<0, \]

then

\[ A'(x)<0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ f<0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A\text{ is decreasing} }. \]

Zeros of the Integrand

If

\[ f(c)=0, \]

then

\[ A'(c)=0. \]

Thus a zero of the integrand becomes a critical point candidate for the accumulation function.

Local Extrema of an Accumulation Function

If \(f\) changes from positive to negative at \(x=c\), then

\[ A' \]

changes from positive to negative.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ A\text{ has a local maximum at }c }. \]

If \(f\) changes from negative to positive, \(A\) has a local minimum.

The Second Derivative of an Accumulation Function

Since

\[ A'(x)=f(x), \]

differentiating again gives

\[ \boxed{ A''(x)=f'(x) }. \]

The rate at which the integrand changes controls the concavity of the accumulation function.

When Is \(A\) Concave Up?

If \(f\) is increasing, then

\[ f'(x)>0. \]

Therefore,

\[ A''(x)>0. \]

So

\[ \boxed{ f\text{ increasing} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A\text{ concave up} }. \]

When Is \(A\) Concave Down?

If \(f\) is decreasing, then

\[ f'(x)<0. \]

Therefore,

\[ A''(x)<0. \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{ f\text{ decreasing} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A\text{ concave down} }. \]

Inflection Points of \(A\)

An inflection point of \(A\) occurs where the concavity of \(A\) changes.

Since

\[ A''=f', \]

this happens when \(f\) changes from increasing to decreasing or from decreasing to increasing.

In other words, local extrema of \(f\) often correspond to inflection points of \(A\).

Graph Translation: From \(f\) to \(A\)

Behavior of \(f\) Behavior of \(A\)
\(f>0\) \(A\) increasing
\(f<0\) \(A\) decreasing
\(f=0\) \(A'=0\)
\(f\) increasing \(A\) concave up
\(f\) decreasing \(A\) concave down
\(f\) changes \(+\to-\) \(A\) local maximum
\(f\) changes \(-\to+\) \(A\) local minimum

Accumulation with a Nonzero Initial Value

Many applications do not begin at zero.

If a quantity \(Q(x)\) has initial value

\[ Q(a)=Q_0 \]

and rate

\[ Q'(x)=f(x), \]

then

\[ \boxed{ Q(x) = Q_0+ \int_a^x f(t)\,dt }. \]

The Net Change Formula

The Fundamental Theorem gives

\[ Q(x)-Q(a) = \int_a^x Q'(t)\,dt. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{current amount} = \text{initial amount} + \text{accumulated change} }. \]

Example: Population Accumulation

Suppose a population begins with

\[ P(0)=1000 \]

and changes at rate \(r(t)\) people per year.

Then

\[ \boxed{ P(t) = 1000+ \int_0^t r(s)\,ds }. \]

Positive \(r\) increases the population, while negative \(r\) decreases it.

Example: Water in a Tank

Suppose a tank contains \(500\) liters initially.

Let \(r(t)\) be the net flow rate in liters per minute, where inflow is positive and outflow is negative.

Then the amount of water at time \(t\) is

\[ \boxed{ V(t) = 500+ \int_0^t r(s)\,ds }. \]

Example: Position from Velocity

If

\[ s(0)=s_0 \]

and velocity is \(v(t)\), then

\[ \boxed{ s(t) = s_0+ \int_0^t v(u)\,du }. \]

Position is therefore an accumulation function of velocity.

Velocity from Acceleration

If acceleration is \(a(t)\) and initial velocity is \(v_0\), then

\[ \boxed{ v(t) = v_0+ \int_0^t a(u)\,du }. \]

Velocity accumulates acceleration in exactly the same way that position accumulates velocity.

Example: Total Cost from Marginal Cost

If \(C'(q)\) is marginal cost and \(C(0)=C_0\), then

\[ \boxed{ C(q) = C_0+ \int_0^q C'(u)\,du }. \]

The integral adds the incremental cost associated with producing each additional unit.

Accumulation with a Composite Upper Limit

Suppose

\[ G(x)=\int_a^{g(x)}f(t)\,dt. \]

By the Fundamental Theorem and chain rule,

\[ \boxed{ G'(x)=f(g(x))g'(x) }. \]

The accumulation changes because the endpoint itself changes with \(x\).

Example: Composite Upper Limit

Differentiate

\[ G(x)=\int_0^{x^2}(1+t^3)\,dt. \]

Evaluate the integrand at \(x^2\):

\[ 1+(x^2)^3=1+x^6. \]

Multiply by

\[ \frac{d}{dx}(x^2)=2x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ G'(x)=2x(1+x^6) }. \]

Accumulation with a Variable Lower Limit

Suppose

\[ H(x)=\int_{g(x)}^b f(t)\,dt. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ H'(x) = -f(g(x))g'(x) }. \]

The negative sign reflects the orientation of the lower limit.

Both Limits Variable

If

\[ K(x)=\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)}f(t)\,dt, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ K'(x) = f(v(x))v'(x) - f(u(x))u'(x) }. \]

Example: Two Variable Limits

Differentiate

\[ K(x)=\int_x^{x^2}(1+t^2)\,dt. \]

The upper contribution is

\[ [1+x^4](2x). \]

The lower contribution is

\[ -(1+x^2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ K'(x) = 2x(1+x^4)-(1+x^2) }. \]

Changing the Reference Point

Suppose

\[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt \]

and

\[ B(x)=\int_c^x f(t)\,dt. \]

Then

\[ B(x)-A(x) \]

is constant because both derivatives equal \(f(x)\).

In fact,

\[ \boxed{ B(x) = A(x)-A(c) }. \]

Accumulation Functions and the Antiderivative Family

If

\[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt, \]

then \(A'(x)=f(x)\).

Every other antiderivative of \(f\) has the form

\[ \boxed{ A(x)+C }. \]

Choosing the starting point and initial value selects one specific member of this family.

Integral Form versus Explicit Formula

Sometimes an accumulation function can be simplified to an elementary formula.

For example,

\[ A(x)=\int_0^x 2t\,dt=x^2. \]

But sometimes no elementary antiderivative exists.

Then the integral expression itself is a perfectly valid definition of the function.

Accumulation Functions Can Define New Functions

Consider

\[ A(x)=\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt. \]

The integrand has no elementary antiderivative.

Nevertheless, \(A(x)\) is a well-defined function, and

\[ \boxed{ A'(x)=e^{-x^2} }. \]

Definite integration can therefore create meaningful functions even when symbolic antidifferentiation is unavailable.

Numerically Evaluating an Accumulation Function

If

\[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt \]

cannot be evaluated exactly, numerical integration can approximate \(A(x)\) at selected values of \(x\).

This is common when \(f\) comes from experimental data or has no elementary antiderivative.

Accumulation from Tabular Data

Suppose a rate is measured at discrete times:

What Happens When \(x

The accumulation function

\[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt \]

still makes sense when \(x

By reversing orientation,

\[ \boxed{ A(x) = -\int_x^a f(t)\,dt }. \]

Accumulation is therefore oriented relative to the reference point.

Net Accumulation versus Total Accumulation

The standard accumulation function

\[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt \]

measures net signed accumulation.

If total magnitude is needed instead, use

\[ \boxed{ T(x)=\int_a^x |f(t)|\,dt }. \]

For velocity, this is the distinction between displacement and total distance.

Accumulation versus Average Value

Accumulation over \([a,x]\) is

\[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt. \]

The average value over that same interval is

\[ \boxed{ f_{\text{avg},[a,x]} = \frac{A(x)}{x-a} } \]

for \(x\neq a\).

Thus average value is accumulation normalized by interval length.

Local Average Approaches the Instantaneous Value

Consider

\[ \frac{1}{x-a}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt. \]

This is the average value of \(f\) from \(a\) to \(x\).

As \(x\to a\), continuity implies

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a} \frac{1}{x-a} \int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(a) }. \]

Over a very small interval, the average value approaches the local value.

Accumulation as a Conservation Balance

Many applied models can be written as

\[ \boxed{ \text{amount now} = \text{amount initially} + \int (\text{inflow}-\text{outflow}) }. \]

This balance structure appears in population, inventory, fluid systems, energy, finance, and many other models.

Common Error: Assuming \(A(x)\) Is Always Positive

An accumulation function measures signed accumulation.

If negative contributions exceed positive ones, then

\[ A(x)<0. \]

Accumulation need not mean positive accumulation.

Common Error: Confusing the Sign of \(A\) with Whether \(A\) Is Increasing

Whether \(A\) is increasing depends on

\[ A'(x)=f(x), \]

not on whether \(A(x)\) itself is positive or negative.

A negative function value for \(A\) can still be increasing if

\[ f(x)>0. \]

Common Error: Using the Sign of \(f\) to Determine Concavity

The sign of \(f\) determines increasing or decreasing behavior of \(A\).

Concavity depends on

\[ A''(x)=f'(x). \]

Therefore, increasing or decreasing behavior of \(f\) determines concavity of \(A\).

Common Error: Forgetting the Chain Rule

For

\[ G(x)=\int_a^{g(x)}f(t)\,dt, \]

do not stop at

\[ f(g(x)). \]

Multiply by

\[ g'(x). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ G'(x)=f(g(x))g'(x) }. \]

Common Error: Forgetting the Initial Amount

If a quantity begins with \(Q_0\), then

\[ Q(x) \neq \int_a^x f(t)\,dt \]

unless \(Q_0=0\).

The correct model is

\[ \boxed{ Q(x)=Q_0+\int_a^x f(t)\,dt }. \]

Common Error: Confusing Net Change with Total Change Magnitude

The integral

\[ \int_a^x f(t)\,dt \]

allows positive and negative contributions to cancel.

To accumulate total magnitude, use

\[ \int_a^x|f(t)|\,dt. \]

A Reliable Procedure for Graph-Based Problems

  1. Write \(A'(x)=f(x)\).
  2. Use the sign of \(f\) to find where \(A\) increases or decreases.
  3. Use sign changes of \(f\) to locate extrema of \(A\).
  4. Write \(A''(x)=f'(x)\).
  5. Use increasing/decreasing behavior of \(f\) to determine concavity of \(A\).
  6. Use signed geometric areas to compute actual values of \(A\).

A Reliable Procedure for Applied Accumulation

  1. Identify the accumulated quantity.
  2. Identify its initial value.
  3. Identify the rate of change.
  4. Choose the correct starting time or position.
  5. Write initial value plus accumulated rate.
  6. Check the sign convention.
  7. Check units.

Example: Reading Behavior from \(f\)

Suppose

\[ A(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt \]

and assume:

  • \(f>0\) on \((0,2)\);
  • \(f<0\) on \((2,5)\);
  • \(f>0\) on \((5,7)\).

Then:

  • \(A\) increases on \((0,2)\);
  • \(A\) decreases on \((2,5)\);
  • \(A\) increases on \((5,7)\).

Therefore \(A\) has a local maximum at \(x=2\) and a local minimum at \(x=5\), assuming the sign changes occur there.

Example: Inventory Accumulation

A warehouse begins with \(800\) units of inventory.

Net inventory changes at rate

\[ r(t)=40-5t \]

units per day.

The inventory after \(t\) days is

\[ I(t) = 800+ \int_0^t(40-5u)\,du. \]

Evaluate:

\[ I(t) = 800+ \left[ 40u-\frac52u^2 \right]_0^t. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ I(t)=800+40t-\frac52t^2 }. \]

What to Remember

The basic accumulation function is

\[ \boxed{ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt }. \]

Its derivative is

\[ \boxed{ A'(x)=f(x) }. \]

Therefore:

  • the sign of \(f\) controls whether \(A\) rises or falls;
  • zeros and sign changes of \(f\) control extrema of \(A\);
  • increasing or decreasing behavior of \(f\) controls concavity of \(A\);
  • signed area under \(f\) determines actual values of \(A\).

With an initial amount \(Q_0\),

\[ \boxed{ Q(x)=Q_0+\int_a^x f(t)\,dt }. \]

This is one of the most reusable modeling patterns in calculus:

\[ \boxed{ \text{amount now} = \text{amount initially} + \text{accumulated rate of change} }. \]

Closing the Integral Analysis and Accumulation Sequence

Accumulation functions bring the major ideas of this section together.

Antiderivatives reverse differentiation. Riemann sums approximate continuous totals. Definite integrals formalize exact signed accumulation. The Fundamental Theorem connects accumulation with antiderivatives. Numerical integration estimates totals when symbolic methods are inconvenient. Applications convert local geometric and physical contributions into totals, and average values normalize those totals over intervals.

Accumulation functions complete the picture by allowing the total itself to vary, making integration a dynamic relationship rather than merely a final numerical calculation.
