Substitution

Substitution is the integration method that most directly reverses the chain rule. When an integrand contains a function nested inside another function together with a matching derivative factor, we can replace the inner expression by a new variable, simplify the integral, and then evaluate using familiar antiderivative rules. This method is often called \(u\)-substitution.

The Central Idea

The chain rule says

\[ \frac{d}{dx}F(g(x)) = F'(g(x))g'(x). \]

Substitution reverses that structure:

\[ \boxed{ \int F'(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = F(g(x))+C }. \]

The central recognition pattern is

\[ \boxed{ \text{outer function of an inner expression} \times \text{derivative of the inner expression} }. \]

Why Substitution Works

Suppose

\[ u=g(x). \]

Differentiate both sides:

\[ \frac{du}{dx}=g'(x). \]

In differential notation,

\[ \boxed{du=g'(x)\,dx}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \int F'(g(x))g'(x)\,dx \]

becomes

\[ \int F'(u)\,du. \]

The complicated composite expression has been replaced by a simpler single-variable integral.

Example: The Simplest Substitution Pattern

Evaluate

\[ \int 2x(x^2+1)^5\,dx. \]

The inner expression is

\[ x^2+1. \]

Its derivative is

\[ 2x. \]

That derivative factor is already present in the integrand.

Carrying Out the Substitution

Let

\[ u=x^2+1. \]

Then

\[ du=2x\,dx. \]

The integral becomes

\[ \int u^5\,du. \]

Apply the power rule:

\[ \frac{u^6}{6}+C. \]

Substitute back:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{(x^2+1)^6}{6}+C }. \]

Checking by Differentiation

Differentiate

\[ \frac{(x^2+1)^6}{6}. \]

By the chain rule,

\[ \frac16 \cdot 6(x^2+1)^5 \cdot 2x. \]

Simplifying,

\[ 2x(x^2+1)^5. \]

The original integrand returns, so the antiderivative is correct.

What Should We Choose for \(u\)?

A good substitution usually replaces the expression that is nested inside another operation.

Common candidates include:

  • the expression inside a power;
  • the expression inside a radical;
  • the expression inside an exponential;
  • the expression inside a logarithm;
  • the expression inside a trigonometric function;
  • the denominator of a rational expression;
  • any recurring composite expression whose derivative also appears nearby.

The Inner-Function Heuristic

In

\[ (3x+4)^7, \]

the inner expression is

\[ 3x+4. \]

In

\[ e^{x^2}, \]

the inner expression is

\[ x^2. \]

In

\[ \sin(5x^3), \]

the inner expression is

\[ 5x^3. \]

Substitution often begins by naming that inner expression \(u\).

The Derivative May Match Only Up to a Constant

Evaluate

\[ \int x(x^2+4)^3\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^2+4. \]

Then

\[ du=2x\,dx. \]

But the integral contains only \(x\,dx\).

Solve for that piece:

\[ \boxed{x\,dx=\frac12\,du}. \]

Compensating for the Constant

The integral becomes

\[ \frac12\int u^3\,du. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac12\cdot\frac{u^4}{4}+C = \frac{u^4}{8}+C. \]

Substitute back:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{(x^2+4)^4}{8}+C }. \]

Example: Linear Inner Function

Evaluate

\[ \int (5x-2)^8\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=5x-2. \]

Then

\[ du=5\,dx, \]

so

\[ dx=\frac15\,du. \]

Therefore,

\[ \int (5x-2)^8\,dx = \frac15\int u^8\,du. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{(5x-2)^9}{45}+C }. \]

Example: Exponential Substitution

Evaluate

\[ \int 6x e^{3x^2}\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=3x^2. \]

Then

\[ du=6x\,dx. \]

Therefore,

\[ \int e^u\,du=e^u+C. \]

Substitute back:

\[ \boxed{ e^{3x^2}+C }. \]

Example: Trigonometric Substitution Pattern

Evaluate

\[ \int 4x^3\cos(x^4)\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^4. \]

Then

\[ du=4x^3\,dx. \]

The integral becomes

\[ \int \cos u\,du. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \sin(x^4)+C }. \]

Example: Sine with a Linear Inner Function

Evaluate

\[ \int \sin(7x+1)\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=7x+1. \]

Then

\[ du=7\,dx, \]

so

\[ dx=\frac17\,du. \]

Thus,

\[ \frac17\int\sin u\,du = -\frac17\cos u+C. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ -\frac17\cos(7x+1)+C }. \]

The Logarithmic Substitution Pattern

Recall

\[ \int \frac1u\,du=\ln|u|+C. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \int \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\,dx = \ln|g(x)|+C }. \]

This is one of the most useful substitution patterns.

Example: Rational Logarithmic Pattern

Evaluate

\[ \int \frac{2x}{x^2+5}\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^2+5. \]

Then

\[ du=2x\,dx. \]

So

\[ \int\frac1u\,du = \ln|u|+C. \]

Since \(x^2+5>0\),

\[ \boxed{ \ln(x^2+5)+C }. \]

Example: Logarithmic Pattern with a Constant Adjustment

Evaluate

\[ \int \frac{x}{x^2-9}\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^2-9. \]

Then

\[ du=2x\,dx, \]

so

\[ x\,dx=\frac12du. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac12\int\frac1u\,du = \frac12\ln|u|+C. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \frac12\ln|x^2-9|+C }. \]

Example: Radical Substitution

Evaluate

\[ \int \frac{3x^2}{\sqrt{x^3+4}}\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^3+4. \]

Then

\[ du=3x^2\,dx. \]

Rewrite the radical:

\[ \frac1{\sqrt{u}}=u^{-1/2}. \]

Thus,

\[ \int u^{-1/2}\,du = 2u^{1/2}+C. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ 2\sqrt{x^3+4}+C }. \]

Example: A Nested Radical

Evaluate

\[ \int x\sqrt{x^2+1}\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^2+1, \]

so

\[ x\,dx=\frac12du. \]

Then

\[ \frac12\int u^{1/2}\,du = \frac12\cdot\frac{u^{3/2}}{3/2}+C. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac13(x^2+1)^{3/2}+C }. \]

Sometimes Algebra Comes Before Substitution

The needed derivative factor may be hidden until the integrand is simplified.

For example,

\[ \int \frac{x^3+x}{x^2+1}\,dx. \]

Factor the numerator:

\[ x^3+x=x(x^2+1). \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{x^3+x}{x^2+1}=x. \]

The integral reduces immediately to

\[ \boxed{\frac{x^2}{2}+C}. \]

Always simplify before choosing a complicated method.

Do Not Expand a Useful Composite Structure

Consider

\[ \int 2x(x^2+1)^{10}\,dx. \]

Expanding

\[ (x^2+1)^{10} \]

would create a long polynomial.

Substitution preserves the useful structure:

\[ u=x^2+1, \qquad du=2x\,dx. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \int u^{10}\,du = \frac{u^{11}}{11}+C }. \]

Replace the Entire Integral Consistently

After choosing \(u\), the goal is to rewrite the integral entirely in terms of \(u\) and \(du\).

For example, after setting

\[ u=x^2+1, \]

we should not leave an unmatched \(x\) inside the transformed integral.

A clean substitution has the form

\[ \boxed{ \int \text{expression in }x\,dx \longrightarrow \int \text{expression in }u\,du }. \]

How to Recognize a Poor Substitution

Suppose we try

\[ u=x^2+1 \]

in

\[ \int (x^2+1)^5\,dx. \]

Then

\[ du=2x\,dx, \]

but no factor of \(x\) appears in the integral.

The substitution does not simplify the problem.

Substitution is most useful when the derivative of the chosen inner expression is present, at least up to a constant factor.

Substitution in Definite Integrals

Substitution also works for definite integrals.

There are two valid workflows:

  1. substitute, integrate in \(u\), return to \(x\), then use the original bounds;
  2. substitute and immediately convert the bounds from \(x\)-values to \(u\)-values.

The second method is often cleaner because no back-substitution is needed.

Changing the Bounds

Suppose

\[ u=g(x) \]

and the original definite integral runs from

\[ x=a \]

to

\[ x=b. \]

Then the new bounds are

\[ \boxed{ u=g(a) \qquad\text{and}\qquad u=g(b) }. \]

Example: Definite Integral with Changed Bounds

Evaluate

\[ \int_0^1 2x(x^2+1)^3\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^2+1. \]

Then

\[ du=2x\,dx. \]

Change the bounds:

\[ x=0\longrightarrow u=1, \] \[ x=1\longrightarrow u=2. \]

Completing the Definite Integral

The integral becomes

\[ \int_1^2u^3\,du. \]

Therefore,

\[ \left[\frac{u^4}{4}\right]_1^2. \]

Evaluate:

\[ \frac{16}{4}-\frac14. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\frac{15}{4}}. \]

Alternative Definite-Integral Workflow

Using the same example,

\[ \int_0^1 2x(x^2+1)^3\,dx, \]

we can first find the antiderivative:

\[ \frac{(x^2+1)^4}{4}. \]

Then evaluate using the original \(x\)-bounds:

\[ \left[ \frac{(x^2+1)^4}{4} \right]_0^1. \]

This produces the same answer:

\[ \boxed{\frac{15}{4}}. \]

Do Not Mix Variables and Bounds

Once an integral has been rewritten entirely in \(u\), its bounds must also be \(u\)-values.

For example, this is inconsistent:

\[ \int_0^1 u^3\,du. \]

if \(0\) and \(1\) are still the original \(x\)-bounds.

Either:

  • keep the original bounds and return to \(x\) before evaluating; or
  • convert the bounds and remain in \(u\).

Example: Definite Logarithmic Substitution

Evaluate

\[ \int_0^1 \frac{2x}{x^2+1}\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^2+1, \qquad du=2x\,dx. \]

The bounds become

\[ x=0\to u=1, \qquad x=1\to u=2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \int_1^2\frac1u\,du = [\ln u]_1^2. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{\ln2}. \]

Example: Definite Trigonometric Substitution

Evaluate

\[ \int_0^{\sqrt{\pi}}2x\cos(x^2)\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^2, \qquad du=2x\,dx. \]

The bounds become

\[ 0\to0, \qquad \sqrt{\pi}\to\pi. \]

Therefore,

\[ \int_0^\pi\cos u\,du = [\sin u]_0^\pi. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{0}. \]

Differential Notation as a Matching Tool

If

\[ u=g(x), \]

then

\[ du=g'(x)\,dx. \]

This notation helps us identify what factor must accompany the inner function.

For

\[ u=x^3+2, \]

we get

\[ du=3x^2\,dx. \]

So an integrand containing \(x^2\,dx\) matches after multiplying by \(1/3\).

Solving the Differential Relation

Sometimes it is convenient to solve for \(dx\).

If

\[ u=4x-7, \]

then

\[ du=4\,dx. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{dx=\frac14\,du}. \]

This is especially useful when the derivative of the inner function is a nonzero constant.

Think in Terms of Function Composition

An expression such as

\[ (1+x^5)^{12} \]

has the structure

\[ F(g(x)), \]

where

\[ g(x)=1+x^5. \]

If the integrand also includes

\[ 5x^4, \]

then the chain-rule pattern is complete.

This compositional viewpoint is more reliable than memorizing isolated substitution tricks.

A General Power Pattern

If \(n\neq-1\), then

\[ \boxed{ \int [g(x)]^n g'(x)\,dx = \frac{[g(x)]^{n+1}}{n+1}+C }. \]

This is simply the ordinary power rule applied after

\[ u=g(x). \]

A General Exponential Pattern

Since

\[ \int e^u\,du=e^u+C, \]

substitution gives

\[ \boxed{ \int e^{g(x)}g'(x)\,dx = e^{g(x)}+C }. \]

General Trigonometric Patterns

Substitution gives

\[ \boxed{ \int \cos(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = \sin(g(x))+C }, \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \int \sin(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = -\cos(g(x))+C }. \]

These are reverse-chain-rule versions of familiar trigonometric derivatives.

More Reverse-Chain-Rule Patterns

Similarly,

\[ \boxed{ \int \sec^2(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = \tan(g(x))+C }, \] \[ \boxed{ \int \sec(g(x))\tan(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = \sec(g(x))+C }. \]

The important feature is always the same: the derivative of the inner expression accompanies the outer derivative pattern.

Example: A Multi-Step Algebraic Substitution

Evaluate

\[ \int \frac{6x^2}{(x^3+1)^2}\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^3+1. \]

Then

\[ du=3x^2\,dx. \]

Since the numerator contains \(6x^2\,dx\),

\[ 6x^2\,dx=2\,du. \]

The integral becomes

\[ 2\int u^{-2}\,du. \]

Therefore,

\[ -2u^{-1}+C. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ -\frac{2}{x^3+1}+C }. \]

Example: Fractional Power after Substitution

Evaluate

\[ \int \frac{x}{(x^2+4)^{2/3}}\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^2+4. \]

Then

\[ x\,dx=\frac12du. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac12\int u^{-2/3}\,du. \]

Apply the power rule:

\[ \frac12\cdot\frac{u^{1/3}}{1/3}+C. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \frac32(x^2+4)^{1/3}+C }. \]

Common Substitution Structures

Integrand structure Natural substitution
\([g(x)]^n g'(x)\) \(u=g(x)\)
\(e^{g(x)}g'(x)\) \(u=g(x)\)
\(\cos(g(x))g'(x)\) \(u=g(x)\)
\(\sin(g(x))g'(x)\) \(u=g(x)\)
\(\dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) \(u=g(x)\)
\(\dfrac{g'(x)}{\sqrt{g(x)}}\) \(u=g(x)\)

Substitution Does Not Solve Every Integral

Some integrals do not contain a useful reverse-chain-rule pattern.

For example,

\[ \int x e^x\,dx \]

does not simplify by choosing \(u=x\) or \(u=e^x\).

That integral is better suited to a later method: integration by parts.

Choosing the right method is part of integration.

Common Error: Choosing \(u\) Without Checking Its Derivative

A substitution is useful only if it simplifies the integral.

After choosing \(u\), always compute

\[ du. \]

Then compare \(du\) with the remaining factors in the integrand.

If the pieces do not match, the substitution may be poor or incomplete.

Common Error: Ignoring a Missing Constant Factor

If

\[ u=x^2+1, \]

then

\[ du=2x\,dx. \]

Therefore,

\[ x\,dx=\frac12du. \]

Replacing \(x\,dx\) directly by \(du\) would make the answer twice as large as it should be.

Common Error: Forgetting to Substitute Back

For an indefinite integral, the final answer should normally be written in the original variable.

If

\[ u=x^2+4 \]

and the transformed antiderivative is

\[ \frac{u^5}{5}+C, \]

then the final answer is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{(x^2+4)^5}{5}+C }. \]

Common Error: Mixing \(x\) and \(u\)

An expression such as

\[ \int u^3x\,du \]

usually indicates an incomplete substitution.

Once the substitution is made, rewrite every required part consistently in the new variable whenever possible.

Common Error: Keeping Old Bounds after Changing Variables

If a definite integral has been rewritten in \(u\), then its limits should be \(u\)-values.

Convert

\[ x=a,\quad x=b \]

into

\[ u=g(a),\quad u=g(b). \]

Do not combine \(u\)-notation with unconverted \(x\)-bounds.

Common Error: Adding \(+C\) to a Definite Integral

Substitution does not change the distinction between definite and indefinite integrals.

An indefinite integral ends with

\[ +C. \]

A definite integral evaluates to one number and does not.

Common Error: Expanding Before Looking for Structure

If an integrand already has the form

\[ g'(x)[g(x)]^n, \]

expanding the power usually destroys the obvious substitution pattern.

Preserve useful composition when possible.

A Reliable Procedure for Indefinite \(u\)-Substitution

  1. Inspect the integrand for a composite inner expression.
  2. Choose \(u\) equal to that inner expression.
  3. Compute \(du\).
  4. Match the derivative factor, adjusting by constants when necessary.
  5. Rewrite the entire integral in \(u\).
  6. Integrate using familiar rules.
  7. Substitute back into the original variable.
  8. Add \(+C\).
  9. Differentiate the result to verify it.

A Reliable Procedure for Definite \(u\)-Substitution

  1. Choose \(u=g(x)\).
  2. Compute \(du=g'(x)\,dx\).
  3. Rewrite the integrand in \(u\).
  4. Convert the lower and upper bounds using \(u=g(x)\).
  5. Evaluate the transformed definite integral.
  6. Do not substitute back to \(x\) if the bounds were converted.
  7. Do not add \(+C\).

A Fast Recognition Checklist

Before integrating, ask:

  • Is there a function inside another function?
  • Does the derivative of the inner function appear elsewhere?
  • Does it match exactly or only up to a constant?
  • Would replacing the inner expression simplify the integrand?
  • Can I rewrite the entire problem in one new variable?

If the answers line up, substitution is likely the right method.

Example: Full Substitution Workflow

Evaluate

\[ \int 15x^2(5x^3-1)^4\,dx. \]

Choose

\[ u=5x^3-1. \]

Then

\[ du=15x^2\,dx. \]

The integral becomes

\[ \int u^4\,du. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{u^5}{5}+C. \]

Substitute back:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{(5x^3-1)^5}{5}+C }. \]

Verifying the Full Workflow

Differentiate

\[ \frac15(5x^3-1)^5. \]

By the chain rule,

\[ \frac15 \cdot 5(5x^3-1)^4 \cdot 15x^2. \]

Simplifying,

\[ 15x^2(5x^3-1)^4. \]

The original integrand returns.

Example: Full Definite-Integral Workflow

Evaluate

\[ \int_1^2 3x^2(x^3+2)^2\,dx. \]

Let

\[ u=x^3+2, \qquad du=3x^2\,dx. \]

Convert the bounds:

\[ x=1\to u=3, \] \[ x=2\to u=10. \]

Therefore,

\[ \int_3^{10}u^2\,du = \left[\frac{u^3}{3}\right]_3^{10}. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1000-27}{3} = \frac{973}{3} }. \]

What to Remember

Substitution reverses the chain rule.

If

\[ u=g(x), \]

then

\[ \boxed{du=g'(x)\,dx}. \]

The central pattern is

\[ \boxed{ \int F'(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = F(g(x))+C }. \]

For definite integrals, bounds can be changed directly:

\[ x=a\to u=g(a), \qquad x=b\to u=g(b). \]

The most important habit is:

look for a nested expression and then check whether its derivative is also present.
