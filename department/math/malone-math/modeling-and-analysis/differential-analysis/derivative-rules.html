Derivative Rules

The limit definition tells us what a derivative means. Derivative rules tell us how to compute many derivatives without rebuilding a difference quotient from the beginning every time. In this lesson, we develop the foundational rules for constants, powers, constant multiples, sums, and differences. These rules turn differentiation into a reliable symbolic process while preserving its meaning as local rate of change.

The Central Idea

From the limit definition,

\[ f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}. \]

This definition is fundamental, but using it directly for every function would be slow. Once recurring patterns have been established from the limit definition, we can use them as derivative rules.

The core idea is:

\[ \boxed{\text{understand the derivative through limits, then compute it efficiently with rules.}} \]

What Differentiation Does

Differentiation is the process of finding a derivative. If

\[ y=f(x), \]

differentiation produces a new function

\[ y'=f'(x). \]

The original function reports output values. The derivative reports how rapidly those outputs are changing with respect to the input.

Derivative Notation

Several notations may describe the same derivative. If

\[ y=f(x), \]

common forms include

\[ f'(x),\qquad y',\qquad \frac{dy}{dx},\qquad \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)]. \]

The operator notation

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[\,\cdot\,] \]

is especially useful when applying rules because it explicitly says: differentiate the expression inside with respect to \(x\).

The Constant Rule

A constant does not change as \(x\) changes. Therefore, its rate of change is zero.

If \(c\) is a constant, then

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[c]=0}. \]

For example,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[7]=0, \]

and

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[-\pi]=0. \]

Why the Constant Rule Works

Let

\[ f(x)=c. \]

Then

\[ f(x+h)=c. \]

The difference quotient becomes

\[ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} =\frac{c-c}{h} =0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=0}. \]

The rule is not arbitrary. It is a compact result obtained from the limit definition.

The Identity Function

The simplest changing function is

\[ f(x)=x. \]

Its graph is a line with slope \(1\), so its derivative is

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[x]=1}. \]

This result is also the \(n=1\) case of the power rule introduced next.

The Power Rule

For a power of \(x\), differentiation follows one of the most useful patterns in elementary calculus.

For appropriate real exponents \(n\) on intervals where the power function is defined and differentiable,

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[x^n]=nx^{n-1}}. \]

In words:

  1. bring the exponent down as a coefficient;
  2. subtract \(1\) from the exponent.

Power Rule Example: A Positive Integer Power

Differentiate

\[ f(x)=x^5. \]

Bring down the exponent \(5\), then reduce the exponent by \(1\):

\[ f'(x)=5x^{5-1}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=5x^4}. \]

Power Rule Example: A First Power

Since

\[ x=x^1, \]

the power rule gives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[x] =1x^{1-1} =x^0 =1. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[x]=1}. \]

Power Rule Example: The Zero Power

For \(x\ne0\),

\[ x^0=1. \]

Applying the power rule formally gives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[x^0]=0x^{-1}=0, \]

which agrees with the constant rule:

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[1]=0}. \]

Power Rule Example: A Negative Exponent

Rewrite the reciprocal expression

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x^3} \]

as

\[ f(x)=x^{-3}. \]

Apply the power rule:

\[ f'(x)=-3x^{-4}. \]

In positive-exponent form,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=-\frac{3}{x^4}},\qquad x\ne0. \]

Power Rule Example: A Fractional Exponent

Consider

\[ f(x)=x^{1/2}=\sqrt{x}. \]

On inputs where the derivative is defined, the power rule gives

\[ f'(x)=\frac12x^{-1/2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}},\qquad x>0. \]

Notice that the original function is defined at \(x=0\), but the displayed derivative formula is not. A derivative can have a more restricted domain than the original function.

Rewrite Radicals as Powers

The power rule becomes easier to use when radicals are rewritten with rational exponents.

For example,

\[ \sqrt[3]{x^2}=x^{2/3}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[x^{2/3}] =\frac23x^{-1/3}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[\sqrt[3]{x^2}]=\frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x}}},\qquad x\ne0. \]

Why the Power Rule Is Plausible

For positive integer powers, the pattern can be seen from the limit definition. For example, let

\[ f(x)=x^3. \]

Then

\[ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} =\frac{(x+h)^3-x^3}{h}. \]

Expand:

\[ (x+h)^3=x^3+3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{(x+h)^3-x^3}{h} =\frac{3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3}{h} =3x^2+3xh+h^2. \]

Taking the limit as \(h\to0\) gives

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=3x^2}. \]

The general power rule packages this recurring algebraic pattern into one reusable rule.

The Constant-Multiple Rule

A constant coefficient scales the derivative by the same amount.

If \(c\) is constant, then

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[c f(x)]=c f'(x)}. \]

The constant can be carried outside the differentiation process.

Constant-Multiple Example

Differentiate

\[ f(x)=7x^4. \]

Keep the coefficient \(7\) and differentiate \(x^4\):

\[ f'(x)=7(4x^3). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=28x^3}. \]

Why the Constant-Multiple Rule Works

Let

\[ F(x)=c f(x). \]

Then

\[ F'(x) =\lim_{h\to0}\frac{cf(x+h)-cf(x)}{h}. \]

Factor out \(c\):

\[ F'(x) =c\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{F'(x)=cf'(x)}. \]

The Sum Rule

A sum can be differentiated term by term.

If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable, then

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[f(x)+g(x)]=f'(x)+g'(x)}. \]

Differentiation distributes across addition.

The Difference Rule

A difference can also be differentiated term by term:

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[f(x)-g(x)]=f'(x)-g'(x)}. \]

Differentiation preserves the subtraction between the two derivatives.

Why the Sum Rule Works

Let

\[ F(x)=f(x)+g(x). \]

Its difference quotient is

\[ \frac{f(x+h)+g(x+h)-f(x)-g(x)}{h}. \]

Rearrange the numerator:

\[ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} + \frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}. \]

Taking limits gives

\[ \boxed{F'(x)=f'(x)+g'(x)}. \]

The difference rule follows by combining the sum rule with the constant-multiple rule using the constant \(-1\).

Term-by-Term Differentiation

The constant-multiple, sum, difference, and power rules work together to differentiate polynomial expressions one term at a time.

For example, if

\[ f(x)=4x^5-3x^2+7x-9, \]

then

\[ f'(x) =4(5x^4)-3(2x)+7(1)-0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=20x^4-6x+7}. \]

A Practical Polynomial Shortcut

For a polynomial, you can usually work directly term by term:

\[ a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0. \]

Differentiate each power term and drop the constant term:

\[ \boxed{ na_nx^{n-1}+(n-1)a_{n-1}x^{n-2}+\cdots+a_1 }. \]

This is not a new rule. It is the repeated use of the rules already established.

Example: Differentiate a Polynomial

Differentiate

\[ y=6x^6-5x^4+2x^3-8x+11. \]

Differentiate each term:

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} =36x^5-20x^3+6x^2-8. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{y'=36x^5-20x^3+6x^2-8}. \]

Example: Negative Powers

Differentiate

\[ f(x)=5x^{-2}-3x^{-5}. \]

Apply the power rule term by term:

\[ f'(x)=5(-2)x^{-3}-3(-5)x^{-6}. \]

Simplify:

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=-10x^{-3}+15x^{-6}}. \]

In positive-exponent form,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=-\frac{10}{x^3}+\frac{15}{x^6}},\qquad x\ne0. \]

Example: Fractional Powers

Differentiate

\[ f(x)=4x^{3/2}-6x^{1/2}. \]

Apply the power rule:

\[ f'(x) =4\left(\frac32\right)x^{1/2} -6\left(\frac12\right)x^{-1/2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=6\sqrt{x}-\frac{3}{\sqrt{x}}},\qquad x>0. \]

Rewrite Before Differentiating

Algebraic rewriting can make a derivative much easier to see.

Suppose

\[ f(x)=\frac{4}{x^2}+3\sqrt{x}. \]

Rewrite using powers:

\[ f(x)=4x^{-2}+3x^{1/2}. \]

Then differentiate:

\[ f'(x)=-8x^{-3}+\frac32x^{-1/2}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=-\frac{8}{x^3}+\frac{3}{2\sqrt{x}}}. \]

Simplify Before Using a More Complicated Rule

When an expression can be simplified into a sum of powers, do that before reaching for a more advanced differentiation rule.

For example,

\[ f(x)=\frac{x^4+3x^2}{x^2},\qquad x\ne0, \]

simplifies to

\[ f(x)=x^2+3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=2x},\qquad x\ne0. \]

The original domain restriction still matters even after the algebraic expression becomes simpler.

Derivative Rules Preserve Linearity

The constant-multiple and sum rules together show that differentiation is a linear operation. For constants \(a\) and \(b\),

\[ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}[af(x)+bg(x)] =af'(x)+bg'(x) }. \]

This single statement packages constant multiples, sums, and differences into one structural rule.

Example: A Linear Combination of Functions

Suppose

\[ F(x)=3f(x)-5g(x). \]

If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable, then

\[ \boxed{F'(x)=3f'(x)-5g'(x)}. \]

We do not need formulas for \(f\) and \(g\) to apply this structural rule.

Evaluate After Differentiating

When asked for a derivative at one input, it is usually efficient to find the derivative function first and substitute afterward.

Let

\[ f(x)=2x^4-3x^2+1. \]

First differentiate:

\[ f'(x)=8x^3-6x. \]

At \(x=2\),

\[ f'(2)=8(2)^3-6(2)=64-12=52. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f'(2)=52}. \]

Derivative Values Have Units

A derivative is a rate, so its units are output units divided by input units.

If position \(s(t)\) is measured in meters and time \(t\) in seconds, then

\[ s'(t) \]

has units of meters per second.

The symbolic rules make differentiation fast, but the resulting derivative should still be interpreted as a rate of change.

Example: Position and Velocity

Suppose an object's position is

\[ s(t)=4t^3-6t^2+2t+5, \]

where \(s\) is measured in meters and \(t\) in seconds.

Differentiate term by term:

\[ s'(t)=12t^2-12t+2. \]

The derivative is the velocity function:

\[ \boxed{v(t)=12t^2-12t+2}. \]

Its units are meters per second.

Example: Marginal Change

Suppose a modeled cost function is

\[ C(q)=0.02q^3+4q+500, \]

where \(q\) is a production quantity.

Its derivative is

\[ C'(q)=0.06q^2+4. \]

The derivative describes how the modeled cost is changing locally as production changes. In applications, this type of derivative is often interpreted as marginal cost.

Rules Do Not Replace Domain Awareness

Differentiation rules operate on formulas, but formulas live on domains. Always keep track of where the original function is defined and where the resulting derivative exists.

For example,

\[ f(x)=\sqrt{x} \]

is defined for \(x\ge0\), while

\[ f'(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \]

is defined for \(x>0\).

A Derivative Formula Can Have a Smaller Domain

Differentiability is a stronger condition than merely being defined. A function may exist at an input where its derivative does not.

The function

\[ f(x)=x^{2/3} \]

is defined at \(x=0\). For \(x\ne0\), the power rule gives

\[ f'(x)=\frac23x^{-1/3}. \]

That derivative expression is undefined at \(x=0\). The original function therefore requires separate local analysis there rather than blind substitution into the rule-generated formula.

What These Rules Can Differentiate Directly

The rules in this lesson are enough to differentiate many expressions made from:

  • constants;
  • powers of the independent variable;
  • constant multiples of those powers;
  • sums of differentiable terms;
  • differences of differentiable terms;
  • polynomials;
  • many radicals and reciprocals after rewriting them as powers.

What These Rules Do Not Yet Cover

Some expressions require additional structure. For example, the derivative of

\[ f(x)g(x), \]

is not found by simply multiplying \(f'(x)\) and \(g'(x)\). Likewise, quotients and compositions require their own rules.

Those methods are developed in the next lesson: Product, Quotient, and Chain Rules.

Common Error: Forgetting to Reduce the Exponent

For

\[ f(x)=x^6, \]

an incorrect result is

\[ f'(x)=6x^6. \]

The exponent must decrease by \(1\):

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=6x^5}. \]

Common Error: Dropping the Coefficient

For

\[ f(x)=8x^3, \]

the coefficient \(8\) remains part of the calculation:

\[ f'(x)=8(3x^2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=24x^2}. \]

Common Error: Differentiating a Constant as Itself

A student may incorrectly write

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[12]=12. \]

But a constant does not change with \(x\), so

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[12]=0}. \]

Common Error: Applying the Power Rule to a Sum as One Power

The power rule applies directly to an expression of the form \(x^n\). It does not justify treating a sum inside a power as though each term were an independent power.

For example,

\[ (x+1)^5 \]

is a power of the entire expression \(x+1\), not merely a single power of \(x\). A systematic rule for differentiating compositions is developed in the chain-rule lesson.

Common Error: Multiplying Derivatives Termwise

The sum rule says

\[ (f+g)'=f'+g'. \]

There is no parallel rule saying

\[ (fg)'=f'g'. \]

Products require the product rule, which is introduced next.

Common Error: Ignoring a Negative Exponent

If

\[ f(x)=x^{-4}, \]

then the exponent \(-4\) becomes the coefficient and is reduced by \(1\):

\[ f'(x)=-4x^{-5}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f'(x)=-\frac{4}{x^5}}. \]

Common Error: Losing the Original Domain

Algebra may simplify a formula, but it does not retroactively add inputs excluded from the original function.

If

\[ f(x)=\frac{x^3}{x},\qquad x\ne0, \]

then \(f(x)=x^2\) only on the original domain \(x\ne0\). Differentiating gives

\[ f'(x)=2x, \]

still for

\[ \boxed{x\ne0}. \]

A Reliable Procedure

  1. Identify the independent variable.
  2. Simplify the expression when useful.
  3. Rewrite radicals and reciprocals as powers when that makes the structure clearer.
  4. Separate sums and differences into terms.
  5. Keep constant coefficients attached to their terms.
  6. Apply the constant rule to constants.
  7. Apply the power rule to powers of the variable.
  8. Simplify the resulting derivative.
  9. Preserve relevant domain restrictions.
  10. Interpret units or rates when the problem has applied meaning.

Example: Full Workflow

Differentiate

\[ f(x)=\frac{3}{x^2}-4\sqrt{x}+5x^3-7. \]

Rewrite the reciprocal and radical:

\[ f(x)=3x^{-2}-4x^{1/2}+5x^3-7. \]

Differentiate term by term:

\[ f'(x) =3(-2)x^{-3} -4\left(\frac12\right)x^{-1/2} +15x^2. \]

Simplify:

\[ \boxed{ f'(x)=-\frac{6}{x^3}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{x}}+15x^2 },\qquad x>0. \]

Checking with a Limit at a Simple Point

Rules should agree with the limit definition. Consider

\[ f(x)=x^2. \]

The power rule gives

\[ f'(x)=2x. \]

At \(x=3\), this predicts

\[ f'(3)=6. \]

From first principles,

\[ f'(3) =\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(3+h)^2-9}{h} =\lim_{h\to0}(6+h) =6. \]

The efficient rule and the defining limit agree.

What to Remember

The foundational derivative rules are:

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[c]=0} \] \[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[x^n]=nx^{n-1}} \] \[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[cf(x)]=cf'(x)} \] \[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[f(x)+g(x)]=f'(x)+g'(x)} \] \[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}[f(x)-g(x)]=f'(x)-g'(x)}. \]

Together, these rules make polynomial and power-function differentiation systematic. The most useful working habit is to simplify and rewrite the expression until its structure matches rules you already know.
