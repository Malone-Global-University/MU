Differential Analysis

Differential analysis studies how quantities change locally and how that local change reveals broader behavior. Beginning with difference quotients and limits, this section develops the derivative as a mathematical tool for measuring instantaneous change, analyzing geometry, connecting related quantities, constructing local approximations, identifying critical behavior, sketching curves, and measuring sensitivity.

Overview

The derivative is one of the central tools of continuous mathematics. It begins as the limiting value of a difference quotient, but its meaning expands quickly: slope, instantaneous rate, local response, sensitivity, and first-order approximation are all derivative interpretations.

This section develops those ideas in a deliberate sequence. The early lessons establish the derivative and its rules. The middle lessons extend differentiation to implicit, parametric, geometric, and time-dependent relationships. The later lessons use derivatives to analyze function shape, extrema, approximation, and sensitivity.

Lessons

  1. Difference Quotients

    Build the bridge from average rate of change to derivative thinking through secant slopes, the \(h\)-form, algebraic simplification, and limiting behavior.

  2. Derivatives from Limits

    Define derivatives at a point and as functions, connect secant slopes to tangent slopes, and examine differentiability through limits.

  3. Derivative Rules

    Develop the constant, identity, power, constant-multiple, sum, and difference rules for efficient differentiation.

  4. Product, Quotient, and Chain Rules

    Differentiate products, quotients, and composite functions while learning to recognize which rule or combination of rules applies.

  5. Implicit and Parametric Differentiation

    Differentiate relationships that are not written explicitly as \(y=f(x)\), including implicit curves and parametrically defined paths.

  6. Higher Derivatives

    Extend differentiation to second, third, and \(n\)th derivatives and interpret repeated derivatives in motion and changing-rate contexts.

  7. Tangent and Normal Relationships

    Use derivative information to construct tangent and normal lines for explicit, implicit, and parametric curves.

  8. Related Rates

    Connect quantities that change together by differentiating their governing relationships with respect to time.

  9. Linearization and Differentials

    Use tangent lines as local models, estimate small changes, and analyze first-order approximation and propagated measurement error.

  10. Critical Points, Monotonicity, and Concavity

    Analyze derivative signs to determine increasing and decreasing intervals, concavity, critical behavior, and inflection points.

  11. Extrema and Curve Sketching

    Combine derivative analysis, extrema, intercepts, asymptotes, end behavior, and concavity into a complete qualitative graph-analysis workflow.

  12. Sensitivity as Derivative Information

    Interpret derivatives as local response measures and develop absolute, relative, parameter, inverse, and uncertainty sensitivity.

Core Progression

The section follows a natural mathematical progression:

\[ \boxed{ \text{difference quotient} \longrightarrow \text{limit} \longrightarrow \text{derivative} \longrightarrow \text{derivative rules} \longrightarrow \text{analysis and application} }. \]

Each stage reuses the previous one. Difference quotients create the derivative definition, derivative rules make computation practical, and derivative information then supports geometry, rates, approximation, qualitative analysis, and sensitivity.

What This Section Builds

By the end of Differential Analysis, a learner should be able to:

  • interpret a difference quotient as average rate of change;
  • define and compute derivatives from limits;
  • apply the major derivative rules accurately;
  • differentiate implicit and parametric relationships;
  • work with higher derivatives;
  • construct tangent and normal lines;
  • solve related-rates problems;
  • build linear approximations and differentials;
  • identify critical points and monotonicity;
  • analyze concavity and inflection points;
  • find local and absolute extrema;
  • construct derivative-informed curve sketches;
  • interpret derivatives as sensitivity information.

Why Differential Analysis Matters

Differential analysis provides the basic language for continuous change. It allows us to replace vague statements such as “this quantity is changing quickly” with precise mathematical information about rate, direction, acceleration, local response, and sensitivity.

These tools support later work in optimization, multidimensional analysis, differential equations, numerical methods, physical modeling, economics, engineering, probability models, and many other applied systems.
