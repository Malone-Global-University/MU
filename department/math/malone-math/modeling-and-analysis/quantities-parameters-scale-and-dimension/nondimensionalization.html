Nondimensionalization

Mathematical models often contain variables with different units and parameters with very different numerical sizes. Nondimensionalization replaces those dimensional quantities with ratios measured relative to characteristic scales. The result is a cleaner model expressed in pure numbers, often with fewer effective parameters and clearer information about which effects control the system.

The Central Idea

Suppose \(x\) has a characteristic scale \(X_c\).

Define a new variable

\[ x^*=\frac{x}{X_c}. \]

Because \(x\) and \(X_c\) have the same units,

\[ [x^*]=1. \]

The variable \(x^*\) is dimensionless.

Instead of asking for the absolute value of \(x\), we ask how large \(x\) is relative to its natural scale.

What Does Nondimensional Mean?

A quantity is dimensionless when its units cancel completely.

For example, if both \(x\) and \(L\) are lengths, then

\[ \frac{x}{L} \]

has dimension

\[ \frac{L}{L}=1. \]

The resulting quantity is a pure number.

Why Nondimensionalize?

Nondimensionalization can:

  • reduce the number of parameters;
  • make typical variables order one;
  • reveal important ratios between competing effects;
  • improve comparison between systems of different size;
  • clarify which parameters actually govern behavior;
  • support approximation and asymptotic reasoning;
  • improve numerical conditioning;
  • make model structure easier to interpret.

Start with Characteristic Scales

Nondimensionalization begins by choosing characteristic scales.

If position \(x\) has scale \(L_c\) and time \(t\) has scale \(T_c\), define

\[ x^*=\frac{x}{L_c} \]

and

\[ t^*=\frac{t}{T_c}. \]

These variables measure position and time relative to the natural scales of the model.

Rewrite the Original Variables

From

\[ x^*=\frac{x}{L_c}, \]

we can write

\[ x=L_cx^*. \]

Similarly,

\[ t=T_ct^*. \]

These substitution forms are what we place into the original model.

A Simple Algebraic Example

Consider

\[ y=ax. \]

Choose characteristic scales \(X_c\) and \(Y_c\), and define

\[ x=X_cx^* \]

and

\[ y=Y_cy^*. \]

Substitute:

\[ Y_cy^*=aX_cx^*. \]

Divide by \(Y_c\):

\[ y^*=\frac{aX_c}{Y_c}x^*. \]

A Dimensionless Parameter Appears

In

\[ y^*=\frac{aX_c}{Y_c}x^*, \]

the quantity

\[ \alpha=\frac{aX_c}{Y_c} \]

is dimensionless.

The original dimensional parameter \(a\) has been converted into a pure number that measures its effect relative to the chosen scales.

Choosing Scales Can Simplify the Equation

If we choose

\[ Y_c=aX_c, \]

then

\[ \frac{aX_c}{Y_c}=1. \]

The model becomes

\[ \boxed{y^*=x^*}. \]

A suitable scale choice can remove parameters entirely.

Nondimensionalizing a Linear Model

Consider

\[ y=mx+b. \]

Set

\[ x=X_cx^* \]

and

\[ y=Y_cy^*. \]

Then

\[ Y_cy^*=mX_cx^*+b. \]

Divide by \(Y_c\):

\[ y^* = \frac{mX_c}{Y_c}x^* + \frac{b}{Y_c}. \]

The two dimensionless parameters are

\[ \alpha=\frac{mX_c}{Y_c} \]

and

\[ \beta=\frac{b}{Y_c}. \]

Dimensionless Variables Preserve Relative Meaning

Suppose

\[ L_c=100\text{ m}. \]

If

\[ x=20\text{ m}, \]

then

\[ x^*=\frac{20}{100}=0.2. \]

If

\[ x=250\text{ m}, \]

then

\[ x^*=2.5. \]

The dimensionless variable immediately reports the size relative to the system scale.

Dimensionless Time

If \(T_c\) is a characteristic time, define

\[ t^*=\frac{t}{T_c}. \]

Then:

  • \(t^*=0.1\) is early relative to the characteristic time;
  • \(t^*=1\) is one characteristic time;
  • \(t^*=10\) is long relative to that time scale.

This interpretation is independent of whether time is measured in seconds, hours, or years.

Exponential Decay Example

Consider

\[ y(t)=y_0e^{-kt}. \]

A natural time scale is

\[ T_c=\frac{1}{k}. \]

Define

\[ t^*=\frac{t}{T_c}=kt \]

and

\[ y^*=\frac{y}{y_0}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{y^*=e^{-t^*}}. \]

The original parameters disappear.

What the Exponential Example Reveals

Models such as

\[ y=10e^{-2t} \]

and

\[ y=500e^{-0.01t} \]

appear very different in dimensional form.

But after scaling output by \(y_0\) and time by \(1/k\), both become

\[ y^*=e^{-t^*}. \]

Nondimensionalization reveals that they share the same underlying shape.

Nondimensionalizing a Differential Equation

Consider

\[ \frac{dy}{dt}=-ky. \]

Let

\[ y=Y_cy^* \]

and

\[ t=T_ct^*. \]

Then

\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{Y_c}{T_c} \frac{dy^*}{dt^*}. \]

Substituting into the Decay Equation

Substitute into

\[ \frac{dy}{dt}=-ky: \] \[ \frac{Y_c}{T_c} \frac{dy^*}{dt^*} = -kY_cy^*. \]

Cancel \(Y_c\):

\[ \frac{1}{T_c} \frac{dy^*}{dt^*} = -ky^*. \]

Multiply by \(T_c\):

\[ \frac{dy^*}{dt^*} = -kT_cy^*. \]

Choose the Natural Time Scale

If

\[ T_c=\frac{1}{k}, \]

then

\[ kT_c=1. \]

The equation reduces to

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dy^*}{dt^*}=-y^* }. \]

The dimensional parameter \(k\) has disappeared from the governing equation.

How Derivatives Transform

If

\[ x=L_cx^* \]

and

\[ t=T_ct^*, \]

then

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{L_c}{T_c} \frac{dx^*}{dt^*}. \]

The factor

\[ \frac{L_c}{T_c} \]

is the natural speed scale.

Second Derivatives

Differentiating again gives

\[ \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = \frac{L_c}{T_c^2} \frac{d^2x^*}{dt^{*2}}. \]

The prefactor

\[ \frac{L_c}{T_c^2} \]

is the characteristic acceleration scale.

This pattern is useful throughout dynamic modeling.

Nondimensionalizing Logistic Growth

Consider

\[ \frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right). \]

Choose

\[ P_c=K \]

and

\[ T_c=\frac{1}{r}. \]

Define

\[ p=\frac{P}{K} \]

and

\[ \tau=rt. \]

Substitute into the Logistic Equation

Since

\[ P=Kp \]

and

\[ t=\frac{\tau}{r}, \]

we have

\[ \frac{dP}{dt} = Kr\frac{dp}{d\tau}. \]

Substituting gives

\[ Kr\frac{dp}{d\tau} = rKp(1-p). \]

Cancel \(Kr\):

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dp}{d\tau}=p(1-p) }. \]

Why the Logistic Result Matters

The original model contains two parameters:

\[ r \]

and

\[ K. \]

After nondimensionalization, neither appears explicitly:

\[ \frac{dp}{d\tau}=p(1-p). \]

The entire family shares one dimensionless governing equation.

Dimensionless Groups

Nondimensionalization often combines several dimensional parameters into a smaller number of dimensionless groups.

For example, suppose

\[ \Pi=\frac{aL}{b}. \]

If the dimensions cancel so that

\[ [\Pi]=1, \]

then \(\Pi\) is a dimensionless group.

Such groups often control model regimes more directly than the original dimensional parameters do.

Dimensionless Parameters Compare Effects

Suppose a model contains two competing rates,

\[ r_1 \]

and

\[ r_2. \]

The ratio

\[ \lambda=\frac{r_1}{r_2} \]

is dimensionless when both rates have the same units.

Then:

  • \(\lambda\ll1\): the first process is much slower;
  • \(\lambda\sim1\): the processes occur on comparable scales;
  • \(\lambda\gg1\): the first process is much faster.

Scale Ratios Can Reveal Small Parameters

Suppose

\[ \varepsilon=\frac{h}{L}, \]

where both \(h\) and \(L\) are lengths.

If

\[ h\ll L, \]

then

\[ \varepsilon\ll1. \]

The small dimensionless parameter \(\varepsilon\) may justify simplifying terms multiplied by \(\varepsilon\) or higher powers of it.

Order-One Variables

A useful nondimensionalization often produces variables of order one.

Suppose typical \(x\)-values are around

\[ 5000\text{ m}. \]

If

\[ X_c=5000\text{ m}, \]

then

\[ x^*=\frac{x}{X_c} \]

will normally be near \(1\).

This makes scale comparisons more transparent.

Nondimensionalization Is More Than Unit Conversion

Unit conversion changes the unit used to express the same dimensional quantity.

For example,

\[ 1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}. \]

Nondimensionalization instead forms a ratio such as

\[ x^*=\frac{x}{L_c}, \]

so that no physical unit remains.

Normalization and Nondimensionalization

The terms normalization and nondimensionalization are related but not always identical.

Normalization broadly means rescaling a quantity to a convenient reference range or magnitude.

Nondimensionalization specifically removes physical dimensions by dividing quantities by compatible scales.

Many nondimensionalizations are also normalizations.

Standardization as a Dimensionless Transformation

In statistics,

\[ z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \]

is dimensionless when \(x\), \(\mu\), and \(\sigma\) have the same units.

The numerator has the same dimension as \(x\), and division by \(\sigma\) removes that dimension.

The resulting \(z\)-score measures deviation relative to a characteristic spread.

Nondimensionalization in Geometry

Suppose a rectangle has length \(L\) and width \(W\).

Instead of treating both dimensions independently, define the aspect ratio

\[ \alpha=\frac{W}{L}. \]

The ratio is dimensionless.

Two rectangles of different absolute sizes but the same \(\alpha\) have the same shape.

Nondimensionalization in Finance

Suppose a firm's monthly expense \(E\) is compared with monthly revenue \(R\).

The ratio

\[ \frac{E}{R} \]

is dimensionless when both are measured in the same currency.

This ratio expresses expense relative to revenue and can be compared across firms of very different absolute size.

Nondimensionalization in Population Models

If \(P\) is a population and \(K\) a carrying capacity, then

\[ p=\frac{P}{K} \]

expresses population as a fraction of capacity.

The interpretation is immediate:

  • \(p=0.2\): population is at 20% of capacity;
  • \(p=1\): population equals the modeled capacity;
  • \(p>1\): population exceeds the nominal capacity.

Nondimensionalization in Transport

If a trip has characteristic distance \(L_c\) and characteristic time \(T_c\), then

\[ x^*=\frac{x}{L_c} \]

and

\[ t^*=\frac{t}{T_c} \]

allow progress to be compared across routes of different physical length and duration.

Nondimensionalization Supports Similarity

Two systems can be very different in absolute units but equivalent in dimensionless form.

If their dimensionless variables and governing parameter groups match, they may exhibit similar behavior after rescaling.

This is one reason scaled experiments can provide information about larger systems.

Parameter Reduction

Suppose a model originally contains five dimensional parameters.

After nondimensionalization, those parameters may appear only through two independent dimensionless combinations.

Then the effective parameter space is reduced from five quantities to two.

This can make analysis, simulation, and experimentation much more efficient.

Model Regimes

Dimensionless groups often classify model behavior.

Suppose

\[ \lambda=\frac{\text{effect A}}{\text{effect B}}. \]

Then:

  • \(\lambda\ll1\): effect B dominates;
  • \(\lambda\sim1\): both effects matter;
  • \(\lambda\gg1\): effect A dominates.

This viewpoint is often more informative than the individual parameter values.

Nondimensionalization and Approximation

Suppose a dimensionless equation contains

\[ y^*=1+\varepsilon x^* \]

with

\[ \varepsilon=0.001. \]

If \(x^*\) remains order one, then

\[ \varepsilon x^* \]

is small relative to the leading term \(1\).

This makes the size of the correction explicit.

Nondimensionalization and Numerical Stability

Numerical methods often behave better when variables have comparable magnitudes.

If one variable is around

\[ 10^{12} \]

and another around

\[ 10^{-8}, \]

direct computation can be awkward.

Scaling both variables near \(1\) can improve interpretation and sometimes improve numerical conditioning.

Nondimensionalization Does Not Remove Meaning

Dimensionless variables may appear abstract, but their interpretation is usually direct.

If

\[ x^*=0.4, \]

then \(x\) is 40% of the chosen characteristic scale.

If

\[ t^*=3, \]

then three characteristic times have elapsed.

The dimensional meaning can always be recovered by rescaling.

Recovering the Original Variables

If

\[ x^*=\frac{x}{X_c}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{x=X_cx^*}. \]

If

\[ t^*=\frac{t}{T_c}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{t=T_ct^*}. \]

Nondimensionalization is reversible when the scales are known.

Common Error: Dividing by an Incompatible Scale

If \(x\) is a length, then the denominator in

\[ x^*=\frac{x}{X_c} \]

must also be a length.

Dividing a length by a time produces speed, not a dimensionless variable.

Common Error: Forgetting to Transform Derivatives

If

\[ x=L_cx^* \]

and

\[ t=T_ct^*, \]

then

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} \neq \frac{dx^*}{dt^*}. \]

The correct transformation is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{L_c}{T_c} \frac{dx^*}{dt^*} }. \]

Common Error: Scaling Only One Side of an Equation

Every occurrence of a dimensional variable must be replaced consistently.

If

\[ x=L_cx^*, \]

then all appearances of \(x\) in the model must use that substitution.

Partial substitution can create inconsistent equations.

Common Error: Choosing Scales That Leave Huge Numbers

A mathematically valid scale is not always a useful one.

If typical values of \(x\) are around

\[ 10^6, \]

choosing

\[ X_c=1 \]

leaves

\[ x^*\sim10^6. \]

A scale near the actual system magnitude would usually be more informative.

Common Error: Assuming the Dimensionless Form Is Unique

Different valid scale choices can produce different dimensionless forms.

One choice may emphasize initial conditions, another equilibrium behavior, and another numerical convenience.

Nondimensionalization is therefore partly a modeling decision.

A Reliable Nondimensionalization Procedure

  1. Write the dimensional model clearly.
  2. Identify the units and dimensions of all variables and parameters.
  3. Choose characteristic scales for the main variables.
  4. Define dimensionless variables such as \(x^*=x/X_c\).
  5. Rewrite each original variable in terms of its scale and dimensionless variable.
  6. Transform derivatives and integrals carefully when present.
  7. Substitute into the original equation.
  8. Divide through by a convenient common scale.
  9. Identify remaining dimensionless parameter groups.
  10. Choose scales strategically when they can eliminate parameters.
  11. Check that every remaining term is dimensionless.
  12. Interpret the dimensionless variables and groups.

Example: Full Algebraic Workflow

Consider

\[ y=ax+b. \]

Choose scales \(X_c\) and \(Y_c\):

\[ x=X_cx^*, \qquad y=Y_cy^*. \]

Substitute:

\[ Y_cy^* = aX_cx^*+b. \]

Divide by \(Y_c\):

\[ y^* = \frac{aX_c}{Y_c}x^* + \frac{b}{Y_c}. \]

Define

\[ \alpha=\frac{aX_c}{Y_c}, \qquad \beta=\frac{b}{Y_c}. \]

The dimensionless model is

\[ \boxed{y^*=\alpha x^*+\beta}. \]

Example: Full Differential Workflow

Consider

\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=-kx. \]

Choose

\[ X_c=x_0 \]

and

\[ T_c=\frac{1}{k}. \]

Define

\[ x=X_cx^*, \qquad t=T_ct^*. \]

Then

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{X_c}{T_c} \frac{dx^*}{dt^*}. \]

Substitute:

\[ \frac{X_c}{T_c} \frac{dx^*}{dt^*} = -kX_cx^*. \]

Cancel \(X_c\):

\[ \frac{dx^*}{dt^*} = -kT_cx^*. \]

Since \(T_c=1/k\),

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dx^*}{dt^*}=-x^* }. \]

What to Remember

Nondimensionalization rewrites a model in terms of pure numerical ratios.

  • choose characteristic scales for the important variables;
  • form dimensionless variables such as \(x^*=x/X_c\);
  • rewrite the original variables in terms of scales and dimensionless variables;
  • transform derivatives carefully;
  • substitute and divide through by convenient scales;
  • identify dimensionless parameter groups;
  • choose scales strategically to simplify the model;
  • interpret dimensionless variables as quantities relative to natural scales;
  • use small and large dimensionless groups to identify model regimes.

The central modeling habit is: replace absolute size with size relative to the system whenever that comparison reveals structure.
