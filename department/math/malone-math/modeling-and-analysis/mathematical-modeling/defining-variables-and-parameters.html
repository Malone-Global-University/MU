Defining Variables and Parameters

Once a modeler has identified the important quantities in a system, those quantities need precise mathematical roles. Some quantities are allowed to vary during the problem, while others are held fixed to describe a particular case. Clear definitions of variables and parameters make a model readable, testable, and much harder to misuse.

The Central Idea

A mathematical symbol is useful only when we know what it represents and what role it plays.

In modeling, two of the most important roles are:

  • variables, whose values are allowed to change within the model;
  • parameters, whose values are treated as fixed while we study a particular version of the model.

A useful first distinction is therefore

\[ \boxed{\text{variable} = \text{changes within the model}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\text{parameter} = \text{fixed for the model under consideration}}. \]

The words variable and parameter describe mathematical roles. They are not permanent properties of particular letters.

Start by Defining Every Important Symbol

Suppose a delivery company charges a fixed fee plus a price per mile. We might write

\[ C=F+rd. \]

The equation becomes meaningful only after the symbols are defined:

  • \(C\) = total delivery cost, in dollars;
  • \(d\) = delivery distance, in miles;
  • \(F\) = fixed delivery fee, in dollars;
  • \(r\) = charge per mile, in dollars per mile.

If we are studying one company's current pricing policy, \(d\) and \(C\) vary from delivery to delivery, while \(F\) and \(r\) are treated as fixed parameters.

A Variable Represents a Quantity That Can Change

A variable is a symbol representing a quantity whose value may change within the mathematical situation being studied.

In

\[ d=vt, \]

the distance \(d\) and time \(t\) may vary during a trip. If speed is being held constant, then \(v\) acts as a parameter.

The same equation could be used differently. If we compare several speeds, then \(v\) may also be allowed to vary.

A Parameter Describes a Particular Version of a Model

A parameter is a quantity that is treated as fixed while a particular model or scenario is analyzed.

Consider the linear model

\[ y=mx+b. \]

If \(m\) and \(b\) are fixed, then changing \(x\) produces changing values of \(y\).

For example,

\[ y=3x+5 \]

is one particular member of the larger family

\[ y=mx+b. \]

Here, \(m=3\) and \(b=5\) specify which line in the family we are using.

Parameters Often Control Shape or Behavior

Parameters frequently control important features of a model.

In

\[ y=mx+b, \]

the parameter \(m\) controls slope and \(b\) controls the vertical intercept.

In

\[ P(t)=P_0(1+r)^t, \]

the parameter \(P_0\) sets the initial amount and \(r\) controls the growth or decay factor.

Changing parameter values can therefore change the behavior of the model without changing its basic mathematical form.

The Same Symbol Can Play Different Roles

There is nothing inherently variable about \(x\), and nothing inherently fixed about \(a\), \(m\), or \(r\).

Roles depend on the question being asked.

Suppose

\[ A=\pi r^2. \]

If we are studying how the area changes as the radius changes, \(r\) is a variable.

If we are studying one circular tank with radius \(r=4\) meters while some other quantity changes, then that radius may be treated as a fixed parameter of the tank.

Parameters Are Not Necessarily Universal Constants

A parameter may be fixed within one modeling task even though it could have a different value in another task.

Suppose a machine produces items at a constant modeled rate of

\[ r=120\text{ items/hour}. \]

That value may be fixed for today's production model. Another machine, or even the same machine under different operating conditions, could have a different value of \(r\).

A parameter is therefore usually model-specific, not universally constant.

Mathematical Constants Are a Different Idea

A mathematical constant has a fixed mathematical value.

Examples include

\[ \pi, \qquad e, \qquad \sqrt{2}. \]

These differ from model parameters such as a tax rate, growth rate, spring stiffness, or fixed operating cost. Such parameters may be treated as fixed in one model while differing across systems or scenarios.

Independent and Dependent Variables

Many models distinguish between an independent variable and a dependent variable.

In

\[ y=f(x), \]

\(x\) is commonly treated as the input or independent variable, while \(y\) is the output or dependent variable.

The language reflects dependency:

\[ \boxed{x\longrightarrow f\longrightarrow y}. \]

We choose or observe \(x\), and the model determines the corresponding value of \(y\).

Example: Cost as a Function of Quantity

Suppose total production cost is modeled by

\[ C(q)=5000+18q. \]

Define the symbols:

  • \(q\) = quantity produced, in units;
  • \(C(q)\) = total cost of producing \(q\) units, in dollars.

In this model, \(q\) is the input variable and \(C\) is the dependent variable.

The numbers \(5000\) and \(18\) play parameter-like roles: they represent the fixed cost and modeled cost per unit for the production system being studied.

Inputs and Outputs

The language of input and output is often more useful than independent and dependent when a model has several quantities.

For example, a model for shipping cost might be written

\[ C=f(w,d,s), \]

where

  • \(w\) = package weight;
  • \(d\) = shipping distance;
  • \(s\) = service-level indicator;
  • \(C\) = total shipping cost.

The three quantities \(w\), \(d\), and \(s\) are inputs. The quantity \(C\) is the output.

A Model Can Have Several Variables

Models are not limited to one input and one output.

A simple revenue model might be

\[ R=pq, \]

where price \(p\) and quantity sold \(q\) may both vary.

If the business fixes the selling price and asks how revenue changes with quantity sold, then \(p\) can be treated as a parameter and \(q\) as the primary input variable.

If the purpose is to study how both price and quantity affect revenue, then both may be variables.

State Variables

In a dynamic model, a state variable records part of the condition of the system at a particular time.

Suppose \(P_t\) denotes the population after \(t\) years. A simple discrete growth model is

\[ P_{t+1}=(1+r)P_t. \]

Here, \(P_t\) is a state variable. Its value changes from one time step to the next.

If \(r\) is fixed during the analysis, then \(r\) is a parameter.

Time Is Often a Special Variable

Many models describe change over time. In such models, time is commonly represented by \(t\), although any clear symbol may be used.

For example,

\[ h(t)=h_0+v_0t-\frac{1}{2}gt^2 \]

might model vertical position under simplified gravitational motion.

In one use of the model:

  • \(t\) is the time variable;
  • \(h(t)\) is the dependent position variable;
  • \(h_0\) is an initial-position parameter;
  • \(v_0\) is an initial-velocity parameter;
  • \(g\) is treated as a fixed acceleration parameter.

Initial Values Often Function as Parameters

Dynamic models frequently need a starting value.

Consider

\[ P(t)=P_0e^{kt}. \]

The quantity \(P_0\) represents the value of \(P\) at the starting time:

\[ P(0)=P_0. \]

For a particular modeled system, \(P_0\) may be treated as a parameter. Different starting values produce different trajectories even when the growth parameter \(k\) remains the same.

Subscripts Can Organize Related Variables

Subscripts help distinguish related quantities without inventing an entirely different symbol for each one.

For example,

\[ x_1,x_2,x_3 \]

may represent three related variables.

In a time-indexed sequence,

\[ Q_0,Q_1,Q_2,\ldots \]

may represent the amount of a quantity at successive times.

The subscript is part of the notation. It should be defined just as carefully as the main symbol.

Parameters Can Also Carry Subscripts

Suppose a model has several regions, products, or populations. Parameters may differ across those groups.

For example,

\[ C_i=F_i+r_iq_i \]

might describe cost for production unit \(i\), where \(F_i\) and \(r_i\) are unit-specific parameters.

Subscripted notation is especially useful when a model must scale from one entity to many similar entities.

Units Are Part of a Variable Definition

Defining a variable without its units often leaves the model incomplete.

Saying

\[ t=\text{time} \]

is less precise than saying

\[ t=\text{elapsed time in hours}. \]

The unit affects the numerical value and may affect parameter values as well.

A speed of \(60\) miles per hour and a speed of \(1\) mile per minute describe the same physical rate but use different numerical values because the time units differ.

Units Are Part of a Parameter Definition Too

Consider

\[ C=F+rq. \]

If \(C\) and \(F\) are measured in dollars and \(q\) is measured in units produced, then \(r\) must have units of dollars per unit.

\[ \frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{unit}} \cdot \text{unit} = \text{dollars}. \]

A parameter's units often reveal what the parameter means physically or operationally.

Domains Belong in Variable Definitions

A variable is usually not allowed to take every real-number value.

Suppose \(q\) counts manufactured units. Then a natural domain might be

\[ q\in\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}. \]

If a machine can produce at most \(5000\) units per day, we may further restrict the variable to

\[ 0\le q\le 5000, \qquad q\in\mathbb{Z}. \]

The equation and the domain work together to define the model.

Continuous and Discrete Variables

A continuous variable can take values throughout an interval, at least at the level of the model.

Examples may include time, temperature, distance, or mass.

A discrete variable takes separated values, often counts.

Examples may include number of employees, number of vehicles, or number of completed transactions.

Correctly identifying this distinction can determine what mathematical tools are appropriate later.

Decision Variables

In optimization and planning models, some variables represent choices made by a decision-maker. These are called decision variables.

Suppose a company chooses how many units of two products to manufacture. We might define

\[ x=\text{units of Product A produced} \]

and

\[ y=\text{units of Product B produced}. \]

Resource limits, demand limits, and profit relationships can then be written in terms of \(x\) and \(y\).

Explanatory and Response Variables

In statistical models, related terminology is often used.

An explanatory variable is used to help explain or predict another quantity. A response variable is the quantity being explained or predicted.

For a simple model

\[ Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\varepsilon, \]

\(X\) may be an explanatory variable and \(Y\) a response variable. The quantities \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) are model parameters, while \(\varepsilon\) represents unexplained variation.

Later lessons will develop statistical and stochastic models more fully.

Parameters Can Be Known or Unknown

A parameter does not have to be numerically known in advance.

Suppose

\[ y=ae^{kt}. \]

The parameters \(a\) and \(k\) may be unknown quantities that must be estimated from observations.

During analysis, however, we may still distinguish them from the variable \(t\) because they describe the particular curve rather than the changing position along that curve.

Unknown Does Not Mean Variable

The words unknown and variable are not interchangeable.

Suppose a model is

\[ y=ax+b, \]

and we do not yet know \(a\) or \(b\).

Those parameters may be unknown numerically, but they can still be treated as fixed characteristics of the relationship we are trying to identify.

Conversely, a variable may have a known value at a particular moment while remaining a variable in the model.

Example: A Linear Temperature Model

Suppose the temperature of an object is approximated over a short interval by

\[ T(t)=T_0-kt. \]

We might define:

  • \(t\) = elapsed time in minutes;
  • \(T(t)\) = temperature in degrees Celsius at time \(t\);
  • \(T_0\) = modeled initial temperature in degrees Celsius;
  • \(k\) = modeled cooling rate in degrees Celsius per minute.

For one run of the model, \(t\) and \(T\) vary while \(T_0\) and \(k\) are parameters.

Example: Compound Growth

Consider

\[ A(t)=P(1+r)^t. \]

A careful definition might be:

  • \(t\) = number of compounding periods;
  • \(A(t)\) = account value after \(t\) periods, in dollars;
  • \(P\) = initial principal, in dollars;
  • \(r\) = growth rate per compounding period, written as a decimal.

For a fixed account scenario, \(P\) and \(r\) are parameters while \(t\) and \(A(t)\) vary.

Example: A Two-Parameter Family

The quadratic family

\[ y=a(x-h)^2+k \]

contains three parameters: \(a\), \(h\), and \(k\).

For each fixed choice of those values, \(x\) is allowed to vary and the equation produces a particular parabola.

Changing \(a\), \(h\), or \(k\) changes which parabola is being modeled.

Parameterized families let us study many related models with one formula.

From One Variable to a State Vector

More complicated systems may require several state variables at once.

Suppose a population model tracks three groups:

\[ S(t),\qquad I(t),\qquad R(t). \]

Instead of repeatedly listing the three quantities, we can collect them into a vector:

\[ \mathbf{x}(t)= \begin{bmatrix} S(t)\\ I(t)\\ R(t) \end{bmatrix}. \]

The vector \(\mathbf{x}(t)\) represents the state of the system at time \(t\). Parameters may then control how the components interact.

This is the same basic idea as defining a single variable, extended to a system with several changing quantities.

Parameters as a Parameter Vector

When a model has many parameters, they can also be collected into a vector.

If the parameters are

\[ a,b,c, \]

we may write

\[ \boldsymbol{\theta}= \begin{bmatrix} a\\ b\\ c \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then a general model may be written compactly as

\[ y=f(x;\boldsymbol{\theta}). \]

The semicolon is sometimes used to visually separate ordinary variables from parameters. This notation is useful, but it is a convention rather than a mathematical requirement.

Variables and Parameters in a Function

Consider

\[ f(x;a,b)=ax+b. \]

Here, \(x\) is the primary input variable and \(a\) and \(b\) are parameters.

Once parameter values are selected, such as

\[ a=2, \qquad b=-3, \]

the model becomes

\[ f(x)=2x-3. \]

The parameterized family has been specialized to one particular function.

Changing a Parameter Is Not the Same as Moving Along a Curve

This distinction is one of the most useful ways to understand parameters.

In

\[ y=mx+b, \]

changing \(x\) while holding \(m\) and \(b\) fixed moves to another point on the same line.

Changing \(m\) or \(b\) changes the line itself.

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{change a variable} \Rightarrow \text{move within one model},\\[4pt] \text{change a parameter} \Rightarrow \text{change the model instance}. \end{array}} \]

Context Determines Which Quantities Are Fixed

Modeling always involves a purpose.

Suppose

\[ D=rt. \]

If we ask, “How far will the vehicle travel over time at a fixed speed?” then \(r\) is a parameter and \(t\) is the input variable.

If we ask, “How does distance after two hours change with speed?” then \(t=2\) is fixed while \(r\) varies.

The algebraic relationship is the same. The modeling roles have changed because the question changed.

Choose Symbols That Support Reading

Symbols should make the model easier to understand rather than harder.

Common choices include:

  • \(t\) for time;
  • \(d\) for distance;
  • \(v\) for velocity;
  • \(P\) for population or principal;
  • \(C\) for cost;
  • \(R\) for revenue;
  • \(r\) for a rate;
  • \(n\) for a count or index.

These are conventions, not rules. The definition beside the symbol is what gives the symbol its meaning.

Avoid Ambiguous Symbol Choices

Some symbols can be difficult to distinguish visually.

For example, lowercase \(l\), uppercase \(I\), and the number \(1\) may be confused in some fonts. Likewise, lowercase \(o\) can resemble zero.

Clear notation reduces avoidable errors, especially in long models with many quantities.

Define the Reference Point for Time

A time variable should specify when \(t=0\).

For example,

\[ t=\text{years since January 1, 2026} \]

is more precise than simply writing

\[ t=\text{year}. \]

The reference point matters because initial values and time-dependent parameters are interpreted relative to it.

Define Whether Rates Are Fractions or Percentages

Rate parameters require especially careful definitions.

If

\[ r=0.06, \]

then \(r\) may represent a \(6\%\) rate expressed as a decimal.

Writing

\[ A=P(1+r)^t \]

requires \(r=0.06\), not \(r=6\), when the formula expects a decimal rate.

A variable or parameter definition should remove this ambiguity.

Parameter Values May Depend on Units

Consider the linear growth model

\[ Q(t)=Q_0+rt. \]

If \(t\) is measured in days, then \(r\) must describe change per day.

If we redefine \(t\) in hours, the numerical value of \(r\) must change so that the model continues to represent the same process.

Parameter values should therefore never be separated from their units and conventions.

Parameters May Represent Physical, Empirical, or Policy Quantities

Parameters can enter models for different reasons.

  • A physical parameter may describe mass, resistance, or stiffness.
  • An empirical parameter may be estimated from observed data.
  • A design parameter may be selected by an engineer.
  • A policy parameter may represent a tax rate, threshold, or benefit level.
  • A behavioral parameter may summarize a modeled response tendency.

The mathematical role may be similar even though the real-world interpretation differs substantially.

Parameter Values Should Be Interpretable

A good model definition explains what changing a parameter means.

In

\[ C(q)=F+cq, \]

increasing \(F\) raises the entire cost relationship by increasing fixed cost. Increasing \(c\) makes cost rise more rapidly with each additional unit produced.

This interpretation connects symbolic mathematics back to the modeled mechanism.

A Parameter Table Can Clarify a Model

In larger models, a parameter table is often more readable than defining everything in prose.

Symbol Meaning Units Role
\(t\) Elapsed time hours variable
\(Q(t)\) Quantity present at time \(t\) units state variable
\(Q_0\) Initial quantity units parameter
\(r\) Net change rate units/hour parameter

A table is especially useful when a model contains many quantities or when documentation will be shared with other people.

Write the Definitions Before Doing the Algebra

A reliable modeling habit is to define symbols before constructing the equation.

Suppose a parking garage charges \($8\) to enter and \($3\) per hour.

First define

\[ h=\text{number of hours parked} \]

and

\[ C=\text{total parking cost in dollars}. \]

Then write

\[ \boxed{C=8+3h}. \]

Defining the quantities first reduces the chance of attaching the wrong number or unit to the wrong symbol.

Example: Build the Variable Definitions First

A tank initially contains \(400\) liters of water and drains at a modeled constant rate of \(12\) liters per minute.

Define

\[ t=\text{elapsed time in minutes} \]

and

\[ V(t)=\text{volume of water remaining after }t\text{ minutes, in liters}. \]

The parameters are

\[ V_0=400\text{ liters} \]

and

\[ r=12\text{ liters/minute}. \]

The model is then

\[ \boxed{V(t)=400-12t}. \]

Variable Definitions Can Reveal Missing Restrictions

The formula

\[ V(t)=400-12t \]

produces negative values when \(t\) is sufficiently large, but a physical tank cannot contain negative water.

Solving

\[ 400-12t\ge 0 \]

gives

\[ t\le \frac{100}{3}. \]

So a physically meaningful time domain is

\[ \boxed{0\le t\le \frac{100}{3}}. \]

Careful variable definitions naturally lead to questions about assumptions, constraints, and domains, which will be developed in the next lesson.

Common Error: Using Symbols Without Defining Them

Writing

\[ y=12x+40 \]

may be algebraically correct but still be a poor model description if the reader does not know what \(x\), \(y\), \(12\), or \(40\) represent.

A model should make the interpretation recoverable from the documentation, not from guesswork.

Common Error: Treating Letter Choice as Meaning

A student may assume that \(x\) must be a variable and \(k\) must be a constant.

That is not generally true.

In one model, \(k\) may be a fixed parameter. In another, the purpose may be to investigate how outcomes change as \(k\) varies.

Always determine the role from the model's purpose and definitions.

Common Error: Confusing a Parameter with a Universal Constant

Suppose a model uses a conversion rate of

\[ r=0.82. \]

The model may treat \(r\) as fixed during one calculation, but that does not mean \(0.82\) is a universal mathematical constant.

A parameter is fixed relative to the model instance, not necessarily fixed for all systems, times, or scenarios.

Common Error: Omitting Units

Suppose a model says

\[ r=5. \]

Is that \(5\) dollars per unit, \(5\) kilometers per hour, \(5\%\) per year, or something else?

A numerical parameter without a definition and unit may be impossible to interpret correctly.

Common Error: Ignoring the Time Scale

Suppose an annual growth rate is used in a model whose time variable is measured in months.

The units are inconsistent unless the rate or time scale is converted.

A parameter and its variable must use compatible units and time intervals.

Common Error: Calling Every Number a Parameter

Numbers can enter equations for many reasons.

In

\[ A=\pi r^2, \]

the exponent \(2\) is part of the geometric relationship, and \(\pi\) is a mathematical constant. Neither needs to be treated as an adjustable model parameter.

A parameter is a quantity used to characterize a model or system, not simply any number appearing in a formula.

Common Error: Letting the Same Symbol Mean Two Things

Within a single model, a symbol should not silently change meanings.

If \(r\) is defined as a growth rate, do not later use \(r\) for radius unless the earlier meaning has been abandoned and the context is completely separate.

Stable notation is part of model quality.

Common Error: Defining a Quantity Too Vaguely

The definition

\[ P=\text{population} \]

may still leave important questions unanswered.

A stronger definition might be

\[ P(t)=\text{number of residents in the city at the end of year }t. \]

The improved definition specifies the population, the time relationship, and the measurement convention.

A Reliable Procedure for Defining Variables

  1. Identify each quantity that may change in the modeling task.
  2. Choose a clear symbol for each quantity.
  3. State exactly what each symbol represents.
  4. Include units.
  5. Specify time references or indexing conventions when relevant.
  6. Identify whether the quantity is continuous, discrete, or otherwise restricted.
  7. State the meaningful domain when it is already known.
  8. Identify input, output, state, or decision roles when those distinctions matter.

A Reliable Procedure for Defining Parameters

  1. Identify quantities that characterize the particular system or scenario.
  2. Decide which of them will be held fixed during the current analysis.
  3. Choose clear symbols.
  4. Define each parameter in words.
  5. Give its units.
  6. State its numerical value if known.
  7. State any meaningful range or sign restriction when relevant.
  8. Explain what changing the parameter would mean for the modeled system.

A Compact Modeling Notation Workflow

Before writing the main equation, a modeler can organize the notation in four passes:

\[ \boxed{ \text{quantities} \longrightarrow \text{roles} \longrightarrow \text{symbols and units} \longrightarrow \text{mathematical relationship}} \]

This prevents the equation from becoming a collection of unexplained letters.

Example: Full Variable-and-Parameter Setup

A subscription service charges a fixed monthly platform fee plus a charge for each active user.

Define

\[ n=\text{number of active users during the month}, \] \[ C(n)=\text{total monthly charge in dollars}, \] \[ F=\text{fixed monthly platform fee in dollars}, \]

and

\[ p=\text{price per active user in dollars/user}. \]

The model is

\[ \boxed{C(n)=F+pn}. \]

For one pricing plan, \(F\) and \(p\) are parameters, \(n\) is the input variable, and \(C(n)\) is the dependent output variable.

Example: Changing the Modeling Question Changes the Roles

Continue with

\[ C=F+pn. \]

If the company fixes \(F\) and \(p\) and studies customers with different user counts, then \(n\) varies.

If management instead studies how changing the per-user price affects the bill of a customer with exactly \(100\) users, then \(n=100\) is fixed and \(p\) is deliberately varied.

The symbol's modeling role follows the question.

where \(I_n\) is inventory at the start of period \(n\), \(s\) is a fixed replenishment amount, and \(d_n\) is demand during period \(n\). Identify the state variable and one parameter.

Practice Answers

Practice 1

The input variable is

\[ d=\text{distance traveled in miles}. \]

The dependent variable is

\[ C=\text{total fare in dollars}. \]

For the fixed fare schedule, \(4.50\) dollars is the base-fare parameter and \(2.80\) dollars per mile is the distance-rate parameter.

Practice 2

Define

\[ t=\text{elapsed time in minutes} \]

and

\[ V(t)=\text{tank volume after }t\text{ minutes, in liters}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{V(t)=900+25t}. \]

Practice 3

Changing \(b\) changes the vertical intercept while leaving the slope equal to \(7\). The line shifts vertically without changing its slope.

Practice 4

  • \(t\): input variable;
  • \(P(t)\): dependent or state variable;
  • \(P_0\): initial-value parameter;
  • \(r\): growth-rate parameter.

Practice 5

Since

\[ cq \]

must be measured in dollars, \(c\) must have units

\[ \boxed{\text{dollars per unit}}. \]

Practice 6

Since trucks are counted and there are at most \(18\), a reasonable domain is

\[ \boxed{n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,18\}}. \]

Practice 7

If speed is fixed and we study distance over time, \(v\) acts as a parameter. If time is fixed and we compare different possible speeds, then \(v\) is allowed to vary and acts as a variable in that analysis.

Practice 8

One clearer definition is

\[ \boxed{t=\text{years elapsed since January 1, 2026}}. \]

Practice 9

The state variable is \(I_n\), the inventory level carried from one period to the next. The fixed replenishment amount \(s\) is a parameter.

Practice 10

Define

\[ n=\text{number of service calls during the month} \]

and

\[ C(n)=\text{total monthly contract cost in dollars}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{C(n)=120+15n}. \]

For the stated contract, \(120\) dollars and \(15\) dollars per call are fixed parameters of the pricing model.

What to Remember

Variables and parameters organize the roles played by quantities in a mathematical model.

  • A variable is allowed to change within the current model analysis.
  • A parameter is treated as fixed for a particular model or scenario.
  • The same symbol can play different roles in different modeling questions.
  • An unknown parameter is still a parameter if it characterizes the model.
  • Units, domains, indexing conventions, and time references belong in definitions.
  • Inputs, outputs, state variables, and decision variables describe more specialized roles.
  • Changing a variable often moves within one model; changing a parameter often selects a different model instance.

The central habit is simple: define the quantities before relying on the equations.
