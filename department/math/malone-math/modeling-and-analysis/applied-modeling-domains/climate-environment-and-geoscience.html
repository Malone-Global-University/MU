Climate, Environment, and Geoscience

Earth systems evolve through exchanges of energy, water, carbon, sediment, momentum, and chemical constituents across the atmosphere, oceans, land, cryosphere, and solid Earth. Mathematical models organize these processes into stocks, flows, fields, feedbacks, and coupled differential equations. Some models describe a single watershed or aquifer; others represent global climate, tectonic deformation, atmospheric transport, or geologic hazards. The modeling objective is to preserve the physical mechanisms and scales needed for a particular scientific or operational decision.

The Central Idea

Many environmental and geoscience models can be organized around a balance law:

\[ \boxed{ \text{rate of change of storage} = \text{inputs} -\text{outputs} +\text{internal sources} -\text{internal sinks} }. \]

This structure applies to water in a reservoir, carbon in the atmosphere, heat in an ocean layer, sediment in a river reach, contaminants in soil, or magma in a subsurface chamber.

Reservoirs and Fluxes

A reservoir stores some quantity \(S(t)\). A flux measures the rate at which that quantity enters or leaves.

A general reservoir model is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dS}{dt} = \sum F_{\text{in}} - \sum F_{\text{out}} }. \]

Reservoir models are among the most reusable structures in Earth-system modeling.

Residence Time

If a reservoir contains amount \(S\) and has characteristic outflow \(F\), a simple residence-time scale is

\[ \boxed{ \tau=\frac{S}{F} }. \]

Residence time helps identify how quickly a reservoir can respond to changing inputs.

Water Balance

A simple watershed water balance can be written

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dS}{dt} = P-E-Q }. \]

Here \(P\) is precipitation input, \(E\) evapotranspiration loss, \(Q\) runoff or discharge, and \(S\) stored water.

More detailed models may add groundwater exchange, snow storage, irrigation, and human withdrawals.

Discrete Water Accounting

Over a finite time interval,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta S = P-E-Q }. \]

Every term must be expressed over compatible areas, times, and units.

Hydrographs

A hydrograph represents discharge \(Q(t)\) through time. Storm response, snowmelt, groundwater contribution, basin storage, and channel routing all influence its shape.

Models may predict peak discharge, total runoff volume, timing, or recession behavior.

Runoff Coefficients

A simple event model may approximate runoff as a fraction \(C\) of rainfall:

\[ \boxed{ Q_{\text{runoff}}=CP }. \]

The coefficient summarizes infiltration, land cover, soil state, slope, and other processes. It is therefore an approximation, not a universal constant.

Groundwater Flow

Groundwater movement is often modeled using Darcy's law:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{q} = -K\nabla h }. \]

Here \(\mathbf{q}\) is specific discharge, \(K\) hydraulic conductivity, and \(h\) hydraulic head.

Why the Negative Sign Appears

Groundwater moves from higher hydraulic head toward lower hydraulic head. The gradient \(\nabla h\) points toward increasing head, so the flow direction is opposite the gradient.

Groundwater Storage

In a confined aquifer, storage change may be related to changes in hydraulic head through a storage coefficient or specific storage parameter.

Pumping therefore alters both flow fields and stored groundwater.

Contaminant Transport

Dissolved contaminants can be transported by fluid motion, spread by diffusion or dispersion, and transformed by chemical or biological reactions.

A generic transport equation is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla C = D\nabla^2 C +R(C) }. \]

This combines advection, diffusion or dispersion, and reaction.

Advection

Advection moves a quantity with a bulk velocity field \(\mathbf{v}\):

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla C =0 }. \]

Advection dominates when transport by organized flow is much stronger than diffusive spreading.

Diffusion

Diffusion smooths concentration gradients:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D\nabla^2C }. \]

The characteristic diffusion distance over time \(t\) is approximately

\[ \boxed{ L\sim\sqrt{Dt} }. \]

The Peclet Number

A Peclet number compares advective and diffusive transport:

\[ \boxed{ \mathrm{Pe} = \frac{UL}{D} }. \]

Large \(\mathrm{Pe}\) indicates stronger advection relative to diffusion at the chosen scales.

Atmospheric Transport

Atmospheric models track temperature, pressure, moisture, momentum, aerosols, and trace gases across space and time.

Transport may involve advection by winds, turbulent mixing, cloud processes, radiative transfer, chemical reactions, and exchange with the surface.

Energy Balance

Climate models are fundamentally constrained by energy conservation. A simple planetary balance compares absorbed solar radiation with outgoing thermal radiation.

If \(S_0\) is incoming solar irradiance and \(\alpha\) planetary albedo, the globally averaged absorbed solar flux is

\[ \boxed{ F_{\text{in}} = \frac{S_0(1-\alpha)}{4} }. \]

Outgoing Thermal Radiation

A simple blackbody approximation is

\[ \boxed{ F_{\text{out}} = \sigma T^4 }. \]

Here \(\sigma\) is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant and \(T\) is absolute temperature.

Zero-Dimensional Climate Model

A simplified energy-balance model may be written

\[ \boxed{ C\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{S_0(1-\alpha)}{4} - \varepsilon\sigma T^4 }. \]

The effective heat capacity \(C\) controls response time, while \(\varepsilon\) represents the efficiency of outgoing thermal emission in the simplified model.

Equilibrium Temperature

At equilibrium,

\[ \frac{dT}{dt}=0, \]

so

\[ \frac{S_0(1-\alpha)}{4} = \varepsilon\sigma T^4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ T_{\text{eq}} = \left[ \frac{S_0(1-\alpha)} {4\varepsilon\sigma} \right]^{1/4} }. \]

Feedback

A feedback occurs when a change in the system alters a process that then influences the original change.

A simplified feedback structure is

\[ \boxed{ \Delta T \longrightarrow \Delta X \longrightarrow \Delta F \longrightarrow \Delta T }. \]

Positive and Negative Feedback

A positive feedback amplifies an initial perturbation. A negative feedback opposes it.

The terms positive and negative describe the direction of amplification or damping, not whether the effect is beneficial or harmful.

Ice-Albedo Feedback

In a simplified model, reduced reflective ice or snow can lower albedo, causing greater absorption of solar energy, which can further increase temperature and promote additional melting.

This is an example of a reinforcing feedback.

Radiative Forcing

Radiative forcing represents a change in the net energy flux entering or leaving the climate system relative to a reference state.

A simple temperature-response model may use

\[ \boxed{ C\frac{dT}{dt} = F(t)-\lambda T }. \]

Here \(F(t)\) is imposed forcing and \(\lambda\) is an effective restoring parameter.

Climate Response Time

For the linear model

\[ C\frac{dT}{dt}=F-\lambda T, \]

the characteristic response time is

\[ \boxed{ \tau=\frac{C}{\lambda} }. \]

Two-Box Climate Models

A more detailed reduced model may separate a fast surface layer from a slower deep-ocean reservoir:

\[ C_1\frac{dT_1}{dt} = F-\lambda T_1-\kappa(T_1-T_2), \] \[ C_2\frac{dT_2}{dt} = \kappa(T_1-T_2). \]

This introduces multiple response time scales.

Carbon Reservoir Models

The carbon cycle can be represented by reservoirs such as atmosphere, surface ocean, deep ocean, vegetation, soils, and sediments.

A generic compartment model is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{d\mathbf{C}}{dt} = A\mathbf{C} +\mathbf{u}(t) }. \]

The matrix \(A\) represents exchanges among reservoirs, while \(\mathbf{u}\) represents external inputs.

Coupled Carbon-Climate Models

Carbon concentrations can influence radiative forcing, while temperature can influence carbon uptake and release.

This creates coupled feedback between biogeochemical and physical climate processes.

Ocean Heat Storage

Ocean heat uptake strongly influences climate response because water has large heat capacity and ocean circulation moves energy across depth and latitude.

Reduced models often represent this with several interacting thermal reservoirs.

Sea-Level Models

Sea-level change can be decomposed conceptually into contributions such as

\[ \boxed{ \Delta H = \Delta H_{\text{thermal}} + \Delta H_{\text{land ice}} + \Delta H_{\text{land water}} + \cdots }. \]

Local relative sea level also depends on vertical land motion and regional ocean dynamics.

Spatial Climate Fields

Temperature, pressure, wind, humidity, precipitation, and ocean variables are fields:

\[ T=T(\mathbf{x},t), \qquad p=p(\mathbf{x},t), \qquad \mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t). \]

Spatial models require partial differential equations and boundary conditions.

Geophysical Fluid Dynamics

Atmospheric and ocean circulation models combine conservation of mass, momentum, energy, and constituent transport.

Rotation, stratification, buoyancy, pressure gradients, turbulence, and boundary effects all influence large-scale motion.

The Coriolis Effect

In a rotating reference frame, motion experiences an apparent Coriolis acceleration

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}_C = -2\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{v} }. \]

This effect becomes important in large-scale atmospheric and oceanic flows.

Geostrophic Balance

At large spatial scales away from strong friction, pressure-gradient and Coriolis effects can approximately balance.

Such scale-dependent approximations simplify full fluid equations while retaining dominant dynamics.

Scale Analysis

Geoscience models span enormous ranges of space and time. Characteristic scales might include

  • meters for soil layers;
  • kilometers for watersheds or weather systems;
  • thousands of kilometers for ocean basins;
  • seconds for turbulence;
  • days for storms;
  • years to millennia for climate and geologic processes.

Scale analysis helps identify which physical terms can be neglected.

Dimensionless Numbers

Dimensionless groups compare competing processes. Common examples in environmental and geophysical modeling include Reynolds, Peclet, Froude, Rossby, and Richardson numbers.

Their specific definitions depend on the system, but their purpose is to reveal which mechanisms dominate at the chosen scales.

Soil Moisture Models

Soil-water storage can be modeled by

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dS}{dt} = P-I-R-E-D }. \]

Terms may represent precipitation \(P\), interception \(I\), surface runoff \(R\), evapotranspiration \(E\), and drainage \(D\).

Unsaturated Flow

More detailed soil-water models describe how hydraulic conductivity and water retention vary with moisture content.

This produces nonlinear partial differential equations rather than simple reservoir balances.

River and Sediment Models

River systems transport both water and sediment. Sediment balance in a reach may be represented schematically as

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dS_s}{dt} = Q_{s,\text{in}} - Q_{s,\text{out}} +E-D }. \]

Here \(E\) and \(D\) represent erosion and deposition.

Erosion and Landscape Evolution

Long-term topography can change through uplift, erosion, sediment transport, landsliding, and diffusion-like smoothing.

A stylized elevation model may be written

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = U-E+\kappa\nabla^2 z }. \]

Here \(U\) is uplift, \(E\) erosion, and \(\kappa\) a smoothing coefficient.

Tectonic Deformation

Geodynamic models describe stress, strain, rheology, faulting, and deformation of the lithosphere and mantle.

Depending on time scale and material, rock may be approximated as elastic, viscous, plastic, brittle, or viscoelastic.

Stress and Strain in Geoscience

Stress describes internal force per area, while strain describes deformation. Constitutive relationships connect them.

The appropriate constitutive law depends strongly on temperature, pressure, composition, deformation rate, and time scale.

Elastic Earth Models

Short-term crustal deformation may sometimes be approximated elastically:

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\sigma} = \mathcal{C}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} }. \]

The tensor \(\mathcal{C}\) contains elastic material properties.

Viscous Flow in the Mantle

Over geologic time scales, mantle deformation is often represented using viscous or viscoelastic flow laws.

This connects geoscience modeling with the same continuum mechanics used for fluids and deformable materials.

Earthquake Source Models

Earthquake models may represent fault slip, stress accumulation, friction, and elastic-wave radiation.

Different models address different questions: long-term recurrence, rupture dynamics, ground motion, or hazard probability.

Seismic Waves

Seismic disturbances propagate through the Earth as elastic waves. A simplified wave equation has the form

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2\nabla^2u }. \]

Real seismic models distinguish wave types and spatially varying material properties.

Travel-Time Tomography

If wave speed varies spatially, observed travel times can be used to infer subsurface structure.

This is an inverse problem: measurements are used to estimate hidden material properties.

Volcanic Systems

Volcano models may represent magma pressure, conduit flow, gas exsolution, chamber storage, deformation, heat transfer, and eruption thresholds.

Strong nonlinearity and incomplete observations make uncertainty especially important.

Landslide Models

Slope stability compares resisting and driving forces. A factor of safety may be represented schematically by

\[ \boxed{ FS= \frac{\text{resisting strength}} {\text{driving stress}} }. \]

Values below or near \(1\) indicate potential instability under the selected model and assumptions.

Flood Models

Flood modeling couples rainfall, runoff, river routing, topography, channel capacity, and sometimes storm surge or tides.

Outputs may include flood depth, velocity, extent, arrival time, and duration.

Coastal Models

Coastal systems involve waves, tides, currents, sediment transport, sea level, shoreline geometry, and human structures.

Long-term shoreline change can require models that couple episodic storms with slower background processes.

Wildfire Spread

Wildfire models may represent fuel, moisture, slope, wind, heat transfer, and fire-front propagation.

Depending on purpose, the model may range from empirical spread-rate formulas to coupled combustion and atmospheric simulations.

Environmental Exposure Models

Exposure models track contaminants through air, water, soil, food, or other pathways.

A dose model may take the form

\[ \boxed{ D = C\times IR\times t }. \]

Here \(C\) is concentration, \(IR\) an intake rate, and \(t\) exposure duration, with additional normalization factors added as needed.

Environmental Risk

Risk models often combine hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and probability.

A generic structure is

\[ \boxed{ \text{risk} = \text{probability} \times \text{consequence} }. \]

More detailed models may integrate across many possible event sizes and locations.

Hazard versus Risk

Hazard describes a potentially damaging physical event or condition. Risk combines hazard with exposure, vulnerability, and consequence.

A large hazard in an unpopulated region can have lower societal risk than a smaller hazard affecting dense infrastructure.

Extreme-Value Models

Design and hazard analysis often focus on rare extremes rather than average conditions.

Flood peaks, wind speeds, wave heights, temperatures, and earthquake motions may be modeled using distributions tailored to extreme behavior.

Return Period

If an event threshold has annual exceedance probability \(p\), its return period is often defined as

\[ \boxed{ T=\frac{1}{p} }. \]

A return period is an average recurrence measure, not a schedule.

Probability over Multiple Years

If annual exceedance events are independent with probability \(p\), the probability of at least one exceedance in \(n\) years is

\[ \boxed{ 1-(1-p)^n }. \]

This is why a "100-year event" can occur more than once within a century.

Spatial Data

Environmental and geoscience models use spatial observations from gauges, satellites, radar, seismic stations, wells, surveys, lidar, and field sampling.

Measurements are irregular in space and time and often contain multiple sources of uncertainty.

Interpolation

Spatial interpolation estimates a field between observation locations.

Methods may use distance weighting, basis functions, splines, geostatistics, or physical constraints.

Geostatistics

Geostatistical methods model spatial dependence explicitly. Covariance or variogram structure describes how similarity changes with separation distance.

These methods can produce both spatial estimates and uncertainty measures.

Remote Sensing

Satellite and airborne sensors infer environmental properties from measured electromagnetic signals.

Retrieval algorithms are inverse models that translate radiance, reflectance, or other sensor quantities into temperature, moisture, vegetation, aerosols, elevation, or composition.

Data Assimilation

Data assimilation combines a dynamical model with observations to estimate the evolving state of a system.

A conceptual update is

\[ \boxed{ \text{forecast state} + \text{observation information} \longrightarrow \text{analysis state} }. \]

Weather prediction is a major application.

Initial Conditions

Time-dependent Earth-system models require an initial state. Small initial-state errors can grow, particularly in chaotic systems.

Initial-condition uncertainty is therefore distinct from parameter or model-form uncertainty.

Boundary Conditions

Spatial models require conditions at domain boundaries. These may specify temperature, flux, pressure, concentration, displacement, flow, radiation, or exchange with neighboring regions.

Boundary choices can materially influence model results.

Numerical Grids

Large environmental models divide space into cells or elements and approximate governing equations numerically.

Grid spacing determines which spatial features can be resolved explicitly.

Subgrid Processes

Processes smaller than the grid scale cannot be resolved directly. Their average effects are represented through parameterizations.

Examples include unresolved turbulence, clouds, soil heterogeneity, vegetation, or small-scale mixing.

Parameterization

A parameterization replaces unresolved processes with an approximate relationship involving resolved variables and calibrated parameters.

Parameterization is essential in large-scale models but is also a major source of model-form uncertainty.

Grid Resolution

Finer grids can resolve smaller structures but increase computational cost.

A finer grid also does not automatically make a model more accurate if forcing, boundary conditions, process representation, or parameter values are wrong.

Time Stepping

Numerical integration advances the state through discrete time steps:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathcal{M}_{\Delta t}(\mathbf{x}_n) }. \]

The step size must resolve relevant time scales and satisfy numerical stability requirements.

Coupled Models

Earth-system components exchange information:

\[ \boxed{ \text{atmosphere} \leftrightarrow \text{ocean} \leftrightarrow \text{land} \leftrightarrow \text{ice} \leftrightarrow \text{biosphere} }. \]

Coupling allows feedbacks that cannot appear in isolated component models.

Model Hierarchies

Environmental science uses model hierarchies ranging from conceptual box models to high-resolution coupled simulations.

Simpler models are useful for mechanism discovery, sensitivity analysis, and transparent reasoning. More complex models are useful when spatial detail and interacting processes materially affect the question.

Calibration

Calibration adjusts uncertain parameters so model output better matches selected observations.

Examples include fitting runoff coefficients, hydraulic conductivity, exchange rates, erosion parameters, or radiative parameters.

Inverse Problems

Many geoscience tasks estimate hidden properties from indirect measurements. Examples include subsurface velocity from seismic travel times, aquifer properties from pumping tests, and atmospheric composition from remote sensing.

Such problems can be ill posed and may require regularization or prior information.

Validation

Validation asks whether the model reproduces the real system adequately for its intended use.

A hydrologic model may reproduce annual runoff but fail at flood peaks. A climate model may reproduce global mean temperature while misrepresenting regional precipitation. A groundwater model may match heads while failing to predict contaminant travel time.

Verification

Verification asks whether the equations and numerical implementation are being solved correctly.

Useful checks include

  • mass and energy conservation;
  • unit consistency;
  • grid and time-step convergence;
  • comparison with analytical special cases;
  • symmetry and boundary checks;
  • independent budget calculations.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis examines how outputs respond to changes in parameters, forcings, initial states, and boundary conditions.

Important sensitivities may include flood peak to rainfall intensity, groundwater drawdown to conductivity, climate response to radiative forcing, or landslide stability to pore pressure.

Uncertainty

Earth-system models contain several distinct uncertainty sources:

  • measurement uncertainty;
  • initial-condition uncertainty;
  • parameter uncertainty;
  • boundary and forcing uncertainty;
  • scenario uncertainty;
  • model-form uncertainty;
  • natural variability.

Ensemble Modeling

An ensemble consists of many model runs using different initial conditions, parameters, forcings, or model structures.

The ensemble distribution provides information about spread, sensitivity, and conditional uncertainty.

Scenario Modeling

A scenario specifies a possible future pathway of external conditions or decisions.

Scenario output is conditional on those assumptions and should not be confused with an unconditional forecast.

Forecasting and Projection

A forecast attempts to predict a future state from current information. A projection describes the model response under specified assumptions.

The distinction becomes increasingly important as the forecast horizon grows and external conditions become less predictable.

Example: Reservoir Balance

Suppose a reservoir receives

\[ 12\ \text{m}^3/\text{s} \]

and releases

\[ 9\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}. \]

Ignoring other terms,

\[ \frac{dS}{dt} =12-9 \] \[ \boxed{ \frac{dS}{dt} =3\ \text{m}^3/\text{s} }. \]

Example: Residence Time

A lake contains

\[ S=5.0\times10^8\ \text{m}^3 \]

and has average outflow

\[ F=2.5\times10^7\ \text{m}^3/\text{year}. \]

Then

\[ \tau=\frac{S}{F} =\frac{5.0\times10^8}{2.5\times10^7} \] \[ \boxed{\tau=20\ \text{years}}. \]

Example: Darcy Flow

Suppose hydraulic conductivity is

\[ K=2.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{m/s} \]

and the hydraulic gradient magnitude is

\[ |\nabla h|=0.01. \]

The specific-discharge magnitude is

\[ q=K|\nabla h| \] \[ =(2.0\times10^{-5})(0.01) \] \[ \boxed{ q=2.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{m/s} }. \]

Example: Diffusion Length

If

\[ D=1.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s} \]

and

\[ t=10^4\ \text{s}, \]

then

\[ L\sim\sqrt{Dt} =\sqrt{10^{-6}\cdot10^4} \] \[ =\sqrt{10^{-2}} \] \[ \boxed{L\sim0.10\ \text{m}}. \]

Example: Return-Period Probability

A threshold with a nominal return period of \(100\) years has annual exceedance probability

\[ p=\frac{1}{100}=0.01. \]

The probability of at least one exceedance in \(30\) years is

\[ 1-(1-0.01)^{30} \] \[ \boxed{\approx0.260}. \]

So the simplified independent-year model gives about a \(26\%\) chance over thirty years.

Common Error: Closing a Budget Incorrectly

If observed storage change does not equal modeled inflows minus outflows, either a term is missing, measurements are inconsistent, or the model contains error.

Budget closure is a fundamental diagnostic.

Common Error: Mixing Spatial and Temporal Scales

A parameter measured at the laboratory scale may not apply directly to a basin, aquifer, or climate grid cell.

Upscaling can change the effective behavior of heterogeneous systems.

Common Error: Treating a Parameterization as a Physical Law

Parameterizations approximate unresolved behavior. They may work well within a calibrated range and poorly outside it.

Their validity must be evaluated for the scale and conditions of use.

Common Error: Confusing Weather and Climate

Weather describes short-term atmospheric conditions and evolution. Climate describes statistical behavior over longer periods, including means, variability, extremes, and distributions.

A model designed for one task is not automatically appropriate for the other.

Common Error: Treating a Return Period as a Schedule

A 100-year return period does not mean an event occurs exactly once every century. It corresponds to an annual exceedance probability under the assumed statistical model.

Common Error: Ignoring Natural Variability

Short-term variability can obscure long-term trends or forced responses.

Comparing a model with observations requires attention to both deterministic forcing and internal variability.

Common Error: Equating Model Agreement with Truth

Different models can reproduce the same observed output for different reasons. Agreement with one variable does not prove all internal mechanisms are correct.

Validation should use multiple independent observations whenever possible.

Common Error: Ignoring Uncertainty in Decisions

Infrastructure, hazard, and environmental decisions may be sensitive to uncertain tails rather than average conditions.

Decision analysis should therefore include ranges, scenarios, ensembles, or probability distributions where appropriate.

A Reliable Modeling Procedure

  1. Define the environmental, climate, or geoscience question.
  2. Identify reservoirs, fields, fluxes, sources, sinks, and relevant spatial and temporal scales.
  3. Write conservation laws before adding empirical parameterizations.
  4. Select constitutive, transport, radiative, hydrologic, or geomechanical relationships appropriate to the system.
  5. Specify initial conditions, boundary conditions, forcing, and external scenarios.
  6. Choose a model resolution capable of resolving the processes needed for the decision.
  7. Verify conservation, units, numerical stability, and grid or time-step convergence.
  8. Calibrate uncertain parameters against relevant observations.
  9. Validate against independent spatial, temporal, and process-level data where possible.
  10. Perform sensitivity, ensemble, scenario, and uncertainty analysis before using the model for consequential decisions.

What to Remember

  • environmental and geoscience models are built around reservoirs, fluxes, fields, and conservation laws;
  • water, energy, carbon, sediment, and contaminants can all be modeled through balance equations;
  • transport commonly combines advection, diffusion or dispersion, and reaction;
  • climate models are constrained by energy balance and include feedback among coupled Earth-system components;
  • groundwater, atmospheric, oceanic, and geodynamic models depend strongly on spatial fields and boundary conditions;
  • hazard models must be distinguished from risk models that also include exposure and consequence;
  • return periods are probability statements rather than event schedules;
  • numerical resolution and parameterization determine which processes are explicitly resolved;
  • data assimilation and inverse methods combine observations with models to estimate hidden states and parameters;
  • verification, validation, sensitivity, ensemble modeling, and uncertainty analysis are essential before Earth-system models support consequential decisions.
