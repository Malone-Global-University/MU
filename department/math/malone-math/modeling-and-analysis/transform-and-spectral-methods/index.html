Transform and Spectral Methods

Transform and spectral methods reveal mathematical structure by changing how a function, signal, system, or operator is represented. These lessons develop the connections among orthogonal expansions, Fourier and Laplace methods, convolution, frequency analysis, eigenfunctions, spectral decomposition, boundary-value problems, and computational transform techniques.

Lessons

  • Orthogonal Expansions — Represent functions using mutually orthogonal basis functions and projection coefficients.
  • Fourier Series — Express periodic functions as sums of sines, cosines, or complex exponentials.
  • Fourier Transforms — Move from discrete harmonic frequencies to continuous frequency spectra.
  • Discrete Fourier Transforms — Analyze finite sampled data using discrete frequency components and the FFT.
  • Laplace Transforms — Transform differential equations and initial-value problems into algebraic forms.
  • Convolution — Combine functions and signals through continuous, discrete, and transform-domain operations.
  • Frequency and Time Domains — Compare time-based and frequency-based representations of signals and systems.
  • Eigenfunction Expansions — Generalize Fourier-style representations using eigenfunctions of differential operators.
  • Spectral Decomposition — Decompose matrices and operators into eigenvalues, eigenspaces, and spectral components.
  • Filtering and Signal Analysis — Use transform and spectral methods to isolate, suppress, measure, and interpret signal components.
  • Boundary-Value Problems — Connect boundary conditions, eigenvalues, Green's functions, and transform methods in differential equations.
  • Computational Transform Methods — Implement transform and spectral techniques numerically using FFTs, discretization, spectral differentiation, and error control.

How the Ideas Connect

The sequence begins by building orthogonal representations, then develops Fourier and Laplace transforms as systematic changes of representation. Convolution and time-frequency analysis show how operations and system behavior appear differently across domains. Eigenfunction expansions and spectral decomposition extend the same principle to differential operators and matrices. The final lessons apply these ideas to filtering, boundary-value problems, and numerical computation.

Central Principle

A difficult problem in one representation may become much simpler in another. Transform and spectral methods exploit this fact by choosing coordinates, basis functions, or domains in which the important structure becomes easier to see and manipulate.
