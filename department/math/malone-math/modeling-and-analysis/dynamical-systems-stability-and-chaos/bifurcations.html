Bifurcations

A dynamical system can change qualitatively when a parameter passes through a critical value. An equilibrium may appear or disappear, exchange stability with another equilibrium, split into several equilibria, or give way to periodic behavior. These structural changes are called bifurcations. Bifurcation theory studies how families of dynamical systems reorganize as parameters vary and provides a bridge from local stability analysis to complex long-term behavior.

The Central Idea

A parameter can change more than the numerical value of a trajectory. It can change the qualitative structure of the dynamical system itself.

\[ \boxed{ \text{change parameter} \longrightarrow \text{possible change in dynamical structure} } \]

Such a qualitative transition is called a bifurcation.

What Counts as a Qualitative Change?

A bifurcation may involve a change in:

  • the number of equilibria;
  • the stability of equilibria;
  • the number of periodic orbits;
  • the geometry of phase portraits;
  • the number or shape of attractors;
  • the long-term behavior of typical trajectories.

Parameterized Dynamical Systems

A continuous parameterized system can be written

\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=f(x;\mu), \]

where \(\mu\) is a parameter.

A discrete parameterized system can be written

\[ x_{n+1}=f(x_n;\mu). \]

For each fixed value of \(\mu\), we obtain a particular dynamical system.

State Variables versus Bifurcation Parameters

The state variable changes as the system evolves through time.

The bifurcation parameter is held fixed while one trajectory is studied, but is varied when comparing different members of the family.

In

\[ x'=\mu x-x^3, \]

\(x\) is the state variable and \(\mu\) is the parameter.

Equilibrium Branches

Equilibria in a parameterized system satisfy

\[ f(x^\ast;\mu)=0. \]

Solving this equation can produce equilibrium values that depend on the parameter:

\[ x^\ast=x^\ast(\mu). \]

Plotting equilibrium position against parameter value creates an equilibrium branch.

Bifurcation Diagrams

A bifurcation diagram plots long-term states against a parameter.

For equilibrium bifurcations, the horizontal axis commonly represents

\[ \mu \]

and the vertical axis represents

\[ x^\ast. \]

Stable and unstable branches are often distinguished graphically.

Critical Parameter Values

A bifurcation occurs at a critical parameter value, often denoted

\[ \mu_c. \]

At this value, the qualitative organization of the system changes.

Linearization frequently becomes nonhyperbolic at the critical value.

Why Nonhyperbolicity Matters

At a hyperbolic equilibrium, small parameter changes usually preserve the local qualitative structure.

A local bifurcation therefore often requires an eigenvalue to reach a stability boundary.

In one-dimensional continuous time, this usually means

\[ f_x(x^\ast;\mu)=0. \]

In one-dimensional discrete time, critical multipliers often satisfy

\[ |f_x(x^\ast;\mu)|=1. \]

The Saddle-Node Bifurcation

One of the simplest bifurcations is the saddle-node bifurcation.

A standard normal form is

\[ \boxed{x'=\mu-x^2}. \]

The number of equilibria changes as \(\mu\) crosses zero.

Equilibria of the Saddle-Node Normal Form

Set

\[ \mu-x^2=0. \]

Then

\[ x^2=\mu. \]

Therefore:

  • if \(\mu<0\), there are no real equilibria;
  • if \(\mu=0\), there is one equilibrium at \(x=0\);
  • if \(\mu>0\), there are two equilibria \(x=\pm\sqrt{\mu}\).

Stability in the Saddle-Node Example

Let

\[ f(x)=\mu-x^2. \]

Then

\[ f'(x)=-2x. \]

At

\[ x^\ast=\sqrt{\mu}, \]

we have

\[ f'(x^\ast)=-2\sqrt{\mu}<0, \]

so this branch is locally asymptotically stable.

The Other Saddle-Node Branch

At

\[ x^\ast=-\sqrt{\mu}, \]

we have

\[ f'(x^\ast)=2\sqrt{\mu}>0. \]

Therefore this branch is unstable.

The stable and unstable equilibria meet at

\[ \mu=0. \]

Geometric Meaning of Saddle-Node Creation

As \(\mu\) moves from negative to positive values, a pair of equilibria is created.

One is attracting and one is repelling.

Reversing the parameter direction causes the two equilibria to collide and disappear.

The Transcritical Bifurcation

A transcritical bifurcation occurs when two equilibrium branches intersect and exchange stability.

A standard normal form is

\[ \boxed{x'=\mu x-x^2}. \]

Factor:

\[ x'=x(\mu-x). \]

Equilibria of the Transcritical Normal Form

Equilibrium requires

\[ x(\mu-x)=0. \]

Therefore the two equilibrium branches are

\[ \boxed{x^\ast=0} \]

and

\[ \boxed{x^\ast=\mu}. \]

Stability Exchange in the Transcritical Case

Let

\[ f(x)=\mu x-x^2. \]

Then

\[ f'(x)=\mu-2x. \]

At

\[ x^\ast=0, \]

we have

\[ f'(0)=\mu. \]

Thus the zero branch is stable for \(\mu<0\) and unstable for \(\mu>0\).

Stability of the Second Transcritical Branch

At

\[ x^\ast=\mu, \]

we obtain

\[ f'(\mu)=\mu-2\mu=-\mu. \]

Therefore this branch is unstable for \(\mu<0\) and stable for \(\mu>0\).

The two branches exchange stability at

\[ \boxed{\mu=0}. \]

The Pitchfork Bifurcation

A pitchfork bifurcation often appears in systems with symmetry.

A standard supercritical normal form is

\[ \boxed{x'=\mu x-x^3}. \]

Factor:

\[ x'=x(\mu-x^2). \]

Pitchfork Equilibria

Equilibrium requires

\[ x(\mu-x^2)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ x^\ast=0 \]

for every \(\mu\), and when

\[ \mu\ge0, \]

two additional branches appear:

\[ \boxed{x^\ast=\pm\sqrt{\mu}}. \]

Stability of the Central Pitchfork Branch

Let

\[ f(x)=\mu x-x^3. \]

Then

\[ f'(x)=\mu-3x^2. \]

At

\[ x^\ast=0, \]

we have

\[ f'(0)=\mu. \]

Thus the central branch is stable for \(\mu<0\) and unstable for \(\mu>0\).

Stability of the Outer Pitchfork Branches

At

\[ x^\ast=\pm\sqrt{\mu}, \]

we have

\[ f'(x^\ast) = \mu-3\mu = -2\mu. \]

When

\[ \mu>0, \]

this is negative.

Therefore both outer branches are locally asymptotically stable.

Why It Is Called a Pitchfork

In a bifurcation diagram, the equilibrium branches resemble a central stem that splits into two symmetric branches.

The shape resembles a pitchfork.

The symmetry

\[ x\mapsto -x \]

is reflected in the two branches

\[ \pm\sqrt{\mu}. \]

Supercritical versus Subcritical Pitchforks

In a supercritical pitchfork, stable outer branches are typically created as the central equilibrium loses stability.

In a subcritical pitchfork, unstable outer branches can exist on the opposite side of the critical parameter.

A standard subcritical normal form is

\[ x'=\mu x+x^3. \]

Normal Forms

A normal form is a simplified equation that captures the essential local structure of a bifurcation.

Important one-dimensional examples include:

\[ x'=\mu-x^2 \]

for saddle-node behavior,

\[ x'=\mu x-x^2 \]

for transcritical behavior, and

\[ x'=\mu x-x^3 \]

for a supercritical pitchfork.

Why Normal Forms Are Useful

Real models may contain many nonlinear terms and parameters.

Near a critical equilibrium, coordinate changes and approximations can often reduce the system to a much simpler normal form.

The normal form then reveals the essential qualitative transition without reproducing every detail of the original model.

Bifurcation Diagrams and Stability

A bifurcation diagram is more informative when it indicates which branches are stable and which are unstable.

A common convention is:

  • solid curves for stable equilibria;
  • dashed curves for unstable equilibria.

The exact visual convention should always be stated when a diagram is used.

Reading a Bifurcation Diagram

To read a diagram:

  1. choose a parameter value on the horizontal axis;
  2. move vertically to find equilibrium branches;
  3. identify how many equilibria exist;
  4. determine which are stable or unstable;
  5. observe what changes as the parameter crosses a critical value.

Bifurcation and Phase Portraits

A bifurcation can also be understood as a change in phase portrait.

Before the critical parameter, trajectories may be organized around one set of equilibria.

After the critical parameter, a different set of equilibria or attracting structures may organize the state space.

Discrete-Time Bifurcations

Discrete dynamical systems also undergo bifurcations.

Consider

\[ x_{n+1}=f(x_n;\mu). \]

Local stability of a fixed point depends on the multiplier

\[ f_x(x^\ast;\mu). \]

Critical events occur when the multiplier reaches the unit circle.

Important Discrete Stability Boundaries

In one-dimensional discrete systems, especially important values are

\[ f'(x^\ast)=1 \]

and

\[ f'(x^\ast)=-1. \]

Crossing \(+1\) can be associated with equilibrium-branch bifurcations.

Crossing \(-1\) often signals a period-doubling bifurcation.

Period-Doubling Bifurcation

A period-doubling bifurcation occurs when a stable fixed point loses stability and a stable period-two orbit appears.

Instead of approaching one state,

\[ x^\ast, \]

the long-term orbit alternates between two states:

\[ x_a,\quad x_b,\quad x_a,\quad x_b,\ldots \]

The Logistic Map as a Bifurcation Model

The logistic map is

\[ \boxed{x_{n+1}=rx_n(1-x_n)}. \]

Its nonzero fixed point is

\[ x^\ast=1-\frac1r. \]

The multiplier there is

\[ f'(x^\ast)=2-r. \]

Loss of Stability in the Logistic Map

The nonzero fixed point is locally stable when

\[ |2-r|<1. \]

This gives

\[ 1<r<3. \]

At

\[ r=3, \]

the multiplier reaches

\[ -1. \]

The fixed point loses stability through a period-doubling bifurcation.

Period-Two Behavior

After the first period-doubling, the attracting long-term behavior is no longer one fixed point.

Instead, the orbit can alternate between two values.

Those two values satisfy

\[ f(f(x))=x \]

but are not themselves fixed points of \(f\).

Further Period Doubling

As the logistic parameter increases further, the period-two orbit can lose stability and produce a period-four orbit.

Further bifurcations can produce periods

\[ 8,\quad16,\quad32,\ldots \]

in a cascade of repeated period doubling.

This sequence provides one route toward chaotic behavior.

Bifurcation Cascades

A bifurcation cascade is a sequence of repeated bifurcations occurring as a parameter changes.

The period-doubling cascade is especially famous because increasingly rapid structural changes can accumulate near a finite parameter value.

Bifurcation Does Not Mean Randomness

A bifurcation is a deterministic structural change in a mathematical model.

The equations remain precise.

What changes is the qualitative behavior generated by those equations as the parameter varies.

Local versus Global Bifurcations

A local bifurcation can often be detected by studying an equilibrium and its linearization.

A global bifurcation involves larger structures in the state space, such as:

  • connections between saddle points;
  • collision of periodic orbits;
  • large-scale changes in invariant manifolds;
  • changes in basin boundaries.

Hopf Bifurcation Preview

In a two-dimensional continuous system, a pair of complex-conjugate eigenvalues can cross the imaginary axis as a parameter changes.

Under appropriate conditions, this can create or destroy a periodic orbit.

This transition is called a Hopf bifurcation.

Eigenvalues at a Hopf Transition

Suppose

\[ \lambda(\mu) = \alpha(\mu)\pm i\beta(\mu). \]

A Hopf-type transition occurs when

\[ \alpha(\mu) \]

changes sign while

\[ \beta(\mu)\ne0. \]

The equilibrium changes from spiraling attraction to spiraling repulsion, or vice versa, and a periodic orbit may emerge.

Supercritical Hopf Bifurcation

In a typical supercritical Hopf bifurcation, a stable equilibrium loses stability and a small stable limit cycle emerges.

The long-term behavior changes from convergence to a point to sustained oscillation.

Subcritical Hopf Bifurcation

In a subcritical Hopf bifurcation, an unstable periodic orbit can interact with an equilibrium as stability changes.

Such systems can display more abrupt transitions and coexistence of different long-term behaviors.

Bifurcation and Oscillation

Bifurcations provide one mathematical explanation for how oscillatory behavior can appear as a control parameter is changed.

This occurs in models of:

  • mechanical vibration;
  • electrical circuits;
  • chemical reactions;
  • biological rhythms;
  • fluid systems;
  • feedback control systems.

Bifurcations in Population Models

In ecological models, changing growth, harvesting, migration, or interaction parameters can:

  • create or eliminate positive population equilibria;
  • change extinction stability;
  • produce population cycles;
  • shift basin boundaries.

Bifurcation analysis helps identify critical thresholds.

Bifurcations in Engineering

In engineered systems, a parameter may represent:

  • gain;
  • load;
  • damping;
  • feedback strength;
  • flow rate;
  • operating speed.

A bifurcation can mark the loss of a stable operating condition or the onset of unwanted oscillation.

Bifurcations as Thresholds

A bifurcation value often acts as a mathematical threshold.

On one side of the threshold, one qualitative regime exists.

On the other side, a different regime exists.

Recognizing such thresholds is important when a model is used for design, control, policy, or risk analysis.

Parameter Sweeps

A practical numerical approach is to vary a parameter over a range and repeatedly simulate the system.

For each parameter value, we can record:

  • equilibria;
  • long-term trajectory values;
  • periods;
  • maximum and minimum states;
  • stability indicators.

This process is called a parameter sweep.

Numerical Bifurcation Diagrams

For a discrete map, a numerical bifurcation diagram can be constructed by:

  1. choosing a parameter value;
  2. iterating the map many times;
  3. discarding early transient iterates;
  4. plotting the remaining long-term values;
  5. repeating for many parameter values.

This reveals fixed points, periodic windows, period-doubling cascades, and chaotic bands.

Transients versus Long-Term Behavior

Bifurcation diagrams usually focus on asymptotic behavior.

Early iterates may reflect the initial condition strongly and are often discarded in numerical studies.

The remaining points approximate the attractor associated with that parameter value.

Hysteresis Preview

Some nonlinear systems can have multiple stable states over the same parameter range.

The observed state may then depend on the direction in which the parameter was changed or on the system's previous history.

This phenomenon is called hysteresis.

Catastrophic Transitions

In some models, a small parameter change near a bifurcation can produce a large change in the long-term state.

The parameter change itself may be small while the resulting state change is substantial.

This is one reason bifurcation analysis is valuable for threshold and resilience studies.

Bifurcations and Structural Stability

A dynamical structure is structurally stable when small changes in the system do not alter its essential qualitative behavior.

Bifurcation points mark parameter values where structural stability can fail.

The system becomes sensitive to changes in its defining parameters.

Bifurcation versus Ordinary Parameter Dependence

Not every parameter change is a bifurcation.

Suppose

\[ x'=-a(x-1) \]

with

\[ a>0. \]

Changing \(a\) changes the rate of convergence, but the equilibrium remains stable and the qualitative phase portrait remains the same.

That is parameter dependence without a bifurcation.

Common Error: Calling Every Parameter Change a Bifurcation

A bifurcation requires a qualitative change in the dynamical structure.

A parameter that merely changes the speed of convergence or the precise numerical location of an equilibrium may not produce a bifurcation.

Common Error: Finding Equilibria without Checking Stability

A bifurcation diagram should not record only where equilibria exist.

Stability can change even when the equilibrium branch itself persists.

The transcritical and pitchfork examples make this especially clear.

Common Error: Ignoring Parameter Restrictions

An equilibrium branch may be algebraically real only over part of the parameter domain.

In

\[ x^\ast=\pm\sqrt{\mu}, \]

real branches exist only when

\[ \mu\ge0. \]

Model-specific restrictions may narrow the admissible range even further.

Common Error: Confusing a Bifurcation Diagram with a Time Plot

A bifurcation diagram does not usually place time on its horizontal axis.

It shows how long-term states depend on a parameter.

A time-series plot instead shows how a state changes through time for one fixed parameter value.

Common Error: Assuming Linearization Always Classifies the Critical Point

At the bifurcation value, the linearization is often nonhyperbolic.

That is exactly where higher-order nonlinear terms become essential.

The normal form captures those terms and reveals the actual local bifurcation structure.

Common Error: Confusing Period Doubling with Two Fixed Points

A period-two orbit consists of two states that map into each other:

\[ x_a\mapsto x_b\mapsto x_a. \]

Neither state needs to satisfy

\[ f(x)=x. \]

Therefore a period-two orbit is not the same as having two fixed points.

A Reliable Procedure for One-Dimensional Continuous Bifurcations

  1. Write the parameterized equation \(x'=f(x;\mu)\).
  2. Find equilibria from \(f(x^\ast;\mu)=0\).
  3. Express equilibrium branches as functions of \(\mu\).
  4. Determine where branches appear, disappear, or intersect.
  5. Compute \(f_x(x^\ast;\mu)\).
  6. Determine stability along each branch.
  7. Locate parameter values where hyperbolicity is lost.
  8. Compare the resulting structure with standard normal forms.
  9. Sketch the bifurcation diagram.
  10. Interpret the transition in the original model.

A Reliable Procedure for Discrete Bifurcations

  1. Write \(x_{n+1}=f(x_n;\mu)\).
  2. Find fixed points from \(f(x^\ast;\mu)=x^\ast\).
  3. Compute the multiplier \(f_x(x^\ast;\mu)\).
  4. Determine where \(|f_x|<1\) or \(|f_x|>1\).
  5. Locate parameter values where \(f_x=1\) or \(f_x=-1\).
  6. Check for new equilibrium or periodic branches.
  7. Iterate numerically when exact analysis becomes difficult.
  8. Plot long-term behavior against the parameter.

What to Remember

A bifurcation is a qualitative change in a dynamical system caused by varying a parameter.

\[ \boxed{ \text{parameter crosses critical value} \Longrightarrow \text{dynamical structure may change} } \]
  • saddle-node bifurcations create or destroy pairs of equilibria;
  • transcritical bifurcations exchange stability between branches;
  • pitchfork bifurcations often reflect symmetry breaking;
  • period-doubling bifurcations replace a fixed point with a two-cycle;
  • Hopf bifurcations can create or destroy periodic motion;
  • critical parameter values are often associated with loss of hyperbolicity;
  • bifurcation diagrams summarize long-term states across parameter values;
  • not every numerical parameter change is a bifurcation.

Bifurcation theory asks not only how a trajectory changes, but how the entire architecture of the dynamical system changes.
