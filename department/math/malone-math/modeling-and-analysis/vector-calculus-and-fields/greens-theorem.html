Green's Theorem

Green's Theorem connects what happens around the boundary of a planar region with what happens throughout the region itself. In one form, it converts circulation around a closed curve into a double integral of planar curl. In another, it converts outward flux across the boundary into a double integral of divergence. It is the two-dimensional prototype for Stokes' Theorem and the Divergence Theorem.

The Central Idea

Suppose a simple closed curve \(C\) encloses a planar region \(D\).

Green's Theorem says that certain line integrals around \(C\) can be replaced by double integrals over \(D\).

The basic pattern is

\[ \boxed{ \text{boundary integral} \longleftrightarrow \text{interior integral} }. \]

This is a local-to-global principle: local derivative information inside the region determines a total effect around its boundary.

The Circulation Form of Green's Theorem

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle P(x,y),Q(x,y)\rangle. \]

If \(C\) is the positively oriented boundary of a suitable planar region \(D\), then

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy = \iint_D \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dA }. \]

What Appears on the Right Side?

The quantity

\[ \boxed{ Q_x-P_y } \]

is the scalar curl of the planar vector field.

Therefore, Green's Theorem can be summarized as

\[ \boxed{ \text{circulation around the boundary} = \text{curl accumulated over the region} }. \]

Positive Orientation

For the standard circulation form, the boundary curve \(C\) must be positively oriented.

In the plane, positive orientation means

\[ \boxed{ \text{counterclockwise} }. \]

As you travel along \(C\), the enclosed region remains on your left.

Negative Orientation

If the same curve is traversed clockwise, the line integral changes sign.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \oint_{-C} P\,dx+Q\,dy = - \iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,dA }. \]

Orientation must always be checked before applying the theorem.

Why Curl Appears

Curl measures local circulation density.

Green's Theorem adds that local rotational tendency over the entire region.

Interior contributions along adjacent tiny boundaries cancel in pairs, leaving only the circulation around the outside boundary.

A Tile Interpretation

Imagine dividing \(D\) into many tiny rectangles.

Each small rectangle contributes a tiny circulation around its boundary.

Shared interior edges are traversed in opposite directions by neighboring rectangles, so their contributions cancel.

Only the outer boundary remains.

Example: Circulation Around a Rectangle

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle -y,x\rangle \]

and let \(C\) be the counterclockwise boundary of

\[ D: 0\le x\le2, \qquad 0\le y\le3. \]

Here

\[ P=-y, \qquad Q=x. \]

Therefore,

\[ Q_x=1, \qquad P_y=-1. \]

Thus,

\[ Q_x-P_y=2. \]

Green's Theorem gives

\[ \oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy = \iint_D2\,dA. \]

Since the rectangle has area \(6\),

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy = 12 }. \]

Why Green's Theorem Can Be Faster

Direct evaluation around a rectangle would require four separate line integrals.

Green's Theorem replaced them with one double integral.

Whenever the derivatives and region are simpler than the boundary parameterizations, the theorem can dramatically reduce the work.

The Flux Form of Green's Theorem

Green's Theorem also has a flux form.

For

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle P,Q\rangle, \]

the outward flux across a positively oriented boundary is

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds = \iint_D \left( P_x+Q_y \right) dA }. \]

Divergence on the Right Side

The quantity

\[ \boxed{ P_x+Q_y } \]

is the two-dimensional divergence.

Therefore, the flux form says

\[ \boxed{ \text{outward boundary flux} = \text{divergence accumulated over the region} }. \]

Outward Normal and Counterclockwise Tangent

For a positively oriented counterclockwise curve,

\[ \mathbf{T} \]

points along the curve.

The outward normal lies to the right of the direction of travel.

This relationship helps derive the flux form from the circulation form.

Flux Form in Differential Notation

If

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle P,Q\rangle, \]

then outward flux around a positively oriented boundary can be written

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C P\,dy-Q\,dx }. \]

Therefore, Green's flux form is

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C P\,dy-Q\,dx = \iint_D (P_x+Q_y)\,dA }. \]

Why the Flux Differential Looks Different

For a counterclockwise planar curve,

\[ d\mathbf{r} = \langle dx,dy\rangle. \]

Rotating the tangent displacement \(90^\circ\) clockwise produces an outward normal direction proportional to

\[ \langle dy,-dx\rangle. \]

Dotting with

\[ \langle P,Q\rangle \]

gives

\[ P\,dy-Q\,dx. \]

Example: Flux Through a Circular Boundary

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle x,y\rangle \]

and let \(C\) be the unit circle.

The divergence is

\[ P_x+Q_y = 1+1 = 2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds = \iint_D2\,dA. \]

The unit disk has area \(\pi\), so

\[ \boxed{ \text{outward flux}=2\pi }. \]

Circulation Form Versus Flux Form

Green's Theorem provides two related conversions.

Circulation:

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy = \iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,dA }. \]

Flux:

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C P\,dy-Q\,dx = \iint_D (P_x+Q_y)\,dA }. \]

A Practical Memory Aid

For circulation, think

\[ \boxed{ \text{curl} = Q_x-P_y }. \]

For outward flux, think

\[ \boxed{ \text{divergence} = P_x+Q_y }. \]

Conditions for Green's Theorem

In the standard introductory form, we assume:

  • \(C\) is a positively oriented simple closed piecewise smooth curve;
  • \(D\) is the region enclosed by \(C\);
  • \(P\) and \(Q\) have continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing \(D\).

More general versions exist, but these conditions cover most standard applications.

Simple Closed Curves

A simple closed curve returns to its starting point without crossing itself.

Examples include:

  • circles;
  • ellipses;
  • rectangles;
  • simple smooth loops.

Regions with Holes

Green's Theorem can also be applied to regions with holes, but boundary orientation must be handled carefully.

The outer boundary is oriented counterclockwise.

Each inner boundary is oriented clockwise.

This keeps the region on the left as the full boundary is traversed.

Positive Orientation for Multiply Connected Regions

For a region with one hole,

\[ \partial D = C_{\text{outer}} \cup C_{\text{inner}}. \]

Positive orientation means

\[ C_{\text{outer}} \text{ counterclockwise} \]

and

\[ C_{\text{inner}} \text{ clockwise}. \]

Why Singularities Matter

Green's Theorem requires the relevant derivatives to behave properly throughout the region.

If the field is undefined at a point inside \(D\), the standard theorem cannot be applied directly across that singularity.

Sometimes the region can be modified by removing a small neighborhood of the singularity and treating the new inner boundary explicitly.

Connection to Conservative Fields

Suppose

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle P,Q\rangle \]

has

\[ Q_x-P_y=0 \]

throughout a simply connected region.

Then Green's Theorem gives

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0 \]

for every suitable closed curve \(C\) in the region.

This supports the conclusion that the field is conservative.

Green's Theorem Explains the Curl Test

If scalar curl vanishes everywhere in a suitable planar region, then

\[ \iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,dA = 0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy=0 }. \]

The local condition produces a global closed-loop result.

Green's Theorem and Area

Green's Theorem can be used to compute area by choosing \(P\) and \(Q\) so that

\[ Q_x-P_y=1. \]

Then

\[ \iint_D1\,dA = \text{Area}(D). \]

Area Formula Using \(\oint x\,dy\)

Choose

\[ P=0, \qquad Q=x. \]

Then

\[ Q_x-P_y=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Area}(D) = \oint_C x\,dy }. \]

Area Formula Using \(-\oint y\,dx\)

Choose

\[ P=-y, \qquad Q=0. \]

Then

\[ Q_x-P_y=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Area}(D) = -\oint_C y\,dx }. \]

Symmetric Area Formula

Choose

\[ P=-\frac{y}{2}, \qquad Q=\frac{x}{2}. \]

Then

\[ Q_x-P_y = \frac12+\frac12 = 1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Area}(D) = \frac12 \oint_C (x\,dy-y\,dx) }. \]

Example: Area of a Circle from Green's Theorem

Let

\[ C: \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle R\cos t, R\sin t \rangle, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi. \]

Use

\[ \text{Area} = \frac12 \oint_C (x\,dy-y\,dx). \]

We have

\[ dx=-R\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=R\cos t\,dt. \]

Then

\[ x\,dy-y\,dx = R^2(\cos^2t+\sin^2t)\,dt = R^2\,dt. \]

Therefore,

\[ \text{Area} = \frac12 \int_0^{2\pi} R^2\,dt = \boxed{\pi R^2}. \]

When Green's Theorem Is the Better Route

Green's Theorem is especially useful when:

  • the boundary has many pieces;
  • direct parameterization is tedious;
  • the scalar curl or divergence is simple;
  • the enclosed region has easy double-integral bounds;
  • a line integral around a closed curve is requested.

When Direct Line Integration May Be Better

Green's Theorem is not always the fastest method.

Direct evaluation may be simpler when:

  • the curve has a very simple parameterization;
  • the double integral over the region is difficult;
  • the curve is not closed;
  • the theorem's smoothness or domain conditions fail.

Example: A Simple Closed Curve with Easy Curl

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle y^2, x^2 \rangle \]

and let \(C\) bound the unit square.

Then

\[ Q_x=2x \]

and

\[ P_y=2y. \]

Therefore,

\[ Q_x-P_y = 2x-2y. \]

Green's Theorem gives

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^1\int_0^1 (2x-2y) \,dy\,dx. \]

By symmetry,

\[ \boxed{ 0 }. \]

Symmetry Can Eliminate the Integral

Before integrating, inspect the integrand and region.

If the integrand is odd across a symmetric region, or if positive and negative contributions cancel by symmetry, the integral may be zero without detailed computation.

Green's Theorem as a Two-Dimensional Stokes Theorem

Embed the planar field as

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf{F}} = \langle P,Q,0\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \nabla\times\widetilde{\mathbf{F}} = \langle0,0,Q_x-P_y\rangle. \]

With upward unit normal

\[ \mathbf{k}, \]

we have

\[ (\nabla\times\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}) \cdot\mathbf{k} = Q_x-P_y. \]

This shows that Green's circulation theorem is the planar case of Stokes' Theorem.

Green's Flux Form as a Two-Dimensional Divergence Theorem

The flux form

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds = \iint_D \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} \,dA \]

is the two-dimensional analogue of the three-dimensional Divergence Theorem.

One Pattern Across Vector Calculus

Green's Theorem, Stokes' Theorem, and the Divergence Theorem all share the same structural idea:

\[ \boxed{ \text{integral over a boundary} = \text{integral of a derivative over the interior} }. \]

The dimension and differential operator change, but the architecture stays the same.

Common Error: Using Green's Theorem on an Open Curve

Green's Theorem applies to a closed boundary.

If the curve has distinct endpoints, the theorem cannot be applied directly unless the curve is completed into a closed loop and the added segment is handled explicitly.

Common Error: Forgetting Orientation

The standard formulas assume positive orientation.

If the boundary is clockwise, multiply the Green's-Theorem result by

\[ \boxed{-1}. \]

Common Error: Using Divergence in the Circulation Form

Circulation uses

\[ \boxed{ Q_x-P_y }. \]

Flux uses

\[ \boxed{ P_x+Q_y }. \]

Mixing these operators changes the theorem.

Common Error: Reversing the Curl Terms

In the circulation form, the correct scalar curl is

\[ \boxed{ Q_x-P_y }. \]

Writing

\[ P_y-Q_x \]

reverses the sign.

Common Error: Ignoring a Singularity Inside the Region

The field and required derivatives must behave appropriately throughout the region.

A singularity inside \(D\) can invalidate direct application of the standard theorem.

Common Error: Using the Wrong Boundary for a Region with a Hole

For a multiply connected region, the full boundary includes every boundary component.

The outer boundary is counterclockwise and inner boundaries are clockwise under positive orientation.

A Reliable Procedure for the Circulation Form

  1. Confirm that \(C\) is closed.
  2. Identify the enclosed region \(D\).
  3. Check the orientation.
  4. Write \(\mathbf{F}=\langle P,Q\rangle\).
  5. Compute \(Q_x-P_y\).
  6. Set up the double integral over \(D\).
  7. Evaluate.
  8. Reverse the sign if the original orientation is clockwise.

A Reliable Procedure for the Flux Form

  1. Confirm that \(C\) is a closed planar boundary.
  2. Identify \(D\).
  3. Use outward normal orientation.
  4. Write \(\mathbf{F}=\langle P,Q\rangle\).
  5. Compute \(P_x+Q_y\).
  6. Integrate over \(D\).
  7. Interpret the sign as net outward or inward flow.

A Reliable Theorem-Selection Checklist

  1. Is the problem in the plane?
  2. Is the curve closed?
  3. Is circulation or flux being requested?
  4. Are the derivatives simpler than the boundary integral?
  5. Is the field smooth on a region containing the interior?
  6. Does the region have holes or singularities that require extra care?

Example: Full Circulation Workflow

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle x^2-y, x+y^2 \rangle \]

and let \(C\) be the counterclockwise boundary of the rectangle

\[ 0\le x\le2, \qquad 0\le y\le1. \]

Here

\[ P=x^2-y, \qquad Q=x+y^2. \]

Compute

\[ Q_x=1 \]

and

\[ P_y=-1. \]

Therefore,

\[ Q_x-P_y=2. \]

Green's Theorem gives

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^2\int_0^1 2 \,dy\,dx. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ 4 }. \]

Example: Full Flux Workflow

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle x^2, 3y \rangle \]

and let \(C\) be the positively oriented boundary of the unit disk.

The divergence is

\[ P_x+Q_y = 2x+3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds = \iint_D (2x+3)\,dA. \]

By symmetry,

\[ \iint_D2x\,dA=0. \]

The remaining term is

\[ 3\iint_DdA = 3\pi. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{outward flux}=3\pi }. \]

What to Remember

Circulation form:

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy = \iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,dA }. \]

Flux form:

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C P\,dy-Q\,dx = \iint_D (P_x+Q_y)\,dA }. \]
  • The standard orientation is counterclockwise.
  • Circulation corresponds to planar curl.
  • Outward flux corresponds to planar divergence.
  • Green's Theorem applies to closed planar boundaries under suitable smoothness conditions.
  • Regions with holes require careful boundary orientation.
  • Singularities inside the region can invalidate direct application.
  • The theorem can convert difficult boundary integrals into easier double integrals.
  • Green's Theorem can generate useful area formulas.
  • It is the planar prototype for Stokes' Theorem and the Divergence Theorem.
