Stokes' Theorem

Stokes' Theorem connects circulation around the boundary of an oriented surface with the flux of curl through the surface itself. It extends the circulation form of Green's Theorem from flat planar regions to curved surfaces in three-dimensional space. The theorem is powerful because it lets us choose whichever side of the relationship is easier to compute.

The Central Idea

Let \(S\) be an oriented surface with boundary curve \(C\).

Stokes' Theorem says

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

The left side measures circulation around the boundary.

The right side measures the flux of curl through the surface.

Boundary and Interior

Stokes' Theorem follows the same general pattern as Green's Theorem:

\[ \boxed{ \text{boundary integral} = \text{integral of a derivative over the interior} }. \]

Here the boundary is a closed curve and the interior is an oriented surface.

The Boundary Curve

The notation

\[ C=\partial S \]

means that \(C\) is the boundary of the surface \(S\).

Examples include:

  • the circle bounding a disk;
  • the edge of a hemisphere;
  • the closed polygon bounding a planar patch;
  • the rim of a curved surface.

Orientation Must Match

The orientation of \(C\) is determined by the chosen normal orientation of \(S\).

The standard rule is the right-hand rule.

Point your right thumb in the direction of the chosen normal.

Your curled fingers indicate the positive direction around the boundary.

Right-Hand Rule

If the surface normal points upward, then the boundary appears counterclockwise when viewed from above.

If the normal points downward, the positive boundary orientation reverses.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{surface orientation} \longleftrightarrow \text{boundary orientation} }. \]

Reversing Orientation

If the normal is replaced by

\[ -\mathbf{n}, \]

then the boundary orientation must also reverse.

Both sides of Stokes' Theorem change sign consistently.

Why Curl Appears

Curl measures local rotational tendency.

The quantity

\[ (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot\mathbf{n} \]

extracts the component of local rotation aligned with the surface normal.

Integrating over the surface accumulates this local circulation density.

The Local-to-Global Interpretation

Imagine dividing the surface into many tiny patches.

Each patch has a small circulation around its edge.

Contributions along shared interior edges cancel because neighboring patches traverse those edges in opposite directions.

Only the circulation around the outer boundary remains.

Stokes' Theorem in Component Form

If

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle P,Q,R\rangle, \]

then

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \left\langle R_y-Q_z,\; P_z-R_x,\; Q_x-P_y \right\rangle. \]

Stokes' Theorem becomes

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S \left\langle R_y-Q_z,\; P_z-R_x,\; Q_x-P_y \right\rangle \cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

Example: Horizontal Disk

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle -y,x,0\rangle \]

and let \(S\) be the unit disk

\[ x^2+y^2\le1, \qquad z=0, \]

oriented upward.

The curl is

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \langle0,0,2\rangle. \]

Since

\[ \mathbf{n}=\mathbf{k}, \]

we have

\[ (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n} = 2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S2\,dS = 2\pi. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 2\pi }. \]

Stokes' Theorem Recovers Green's Theorem

Suppose the surface lies in the \(xy\)-plane and

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle P,Q,0\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \langle0,0,Q_x-P_y\rangle. \]

With upward normal

\[ \mathbf{k}, \]

the surface integral becomes

\[ \iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,dA. \]

Therefore Stokes' Theorem reduces exactly to the circulation form of Green's Theorem.

Green's Theorem Is a Special Case

This gives an important hierarchy:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Green's Theorem} \subset \text{Stokes' Theorem} }. \]

Green's Theorem handles planar regions.

Stokes' Theorem handles general oriented surfaces in three dimensions.

Choosing the Easier Side

Stokes' Theorem is not merely an identity to verify.

It is a computational tool.

If the line integral around \(C\) is difficult but curl and the surface are simple, use the surface integral.

If the surface integral is difficult but the boundary curve is simple, use the line integral.

Surface Choice Is Flexible

A major advantage of Stokes' Theorem is that the circulation around \(C\) depends only on the boundary curve and field, not on which spanning surface is chosen, provided the theorem's conditions hold.

Therefore, if two oriented surfaces \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) share the same boundary \(C\),

\[ \boxed{ \iint_{S_1} (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}_1\,dS = \iint_{S_2} (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}_2\,dS }. \]

The Simplest Spanning Surface

When a boundary curve lies in a plane, the simplest choice is often the planar region it encloses.

This can replace a complicated curved surface with a flat disk or polygonal region.

Example: Replace a Hemisphere with a Disk

Suppose \(S\) is the upper hemisphere

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2=R^2, \qquad z\ge0, \]

with upward-compatible orientation.

Its boundary is the circle

\[ x^2+y^2=R^2, \qquad z=0. \]

Stokes' Theorem allows us to replace the hemisphere by the flat disk

\[ x^2+y^2\le R^2, \qquad z=0, \]

as long as the boundary orientation matches.

Why Surface Replacement Works

Both spanning surfaces have the same boundary curve.

Stokes' Theorem says each curl-flux integral equals the same boundary circulation.

Therefore the two surface integrals are equal.

Stokes' Theorem with a Parameterized Surface

If

\[ \mathbf{r}(u,v) \]

parameterizes \(S\), then

\[ d\mathbf{S} = (\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v)\,du\,dv \]

for the corresponding orientation.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_D (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})(\mathbf{r}(u,v)) \cdot (\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v) \,du\,dv }. \]

Graph-Surface Form

If

\[ z=g(x,y) \]

and the surface is oriented upward, then a convenient vector area factor is

\[ \langle-g_x,-g_y,1\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iint_D (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})(x,y,g(x,y)) \cdot \langle-g_x,-g_y,1\rangle \,dx\,dy }. \]

Example: Stokes on a Tilted Plane

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle -y,x,z\rangle. \]

Let \(S\) be the portion of

\[ z=1-x-y \]

above the triangle

\[ D: x\ge0, \qquad y\ge0, \qquad x+y\le1, \]

oriented upward.

First compute

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \langle0,0,2\rangle. \]

Since

\[ g_x=-1, \qquad g_y=-1, \]

an upward vector area factor is

\[ \langle1,1,1\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot \langle1,1,1\rangle = 2. \]

The triangle has area

\[ \frac12. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 1 }. \]

Orientation Check for the Triangle

Because the surface normal points upward, the boundary must be traversed counterclockwise when viewed from above.

If the curve were given in the opposite direction, the line-integral answer would be

\[ \boxed{-1}. \]

Stokes' Theorem and Conservative Fields

If

\[ \mathbf{F}=\nabla f, \]

then

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \]

under suitable smoothness conditions.

Therefore Stokes' Theorem gives

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0 }. \]

Stokes' Theorem Explains Zero Closed-Loop Circulation

For a curl-free field on a suitable surface,

\[ \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = 0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0. \]

This provides another local-to-global explanation of conservative behavior.

Stokes' Theorem and Irrotational Fields

If

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \]

throughout a suitable region, then the circulation around any boundary of a surface lying entirely in that region is zero.

Domain topology still matters when making global conclusions about potential functions.

Surface Boundaries Must Match

You may replace the surface, but not the boundary.

If a substitute surface has a different boundary curve, Stokes' Theorem does not justify the replacement.

Closed Surfaces Have No Boundary

A closed surface satisfies

\[ \partial S=\varnothing. \]

Therefore, Stokes' Theorem gives

\[ \boxed{ \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = 0 }. \]

This is consistent with the identity

\[ \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})=0. \]

Flux of Curl Through a Closed Surface

For a sufficiently smooth vector field,

\[ \boxed{ \oiint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = 0 }. \]

Curl has no net source or sink behavior.

Conditions for Stokes' Theorem

In the standard introductory form, we assume:

  • \(S\) is an oriented piecewise smooth surface;
  • \(C=\partial S\) is its positively oriented boundary;
  • \(\mathbf{F}\) has continuous first partial derivatives on an open region containing \(S\).

More general formulations exist, but these assumptions cover the usual computational setting.

Singularities Matter

If \(\mathbf{F}\) is undefined or not sufficiently smooth on the surface or in a required surrounding region, direct application of the standard theorem may fail.

A singularity can also affect whether different spanning surfaces are interchangeable.

Example of a Surface-Choice Warning

Suppose two surfaces share the same boundary, but one passes through a point where the vector field is undefined.

We cannot automatically use Stokes' Theorem on that surface.

Surface replacement is valid only when the hypotheses hold for the chosen surface.

Circulation Versus Flux

The line integral

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} \]

measures circulation of the original field around the boundary.

The surface integral

\[ \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS \]

measures flux of the curl through the surface.

Do Not Confuse Stokes with Ordinary Flux

Stokes' Theorem does not generally equate

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} \]

with

\[ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS. \]

The correct surface integrand is

\[ \boxed{ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} }. \]

Stokes' Theorem and Vorticity

In fluid mechanics, if

\[ \boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla\times\mathbf{v}, \]

then Stokes' Theorem gives

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S \boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

Circulation around a loop equals vorticity flux through a spanning surface.

Stokes' Theorem in Electromagnetic Models

Stokes-type relations appear naturally in electromagnetic field equations, where circulation of one field can be related to curl throughout a surface.

The mathematical structure is the same even though the physical interpretation depends on the model.

Units

Curl introduces one spatial derivative, so

\[ [\nabla\times\mathbf{F}] = \frac{[\mathbf{F}]}{\text{length}}. \]

Multiplying by area gives

\[ [\mathbf{F}] \times \text{length}, \]

which matches the units of

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}. \]

This provides a useful dimensional consistency check.

Common Error: Using the Wrong Orientation

The boundary orientation must agree with the surface normal by the right-hand rule.

If they disagree, the answer has the wrong sign.

Common Error: Using \(\mathbf{F}\) Instead of \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\)

Stokes' surface integral is

\[ \boxed{ \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

It is not the ordinary flux of \(\mathbf{F}\).

Common Error: Keeping an Unnecessarily Complicated Surface

If the boundary curve is simple and another spanning surface is easier, Stokes' Theorem often permits the replacement.

Always inspect the boundary before parameterizing a complicated surface.

Common Error: Replacing the Surface with One Having a Different Boundary

Surface flexibility does not mean arbitrary surface choice.

The replacement surface must have exactly the same oriented boundary.

Common Error: Forgetting a Singularity

A surface crossing a singular point may violate the theorem's hypotheses.

Check the domain of the vector field before applying Stokes' Theorem.

Common Error: Treating a Closed Surface Like a Surface with Boundary

A closed surface has no boundary curve.

Therefore, the flux of a smooth curl through a closed surface is zero.

A Reliable Procedure for Using Stokes' Theorem

  1. Identify the boundary curve \(C\).
  2. Identify or choose a spanning surface \(S\).
  3. Determine the orientation.
  4. Check that the field is sufficiently smooth on the relevant region.
  5. Compute \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\).
  6. Choose the easiest valid surface.
  7. Find the oriented vector area element.
  8. Compute the curl-flux integral.
  9. Check the sign against the boundary orientation.

A Reliable Surface-Choice Checklist

  1. What is the boundary curve?
  2. Does it lie in a plane?
  3. Can a flat disk or planar patch replace the given surface?
  4. Is the field smooth on the replacement surface?
  5. Does the replacement use the same boundary orientation?
  6. Does symmetry simplify the curl-flux integral?

A Reliable Orientation Checklist

  1. Identify the stated surface normal direction.
  2. Point the right thumb with that normal.
  3. Follow the curl of the fingers around the boundary.
  4. Compare with the given curve orientation.
  5. Reverse the sign if the given orientation is opposite.

Example: Full Stokes Workflow

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle z, x, y \rangle. \]

Let \(C\) be the boundary of the unit disk in the plane

\[ z=0, \]

oriented counterclockwise when viewed from above.

Use the flat unit disk as \(S\).

First compute the curl:

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \left\langle R_y-Q_z,\; P_z-R_x,\; Q_x-P_y \right\rangle. \]

Since

\[ P=z, \qquad Q=x, \qquad R=y, \]

we get

\[ R_y-Q_z = 1-0 = 1, \] \[ P_z-R_x = 1-0 = 1, \] \[ Q_x-P_y = 1-0 = 1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \langle1,1,1\rangle. \]

The upward normal is

\[ \mathbf{k}. \]

Hence,

\[ (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{k} = 1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S1\,dS = \boxed{\pi}. \]

Example: Use a Replacement Disk

Suppose \(C\) is the circle

\[ x^2+y^2=4, \qquad z=3, \]

oriented counterclockwise when viewed from above.

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle -y,x,z^2\rangle. \]

Even if \(C\) is originally described as the boundary of a curved cap, we may use the flat disk

\[ x^2+y^2\le4, \qquad z=3. \]

The curl is

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \langle0,0,2\rangle. \]

Dot with the upward normal:

\[ 2. \]

The disk has area

\[ 4\pi. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 8\pi }. \]

What to Remember

Stokes' Theorem is

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]
  • The boundary curve is \(C=\partial S\).
  • Boundary and surface orientations must agree by the right-hand rule.
  • The line integral measures circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\).
  • The surface integral measures flux of \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\).
  • Different spanning surfaces with the same boundary can often be exchanged.
  • Planar Green's Theorem is a special case of Stokes' Theorem.
  • Curl-free fields have zero circulation around suitable boundaries.
  • Closed surfaces have zero net flux of a smooth curl.
  • Domain and smoothness conditions must be checked before replacing surfaces.
