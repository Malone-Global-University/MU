Flux

Flux measures how much of a vector field passes through an oriented surface. A field pointing directly through the surface contributes strongly, a field tangent to the surface contributes nothing, and reversing the surface orientation reverses the sign. Flux is one of the central accumulation ideas in vector calculus and connects surface integrals, fluid flow, electric and gravitational fields, and the Divergence Theorem.

The Central Idea

Suppose a vector field

\[ \mathbf{F} \]

passes through an oriented surface \(S\).

We want to measure the field component that crosses the surface rather than the component that slides along it.

The local quantity is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} } \]

where \(\mathbf{n}\) is the chosen unit normal.

Flux accumulates this normal component across the entire surface.

The Flux Integral

The flux of a vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) through an oriented surface \(S\) is

\[ \boxed{ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

This is a vector surface integral, but the final result is a scalar.

What the Dot Product Does

Since

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = \|\mathbf{F}\| \|\mathbf{n}\| \cos\theta \]

and

\[ \|\mathbf{n}\|=1, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = \|\mathbf{F}\|\cos\theta }. \]

Thus the dot product extracts the component of the vector field normal to the surface.

Maximum Positive Flux Density

If the vector field points in exactly the same direction as the chosen normal, then

\[ \theta=0 \]

and

\[ \cos\theta=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = \|\mathbf{F}\|. \]

The field passes directly through the surface in the positive orientation.

Negative Flux Density

If the field points opposite the chosen normal, then

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}<0. \]

This means the field passes through the surface in the negative orientation.

Zero Flux Density

If the vector field is tangent to the surface, then it is perpendicular to the normal:

\[ \theta=\frac{\pi}{2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0 }. \]

The field moves along the surface rather than through it.

Orientation Is Essential

Flux requires an oriented surface.

If the normal \(\mathbf{n}\) is replaced by

\[ -\mathbf{n}, \]

then

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot(-\mathbf{n}) = - \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{reversing orientation reverses flux sign} }. \]

Vector Area Element

Recall the oriented vector area element

\[ d\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{n}\,dS. \]

Then the flux integral can be written compactly as

\[ \boxed{ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S} }. \]

Flux Through a Parameterized Surface

Suppose

\[ \mathbf{r}(u,v) \]

parameterizes \(S\).

If

\[ \mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v \]

has the desired orientation, then

\[ \boxed{ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iint_D \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(u,v)) \cdot (\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v) \,du\,dv }. \]

Why We Do Not Normalize the Cross Product

The cross product can be written

\[ \mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v = \mathbf{n} \|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\|. \]

Therefore,

\[ (\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v) \,du\,dv = \mathbf{n}\,dS. \]

The cross product already combines orientation and the correct area scale.

Example: Flux Through a Plane Patch

Let

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle1,2,3\rangle \]

and let the surface be

\[ \mathbf{r}(u,v) = \langle u,v,0\rangle, \]

over

\[ 0\le u\le2, \qquad 0\le v\le1. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf{r}_u = \langle1,0,0\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{r}_v = \langle0,1,0\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v = \langle0,0,1\rangle. \]

The dot product is

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot (\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v) = 3. \]

Hence,

\[ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \int_0^2\int_0^1 3 \,dv\,du = \boxed{6}. \]

Flux Through Graph Surfaces

Suppose the surface is

\[ z=g(x,y). \]

A natural parameterization is

\[ \mathbf{r}(x,y) = \langle x,y,g(x,y)\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf{r}_x\times\mathbf{r}_y = \langle-g_x,-g_y,1\rangle. \]

This vector points upward because its \(z\)-component is positive.

Upward Flux Through a Graph

If

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle P,Q,R\rangle \]

and \(S\) is

\[ z=g(x,y) \]

with upward orientation, then

\[ \boxed{ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iint_D \mathbf{F}(x,y,g(x,y)) \cdot \langle-g_x,-g_y,1\rangle \,dx\,dy }. \]

Component Formula for Upward Flux

Expanding the dot product gives

\[ \boxed{ \iint_D \left( -Pg_x-Qg_y+R \right) \,dx\,dy } \]

where \(P\), \(Q\), and \(R\) are evaluated on the surface

\[ z=g(x,y). \]

Downward Flux Through a Graph

For downward orientation, use

\[ \langle g_x,g_y,-1\rangle. \]

Therefore, downward flux is the negative of upward flux:

\[ \boxed{ \Phi_{\text{down}} = - \Phi_{\text{up}} }. \]

Example: Upward Flux Through \(z=x+y\)

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle x,y,1\rangle \]

and let

\[ z=x+y \]

over

\[ 0\le x\le1, \qquad 0\le y\le1. \]

Here

\[ g_x=1, \qquad g_y=1. \]

The upward vector area factor is

\[ \langle-1,-1,1\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot \langle-1,-1,1\rangle = -x-y+1. \]

Thus,

\[ \Phi = \int_0^1\int_0^1 (1-x-y) \,dy\,dx. \]

Evaluating,

\[ \boxed{ \Phi=0 }. \]

Flux Through a Flat Coordinate Plane

Coordinate planes produce especially simple normals.

For the \(xy\)-plane,

\[ \mathbf{n} = \pm\mathbf{k}. \]

For the \(yz\)-plane,

\[ \mathbf{n} = \pm\mathbf{i}. \]

For the \(xz\)-plane,

\[ \mathbf{n} = \pm\mathbf{j}. \]

Example: Flux Through the \(yz\)-Plane

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle2x+1,y,z\rangle \]

and let \(S\) be the rectangle in the \(yz\)-plane

\[ x=0, \qquad 0\le y\le2, \qquad 0\le z\le3. \]

Choose the positive \(x\)-direction normal

\[ \mathbf{n}=\mathbf{i}. \]

On the surface,

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = 1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \Phi = (1)(2)(3) = \boxed{6}. \]

Flux Through a Cylinder

The lateral surface of a cylinder of radius \(R\) can be parameterized by

\[ \mathbf{r}(\theta,z) = \langle R\cos\theta, R\sin\theta, z \rangle. \]

A convenient outward vector area element is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}_\theta\times\mathbf{r}_z = \langle R\cos\theta, R\sin\theta, 0 \rangle }. \]

Example: Radial Flux Through a Cylinder

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle x,y,0\rangle \]

and consider the lateral surface of the cylinder

\[ x^2+y^2=R^2, \qquad 0\le z\le H. \]

On the surface,

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle R\cos\theta, R\sin\theta, 0 \rangle. \]

Dotting with the outward vector area element gives

\[ R^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \Phi = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^H R^2 \,dz\,d\theta. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \Phi = 2\pi R^2H }. \]

Flux Through a Sphere

For a sphere of radius \(R\) centered at the origin, the outward unit normal is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{n} = \frac{\langle x,y,z\rangle}{R} }. \]

This radial geometry often makes flux calculations especially simple.

Example: Radial Flux Through a Sphere

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle x,y,z\rangle \]

on the sphere

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2=R^2. \]

Since

\[ \mathbf{n} = \frac{\langle x,y,z\rangle}{R}, \]

we have

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = \frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{R} = R. \]

Therefore,

\[ \Phi = R \iint_S dS. \]

Since the sphere has area \(4\pi R^2\),

\[ \boxed{ \Phi = 4\pi R^3 }. \]

Closed-Surface Flux

If \(S\) is closed, flux is often written

\[ \boxed{ \oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

The standard orientation is outward.

Positive closed-surface flux means more field leaves the enclosed region than enters it.

Outward Versus Inward Flux

For a closed surface,

\[ \boxed{ \Phi_{\text{inward}} = - \Phi_{\text{outward}} }. \]

A problem should specify orientation, or use the standard convention that closed surfaces are oriented outward.

Flux and Fluid Flow

If

\[ \mathbf{v}(x,y,z) \]

is a fluid velocity field, then

\[ \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n} \]

measures normal velocity through the surface.

Integrating across \(S\) gives volumetric flow rate when the velocity field and units are appropriate.

Mass Flux

If a fluid has mass density

\[ \rho(x,y,z) \]

and velocity field

\[ \mathbf{v}, \]

then the mass-flux vector is

\[ \rho\mathbf{v}. \]

The rate of mass crossing an oriented surface is

\[ \boxed{ \iint_S \rho\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

Flux and Electric Fields

Electric flux uses an electric field

\[ \mathbf{E} \]

and an oriented surface:

\[ \boxed{ \Phi_E = \iint_S \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

The geometric meaning is the same: measure the field component passing through the surface.

Flux and Gravitational Fields

Gravitational vector fields can also be integrated through surfaces.

The sign and interpretation depend on the chosen orientation and the fact that idealized gravitational fields generally point toward mass sources.

Flux and Divergence

Divergence measures local source or sink behavior:

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}. \]

Flux measures net field crossing a finite surface:

\[ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS. \]

The Divergence Theorem will connect these two ideas exactly.

Local Flux Density

Divergence can be viewed as the limiting outward flux per unit volume:

\[ \boxed{ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = \lim_{V\to0} \frac{ \text{outward flux through }\partial V }{ \text{volume of }V } }. \]

This interpretation explains why positive divergence is source-like and negative divergence is sink-like.

Constant Fields and Closed Surfaces

A constant vector field has zero divergence.

Intuitively, whatever enters one side of a closed surface exits elsewhere.

Therefore, its net outward flux through a closed surface is zero.

Example: Constant Field Through a Closed Box

Let

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle1,0,0\rangle. \]

Through the right face of a rectangular box, outward flux is positive.

Through the left face, outward flux is equally negative.

Through the remaining faces, the field is tangent.

Therefore the total outward flux is

\[ \boxed{0}. \]

Flux Through Piecewise Surfaces

If a surface is built from pieces,

\[ S=S_1\cup S_2\cup\cdots\cup S_n, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \sum_{k=1}^n \iint_{S_k} \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}_k\,dS }. \]

Each piece must use an orientation consistent with the overall surface.

Example: Closed Cylinder as Three Pieces

A closed cylinder consists of:

  1. the lateral surface;
  2. the top disk;
  3. the bottom disk.

Direct flux calculation requires summing the flux through all three pieces.

The Divergence Theorem will often provide a more efficient alternative.

Surface Symmetry Can Simplify Flux

Before parameterizing, inspect the relationship between the field and the surface.

If

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} \]

is constant on the surface, then

\[ \boxed{ \Phi = (\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}) \times \text{surface area} }. \]

This can eliminate most of the integration.

Radial Symmetry

For a radial field and a sphere centered at the same origin, the field is parallel or antiparallel to the surface normal.

This makes

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} \]

especially simple.

Radial symmetry is one of the most useful patterns in flux problems.

Units of Flux

Since flux integrates a field across area,

\[ \boxed{ [\text{flux}] = [\mathbf{F}] \times [\text{area}] }. \]

The exact physical unit depends on what the vector field represents.

Common Error: Using \(\|\mathbf{F}\|\) Instead of \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\)

Flux measures the normal component of the field, not the full magnitude.

The correct local quantity is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} }. \]

A large vector tangent to the surface contributes zero flux.

Common Error: Forgetting Orientation

Flux is signed.

The same field and same geometric surface can produce opposite flux values under opposite orientations.

Common Error: Normalizing the Cross Product Unnecessarily

In a parameterized flux integral,

\[ \mathbf{F} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v) \,du\,dv \]

already includes the area scaling.

Normalizing the cross product and forgetting to multiply by \(dS\) loses that scaling factor.

Common Error: Using the Wrong Cross-Product Order

Since

\[ \mathbf{r}_v\times\mathbf{r}_u = - \mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v, \]

changing the cross-product order changes the orientation and therefore the flux sign.

Common Error: Forgetting to Evaluate the Field on the Surface

If the surface is parameterized by

\[ \mathbf{r}(u,v), \]

then use

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(u,v)) } \]

inside the parameter integral.

Common Error: Confusing Flux with Surface Area

Surface area is

\[ \iint_S 1\,dS. \]

Flux is

\[ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS. \]

The second integral depends on both the vector field and surface orientation.

A Reliable Procedure for Parameterized Flux Integrals

  1. Parameterize the surface as \(\mathbf{r}(u,v)\).
  2. Determine the parameter domain \(D\).
  3. Compute \(\mathbf{r}_u\) and \(\mathbf{r}_v\).
  4. Compute \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\).
  5. Check whether the cross product has the requested orientation.
  6. Reverse its sign if necessary.
  7. Evaluate \(\mathbf{F}\) on the surface.
  8. Compute the dot product.
  9. Integrate over \(D\).
  10. Interpret the sign and units.

A Reliable Procedure for Graph-Surface Flux

  1. Write the surface as \(z=g(x,y)\) when convenient.
  2. Identify the projection region \(D\).
  3. Compute \(g_x\) and \(g_y\).
  4. For upward orientation, use \(\langle-g_x,-g_y,1\rangle\).
  5. For downward orientation, reverse the sign.
  6. Replace \(z\) in the field by \(g(x,y)\).
  7. Compute the dot product.
  8. Integrate over \(D\).

A Reliable Procedure for Closed-Surface Flux

  1. Confirm the surface is closed.
  2. Use outward orientation unless told otherwise.
  3. Inspect symmetry before setting up integrals.
  4. If computing directly, divide the surface into manageable pieces.
  5. Compute outward flux on every piece.
  6. Add all contributions.
  7. Later, compare whether the Divergence Theorem offers a shorter route.

Example: Full Parameterized Flux Workflow

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle x,y,z\rangle \]

and let \(S\) be the surface

\[ \mathbf{r}(u,v) = \langle u,v,u+v\rangle \]

over

\[ 0\le u\le1, \qquad 0\le v\le1, \]

oriented upward.

Compute tangent vectors:

\[ \mathbf{r}_u = \langle1,0,1\rangle, \] \[ \mathbf{r}_v = \langle0,1,1\rangle. \]

Their cross product is

\[ \mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v = \langle-1,-1,1\rangle. \]

Its \(z\)-component is positive, so the orientation is upward.

On the surface,

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle u,v,u+v\rangle. \]

Dot:

\[ \langle u,v,u+v\rangle \cdot \langle-1,-1,1\rangle = -u-v+u+v = 0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \Phi=0 }. \]

Example: Full Graph-Flux Workflow

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle0,0,z\rangle \]

and let

\[ z=x^2+y^2 \]

above the disk

\[ x^2+y^2\le1, \]

oriented upward.

Here

\[ g_x=2x, \qquad g_y=2y. \]

The upward vector area factor is

\[ \langle-2x,-2y,1\rangle. \]

On the surface,

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle0,0,x^2+y^2\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot \langle-2x,-2y,1\rangle = x^2+y^2. \]

Thus,

\[ \Phi = \iint_D (x^2+y^2) \,dA. \]

Use polar coordinates:

\[ \Phi = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r^2r \,dr\,d\theta. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \Phi = \frac{\pi}{2} }. \]

What to Remember

Flux through an oriented surface is

\[ \boxed{ \Phi = \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

For a parameterized surface,

\[ \boxed{ \Phi = \iint_D \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(u,v)) \cdot (\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v) \,du\,dv }. \]
  • Flux measures the normal component of a vector field across a surface.
  • Positive flux follows the chosen normal orientation.
  • Negative flux goes opposite the chosen normal orientation.
  • A tangent field contributes zero local flux.
  • Reversing orientation reverses flux sign.
  • Closed surfaces are usually oriented outward.
  • Symmetry can simplify flux calculations substantially.
  • Divergence measures local source-sink behavior, while flux measures total crossing through a finite surface.
