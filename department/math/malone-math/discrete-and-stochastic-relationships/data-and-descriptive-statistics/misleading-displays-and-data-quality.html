Misleading Displays and Data Quality

Statistical analysis can be mathematically correct and still produce a misleading conclusion if the graph is distorted, the denominator is chosen poorly, important observations are missing, the sample does not represent the population, or the measurement process is unreliable. Good statistical practice therefore requires two forms of discipline: presenting data honestly and evaluating whether the underlying data are fit for the intended conclusion.

The Central Idea

A statistical result is only as trustworthy as:

  1. the data that produced it;
  2. the methods used to summarize it;
  3. the way the result is displayed;
  4. the claims made from it.

A useful question is:

Does the display faithfully represent data collected by a process capable of supporting the conclusion?

Misleading Does Not Always Mean False

A graph can contain numerically correct values and still create a distorted visual impression.

Likewise, a statistic can be calculated correctly from data that are incomplete, biased, poorly measured, or irrelevant to the target population.

Statistical quality requires more than correct arithmetic.

Truncated Axes

A truncated axis begins at a value other than a natural baseline, often zero.

Suppose two values are

\[ 98 \]

and

\[ 100. \]

A bar chart whose vertical axis begins at \(97\) can make the second bar appear several times taller than the first even though the actual difference is only

\[ 100-98=2. \]

When a Truncated Axis Can Be Useful

Truncation is not automatically wrong.

It can be useful when:

  • small changes are substantively important;
  • the baseline is clearly marked;
  • the viewer is not encouraged to interpret bar length as absolute magnitude;
  • the purpose is to reveal fine variation rather than compare total size.

Why Bar Charts Deserve Special Care

In a bar chart, the length of the bar often encodes magnitude directly.

If the baseline is shifted upward, the visible bar length can exaggerate relative differences.

For many bar charts, a zero baseline is therefore the clearest default.

Unequal Axis Intervals

Axis tick marks should represent consistent numerical intervals unless a special scale is explicitly identified.

A sequence such as

\[ 0,\ 10,\ 20,\ 100 \]

shown at equal visual spacing would distort numerical distance.

Compressed and Expanded Scales

A graph can make variation appear:

  • larger by compressing the numerical range shown on an axis;
  • smaller by expanding the numerical range.

The scale should be chosen to clarify the data rather than force a desired visual narrative.

Changing the Aspect Ratio

The apparent steepness of a line can change when the graph is stretched vertically or horizontally.

The underlying numerical relationship is unchanged, but the visual impression can become much more dramatic or much flatter.

Three-Dimensional Effects

Three-dimensional bars, cylinders, cones, and perspective effects can make simple comparisons harder.

Perspective may cause:

  • front objects to appear larger;
  • back objects to appear smaller;
  • bar tops to become difficult to align with the scale;
  • areas and volumes to exaggerate magnitude.

Area Can Exaggerate Change

Suppose a pictorial symbol doubles in both width and height.

Its area becomes

\[ 2\cdot2=4 \]

times as large.

A quantity that doubled may therefore appear four times as large.

Volume Can Exaggerate Even More

If all three dimensions of a three-dimensional object double, volume changes by

\[ 2^3=8. \]

A value that doubled can appear eight times as large if volume is allowed to scale unintentionally.

Selective Time Windows

Choosing a particular start or end date can change the apparent trend.

For example, a market series may appear to be:

  • rising over one short interval;
  • flat over a longer interval;
  • falling when measured from a different starting point.

The time window should match the question being answered.

Cherry-Picking

Cherry-picking means selecting only observations, periods, subgroups, or comparisons that support a preferred conclusion.

Reliable analysis should use a defensible inclusion rule established by the study question or design.

Omitted Categories

A categorical display can be misleading if important categories are omitted.

If a chart shows only the three largest departments, the title should not imply that those are the only departments in the organization.

Pie Charts Must Represent a Whole

Pie-chart sectors should represent mutually compatible parts of one meaningful total.

If categories overlap, percentages may sum to more than

\[ 100\%. \]

A pie chart would then suggest a partition that does not actually exist.

Percentages Need Denominators

A percentage is meaningful only when its denominator is clear.

For example,

\[ 40\% \]

could mean:

  • \(40\) of \(100\) sampled people;
  • \(40\) of \(100\) valid respondents;
  • \(40\) of \(100\) eligible cases;
  • \(40\) of \(100\) transactions after exclusions.

The Denominator Can Change the Story

Suppose \(100\) people are contacted, \(80\) respond, and \(48\) say yes.

Among respondents:

\[ \frac{48}{80}=60\%. \]

Among everyone contacted:

\[ \frac{48}{100}=48\%. \]

Both calculations are correct, but they answer different questions.

Counts Versus Rates

Raw counts can mislead when groups differ in size.

Suppose Region A has \(200\) incidents among \(100{,}000\) people and Region B has \(150\) incidents among \(20{,}000\) people.

Region A has the larger count, but the rates are

\[ \frac{200}{100000}=0.002 \]

and

\[ \frac{150}{20000}=0.0075. \]

Region B has the larger rate.

Relative Change Versus Absolute Change

Suppose a quantity rises from \(2\) to \(4\).

The absolute increase is

\[ 4-2=2. \]

The relative increase is

\[ \frac{4-2}{2}=1=100\%. \]

Reports should distinguish absolute and percentage changes.

Percentage Points Versus Percent Change

Suppose a rate rises from \(20\%\) to \(25\%\).

The increase is

\[ 25\%-20\%=5 \]

percentage points.

The relative percent increase is

\[ \frac{25-20}{20}=0.25=25\%. \]

These are different quantities.

Aggregation Can Hide Important Patterns

Combining groups can conceal differences between subgroups.

An overall average may hide:

  • regional differences;
  • age effects;
  • product-type effects;
  • different baseline risks;
  • different group sizes.

Simpson's Paradox

An important advanced phenomenon is Simpson's paradox.

A trend that appears within several separate groups can weaken, disappear, or reverse when the groups are combined.

This can occur because group sizes and lurking variables affect the aggregate result.

Why Group Structure Matters

Before reporting one overall mean, proportion, or correlation, ask whether relevant subgroups have materially different distributions.

Aggregation is not wrong, but it may answer a different question from subgroup analysis.

What Is Data Quality?

Data quality refers to whether data are suitable for the intended analytical purpose.

Useful dimensions include:

  • accuracy;
  • completeness;
  • consistency;
  • timeliness;
  • validity;
  • representativeness;
  • traceability.

Accuracy

Accuracy concerns whether recorded values correspond reasonably to the quantities they are intended to measure.

Sources of inaccuracy include:

  • faulty instruments;
  • incorrect self-reports;
  • data-entry errors;
  • unit conversion mistakes;
  • misclassified categories.

Completeness

Completeness concerns whether necessary observations and variables are present.

Missing data can reduce effective sample size and can introduce bias if the missingness is systematic.

Consistency

Consistency means that values follow compatible definitions and coding rules.

Problems include:

  • mixing kilograms and pounds;
  • using different date formats without conversion;
  • changing category definitions over time;
  • recording missing values inconsistently.

Validity

Validity asks whether a value satisfies the logical rules of the variable.

Examples:

  • a negative age may be invalid;
  • a percentage above \(100\%\) may be invalid in some contexts;
  • a date may fall outside the study period;
  • a category code may not belong to the allowed set.

Timeliness

Data can be accurate but too old for the decision being made.

Timeliness asks whether the data reflect the relevant period.

This is especially important in rapidly changing systems.

Representativeness

A dataset is representative when the observed cases adequately reflect the population relevant to the intended conclusion.

Large datasets can still be unrepresentative.

Traceability

Traceability means being able to determine:

  • where the data came from;
  • how they were collected;
  • what transformations were applied;
  • which records were excluded;
  • which definitions were used.

Traceability supports reproducibility and auditability.

Missing Data

Missing values require explicit treatment.

Common missing-data codes include:

  • blank cells;
  • NA;
  • unknown;
  • not applicable;
  • special numerical codes such as \(-999\).

Special codes must not accidentally be treated as real measurements.

Missing Completely at Random

In introductory terminology, data are missing completely at random when the probability of missingness is unrelated to observed or unobserved values.

This is a strong condition and should not be assumed casually.

Systematic Missingness

Missingness can be related to the phenomenon being studied.

For example, people with very high incomes may be less willing to report income.

In such cases, simply analyzing complete responses can bias the result.

Nonresponse Bias

Nonresponse bias occurs when people who do not respond differ systematically from those who do.

A high response count alone does not eliminate this problem.

Undercoverage

Undercoverage occurs when some members of the target population have little or no chance of being included.

An online-only survey may underrepresent people without reliable internet access.

Voluntary-Response Bias

A voluntary-response sample allows people to choose themselves into the study.

People with unusually strong opinions may be more likely to participate, making the sample unrepresentative.

Convenience Samples

A convenience sample uses cases that are easy to reach.

Convenience can reduce cost, but it can also create serious selection bias when accessibility is related to the variables being studied.

Measurement Error

Measurement error is the difference between the recorded value and the intended underlying quantity.

It can arise from:

  • instrument limits;
  • calibration problems;
  • rounding;
  • recall error;
  • ambiguous questions;
  • observer differences.

Systematic Versus Random Measurement Error

Random measurement error introduces unpredictable variation.

Systematic error pushes measurements consistently in one direction.

For example, a scale that always reads \(2\) kg too high introduces systematic error.

Question Wording

Survey responses can depend on how a question is phrased.

Leading, loaded, ambiguous, or double-barreled questions can alter the distribution of responses.

Order Effects

Earlier questions can influence answers to later questions.

This is called an order effect.

Survey design should therefore consider question sequence as part of the measurement process.

Duplicate Records

Duplicate cases can inflate frequencies, counts, and apparent sample size.

Before analysis, determine whether repeated records represent:

  • true repeated events;
  • multiple measurements on the same case;
  • accidental duplicates.

Unit Errors

Mixing units can create extreme values that look like outliers.

For example, a dataset of heights in centimeters may contain one value entered in meters:

\[ 1.75 \]

instead of

\[ 175. \]

Data validation should catch such inconsistencies before interpretation.

Impossible and Implausible Values

Analysts should distinguish:

  • impossible values that violate the definition;
  • implausible values that are unusual but possible.

Impossible values can often be corrected or excluded with clear justification. Implausible values require investigation.

Outliers Are Data-Quality Signals, Not Automatic Errors

An outlier can reveal:

  • a recording error;
  • a rare event;
  • a separate subgroup;
  • a process failure;
  • an important discovery.

The appropriate response is investigation.

Data Cleaning Should Be Documented

Any cleaning decision should be reproducible.

Document:

  • which values were changed;
  • which cases were removed;
  • which missing-data rules were used;
  • which unit conversions were applied;
  • why each step was justified.

Do Not Clean Toward a Desired Result

Data cleaning should improve validity and consistency.

It should not be used to selectively remove observations that weaken a preferred conclusion.

Metadata Matters

Good datasets should be accompanied by information describing:

  • variable definitions;
  • units;
  • coding schemes;
  • collection dates;
  • sampling procedures;
  • missing-value conventions;
  • source systems.

These supporting descriptions are often called metadata.

Reproducibility

A statistical analysis is more trustworthy when another analyst can reproduce the result from the same data and documented procedures.

Reproducibility requires:

  • clear data provenance;
  • documented cleaning;
  • defined formulas;
  • stated exclusions;
  • stable code or procedures.

Visual Integrity

A useful display should:

  • use appropriate scales;
  • label axes and units;
  • distinguish counts from percentages;
  • avoid unnecessary dimensional effects;
  • show relevant categories;
  • identify the time period;
  • state important exclusions.

Statistical Integrity

A useful analysis should:

  • match the statistic to the data type;
  • match the sample to the target population;
  • use appropriate denominators;
  • investigate missingness;
  • consider outliers and subgroup structure;
  • avoid causal claims unsupported by design.

Example: Misleading Bar Chart

Suppose two departments have satisfaction rates of

\[ 91\% \]

and

\[ 94\%. \]

If the vertical axis begins at \(90\%\), the second bar may appear several times taller.

The actual difference is

\[ 94\%-91\%=3 \]

percentage points.

Example: Misleading Denominator

A company reports that "80\% of customers recommend the product."

If this means \(80\) of \(100\) respondents but only \(100\) of \(10{,}000\) customers responded, the statement requires context.

The recommendation rate among respondents is \(80\%\), but nonresponse may limit generalization to all customers.

Example: Data Quality Failure

Suppose a dataset records delivery times in minutes, but one source system supplies seconds.

A value of

\[ 900 \]

seconds may be interpreted incorrectly as

\[ 900 \]

minutes unless units are standardized.

The correct conversion is

\[ \frac{900}{60}=15\text{ minutes}. \]

Example: Selection Bias

Suppose a university measures campus satisfaction by surveying only students attending a voluntary alumni-networking event.

The sample may differ systematically from the full student population.

A large number of responses does not eliminate this selection problem.

Common Error: Trusting a Polished Graphic Automatically

Professional design does not guarantee statistical quality.

Always inspect:

  • axes;
  • denominators;
  • sample definition;
  • time period;
  • missing categories;
  • data source.

Common Error: Assuming Large Data Means Good Data

A dataset with millions of observations can still be systematically biased.

More data reduce some forms of random variation, but they do not automatically correct:

  • undercoverage;
  • measurement bias;
  • selection bias;
  • bad definitions;
  • incorrect units.

Common Error: Treating Missing Values as Zero

Missing and zero are different concepts.

A zero may be a legitimate observed quantity.

A missing value means the quantity was not observed or not applicable.

Common Error: Ignoring Changing Definitions

A time series may become incomparable if the definition of a variable changes.

If "active customer" is defined differently before and after a policy change, apparent trend changes may partly reflect the definition rather than behavior.

Common Error: Reporting Precision the Data Do Not Support

If measurements are recorded only to the nearest whole unit, reporting a mean to six decimal places can create false precision.

Numerical reporting should reflect measurement and sampling precision.

Common Error: Hiding Uncertainty

A sample statistic is not generally identical to the population parameter.

Later statistical inference will quantify uncertainty more formally, but even descriptive reports should avoid presenting sample estimates as exact population facts.

Common Error: Causal Language from Descriptive Data

A graph or correlation may show association.

It does not automatically justify statements such as:

"X causes Y."

Study design determines whether causal interpretation is defensible.

A Reliable Display Review Procedure

  1. Read the title carefully.
  2. Identify the variables and units.
  3. Inspect both axes and baselines.
  4. Check whether spacing reflects actual numerical intervals.
  5. Determine whether values are counts, rates, percentages, or indices.
  6. Identify the denominator.
  7. Check the time period and starting point.
  8. Look for omitted categories or selective ranges.
  9. Ask whether visual area or volume exaggerates magnitude.
  10. Compare the visual impression with the actual numerical differences.

A Reliable Data-Quality Review Procedure

  1. Define the target population and sample.
  2. Review the sampling frame and selection process.
  3. Check variable definitions and units.
  4. Inspect missing-value patterns.
  5. Check for duplicates and invalid values.
  6. Investigate unusual observations.
  7. Verify time periods and category definitions.
  8. Review measurement and survey procedures.
  9. Document cleaning and transformation rules.
  10. Confirm that the data support the intended claim.

Practice Answers

Practice 1

Beginning the axis at \(47\) removes most of the baseline and magnifies a numerical difference of only

\[ 50-48=\boxed{2}. \]

Practice 2

Among respondents:

\[ \frac{70}{100}=0.70=\boxed{70\%}. \]

Among everyone contacted:

\[ \frac{70}{200}=0.35=\boxed{35\%}. \]

Practice 3

\[ 36\%-30\% = \boxed{6\text{ percentage points}}. \]

Practice 4

\[ \frac{36-30}{30} = \frac{6}{30} = 0.20 = \boxed{20\%}. \]

Practice 5

Hospital A:

\[ \frac{120}{12000}=0.01=1\%. \]

Hospital B:

\[ \frac{80}{4000}=0.02=2\%. \]

Hospital B has the higher infection rate.

Practice 6

The value \(-999\) is a code for missingness rather than a measured income. Treating it as a real value would distort the mean.

Practice 7

The dataset contains a unit inconsistency. The value should be converted to the common unit before analysis.

Practice 8

The study may suffer from voluntary-response bias because people with stronger opinions may be more likely to participate.

What to Remember

  • A display can be numerically correct yet visually misleading.
  • Axes, scales, denominators, time windows, and omitted categories all affect interpretation.
  • Counts and rates answer different questions.
  • Percentage points and percent change are not the same.
  • Large datasets can still be biased or poorly measured.
  • Missing values, duplicates, invalid values, and unit inconsistencies must be handled explicitly.
  • Sampling bias and measurement error can undermine otherwise correct calculations.
  • Outliers should be investigated rather than deleted automatically.
  • Data cleaning and transformations should be documented and reproducible.
  • Statistical conclusions should not exceed what the data and study design can support.
