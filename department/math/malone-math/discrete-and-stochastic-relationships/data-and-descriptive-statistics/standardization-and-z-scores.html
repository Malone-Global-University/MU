Standardization and Z-Scores

Raw values are often difficult to compare when they come from distributions with different centers, spreads, or measurement units. Standardization converts an observation into a relative position measured in standard deviation units. The resulting z-score tells us whether a value lies above or below the mean and how far it lies from that mean relative to the distribution's variability.

The Central Idea

A raw observation tells us its value on the original measurement scale.

A z-score tells us its relative location within a distribution.

Specifically, a z-score measures:

how many standard deviations an observation lies above or below the mean.

The Population Z-Score

For a population with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\), the z-score of an observation \(x\) is

\[ \boxed{ z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} }. \]

The numerator measures the observation's deviation from the mean.

Dividing by the standard deviation expresses that deviation in standard deviation units.

The Sample Standardized Score

When a sample mean \(\bar{x}\) and sample standard deviation \(s\) are used, an observation can be standardized as

\[ \boxed{ z=\frac{x-\bar{x}}{s} }. \]

The interpretation is similar: the standardized value describes how far the observation lies from the sample mean in sample-standard-deviation units.

Interpreting the Sign of a Z-Score

The sign tells us which side of the mean contains the observation.

  • If \(z>0\), the observation is above the mean.
  • If \(z<0\), the observation is below the mean.
  • If \(z=0\), the observation equals the mean.

Interpreting the Magnitude

The absolute value

\[ |z| \]

tells us how many standard deviations separate the observation from the mean.

For example:

  • \(z=1\): one standard deviation above the mean;
  • \(z=-1.5\): one and one-half standard deviations below the mean;
  • \(z=2.4\): \(2.4\) standard deviations above the mean.

Example: Positive Z-Score

Suppose a distribution has

\[ \mu=70,\qquad \sigma=8, \]

and an observation is

\[ x=86. \]

Then

\[ z = \frac{86-70}{8} = \frac{16}{8} = \boxed{2}. \]

The observation lies two standard deviations above the mean.

Example: Negative Z-Score

Suppose

\[ \mu=50,\qquad \sigma=5, \]

and

\[ x=42.5. \]

Then

\[ z = \frac{42.5-50}{5} = \frac{-7.5}{5} = \boxed{-1.5}. \]

The observation lies \(1.5\) standard deviations below the mean.

Example: Z-Score of the Mean

If

\[ x=\mu, \]

then

\[ z = \frac{\mu-\mu}{\sigma} = \boxed{0}. \]

The mean always has z-score \(0\).

Why Standardization Is Useful

Standardization removes the original unit and replaces it with a common measure of relative distance.

This makes it possible to compare observations from distributions with:

  • different means;
  • different standard deviations;
  • different numerical scales;
  • different units.

Z-Scores Are Unit-Free

In

\[ z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}, \]

the numerator and denominator have the same units.

If \(x\), \(\mu\), and \(\sigma\) are all measured in kilograms, then the kilograms cancel:

\[ \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{kg}}=1. \]

A z-score therefore has no physical unit.

Comparing Values from Different Scales

Suppose one exam has

\[ \mu_A=80,\qquad \sigma_A=5, \]

and another has

\[ \mu_B=60,\qquad \sigma_B=12. \]

A raw score of \(88\) on Exam A and \(78\) on Exam B cannot be compared fairly using raw points alone because the distributions differ.

Example: Comparing Two Exam Scores

For Exam A:

\[ z_A = \frac{88-80}{5} = \boxed{1.6}. \]

For Exam B:

\[ z_B = \frac{78-60}{12} = \boxed{1.5}. \]

The score on Exam A is slightly farther above its exam mean in standard deviation units.

Relative Standing Versus Raw Magnitude

A larger raw value does not automatically imply a stronger relative standing.

The z-score compares an observation with the center and spread of its own distribution.

This distinction is essential when scales differ.

Standardization as a Two-Step Transformation

The z-score formula performs two conceptual operations.

  1. Subtract the mean to center the distribution around zero.
  2. Divide by the standard deviation to express distance in standard deviation units.

In symbols:

\[ x \longrightarrow x-\mu \longrightarrow \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

Centering

Subtracting the mean creates a centered value

\[ x-\mu. \]

Values above the mean become positive.

Values below the mean become negative.

The mean itself becomes zero.

Scaling

Dividing by \(\sigma\) expresses the centered distance relative to the distribution's typical spread:

\[ \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

A deviation of \(10\) units may be large in a distribution with \(\sigma=2\) but small in a distribution with \(\sigma=50\).

Example: Same Raw Deviation, Different Z-Scores

Consider two distributions where an observation lies \(10\) units above the mean.

Distribution A:

\[ \sigma_A=5. \] \[ z_A=\frac{10}{5}=2. \]

Distribution B:

\[ \sigma_B=20. \] \[ z_B=\frac{10}{20}=0.5. \]

The same raw deviation represents very different relative positions.

Reverse Standardization

If the z-score, mean, and standard deviation are known, the original value can be recovered.

Starting from

\[ z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}, \]

multiply by \(\sigma\):

\[ z\sigma=x-\mu. \]

Add \(\mu\):

\[ \boxed{x=\mu+z\sigma}. \]

Example: Recovering an Original Value

Suppose

\[ \mu=100,\qquad \sigma=15,\qquad z=1.2. \]

Then

\[ x = 100+1.2(15) = 100+18 = \boxed{118}. \]

Example: Recovering a Value Below the Mean

Suppose

\[ \mu=50,\qquad \sigma=8,\qquad z=-1.5. \]

Then

\[ x = 50+(-1.5)(8) = 50-12 = \boxed{38}. \]

Standardizing an Entire Dataset

Each observation in a population can be transformed using

\[ z_i=\frac{x_i-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

The resulting collection of z-scores is called a standardized distribution.

Mean of Population Z-Scores

If every value in a population is standardized using the population mean and standard deviation, the mean of the resulting z-scores is

\[ \boxed{0}. \]

This follows because the original deviations from the mean sum to zero.

Standard Deviation of Population Z-Scores

Standardization rescales the population so that its standard deviation becomes

\[ \boxed{1}. \]

Thus a fully standardized population has:

\[ \boxed{\text{mean}=0,\qquad \text{standard deviation}=1}. \]

Standardization Does Not Change Distribution Shape

The transformation

\[ z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \]

shifts and rescales the horizontal axis.

It does not change:

  • the order of observations;
  • the number of peaks;
  • the direction of skewness;
  • which observations are unusually far from the main body.

Order Is Preserved

Because \(\sigma>0\), standardization is an increasing linear transformation.

Z-Scores and Unusual Values

Large absolute z-scores indicate observations far from the mean relative to the standard deviation.

For example,

\[ |z|=3 \]

means the observation lies three standard deviations from the mean.

Such values may deserve attention, but a large z-score alone does not prove that an observation is erroneous.

Z-Scores Are Not Automatically Outlier Rules

Rules such as "\( |z|>2 \)" or "\( |z|>3 \)" are sometimes used as informal screening guidelines.

Their appropriateness depends on the distribution and analytical context.

The \(1.5\)-IQR rule provides a different outlier criterion based on quartiles rather than the mean and standard deviation.

Effect of Skewness on Z-Score Interpretation

A z-score always measures distance from the mean in standard deviation units.

But the same z-score does not necessarily correspond to the same percentile in distributions with different shapes.

Converting z-scores directly into probabilities requires additional assumptions about the distribution.

Z-Scores Do Not Assume Normality

The calculation

\[ z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \]

can be performed for any quantitative distribution with a defined, nonzero standard deviation.

Normal-distribution assumptions become relevant only when z-scores are used with normal probability tables or normal probability models.

Standard Deviation Must Be Positive

If

\[ \sigma=0, \]

then every population observation is identical.

The z-score formula would require division by zero:

\[ \frac{x-\mu}{0}, \]

which is undefined.

Standardization therefore requires a positive standard deviation.

Example: Comparing Heights and Test Scores

Suppose a person's height is \(185\) cm in a population with

\[ \mu_H=170,\qquad \sigma_H=10. \]

The height z-score is

\[ z_H = \frac{185-170}{10} = 1.5. \]

Suppose the same person scores \(92\) on an exam with

\[ \mu_E=80,\qquad \sigma_E=6. \]

The exam z-score is

\[ z_E = \frac{92-80}{6} = 2. \]

Relative to the corresponding distributions, the exam score lies farther above its mean than the height does.

Example: Comparing Values with Different Units

A runner's speed and a student's exam score cannot be compared directly because their units differ.

After each is standardized relative to its own distribution, both can be expressed on the common z-score scale.

Standardization therefore supports comparison of relative standing, not comparison of the physical quantities themselves.

Common Error: Forgetting the Mean

A z-score is not

\[ \frac{x}{\sigma}. \]

It is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} }. \]

The observation must first be centered by subtracting the mean.

Common Error: Reversing the Numerator

The correct numerator is

\[ x-\mu, \]

not

\[ \mu-x. \]

Reversing the subtraction changes the sign and therefore reverses the interpretation of above versus below the mean.

Common Error: Ignoring the Sign

The z-scores

\[ 1.5 \]

and

\[ -1.5 \]

have equal distance from the mean but lie on opposite sides of it.

The sign carries directional information.

Common Error: Comparing Raw Scores Instead of Relative Position

A score of \(90\) on one exam may represent a weaker relative performance than a score of \(75\) on another if the exams have different means and standard deviations.

Use z-scores when the goal is to compare relative standing across different distributions.

Common Error: Treating Z-Scores as Percentiles

A z-score is a standardized distance from the mean.

A percentile describes cumulative relative position.

These are related only after the shape of the distribution is considered.

A z-score of \(1\) does not by itself mean the \(100\)th, \(90\)th, or any other fixed percentile for every possible distribution.

Common Error: Assuming a Large Z-Score Proves an Error

A large absolute z-score indicates an unusual distance from the mean.

The observation may still be completely valid.

Investigate unusual observations rather than deleting them automatically.

Common Error: Mixing Population and Sample Quantities

If a population mean and population standard deviation are used, write

\[ z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

If the data are being standardized relative to sample summaries, use

\[ z=\frac{x-\bar{x}}{s}. \]

Keep the reference center and spread internally consistent.

A Reliable Procedure for Computing a Z-Score

  1. Identify the observation \(x\).
  2. Identify the appropriate mean.
  3. Identify the appropriate standard deviation.
  4. Subtract the mean from the observation.
  5. Divide the deviation by the standard deviation.
  6. Keep the sign.
  7. Interpret the result in standard deviation units.

A Reliable Procedure for Comparing Z-Scores

  1. Standardize each observation relative to its own distribution.
  2. Compare the signs to determine above- or below-mean position.
  3. Compare absolute values to determine distance from each mean.
  4. Use the signed z-scores to compare relative standing.
  5. Do not infer probabilities or percentiles without additional distributional information.

A Reliable Procedure for Reverse Standardization

  1. Start with the z-score \(z\).
  2. Multiply by the standard deviation.
  3. Add the mean.
  4. Restore the original measurement units.

The formula is

\[ \boxed{x=\mu+z\sigma}. \]

Example: Full Standardization Workflow

Suppose

\[ \mu=120,\qquad \sigma=15,\qquad x=96. \]

Step 1: Find the deviation:

\[ 96-120=-24. \]

Step 2: Divide by the standard deviation:

\[ z = \frac{-24}{15} = \boxed{-1.6}. \]

Interpretation: the observation lies \(1.6\) standard deviations below the mean.

Example: Full Comparison Workflow

Student A scores \(84\) on an exam with

\[ \mu_A=72,\qquad \sigma_A=8. \]

Student B scores \(760\) on another test with

\[ \mu_B=700,\qquad \sigma_B=50. \]

Student A:

\[ z_A = \frac{84-72}{8} = 1.5. \]

Student B:

\[ z_B = \frac{760-700}{50} = 1.2. \]

Student A has the stronger relative standing because \(1.5>1.2\).

What to Remember

  • A z-score measures distance from the mean in standard deviation units.
  • For a population, \(z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\).
  • A positive z-score lies above the mean.
  • A negative z-score lies below the mean.
  • The magnitude \(|z|\) measures standardized distance from the mean.
  • Z-scores are unit-free.
  • Standardization allows comparison of relative standing across different scales.
  • Reverse standardization uses \(x=\mu+z\sigma\).
  • A standardized population has mean \(0\) and standard deviation \(1\).
  • Standardization changes location and scale but does not change distribution shape.
  • Z-scores do not require normality, although normal probability calculations require additional assumptions.
