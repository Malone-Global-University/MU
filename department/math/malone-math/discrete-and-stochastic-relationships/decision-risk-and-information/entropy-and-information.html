Entropy and Information

Probability tells us how likely outcomes are. Entropy summarizes how much uncertainty remains across those outcomes. Information measures how much an observation reduces that uncertainty. These ideas create a mathematical bridge between probability, prediction, communication, coding, statistics, and decision making.

The Central Idea

An uncertain variable carries information because we do not yet know which outcome will occur.

When an outcome is learned, some uncertainty disappears.

Information theory gives us numerical tools for measuring:

  • how surprising one outcome is;
  • how uncertain a probability distribution is overall;
  • how much uncertainty remains after observing another variable;
  • how much one variable tells us about another.

Information and Surprise

Rare events generally carry more information than common events.

Learning that a nearly certain event occurred tells us little.

Learning that a very unlikely event occurred is much more surprising.

Self-Information

The self-information of an event with probability \(p\) is

\[ \boxed{I(p)=-\log p}. \]

The logarithm base determines the unit of information.

Bits

When logarithms use base \(2\),

\[ \boxed{I(p)=-\log_2 p}, \]

information is measured in bits.

One bit corresponds to the information gained from resolving one equally likely binary choice.

Nats

When the natural logarithm is used,

\[ \boxed{I(p)=-\ln p}, \]

information is measured in nats.

Rare Events Carry More Self-Information

Compare probabilities

\[ p=0.5 \]

and

\[ p=0.125. \]

In bits,

\[ I(0.5)=-\log_2(0.5)=1 \]

while

\[ I(0.125)=-\log_2(0.125)=3. \]

The rarer event carries more information when it occurs.

Certain Events Carry No New Information

If

\[ p=1, \]

then

\[ I(1)=-\log_2(1)=0. \]

An event that was already certain produces no surprise.

Why the Logarithm Appears

Independent probabilities multiply, while information adds.

If independent events A and B have probabilities \(p_A\) and \(p_B\),

\[ P(A\cap B)=p_Ap_B. \]

Their information is

\[ -\log(p_Ap_B) \] \[ =-\log p_A-\log p_B. \]

The logarithm converts multiplication of probabilities into addition of information.

Entropy

Entropy is the expected self-information of a random variable.

For a discrete random variable \(X\),

\[ \boxed{ H(X) = -\sum_x P(X=x)\log P(X=x) }. \]

With base-2 logarithms, entropy is measured in bits.

Entropy Is an Average Uncertainty Measure

Self-information applies to one realized outcome.

Entropy averages self-information across all possible outcomes.

It therefore describes uncertainty before we know which outcome will occur.

Example: Fair Coin

For a fair coin,

\[ P(H)=0.5,\qquad P(T)=0.5. \]

Therefore,

\[ H(X) = -0.5\log_2(0.5) -0.5\log_2(0.5). \] \[ =0.5+0.5. \] \[ \boxed{H(X)=1\text{ bit}}. \]

Example: Biased Coin

Suppose

\[ P(H)=0.9,\qquad P(T)=0.1. \]

Then

\[ H(X) = -0.9\log_2(0.9) -0.1\log_2(0.1). \]

Numerically,

\[ \boxed{H(X)\approx0.469\text{ bits}}. \]

The biased coin is less uncertain than the fair coin.

Maximum Entropy for Two Outcomes

A binary variable has maximum entropy when its two outcomes are equally likely.

\[ P(A)=P(B)=0.5. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{H=1\text{ bit}}. \]

Minimum Entropy

If one outcome is certain, entropy is zero.

\[ \boxed{H=0}. \]

There is no uncertainty left to resolve.

Uniform Distributions Maximize Entropy

For \(n\) equally likely outcomes,

\[ P(X=x_i)=\frac{1}{n}. \]

Entropy is

\[ H(X) = -n\left(\frac{1}{n}\log_2\frac{1}{n}\right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{H(X)=\log_2 n}. \]

Example: Four Equally Likely Outcomes

If there are four equally likely outcomes,

\[ H(X)=\log_2 4. \] \[ \boxed{H(X)=2\text{ bits}}. \]

Two binary questions can distinguish among four equally likely possibilities.

Entropy Depends on the Distribution, Not the Labels

Entropy depends on the probabilities assigned to outcomes.

Renaming the outcomes does not change entropy.

What matters is how probability is distributed across the possibilities.

Entropy and Prediction

Low entropy means one or a few outcomes dominate the distribution.

High entropy means probability is spread more evenly.

A lower-entropy variable is generally easier to predict before observing additional information.

Joint Entropy

For two discrete random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), the joint entropy is

\[ \boxed{ H(X,Y) = -\sum_x\sum_y P(x,y)\log P(x,y) }. \]

It measures uncertainty in the pair \((X,Y)\).

Conditional Entropy

Conditional entropy measures how much uncertainty remains about one variable after another variable is known.

\[ \boxed{ H(X\mid Y) = -\sum_y P(y) \sum_x P(x\mid y)\log P(x\mid y) }. \]

Interpretation of Conditional Entropy

If \(Y\) tells us a great deal about \(X\), then

\[ H(X\mid Y) \]

is small.

If knowing \(Y\) tells us nothing about \(X\), then conditional entropy remains as large as the original entropy.

Chain Rule for Entropy

Joint entropy can be decomposed:

\[ \boxed{ H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X\mid Y) }. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X) }. \]

The total uncertainty in two variables can be separated into uncertainty in one plus remaining uncertainty in the other.

Mutual Information

Mutual information measures how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about another.

\[ \boxed{ I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y) }. \]

Symmetrically,

\[ \boxed{ I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X) }. \]

Another Formula for Mutual Information

Mutual information can also be written

\[ \boxed{ I(X;Y) = \sum_x\sum_y P(x,y) \log \frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)P(y)} }. \]

This compares the observed joint distribution with what would occur under independence.

Independent Variables Have Zero Mutual Information

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent,

\[ P(x,y)=P(x)P(y). \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)P(y)}=1, \]

and

\[ \boxed{I(X;Y)=0}. \]

Perfectly Informative Variables

If knowing \(Y\) determines \(X\) completely, then

\[ H(X\mid Y)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ I(X;Y)=H(X). \]

The observation removes all uncertainty about \(X\).

Information Gain

Information gain is often described as the reduction in entropy produced by an observation or partition.

\[ \boxed{ \text{Information Gain} = H(\text{before}) - H(\text{after}) }. \]

This is closely related to mutual information.

Example: Perfectly Revealing Signal

Suppose a fair binary state has entropy

\[ H(X)=1\text{ bit}. \]

A perfect signal reveals the state exactly, so

\[ H(X\mid Y)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ I(X;Y)=1-0 \] \[ \boxed{1\text{ bit}}. \]

Example: Useless Signal

Suppose the same binary state has entropy

\[ H(X)=1. \]

If the signal is independent of the state,

\[ H(X\mid Y)=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ I(X;Y)=1-1 \] \[ \boxed{0}. \]

Entropy and Value of Information Are Different

Entropy measures uncertainty.

Value of information measures decision improvement.

A signal can remove substantial entropy while having little decision value if the optimal action does not change.

Conversely, a small amount of information near a decision threshold can be economically valuable.

More Information Does Not Automatically Mean More Value

Information theory measures statistical dependence and uncertainty reduction.

Decision theory also cares about:

  • available actions;
  • payoffs;
  • losses;
  • timing;
  • cost of obtaining information.

Information content and decision value are related but distinct.

Entropy and Coding

Entropy has a fundamental connection to data compression.

More probable symbols can be represented using shorter codewords, while rare symbols generally require longer codewords.

Entropy gives a lower-bound benchmark for average code length under idealized lossless coding assumptions.

Expected Code Length

If symbol \(i\) has probability \(p_i\) and codeword length \(\ell_i\), the expected code length is

\[ \boxed{ E[L]=\sum_i p_i\ell_i }. \]

Efficient codes assign shorter codewords to more probable symbols.

Binary Questions and Information

Bits can be interpreted through yes-or-no questions.

If there are eight equally likely possibilities,

\[ H=\log_2 8=3. \]

In an ideal balanced search, three binary answers are enough to identify one of eight possibilities.

Entropy and Decision Trees

Decision trees in machine learning often choose splits that reduce uncertainty about a target variable.

A split can be evaluated by comparing entropy before and after the split.

Larger entropy reduction means the split creates more information about the target.

Weighted Entropy after a Split

Suppose a dataset is divided into groups \(G_1,\ldots,G_k\).

The post-split entropy is often computed as

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{j=1}^{k} P(G_j)H(Y\mid G_j) }. \]

Information gain is the original entropy minus this weighted remainder.

Example: Complete Separation

Suppose a binary target initially has entropy \(1\) bit.

A split produces two groups, each containing only one class.

Each group's entropy is

\[ 0. \]

Therefore post-split entropy is \(0\), and information gain is

\[ \boxed{1\text{ bit}}. \]

Example: No Separation

Suppose each child group has exactly the same class proportions as the original dataset.

Then the weighted post-split entropy equals the original entropy.

Information gain is

\[ \boxed{0}. \]

Cross-Entropy

Cross-entropy measures how well one probability distribution \(q\) represents outcomes generated according to another distribution \(p\).

\[ \boxed{ H(p,q) = -\sum_x p(x)\log q(x) }. \]

It appears frequently in statistics, coding, and machine learning.

Cross-Entropy Penalizes Confident Wrong Predictions

If an event occurs and a model assigned that event very small probability, then

\[ -\log q(x) \]

becomes large.

This means confident incorrect probability assignments can receive large loss.

Entropy and Log Loss

For a realized outcome \(x\), logarithmic loss is

\[ \boxed{ L(q,x)=-\log q(x) }. \]

The expected log loss under the true distribution \(p\) is cross-entropy.

This connects information theory directly to loss functions.

Kullback-Leibler Divergence

The Kullback-Leibler divergence from distribution \(q\) to distribution \(p\) is

\[ \boxed{ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} }. \]

It measures how much additional log-loss is incurred when \(q\) is used in place of the true distribution \(p\).

Cross-Entropy Decomposition

Cross-entropy satisfies

\[ \boxed{ H(p,q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) }. \]

The cross-entropy equals unavoidable uncertainty plus the penalty for using the wrong probability model.

KL Divergence Is Nonnegative

Under standard conditions,

\[ \boxed{ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\ge0. } \]

Equality occurs when the distributions agree on the relevant outcomes.

KL Divergence Is Not an Ordinary Distance

KL divergence is useful for comparing distributions, but it is not a metric.

In general,

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) \ne D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p). \]

It is directional.

Entropy in Continuous Models

Continuous random variables use a related quantity called differential entropy.

For density \(f(x)\),

\[ h(X) = -\int f(x)\log f(x)\,dx. \]

Differential entropy behaves differently from discrete entropy and can even be negative, so it should not be interpreted identically.

Information and Bayesian Updating

A Bayesian update changes a prior distribution into a posterior distribution after observing evidence.

Information measures can quantify how much the evidence changes the distribution or reduces uncertainty.

This provides another bridge between Bayesian reasoning and information theory.

Entropy Is Not Disorder in Every Context

The word entropy is used in several fields.

In information theory, entropy is specifically a function of a probability distribution.

Informal phrases such as "disorder" can sometimes be suggestive, but the mathematical definition should remain primary.

Common Error: Forgetting the Negative Sign

Since probabilities lie between \(0\) and \(1\),

\[ \log p\le0. \]

The negative sign makes self-information and entropy nonnegative in the ordinary discrete setting.

Common Error: Mixing Logarithm Bases

Base \(2\) produces bits.

Base \(e\) produces nats.

Different bases scale the numerical value, so a calculation should use one base consistently.

Common Error: Treating Entropy as the Number of Outcomes

Two variables can have the same number of possible outcomes and different entropy.

What matters is the probability distribution over those outcomes.

Common Error: Assuming More Outcomes Means More Entropy

More possible outcomes can increase maximum possible entropy, but actual entropy may remain low if nearly all probability is concentrated on one outcome.

Common Error: Confusing Mutual Information with Correlation

Correlation measures a particular kind of association, especially linear association for numerical variables.

Mutual information can detect more general statistical dependence.

The two quantities are related but not interchangeable.

Common Error: Equating Information Gain with Economic Value

A signal can have high statistical information content but low economic or strategic value.

Decision value depends on how the information changes actions and consequences.

A Reliable Procedure for Computing Entropy

  1. List all possible outcomes.
  2. Assign a valid probability to each outcome.
  3. Choose a logarithm base and use it consistently.
  4. Compute \(p_i\log p_i\) for each positive-probability outcome.
  5. Add the terms.
  6. Apply the negative sign.
  7. Interpret the result as average uncertainty before observing the outcome.

A Reliable Procedure for Mutual Information

  1. Compute the original entropy \(H(X)\).
  2. Determine the conditional distributions of \(X\) given \(Y\).
  3. Compute the conditional entropy \(H(X\mid Y)\).
  4. Subtract:
\[ I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y). \]

The result measures how much uncertainty about \(X\) is removed by knowing \(Y\).

What to Remember

Entropy and information provide a mathematical language for uncertainty and uncertainty reduction.

  • Self-information measures the surprise of one outcome.
  • Rare events carry more self-information than common events.
  • Entropy is expected self-information.
  • Uniform distributions maximize entropy among fixed finite outcome sets.
  • Conditional entropy measures uncertainty remaining after an observation.
  • Mutual information measures how much one variable tells us about another.
  • Independent variables have zero mutual information.
  • Information gain is a reduction in entropy.
  • Cross-entropy connects probability forecasts to logarithmic loss.
  • Information content and decision value are related but not identical.

The most important conceptual distinction is: entropy measures uncertainty, while information measures how much of that uncertainty is removed.
