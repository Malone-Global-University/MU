Congruence and Modular Arithmetic

Modular arithmetic studies integers through their remainders after division by a fixed positive integer called the modulus. Instead of treating numbers as different merely because their ordinary values differ, modular arithmetic groups together numbers that leave the same remainder. This creates a powerful arithmetic system for cycles, divisibility, number theory, and cryptography.

The Central Idea

Let \(n\) be a positive integer. Two integers \(a\) and \(b\) are congruent modulo \(n\) when they leave the same remainder after division by \(n\).

We write

\[ \boxed{a\equiv b\pmod n}. \]

This is read “\(a\) is congruent to \(b\) modulo \(n\).”

A First Example

Divide \(17\) and \(5\) by \(12\).

\[ 17=12(1)+5 \]

and

\[ 5=12(0)+5. \]

Both leave remainder \(5\). Therefore,

\[ \boxed{17\equiv5\pmod{12}}. \]

Another Equivalent Definition

Congruence modulo \(n\) can also be defined using divisibility:

\[ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \iff n\mid(a-b) }. \]

In other words, \(a\) and \(b\) are congruent modulo \(n\) exactly when their difference is divisible by \(n\).

Checking the First Example by Divisibility

We claimed

\[ 17\equiv5\pmod{12}. \]

Subtract:

\[ 17-5=12. \]

Since

\[ 12\mid12, \]

the congruence is confirmed.

When Congruence Fails

Consider whether

\[ 17\equiv6\pmod{12}. \]

Their difference is

\[ 17-6=11. \]

Since

\[ 12\nmid11, \]

we conclude

\[ \boxed{17\not\equiv6\pmod{12}}. \]

The Modulus

In

\[ a\equiv b\pmod n, \]

the positive integer \(n\) is called the modulus.

The modulus determines the cycle length used to compare integers.

For example, modulo \(5\),

\[ 0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4 \]

are the possible remainders.

Canonical Remainders

For modulus \(n\), every integer is congruent to exactly one number in

\[ \boxed{0,1,2,\ldots,n-1}. \]

These are the standard nonnegative remainders modulo \(n\).

For example, modulo \(7\), every integer is congruent to exactly one of

\[ 0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6. \]

Reducing an Integer Modulo \(n\)

To reduce an integer modulo \(n\), divide by \(n\) and keep the remainder.

For example,

\[ 43=7(6)+1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{43\equiv1\pmod7}. \]

Negative Integers in Modular Arithmetic

Negative integers can also be reduced to standard nonnegative remainders.

Consider

\[ -3\pmod7. \]

Since

\[ -3+7=4, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{-3\equiv4\pmod7}. \]

Many Integers Can Represent the Same Remainder

Modulo \(5\),

\[ \ldots,-9,-4,1,6,11,16,\ldots \]

all differ from one another by multiples of \(5\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \cdots\equiv-9\equiv-4\equiv1\equiv6\equiv11\equiv16\pmod5 }. \]

Congruence Is Not Ordinary Equality

The statement

\[ 17\equiv5\pmod{12} \]

does not mean

\[ 17=5. \]

It means the two integers occupy the same remainder position in a cycle of length \(12\).

Congruence is therefore a relationship relative to a chosen modulus.

Changing the Modulus Can Change the Relationship

The integers \(17\) and \(5\) satisfy

\[ 17\equiv5\pmod{12}. \]

But modulo \(7\),

\[ 17\equiv3\pmod7 \]

while

\[ 5\equiv5\pmod7. \]

Therefore,

\[ 17\not\equiv5\pmod7. \]

Congruence must always be interpreted together with its modulus.

Clock Arithmetic

A clock provides a familiar model of modular arithmetic.

On a \(12\)-hour clock, moving forward \(12\) hours returns to the same position.

Therefore,

\[ 15\equiv3\pmod{12}. \]

A time labeled \(15\) hours after a reference point occupies the same clock position as \(3\) hours after that point.

Example: Clock Addition

Suppose it is \(9\) o'clock. What clock position occurs \(7\) hours later?

\[ 9+7=16. \]

Reduce modulo \(12\):

\[ 16\equiv4\pmod{12}. \]

Therefore the clock reads

\[ \boxed{4}. \]

Addition Preserves Congruence

If

\[ a\equiv b\pmod n \]

and

\[ c\equiv d\pmod n, \]

then

\[ \boxed{a+c\equiv b+d\pmod n}. \]

Congruent values may therefore be replaced by simpler representatives before adding.

Example: Modular Addition

Compute

\[ 38+47\pmod9. \]

Reduce first:

\[ 38\equiv2\pmod9 \]

and

\[ 47\equiv2\pmod9. \]

Therefore,

\[ 38+47\equiv2+2\equiv4\pmod9. \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{38+47\equiv4\pmod9}. \]

Subtraction Preserves Congruence

Under the same assumptions,

\[ \boxed{a-c\equiv b-d\pmod n}. \]

For example,

\[ 29-17=12 \]

and

\[ 12\equiv2\pmod5. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{29-17\equiv2\pmod5}. \]

Multiplication Preserves Congruence

If

\[ a\equiv b\pmod n \]

and

\[ c\equiv d\pmod n, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ac\equiv bd\pmod n}. \]

This makes modular arithmetic especially useful for simplifying large products.

Example: Modular Multiplication

Compute

\[ 37\cdot28\pmod5. \]

Reduce each factor:

\[ 37\equiv2\pmod5 \]

and

\[ 28\equiv3\pmod5. \]

Then

\[ 37\cdot28\equiv2\cdot3=6\equiv1\pmod5. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{37\cdot28\equiv1\pmod5}. \]

Powers Preserve Congruence

If

\[ a\equiv b\pmod n, \]

then for every positive integer \(k\),

\[ \boxed{a^k\equiv b^k\pmod n}. \]

This follows by repeated multiplication.

Example: Reducing Before Exponentiation

Compute

\[ 13^4\pmod5. \]

Since

\[ 13\equiv3\pmod5, \]

we may write

\[ 13^4\equiv3^4=81\pmod5. \]

Since

\[ 81\equiv1\pmod5, \]

we conclude

\[ \boxed{13^4\equiv1\pmod5}. \]

Reduce Early and Often

Modular arithmetic allows intermediate values to be reduced whenever doing so simplifies the calculation.

For example,

\[ 18^3\pmod7. \]

First reduce:

\[ 18\equiv4\pmod7. \]

Then

\[ 4^2=16\equiv2\pmod7, \]

so

\[ 4^3\equiv2\cdot4=8\equiv1\pmod7. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{18^3\equiv1\pmod7}. \]

Congruence Is Reflexive

Every integer is congruent to itself modulo \(n\):

\[ \boxed{a\equiv a\pmod n}. \]

This follows because

\[ a-a=0 \]

and every positive integer \(n\) divides \(0\).

Congruence Is Symmetric

If

\[ a\equiv b\pmod n, \]

then

\[ \boxed{b\equiv a\pmod n}. \]

If \(n\mid(a-b)\), then \(n\mid-(a-b)=b-a\).

Congruence Is Transitive

If

\[ a\equiv b\pmod n \]

and

\[ b\equiv c\pmod n, \]

then

\[ \boxed{a\equiv c\pmod n}. \]

Since \(n\mid(a-b)\) and \(n\mid(b-c)\), divisibility of sums gives

\[ n\mid[(a-b)+(b-c)]=a-c. \]

Congruence as an Equivalence Relation

Because congruence modulo \(n\) is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, it is an equivalence relation on the integers.

This means integers can be partitioned into groups whose members are all congruent to one another modulo \(n\).

The next lesson will formalize these groups as residue classes.

Parity as Modular Arithmetic

Even and odd integers can be described modulo \(2\).

Every even integer satisfies

\[ \boxed{n\equiv0\pmod2}, \]

while every odd integer satisfies

\[ \boxed{n\equiv1\pmod2}. \]

Thus parity is the simplest nontrivial modular classification.

Example: Odd Plus Odd

Suppose

\[ a\equiv1\pmod2 \]

and

\[ b\equiv1\pmod2. \]

Then

\[ a+b\equiv1+1=2\equiv0\pmod2. \]

Therefore the sum of two odd integers is even.

Divisibility as Congruence to Zero

The statement

\[ n\mid a \]

is equivalent to

\[ \boxed{a\equiv0\pmod n}. \]

For example,

\[ 7\mid42 \]

can be written

\[ 42\equiv0\pmod7. \]

Divisibility Tests in Modular Language

Many familiar divisibility tests can be interpreted using congruence.

For example, a number is divisible by \(3\) exactly when

\[ \boxed{n\equiv0\pmod3}. \]

The digit-sum test works because powers of \(10\) satisfy

\[ 10\equiv1\pmod3. \]

This lets place-value expressions collapse into their digit sums modulo \(3\).

Example: Why the Digit-Sum Test Works Modulo \(3\)

Consider a three-digit number

\[ 100a+10b+c. \]

Since

\[ 100\equiv1\pmod3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 10\equiv1\pmod3, \]

we have

\[ 100a+10b+c \equiv a+b+c \pmod3. \]

Therefore the original number and its digit sum have the same remainder modulo \(3\).

Modular Arithmetic and Repeating Cycles

Modular arithmetic is useful whenever a process repeats after a fixed number of steps.

Examples include:

  • hours on a clock;
  • days of the week;
  • rotational positions;
  • cyclic schedules;
  • repeating digital states.

Example: Days of the Week

A week repeats every \(7\) days.

To determine the day position \(100\) days later, reduce \(100\) modulo \(7\):

\[ 100=7(14)+2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{100\equiv2\pmod7}. \]

So the weekday shifts forward by \(2\) positions.

Solving a Simple Modular Equation

Consider

\[ x+3\equiv1\pmod5. \]

Subtract \(3\) from both sides:

\[ x\equiv-2\pmod5. \]

Reduce:

\[ -2\equiv3\pmod5. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x\equiv3\pmod5}. \]

Adding or Subtracting the Same Quantity

From

\[ a\equiv b\pmod n, \]

we may add or subtract the same integer \(c\) on both sides:

\[ \boxed{a+c\equiv b+c\pmod n} \]

and

\[ \boxed{a-c\equiv b-c\pmod n}. \]

These rules resemble ordinary equality because addition and subtraction interact cleanly with divisibility.

Multiplying Both Sides

If

\[ a\equiv b\pmod n, \]

then for any integer \(c\),

\[ \boxed{ac\equiv bc\pmod n}. \]

For example, from

\[ 8\equiv3\pmod5, \]

multiplying by \(4\) gives

\[ 32\equiv12\pmod5. \]

Both sides reduce to \(2\) modulo \(5\).

Cancellation Requires Care

Multiplication preserves congruence, but cancellation is more delicate.

For example,

\[ 2\cdot1\equiv2\cdot4\pmod6 \]

because

\[ 2\equiv8\pmod6. \]

But cancelling the factor \(2\) would incorrectly suggest

\[ 1\equiv4\pmod6. \]

This is false.

Why Cancellation Can Fail

From

\[ ac\equiv bc\pmod n, \]

we know

\[ n\mid c(a-b). \]

This does not always imply

\[ n\mid(a-b). \]

Cancellation becomes valid under additional conditions involving the common divisors of \(c\) and \(n\). Later lessons on linear congruences will develop those conditions systematically.

Division Is Not Automatically Available

Ordinary arithmetic allows division by every nonzero real number. Modular arithmetic does not.

To “divide” by a number modulo \(n\), that number must have a suitable multiplicative inverse modulo \(n\).

This is one reason modular inverses and the Euclidean Algorithm become important in later number theory.

Common Error: Using \(=\) Instead of \(\equiv\)

The statement

\[ 17\equiv5\pmod{12} \]

is correct.

Writing

\[ 17=5 \]

is not.

Congruence records equality of remainder behavior, not ordinary numerical equality.

Common Error: Forgetting the Modulus

A congruence statement is incomplete without specifying the modulus.

The expression

\[ 17\equiv5 \]

does not identify the arithmetic system being used.

The meaningful statement is

\[ \boxed{17\equiv5\pmod{12}}. \]

Common Error: Keeping a Nonstandard Remainder

Modulo \(7\), the standard remainder should lie in

\[ 0,1,2,3,4,5,6. \]

Although

\[ 20\equiv13\pmod7 \]

is true, both numbers can be reduced further:

\[ 20\equiv13\equiv6\pmod7. \]

Using the canonical remainder \(6\) usually makes calculations clearer.

Common Error: Mishandling Negative Remainders

A negative representative can be valid, but it may not be the standard nonnegative remainder.

For example,

\[ -1\equiv4\pmod5. \]

Both represent the same modular position, but \(4\) is the canonical remainder.

Common Error: Cancelling Without Checking

From

\[ 2x\equiv2\pmod6, \]

it is not valid to conclude immediately that

\[ x\equiv1\pmod6. \]

The factor \(2\) shares a nontrivial common divisor with the modulus \(6\). Modular cancellation requires additional justification.

A Reliable Procedure for Reducing Modulo \(n\)

  1. Identify the positive modulus \(n\).
  2. Divide the integer by \(n\).
  3. Find the remainder \(r\) satisfying \(0\le r
  4. Write the result as \(a\equiv r\pmod n\).
  5. For negative integers, add or subtract multiples of \(n\) until the representative lies between \(0\) and \(n-1\).

A Reliable Procedure for Modular Arithmetic

  1. Reduce large inputs modulo \(n\) when convenient.
  2. Perform the required addition, subtraction, or multiplication.
  3. Reduce intermediate results again if they become large.
  4. For powers, reduce the base first and look for repeating patterns.
  5. Express the final answer using a standard remainder when appropriate.
  6. Do not cancel or divide unless the required modular conditions have been established.

Example: Full Modular Workflow

Compute

\[ 47\cdot38+29\pmod7. \]

Reduce each input:

\[ 47\equiv5\pmod7, \qquad 38\equiv3\pmod7, \qquad 29\equiv1\pmod7. \]

Substitute:

\[ 47\cdot38+29 \equiv 5\cdot3+1 = 16 \pmod7. \]

Finally,

\[ 16\equiv2\pmod7. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{47\cdot38+29\equiv2\pmod7}. \]

Example: A Power Pattern

Find the remainder when

\[ 2^{10} \]

is divided by \(5\).

Compute small powers modulo \(5\):

\[ 2^1\equiv2, \] \[ 2^2\equiv4, \] \[ 2^3\equiv3, \] \[ 2^4\equiv1 \pmod5. \]

The pattern repeats every four powers. Since

\[ 10\equiv2\pmod4, \]

we obtain

\[ 2^{10}\equiv2^2\equiv4\pmod5. \]

Therefore the remainder is

\[ \boxed{4}. \]

What to Remember

  • \(a\equiv b\pmod n\) means \(a\) and \(b\) have the same remainder modulo \(n\).
  • Equivalently, \(a\equiv b\pmod n\) exactly when \(n\mid(a-b)\).
  • Every integer is congruent to exactly one standard remainder \(0,1,\ldots,n-1\).
  • Congruence is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
  • Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and positive integer powers preserve congruence.
  • Divisibility by \(n\) is the same as congruence to \(0\) modulo \(n\).
  • Reducing values early can simplify large calculations dramatically.
  • Cancellation and division require extra care in modular arithmetic.

The central habit is: replace integers by simpler representatives with the same remainder, while always keeping track of the modulus.
