Total Probability

Many events can occur through several different paths. The law of total probability calculates an overall probability by dividing the sample space into nonoverlapping cases, finding the probability of the target event within each case, and then adding the resulting path probabilities. It is a systematic way to combine conditional information into one unconditional probability.

The Central Idea

Suppose an event \(A\) can occur through several mutually exclusive cases

\[ B_1,B_2,\ldots,B_n. \]

If these cases together cover the entire sample space, then every occurrence of \(A\) must occur through exactly one of them.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i) }. \]

This is the law of total probability.

A Partition of the Sample Space

The events

\[ B_1,B_2,\ldots,B_n \]

form a partition of the sample space when:

  1. the events are mutually exclusive;
  2. their union is the entire sample space.

Symbolically,

\[ B_i\cap B_j=\varnothing \qquad\text{for }i\neq j \]

and

\[ B_1\cup B_2\cup\cdots\cup B_n=S. \]

Why Partitions Matter

A partition breaks one complicated probability problem into separate cases.

Because the cases do not overlap, the corresponding path probabilities can be added without double counting.

Because the cases cover the entire sample space, no possible route to the target event is omitted.

The Two-Case Version

The simplest partition uses an event \(B\) and its complement \(B^c\).

Since

\[ B\cap B^c=\varnothing \]

and

\[ B\cup B^c=S, \]

the event \(A\) can occur either with \(B\) or with \(B^c\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P(A) = P(B)P(A\mid B) + P(B^c)P(A\mid B^c) }. \]

Deriving the Two-Case Formula

The event \(A\) can be split into two disjoint pieces:

\[ A=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap B^c). \]

Since these pieces are mutually exclusive,

\[ P(A) = P(A\cap B)+P(A\cap B^c). \]

Apply the multiplication rule to each intersection:

\[ P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B) \]

and

\[ P(A\cap B^c)=P(B^c)P(A\mid B^c). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P(A) = P(B)P(A\mid B) + P(B^c)P(A\mid B^c) }. \]

Multiply Along, Add Across

Probability trees provide a useful visual rule:

\[ \boxed{\text{multiply along each path}} \]

and then

\[ \boxed{\text{add across alternative paths}}. \]

The multiplication rule gives the probability of each complete path. The addition rule combines mutually exclusive paths leading to the same target event.

A First Example

Suppose \(60\%\) of customers are new customers and \(40\%\) are returning customers.

Let

\[ N=\{\text{new customer}\} \]

and

\[ R=\{\text{returning customer}\}. \]

Suppose the probability of purchasing is

\[ P(P\mid N)=0.20 \]

for new customers and

\[ P(P\mid R)=0.50 \]

for returning customers.

Calculate Each Purchase Path

The probability of being new and purchasing is

\[ P(N\cap P) = P(N)P(P\mid N) = 0.60(0.20) = 0.12. \]

The probability of being returning and purchasing is

\[ P(R\cap P) = P(R)P(P\mid R) = 0.40(0.50) = 0.20. \]

Add the Mutually Exclusive Purchase Paths

A customer cannot be both new and returning under this classification, so the two purchase paths are mutually exclusive.

Therefore,

\[ P(P) = 0.12+0.20 = \boxed{0.32}. \]

Thus the overall purchase probability is \(32\%\).

The Overall Probability Is a Weighted Average

In the customer example,

\[ P(P) = 0.60(0.20)+0.40(0.50). \]

The group-specific purchase probabilities,

\[ 0.20 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 0.50, \]

are weighted by the population shares

\[ 0.60 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 0.40. \]

Total probability can therefore be understood as a weighted average of conditional probabilities.

The Weights Must Sum to One

Because the partition events cover the sample space,

\[ P(B_1)+P(B_2)+\cdots+P(B_n)=1. \]

These probabilities act as weights in

\[ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i). \]

The structure is exactly that of a weighted average.

The Overall Probability Lies Between the Conditional Probabilities

Because total probability is a weighted average, the overall probability cannot exceed the largest conditional probability or fall below the smallest one, provided the partition probabilities are nonnegative and sum to \(1\).

If

\[ 0.20\leq P(A\mid B_i)\leq0.70 \]

for every partition group, then

\[ 0.20\leq P(A)\leq0.70. \]

This provides a useful reasonableness check.

Example: Manufacturing Lines

A factory uses three production lines:

  • Line 1 produces \(50\%\) of all units;
  • Line 2 produces \(30\%\);
  • Line 3 produces \(20\%\).

Their defect rates are:

\[ P(D\mid L_1)=0.01, \] \[ P(D\mid L_2)=0.03, \] \[ P(D\mid L_3)=0.05. \]

Calculate the Defect Paths

Line 1 contributes

\[ P(L_1)P(D\mid L_1) = 0.50(0.01) = 0.005. \]

Line 2 contributes

\[ 0.30(0.03)=0.009. \]

Line 3 contributes

\[ 0.20(0.05)=0.010. \]

Add the Defect Paths

The overall defect probability is

\[ P(D) = 0.005+0.009+0.010. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P(D)=0.024}. \]

The factory-wide defect rate is \(2.4\%\).

The Most Defective Line Need Not Produce Most Defects

Line 3 has the highest conditional defect rate:

\[ 5\%. \]

But the number of defective units contributed by each line also depends on how much that line produces.

Total probability combines both:

\[ \text{group size}\times\text{within-group event rate}. \]

This distinction is important whenever subgroups have different sizes.

Example: Medical Screening Population

Suppose \(2\%\) of a population has a condition:

\[ P(D)=0.02. \]

Therefore,

\[ P(D^c)=0.98. \]

A test returns positive with probability

\[ P(+\mid D)=0.95 \]

among people with the condition and

\[ P(+\mid D^c)=0.04 \]

among people without it.

Find the Overall Positive-Test Probability

A positive test can occur through two paths:

\[ D\cap+ \]

or

\[ D^c\cap+. \]

Therefore,

\[ P(+) = P(D)P(+\mid D) + P(D^c)P(+\mid D^c). \]

Substitute:

\[ P(+) = 0.02(0.95)+0.98(0.04). \]

Thus,

\[ P(+) = 0.019+0.0392 = \boxed{0.0582}. \]

The overall positive-test rate is \(5.82\%\).

Why the Overall Positive Rate Exceeds the Disease Rate

The disease rate is only

\[ 2\%. \]

However, positive results arise both from true positives and from false positives.

Total probability combines both sources into the overall positive-test probability.

A later lesson on diagnostic testing will examine this structure in more detail.

Total Probability from Counts

The law of total probability can also be applied with counts.

Suppose a school has:

  • \(300\) first-year students with a \(70\%\) course-completion rate;
  • \(200\) second-year students with an \(80\%\) completion rate.

The total student population is

\[ 500. \]

The group weights are

\[ \frac{300}{500}=0.60 \]

and

\[ \frac{200}{500}=0.40. \]

Calculate the Overall Completion Rate

Apply total probability:

\[ P(C) = 0.60(0.70)+0.40(0.80). \]

Therefore,

\[ P(C) = 0.42+0.32 = \boxed{0.74}. \]

The overall completion rate is \(74\%\).

The Same Count Example Directly

The number of first-year students who complete the course is

\[ 300(0.70)=210. \]

The number of second-year students who complete is

\[ 200(0.80)=160. \]

Thus total completions equal

\[ 210+160=370. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{370}{500}=0.74. \]

The count method and total-probability method agree.

Total Probability from a Two-Way Table

Consider the following data:

Purchased Did Not Purchase Total
New Customer 24 96 120
Returning Customer 40 40 80
Total 64 136 200

Directly from the table,

\[ P(P)=\frac{64}{200}=0.32. \]

Verify the Table with Total Probability

The group probabilities are

\[ P(N)=\frac{120}{200}=0.60 \]

and

\[ P(R)=\frac{80}{200}=0.40. \]

The conditional purchase probabilities are

\[ P(P\mid N)=\frac{24}{120}=0.20 \]

and

\[ P(P\mid R)=\frac{40}{80}=0.50. \]

Therefore,

\[ P(P) = 0.60(0.20)+0.40(0.50) = \boxed{0.32}. \]

Partitioning by More Than One Category

A partition may contain any finite number of mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive categories.

For example:

\[ B_1=\text{small}, \qquad B_2=\text{medium}, \qquad B_3=\text{large}. \]

Then

\[ P(A) = P(B_1)P(A\mid B_1) + P(B_2)P(A\mid B_2) + P(B_3)P(A\mid B_3). \]

Partitions Must Not Overlap

Suppose we classify customers as:

  • customers under age \(40\);
  • customers who use mobile banking.

These groups can overlap, so they do not form a partition by themselves.

Adding path probabilities based on overlapping groups could double count customers belonging to both categories.

Partitions Must Cover All Possibilities

Suppose a population is divided into:

  • ages \(18\) through \(29\);
  • ages \(30\) through \(49\).

If people aged \(50\) or older are also part of the population, these two categories do not form a complete partition.

Any total-probability calculation based only on them would omit part of the sample space.

Check the Partition Before Calculating

A reliable partition should satisfy both:

\[ B_i\cap B_j=\varnothing \qquad(i\neq j) \]

and

\[ \bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_i=S. \]

In plain language:

  • no outcome belongs to more than one case;
  • every outcome belongs to some case.

Changing the Group Mix Changes the Total

Suppose the conditional conversion rates remain unchanged, but traffic shifts toward the highest-converting source.

Even if no within-group rate changes, the overall conversion rate can change because the weights

\[ P(B_i) \]

have changed.

Total probability therefore separates two drivers:

  • the size of each subgroup;
  • the event rate within each subgroup.

Composition Effects

An overall probability can rise or fall because the population composition changes, even when every conditional probability stays fixed.

This is sometimes called a composition effect.

Recognizing composition effects is important in business metrics, demography, risk analysis, education data, and many other applications.

Overall Rates Can Hide Subgroup Differences

A single unconditional probability such as

\[ P(A) \]

summarizes the entire population.

But the conditional probabilities

\[ P(A\mid B_1), \quad P(A\mid B_2), \quad\ldots \]

reveal how the event behaves within specific groups.

Total probability connects these two levels of description.

Using Complements Within Total Probability

Suppose a problem provides failure probabilities but asks for success.

For each group,

\[ P(S\mid B_i)=1-P(F\mid B_i). \]

These complementary conditional probabilities can then be inserted into the total-probability formula:

\[ P(S) = \sum_i P(B_i)P(S\mid B_i). \]

Example: Convert Failure Rates to Success Rates

Suppose two systems are used:

\[ P(S_1)=0.40,\qquad P(S_2)=0.60. \]

Their failure probabilities are

\[ P(F\mid S_1)=0.10 \]

and

\[ P(F\mid S_2)=0.04. \]

Therefore,

\[ P(F) = 0.40(0.10)+0.60(0.04) = 0.064. \]

Hence,

\[ P(F^c)=1-0.064=\boxed{0.936}. \]

Total Probability and Probability Trees

A tree organizes total probability naturally.

At the first level, branch probabilities represent the partition:

\[ P(B_1),P(B_2),\ldots,P(B_n). \]

At the second level, branches represent conditional probabilities such as

\[ P(A\mid B_i). \]

Multiply each path:

\[ P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i). \]

Then add all paths ending in \(A\).

A Tree Is an Organizational Tool

The law of total probability does not require drawing a tree.

A tree is useful because it makes three things visible:

  • the partition at the first stage;
  • the conditional probabilities within each branch;
  • the separate paths leading to the target event.

The underlying calculation is still the same multiplication-and-addition structure.

Total Probability with Four Groups

Suppose

\[ P(B_1)=0.10,\quad P(B_2)=0.20,\quad P(B_3)=0.30,\quad P(B_4)=0.40, \]

and

\[ P(A\mid B_1)=0.20, \] \[ P(A\mid B_2)=0.30, \] \[ P(A\mid B_3)=0.40, \] \[ P(A\mid B_4)=0.50. \]

Then

\[ P(A) = 0.10(0.20) +0.20(0.30) +0.30(0.40) +0.40(0.50). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P(A)=0.40}. \]

Recovering an Unknown Conditional Probability

Total probability can also be used as an equation.

Suppose

\[ P(B)=0.60, \qquad P(B^c)=0.40, \] \[ P(A\mid B)=0.30, \]

and

\[ P(A)=0.42. \]

Let

\[ x=P(A\mid B^c). \]

Then

\[ 0.42=0.60(0.30)+0.40x. \]

Solve for the Missing Conditional Probability

Continue:

\[ 0.42=0.18+0.40x. \]

Subtract \(0.18\):

\[ 0.24=0.40x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=0.60}. \]

Thus,

\[ P(A\mid B^c)=0.60. \]

Recovering an Unknown Group Weight

Suppose a population consists of groups \(B\) and \(B^c\).

Let

\[ P(B)=x, \qquad P(B^c)=1-x. \]

If

\[ P(A\mid B)=0.80, \] \[ P(A\mid B^c)=0.30, \]

and

\[ P(A)=0.50, \]

then

\[ 0.50=0.80x+0.30(1-x). \]

Solve for the Group Weight

Expand:

\[ 0.50=0.80x+0.30-0.30x. \]

Combine terms:

\[ 0.20=0.50x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=0.40}. \]

Thus \(40\%\) of the population belongs to \(B\).

Total Probability as Decomposition

The formula

\[ P(A) = \sum_i P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i) \]

decomposes an overall probability into subgroup contributions.

Each term

\[ P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i) \]

is the joint probability

\[ P(A\cap B_i). \]

Therefore,

\[ P(A) = \sum_i P(A\cap B_i). \]

The Joint Contributions Sum to the Total

Because the partition events are mutually exclusive,

\[ (A\cap B_i)\cap(A\cap B_j)=\varnothing \qquad(i\neq j). \]

Because the partition covers the sample space,

\[ A = (A\cap B_1) \cup (A\cap B_2) \cup \cdots \cup (A\cap B_n). \]

The total probability formula follows by adding these disjoint pieces.

Why This Matters for Bayesian Reasoning

Bayes' theorem often requires a denominator such as

\[ P(A). \]

When \(A\) can occur through several underlying groups, total probability supplies that denominator:

\[ P(A) = \sum_i P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i). \]

The next lesson will use this structure to reverse conditional probabilities.

Common Error: Forgetting a Path

Suppose an event \(A\) can occur through three partition groups:

\[ B_1,\quad B_2,\quad B_3. \]

A calculation using only

\[ P(B_1)P(A\mid B_1) + P(B_2)P(A\mid B_2) \]

is incomplete if the \(B_3\) path can also produce \(A\).

Every relevant partition path must be included.

Common Error: Using Overlapping Groups

Total probability requires a partition.

If two groups overlap, an outcome in the overlap can be counted twice when path probabilities are added.

Before using the formula, verify that the cases are mutually exclusive.

Common Error: Using Groups That Do Not Cover the Sample Space

If the selected cases omit possible outcomes, then the sum

\[ \sum_i P(B_i) \]

will be less than \(1\).

Any resulting total probability may therefore omit part of the target event.

Common Error: Adding Conditional Probabilities Directly

Suppose

\[ P(A\mid B_1)=0.20 \]

and

\[ P(A\mid B_2)=0.60. \]

We cannot simply write

\[ P(A)=0.20+0.60. \]

Each conditional probability must first be weighted by the probability of its group:

\[ P(A) = P(B_1)P(A\mid B_1) + P(B_2)P(A\mid B_2). \]

Common Error: Multiplying All Paths Together

Alternative partition paths represent mutually exclusive routes to the same event.

Multiply within each route:

\[ P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i). \]

Then add the route probabilities:

\[ \sum_i P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i). \]

Do not multiply probabilities from separate alternative paths together.

Common Error: Forgetting That the Weights Are Probabilities

The partition weights must satisfy

\[ P(B_i)\geq0 \]

and

\[ \sum_iP(B_i)=1. \]

If given percentages sum to \(100\%\), they form valid population weights. If they do not, the classification may be incomplete or inconsistent.

Common Error: Confusing \(P(A)\) with \(P(A\mid B_i)\)

The quantity

\[ P(A\mid B_i) \]

is the event rate within group \(B_i\).

The quantity

\[ P(A) \]

is the overall event rate across the full population.

Total probability is the bridge between them.

Common Error: Expecting the Overall Rate to Equal an Unweighted Average

Suppose two groups have event rates

\[ 0.20 \]

and

\[ 0.80. \]

Their simple average is

\[ 0.50. \]

But the overall probability equals \(0.50\) only if the two groups have equal probability weights.

In general, use the weighted average supplied by total probability.

A Reliable Procedure

  1. Identify the target event \(A\).
  2. Identify a partition \(B_1,\ldots,B_n\) of the sample space.
  3. Verify that the partition groups are mutually exclusive.
  4. Verify that their probabilities sum to \(1\).
  5. Find \(P(A\mid B_i)\) for each group.
  6. Multiply \(P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i)\) for each path.
  7. Add all path probabilities.
  8. Check that the result is between the smallest and largest relevant conditional rates.
  9. Interpret the result as the overall probability of \(A\).

What to Remember

If

\[ B_1,B_2,\ldots,B_n \]

form a partition of the sample space, then

\[ \boxed{ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i) }. \]
  • The partition cases must be mutually exclusive.
  • The partition cases must cover the full sample space.
  • Multiply within each path.
  • Add across alternative paths leading to the target event.
  • The group probabilities act as weights.
  • The overall probability is a weighted average of conditional probabilities.
  • Changing subgroup proportions can change the overall rate even when within-group rates stay fixed.

The central habit is: decompose the event into complete, nonoverlapping paths before adding their probabilities.
