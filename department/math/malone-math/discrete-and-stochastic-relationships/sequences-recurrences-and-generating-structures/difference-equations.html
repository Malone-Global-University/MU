Difference Equations

A difference equation describes how a discrete quantity changes from one indexed step to the next. Instead of focusing only on the values of a sequence, we study differences between consecutive values and equations governing those differences. This provides a natural discrete counterpart to equations of continuous change.

The Central Idea

For a sequence

\[ a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots, \]

the change from one term to the next is measured by a difference.

The forward difference is

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=a_{n+1}-a_n}. \]

A difference equation specifies a relationship involving one or more of these discrete changes.

For example,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=4} \]

says that the sequence increases by \(4\) at every step.

From Differences to Recurrences

Start with

\[ \Delta a_n=4. \]

By definition,

\[ a_{n+1}-a_n=4. \]

Add \(a_n\) to both sides:

\[ \boxed{a_{n+1}=a_n+4}. \]

Thus, a difference equation and a recurrence can express the same discrete process in different forms.

From Recurrences to Differences

Suppose

\[ a_{n+1}=a_n+7. \]

Subtract \(a_n\):

\[ a_{n+1}-a_n=7. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=7}. \]

The recurrence emphasizes the next value. The difference equation emphasizes the change.

First Differences

The first difference sequence is obtained by subtracting each term from the next.

Consider

\[ 3,\ 8,\ 13,\ 18,\ 23,\ldots \]

The first differences are

\[ 8-3=5, \] \[ 13-8=5, \] \[ 18-13=5. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=5}. \]

Constant First Differences

Constant first differences characterize arithmetic sequences.

If

\[ \Delta a_n=d, \]

then

\[ a_{n+1}=a_n+d. \]

Thus, arithmetic recurrences can be interpreted directly as constant-change difference equations.

Variable First Differences

A difference equation does not need to have a constant right-hand side.

Suppose

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=2n+1}. \]

Then

\[ a_{n+1}=a_n+2n+1. \]

The amount added changes with the index.

Example: Generate Terms from a Difference Equation

Let

\[ a_0=4 \]

and

\[ \Delta a_n=2n+1. \]

Since

\[ a_{n+1}=a_n+2n+1, \]

we obtain

\[ a_1=4+1=5, \] \[ a_2=5+3=8, \] \[ a_3=8+5=13, \] \[ a_4=13+7=20. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{4,\ 5,\ 8,\ 13,\ 20,\ldots}. \]

The Forward-Difference Operator

The symbol

\[ \Delta \]

is called the forward-difference operator.

Applied to a sequence \(a_n\),

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=a_{n+1}-a_n}. \]

It transforms the original sequence into a new sequence describing step-to-step change.

Difference Tables

A difference table places a sequence and its successive differences in layers.

Consider

\[ 1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ 25. \]

First differences:

\[ 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 9. \]

Second differences:

\[ 2,\ 2,\ 2. \]

The constant second difference reveals important polynomial structure.

Second Differences

The second difference is the difference of the first-difference sequence:

\[ \boxed{\Delta^2a_n=\Delta(\Delta a_n)}. \]

Expanding,

\[ \Delta^2a_n = (a_{n+2}-a_{n+1})-(a_{n+1}-a_n). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta^2a_n=a_{n+2}-2a_{n+1}+a_n}. \]

Example: Constant Second Difference

Let

\[ a_n=n^2. \]

Then

\[ \Delta a_n = (n+1)^2-n^2 = 2n+1. \]

Apply the difference operator again:

\[ \Delta^2a_n = [2(n+1)+1]-(2n+1). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\Delta^2a_n=2}. \]

Polynomial Sequences and Differences

Successive differences reduce polynomial degree.

Roughly:

  • a linear sequence has constant first differences;
  • a quadratic sequence has constant second differences;
  • a cubic sequence has constant third differences.

This provides a practical way to recognize polynomial patterns in finite data.

Third and Higher Differences

Higher differences are defined repeatedly:

\[ \Delta^3a_n=\Delta(\Delta^2a_n), \] \[ \Delta^4a_n=\Delta(\Delta^3a_n), \]

and so on.

Each layer measures how the previous layer of discrete change is itself changing.

Difference Equations as Discrete Change Laws

A difference equation can be read as a rule for change.

For example,

\[ \Delta P_n=50 \]

means that \(P_n\) increases by \(50\) each period.

The equation

\[ \Delta P_n=0.1P_n \]

means that the change equals \(10\%\) of the current amount.

State-Dependent Difference Equations

Suppose

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=0.2a_n}. \]

Then

\[ a_{n+1}-a_n=0.2a_n. \]

Therefore,

\[ a_{n+1}=1.2a_n. \]

This is a geometric recurrence with growth factor \(1.2\).

Decay in Difference Form

Suppose

\[ \Delta a_n=-0.15a_n. \]

Then

\[ a_{n+1}=a_n-0.15a_n. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{a_{n+1}=0.85a_n}. \]

The quantity retains \(85\%\) of its value at every step.

Difference Form of a First-Order Linear Recurrence

Consider

\[ a_{n+1}=ra_n+b. \]

Subtract \(a_n\):

\[ a_{n+1}-a_n=(r-1)a_n+b. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=(r-1)a_n+b}. \]

This form isolates the change produced by the current state and the external input.

Example: Growth with Constant Input

Suppose

\[ a_{n+1}=1.05a_n+20. \]

Then

\[ \Delta a_n = 1.05a_n+20-a_n. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=0.05a_n+20}. \]

The change consists of \(5\%\) growth plus a constant addition of \(20\).

Equilibrium Values

An equilibrium is a state that does not change under the difference equation.

At equilibrium,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=0}. \]

If

\[ \Delta a_n=0.2a_n-10, \]

then equilibrium satisfies

\[ 0=0.2a-10. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{a=50}. \]

Checking an Equilibrium

For

\[ \Delta a_n=0.2a_n-10, \]

substitute \(a_n=50\):

\[ \Delta a_n=0.2(50)-10=0. \]

Therefore, if the sequence reaches \(50\), the next value remains \(50\).

Equilibrium in Recurrence Form

Suppose

\[ a_{n+1}=0.8a_n+10. \]

An equilibrium \(L\) satisfies

\[ L=0.8L+10. \]

Therefore,

\[ 0.2L=10, \] \[ \boxed{L=50}. \]

This matches the zero-change condition obtained from the equivalent difference equation.

Positive and Negative Change

A difference equation immediately reveals whether the sequence rises or falls at a particular state.

If

\[ \Delta a_n>0, \]

then

\[ a_{n+1}>a_n. \]

If

\[ \Delta a_n<0, \]

then

\[ a_{n+1} If

\[ \Delta a_n=0, \]

the value remains unchanged for that step.

Example: Sign Analysis

Consider

\[ \Delta a_n=12-0.3a_n. \]

The equilibrium is found from

\[ 12-0.3a=0, \]

giving

\[ a=40. \]

If \(a_n<40\), then

\[ \Delta a_n>0. \]

If \(a_n>40\), then

\[ \Delta a_n<0. \]

The sign of the difference points toward the equilibrium.

Difference Equations and Initial Conditions

A difference equation generally requires starting information.

For example,

\[ \Delta a_n=3 \]

does not determine one unique sequence.

With

\[ a_0=2, \]

the sequence is

\[ 2,\ 5,\ 8,\ 11,\ldots \]

while with

\[ a_0=10, \]

it becomes

\[ 10,\ 13,\ 16,\ 19,\ldots \]

Summing Differences

Since

\[ \Delta a_k=a_{k+1}-a_k, \]

summing consecutive differences gives cancellation:

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta a_k = (a_1-a_0)+(a_2-a_1)+\cdots+(a_n-a_{n-1}). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta a_k=a_n-a_0}. \]

Discrete Accumulation

Rearranging the telescoping identity gives

\[ \boxed{ a_n = a_0+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta a_k }. \]

This says:

final value = initial value + accumulated discrete changes.

This principle is fundamental in discrete modeling.

Example: Accumulating a Variable Difference

Suppose

\[ \Delta a_k=2k+1 \]

and

\[ a_0=4. \]

Then

\[ a_n = 4+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(2k+1). \]

The sum of the first \(n\) odd positive integers is \(n^2\), so

\[ \boxed{a_n=4+n^2}. \]

Thus, summing a difference equation can reveal an explicit form.

Differences as a Discrete Analogue of Derivatives

In continuous mathematics, a derivative measures change with respect to a continuously varying input.

In discrete mathematics,

\[ \Delta a_n=a_{n+1}-a_n \]

measures change across one index step.

The ideas are related, but a finite difference is not literally the same object as a derivative.

One belongs to discrete change; the other belongs to continuous change.

First Difference of a Linear Explicit Rule

Let

\[ a_n=mn+b. \]

Then

\[ a_{n+1}=m(n+1)+b. \]

Therefore,

\[ \Delta a_n = [m(n+1)+b]-(mn+b). \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=m}. \]

This is the discrete version of constant linear rate of change.

First Difference of a Quadratic Explicit Rule

Let

\[ a_n=an^2+bn+c. \]

Then

\[ \Delta a_n = a(n+1)^2+b(n+1)+c-(an^2+bn+c). \]

Simplifying,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=2an+a+b}. \]

The first difference of a quadratic sequence is linear in \(n\).

Second Difference of a Quadratic Rule

From

\[ \Delta a_n=2an+a+b, \]

take another difference:

\[ \Delta^2a_n = [2a(n+1)+a+b]-(2an+a+b). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta^2a_n=2a}. \]

The constant second difference is twice the quadratic coefficient.

Recovering a Quadratic Coefficient

Suppose a sequence comes from

\[ a_n=An^2+Bn+C \]

and has constant second difference

\[ 6. \]

Since

\[ \Delta^2a_n=2A, \]

we have

\[ 2A=6. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A=3}. \]

Difference Equations in Inventory Models

Suppose inventory receives \(40\) units and ships \(55\) units each day.

The net daily change is

\[ 40-55=-15. \]

If \(I_n\) is the inventory after day \(n\), then

\[ \boxed{\Delta I_n=-15}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ I_{n+1}=I_n-15. \]

Difference Equations in Population Models

Suppose a population has \(B_n\) births and \(D_n\) deaths during period \(n\).

Then a simple balance equation is

\[ \boxed{\Delta P_n=B_n-D_n}. \]

Additional migration terms could also be included.

The difference equation separates the change into interpretable contributions.

Difference Equations in Financial Models

Suppose a balance earns rate \(r\) per period and then receives a deposit \(D\).

The recurrence is

\[ B_{n+1}=(1+r)B_n+D. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta B_n=rB_n+D}. \]

The balance changes through earned growth plus an external contribution.

Difference Equations in Motion

Suppose \(x_n\) is position at discrete time \(n\).

Then

\[ \Delta x_n=x_{n+1}-x_n \]

measures the displacement over one time step.

If the time step is one unit, this difference can serve as a discrete velocity measure.

Second Differences and Discrete Acceleration

If

\[ \Delta x_n \]

represents stepwise displacement, then

\[ \Delta^2x_n \]

measures how that displacement changes from step to step.

This creates a discrete analogue of acceleration.

Example: Constant Discrete Acceleration

Consider positions

\[ 0,\ 1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ldots \]

First differences are

\[ 1,\ 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ldots \]

and second differences are

\[ 2,\ 2,\ 2,\ldots \]

Thus, the position sequence has constant second difference.

Nonlinear Difference Equations

Difference equations can be nonlinear.

For example,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=(a_n)^2-1} \]

becomes

\[ a_{n+1}=a_n+(a_n)^2-1. \]

The squared state makes the equation nonlinear.

Difference Equations Can Encode Logistic-Type Change

A nonlinear discrete growth model may have the form

\[ \Delta P_n=rP_n\left(1-\frac{P_n}{K}\right). \]

Here \(r\) controls growth and \(K\) acts as a capacity scale.

This example shows how difference equations can represent state-dependent growth that weakens as the state becomes large.

Detailed nonlinear dynamics are beyond the scope of this lesson.

Difference Equations and Fibonacci-Type Sequences

For a Fibonacci-type sequence,

\[ a_{n+1}=a_n+a_{n-1}. \]

Subtract \(a_n\):

\[ \Delta a_n=a_{n-1}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=a_{n-1}}. \]

The stepwise change is itself an earlier term of the sequence.

Difference Equations and Linear Recurrences

Difference equations and linear recurrences are closely related.

For example,

\[ \Delta a_n=2a_n+3 \]

is equivalent to

\[ a_{n+1}=3a_n+3. \]

Choosing one form or the other depends on whether we want to emphasize the new state or the change itself.

Common Error: Reversing the Difference

With the forward-difference convention,

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=a_{n+1}-a_n}. \]

Reversing the subtraction changes the sign.

For the sequence

\[ 3,\ 8,\ 13,\ldots, \]

the forward difference is

\[ 8-3=5, \]

not \(-5\).

Common Error: Confusing a Difference with a Ratio

The expression

\[ a_{n+1}-a_n \]

measures additive change.

The expression

\[ \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \]

measures multiplicative change when \(a_n\neq0\).

These are different quantities.

Common Error: Assuming Every Difference Is Constant

A difference equation can have

\[ \Delta a_n=n, \]

or

\[ \Delta a_n=0.1a_n, \]

or many other forms.

Constant differences are only one special case.

Common Error: Forgetting the Initial Condition

The equation

\[ \Delta a_n=4 \]

does not identify a unique sequence.

An initial value such as

\[ a_0=7 \]

is also needed.

Common Error: Misreading \(\Delta^2 a_n\)

The notation

\[ \Delta^2a_n \]

does not mean

\[ (\Delta a_n)^2. \]

It means apply the difference operator twice:

\[ \boxed{\Delta^2a_n=\Delta(\Delta a_n)}. \]

Common Error: Treating a Difference as a Derivative

A finite difference measures change over a discrete step.

A derivative measures an instantaneous continuous rate of change.

They are conceptually related, but they are not interchangeable without additional assumptions or limiting arguments.

A Reliable Procedure for First-Difference Problems

  1. Write \(\Delta a_n=a_{n+1}-a_n\).
  2. Substitute the stated difference rule.
  3. Rearrange to a recurrence if needed.
  4. Use the initial condition to generate terms.
  5. Check the sign and indexing of each difference.
  6. Interpret the result as stepwise change.

A Reliable Procedure for Difference Tables

  1. Write the original sequence in order.
  2. Subtract each term from the next to form first differences.
  3. Repeat the process to form second differences.
  4. Continue as needed.
  5. Look for a constant layer or another recognizable pattern.
  6. Use the pattern to infer possible structure.

A Reliable Procedure for Equilibrium Analysis

  1. Write the difference equation.
  2. Set \(\Delta a_n=0\).
  3. Solve for the equilibrium state.
  4. Check the sign of \(\Delta a_n\) below and above that state.
  5. Interpret whether nearby values move toward or away from equilibrium.

A Reliable Procedure for Translating a Recurrence

  1. Start with a recurrence such as \(a_{n+1}=F(a_n,n)\).
  2. Subtract \(a_n\) from both sides.
  3. Replace \(a_{n+1}-a_n\) by \(\Delta a_n\).
  4. Simplify the resulting change law.

What to Remember

A difference equation describes discrete change.

\[ \boxed{\Delta a_n=a_{n+1}-a_n}. \]
  • Difference equations and recurrences can often be translated into each other.
  • Constant first differences characterize arithmetic sequences.
  • Second and higher differences measure changes in lower-order differences.
  • Polynomial sequences produce constant differences at a predictable level.
  • State-dependent differences can model growth, decay, finance, inventory, and population change.
  • Equilibrium occurs where the discrete change is zero.
  • Summing differences reconstructs accumulated change.
  • Finite differences are discrete analogues of continuous change measures, but they are not derivatives.
  • Initial conditions remain necessary to identify a particular solution.

The central accumulation identity is

\[ \boxed{ a_n=a_0+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta a_k }. \]
