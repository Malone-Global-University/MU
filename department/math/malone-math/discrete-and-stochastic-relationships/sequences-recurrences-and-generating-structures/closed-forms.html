Closed Forms

A recursive rule tells us how to move from earlier terms to later ones. A closed form gives a term directly from its index, without requiring us to generate every preceding term. Closed forms can make distant terms easier to evaluate, reveal large-scale structure, and connect discrete sequences with familiar algebraic functions.

The Central Idea

A recursive description might say

\[ a_1=4,\qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+3. \]

This tells us how to build the sequence step by step:

\[ 4,\ 7,\ 10,\ 13,\ldots \]

A direct formula for the same sequence is

\[ \boxed{a_n=3n+1}. \]

The formula gives \(a_n\) directly from \(n\). This is the essential idea of a closed form.

What Is a Closed Form?

A closed form is an expression that gives the value of a sequence term directly in terms of the index and standard operations or familiar functions.

In a simple sequence setting, a closed form often looks like

\[ \boxed{a_n=f(n)}. \]

The important feature is that computing \(a_n\) does not require first computing

\[ a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{n-1}. \]

Closed Form versus Recursive Form

Compare the recursive description

\[ a_1=5,\qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+4 \]

with the closed form

\[ \boxed{a_n=5+4(n-1)}. \]

The recursive form emphasizes the repeated process.

The closed form emphasizes direct dependence on the index.

Why Closed Forms Are Useful

A closed form can make it easier to:

  • evaluate distant terms quickly;
  • compare values at different indices;
  • study long-term growth;
  • identify algebraic structure;
  • prove identities;
  • graph a sequence through points \((n,a_n)\);
  • connect recurrence relations with familiar functions.

Direct Evaluation

Suppose

\[ a_n=7n-2. \]

To find \(a_{100}\), substitute \(n=100\):

\[ a_{100}=7(100)-2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a_{100}=698}. \]

No intermediate terms are required.

Arithmetic Sequences Have Simple Closed Forms

If

\[ a_1=A \]

and

\[ a_n=a_{n-1}+d, \]

then

\[ \boxed{a_n=A+(n-1)d}. \]

This is the standard closed form of an arithmetic sequence.

Deriving the Arithmetic Closed Form

Starting from

\[ a_1=A, \]

we obtain

\[ a_2=A+d, \] \[ a_3=A+2d, \] \[ a_4=A+3d. \]

Reaching the \(n\)th term requires \(n-1\) additions of \(d\), so

\[ \boxed{a_n=A+(n-1)d}. \]

Example: Arithmetic Recurrence to Closed Form

Let

\[ a_1=9, \] \[ a_n=a_{n-1}+5. \]

Then

\[ a_n=9+5(n-1). \]

Simplifying,

\[ \boxed{a_n=5n+4}. \]

Geometric Sequences Have Simple Closed Forms

If

\[ a_1=A \]

and

\[ a_n=ra_{n-1}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{a_n=Ar^{n-1}}. \]

This is the standard closed form of a geometric sequence.

Deriving the Geometric Closed Form

Starting from

\[ a_1=A, \]

repeated multiplication gives

\[ a_2=Ar, \] \[ a_3=Ar^2, \] \[ a_4=Ar^3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a_n=Ar^{n-1}}. \]

Example: Geometric Recurrence to Closed Form

Let

\[ a_1=6, \] \[ a_n=3a_{n-1}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{a_n=6\cdot3^{n-1}}. \]

Indexing from Zero

If an arithmetic sequence begins at

\[ a_0=A, \]

then

\[ \boxed{a_n=A+nd}. \]

If a geometric sequence begins at

\[ a_0=A, \]

then

\[ \boxed{a_n=Ar^n}. \]

The exponent or step count depends on the indexing convention.

Closed Forms from Constant Differences

If

\[ \Delta a_n=d, \]

then

\[ a_n=a_0+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d. \]

Since there are \(n\) copies of \(d\),

\[ \boxed{a_n=a_0+nd}. \]

Thus, summing the discrete change produces the closed form.

Closed Forms from Variable Differences

Suppose

\[ \Delta a_k=g(k) \]

and

\[ a_0=A. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ a_n=A+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(k) }. \]

If the finite sum can be simplified to a direct expression in \(n\), that expression gives a closed form.

Example: Sum of Odd Differences

Suppose

\[ a_0=2 \]

and

\[ \Delta a_k=2k+1. \]

Then

\[ a_n = 2+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(2k+1). \]

Since

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(2k+1)=n^2, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{a_n=n^2+2}. \]

Example: Sum of Consecutive Integers

Suppose

\[ a_0=0 \]

and

\[ \Delta a_k=k+1. \]

Then

\[ a_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(k+1). \]

This is

\[ 1+2+\cdots+n. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a_n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}. \]

Closed Forms Can Contain Finite Sums

Definitions of "closed form" vary by context.

In an elementary sequence lesson, we usually reserve the term for an expression that does not require recursively generating earlier sequence terms.

A finite summation such as

\[ a_n=A+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(k) \]

is already nonrecursive, but simplifying the sum further may produce a more compact closed expression.

Polynomial Closed Forms

Many sequences with constant higher differences have polynomial closed forms.

For example,

\[ 1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ldots \]

has closed form

\[ \boxed{a_n=n^2}. \]

Its constant second difference reflects the quadratic structure.

Exponential Closed Forms

Geometric recurrences lead naturally to exponential closed forms.

For example,

\[ a_0=5,\qquad a_{n+1}=2a_n \]

has closed form

\[ \boxed{a_n=5\cdot2^n}. \]

Alternating Closed Forms

The recurrence

\[ a_{n+1}=-a_n \]

with

\[ a_0=A \]

alternates sign:

\[ A,\ -A,\ A,\ -A,\ldots \]

A closed form is

\[ \boxed{a_n=A(-1)^n}. \]

Affine First-Order Recurrences

A recurrence of the form

\[ a_{n+1}=ra_n+b \]

combines multiplication by \(r\) with constant input \(b\).

Such recurrences also admit useful closed forms when \(r\neq1\).

A convenient strategy is to identify the equilibrium value first.

Equilibrium Shift

Suppose

\[ a_{n+1}=ra_n+b. \]

A fixed value \(L\) satisfies

\[ L=rL+b. \]

If \(r\neq1\), then

\[ \boxed{L=\frac{b}{1-r}}. \]

Subtracting \(L\) from the sequence often converts the recurrence into a geometric one.

Deriving a Closed Form by Shifting

Let

\[ b_n=a_n-L. \]

Since

\[ a_{n+1}=ra_n+b \]

and

\[ L=rL+b, \]

subtracting gives

\[ b_{n+1}=rb_n. \]

Therefore,

\[ b_n=(a_0-L)r^n. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ a_n=L+(a_0-L)r^n }. \]

Example: Affine Closed Form

Let

\[ a_0=10, \] \[ a_{n+1}=0.5a_n+6. \]

The equilibrium satisfies

\[ L=0.5L+6, \]

so

\[ L=12. \]

Therefore,

\[ a_n = 12+(10-12)(0.5)^n. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{a_n=12-2(0.5)^n}. \]

Checking the Affine Closed Form

For

\[ a_n=12-2(0.5)^n, \]

the initial value is

\[ a_0=12-2=10. \]

Also,

\[ a_{n+1} = 12-2(0.5)^{n+1}. \]

Meanwhile,

\[ 0.5a_n+6 = 0.5[12-2(0.5)^n]+6. \]

Simplifying gives the same expression, so the formula satisfies the recurrence.

Verification Is Essential

A proposed closed form should be checked against:

  1. the recurrence relation;
  2. the initial conditions.

Satisfying only one of these requirements is not enough.

Example: Verify a Geometric Closed Form

Suppose

\[ a_1=4, \] \[ a_n=3a_{n-1}. \]

Proposed closed form:

\[ a_n=4\cdot3^{n-1}. \]

Initial condition:

\[ a_1=4\cdot3^0=4. \]

Recurrence:

\[ 3a_{n-1} = 3\left(4\cdot3^{n-2}\right) = 4\cdot3^{n-1} = a_n. \]

Both requirements hold.

A Formula Can Satisfy the Recurrence but Miss the Initial Condition

Consider

\[ a_n=2a_{n-1}. \]

Both

\[ a_n=2^{n-1} \]

and

\[ a_n=5\cdot2^{n-1} \]

satisfy the recurrence.

If the initial condition is

\[ a_1=5, \]

only the second formula matches the full definition.

Closed Form Does Not Mean Polynomial

Closed forms can involve many familiar functions and operations.

Examples include:

\[ a_n=3n+2, \] \[ a_n=n^2-4n+7, \] \[ a_n=5\cdot2^n, \] \[ a_n=(-1)^n, \] \[ a_n=\frac{1}{n+1}. \]

Closed form refers to direct evaluation, not to one particular algebraic family.

Closed Forms Can Be Piecewise

A direct formula may depend on the parity or range of the index.

For example,

\[ a_n= \begin{cases} n, & n\text{ even},\\ -n, & n\text{ odd}. \end{cases} \]

This is still an explicit, nonrecursive description.

Closed Forms and Function Viewpoint

A closed form treats a sequence as a discrete function:

\[ n\longmapsto a_n. \]

For example,

\[ a_n=n^2+1 \]

assigns each allowed integer index \(n\) the value \(n^2+1\).

The graph consists of discrete points

\[ (n,n^2+1). \]

Closed Forms and Long-Run Behavior

A direct formula can make growth comparisons easier.

For example,

\[ a_n=3n+1 \]

grows linearly, while

\[ b_n=2^n \]

grows exponentially.

The algebraic form reveals the large-scale growth mechanism immediately.

Closed Forms and Limits

Suppose

\[ a_n=12-2(0.5)^n. \]

Since

\[ (0.5)^n\to0 \]

as \(n\) grows,

\[ a_n\to12. \]

The closed form makes the equilibrium behavior transparent.

Recursive Form Can Still Be Better

A closed form is not always the most useful representation.

If a process is naturally defined step by step, a recurrence may more clearly express the mechanism.

For example,

\[ B_{n+1}=1.03B_n+100 \]

immediately communicates interest growth plus a periodic deposit.

A closed form may be better for distant-term evaluation, but less direct as a model of the process.

Not Every Recurrence Has a Simple Closed Form

Some recurrences have elegant formulas.

Others have closed forms that are complicated, involve special functions, or are not useful for elementary computation.

Some recursive systems are best studied through iteration, bounds, asymptotics, generating functions, or other methods rather than a simple explicit expression.

Fibonacci Closed Form Exists

The Fibonacci sequence has a famous nonrecursive formula.

Its derivation comes from solving the associated second-order linear recurrence.

Rather than introduce that machinery here, the next lesson develops the characteristic method that explains why exponential terms arise in such closed forms.

Closed Forms Can Be Guessed from Patterns

Sometimes listing several terms suggests a candidate formula.

For example,

\[ 2,\ 5,\ 10,\ 17,\ 26,\ldots \]

resembles

\[ n^2+1 \]

for \(n=1,2,3,\ldots\).

A candidate is therefore

\[ \boxed{a_n=n^2+1}. \]

But a guessed formula should still be verified.

Finite Data Does Not Determine a Unique Closed Form

A finite list of values can be matched by many different formulas.

Therefore, observing

\[ 1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16 \]

strongly suggests \(a_n=n^2\), but those four values alone do not logically force that continuation.

Context, a recurrence, or another structural assumption is needed to justify the intended closed form.

Closed Forms and Induction

Mathematical induction is often used to prove that a proposed closed form satisfies a recurrence for all allowed indices.

The general pattern is:

  1. verify the initial case;
  2. assume the formula holds for earlier required terms;
  3. use the recurrence to derive the formula for the next term.

This converts a pattern observation into a proof.

Example: Inductive Structure for an Arithmetic Formula

Suppose

\[ a_1=A,\qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+d. \]

Claim:

\[ a_n=A+(n-1)d. \]

If the formula holds at \(n=k\), then

\[ a_{k+1} = a_k+d = A+(k-1)d+d. \]

Therefore,

\[ a_{k+1}=A+kd, \]

which is exactly the claimed formula at \(k+1\).

Closed Form versus Algorithm

A recurrence describes an algorithm for generating sequence values.

A closed form describes the output directly.

For

\[ a_{n+1}=a_n+3, \]

the recursive algorithm performs repeated addition.

The closed form

\[ a_n=a_0+3n \]

summarizes the result of all those repeated steps.

Computational Tradeoffs

A closed form can reduce the number of sequential steps required to access a distant term.

But a recurrence may be easier to implement, numerically stable, or more natural when every intermediate state is needed.

The best representation depends on the task.

Common Error: Calling Any Explicit Formula Recursive

The formula

\[ a_n=4n+1 \]

is explicit because \(a_n\) is computed directly from \(n\).

The recurrence

\[ a_n=a_{n-1}+4 \]

is recursive because the current term depends on an earlier term.

Common Error: Forgetting the Starting Index

If

\[ a_1=A, \]

then a geometric closed form is

\[ Ar^{n-1}. \]

If instead

\[ a_0=A, \]

the corresponding form is

\[ Ar^n. \]

Indexing changes the exponent.

Common Error: Verifying Only a Few Terms

Matching the first several terms is useful evidence, but it does not prove that a formula satisfies a recurrence for every index.

General substitution or induction provides stronger verification.

Common Error: Ignoring Initial Conditions

A formula may satisfy the recurrence but correspond to the wrong initial state.

Always check both the recurrence and the starting data.

Common Error: Assuming Every Sequence Has a Simple Closed Form

Recursive definitions can be mathematically complete even when no simple closed form is known.

A sequence does not need an elementary direct formula in order to be useful or well defined.

Common Error: Treating Closed Form as Automatically Better

Closed form and recursive form answer different practical needs.

Closed form emphasizes direct access.

Recursive form emphasizes process and local dependence.

Neither representation is universally superior.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding Simple Closed Forms

  1. Identify the recurrence type.
  2. Check the initial index and initial values.
  3. Look for constant additive or multiplicative structure.
  4. Use discrete accumulation when differences are known.
  5. Look for a useful equilibrium shift in affine first-order recurrences.
  6. Simplify the resulting expression in terms of \(n\).
  7. Verify both the recurrence and the initial conditions.

A Reliable Procedure for Checking a Closed Form

  1. Evaluate the formula at the required initial index.
  2. Write formulas for earlier terms such as \(a_{n-1}\).
  3. Substitute them into the recurrence.
  4. Simplify both sides.
  5. Confirm equality for the full valid index range.

A Reliable Procedure for Using Differences

  1. Write \(\Delta a_k=g(k)\).
  2. Use \(a_n=a_0+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta a_k\).
  3. Substitute the known difference expression.
  4. Evaluate or simplify the finite sum.
  5. Check the resulting formula against the initial value.

Constant difference \(4\) accumulates over \(n\) steps:

\[ a_n=2+4n. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a_n=4n+2}. \]

Use discrete accumulation:

\[ a_n = 1+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(2k+1). \]

\[ \boxed{a_n=n^2+1}. \]

The equilibrium satisfies

\[ L=0.5L+10. \]

Thus,

\[ L=20. \]

Using the shifted geometric form,

\[ a_n = 20+(0-20)(0.5)^n. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a_n=20-20(0.5)^n}. \]

\[ a_0=5\cdot2^0=5. \]

\[ 12\cdot2^1=24, \]

\[ \boxed{a_n=12\cdot2^{n-1}}. \]

What to Remember

A closed form gives a sequence term directly from its index.

\[ \boxed{a_n=f(n)}. \]
  • Closed forms avoid recursive dependence on earlier sequence terms.
  • Arithmetic sequences have linear closed forms.
  • Geometric sequences have exponential closed forms.
  • Finite differences can be accumulated to obtain explicit formulas.
  • Affine first-order recurrences can often be simplified by shifting around an equilibrium.
  • A proposed closed form must satisfy both the recurrence and the initial conditions.
  • Indexing determines the correct step count and exponent.
  • Closed forms can be polynomial, exponential, alternating, piecewise, or other familiar function types.
  • Not every recurrence has a simple or useful closed form.
  • Recursive and closed-form representations serve different practical purposes.
