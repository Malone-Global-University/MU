Queues and Waiting Systems

A queueing system models entities that arrive, wait when service is not immediately available, receive service, and then depart. Queueing theory combines stochastic arrivals, service-time distributions, state processes, and long-run balance to study congestion, utilization, waiting time, and system capacity. These models appear in call centers, computer networks, hospitals, manufacturing, transportation, and service operations.

The Central Idea

A basic queue has three ingredients:

  • an arrival process;
  • a service process;
  • a rule describing how waiting customers are selected for service.

If customers arrive faster than the system can process them for long enough, a queue forms. The central operational question is: how do arrival intensity and service capacity interact to determine congestion and delay?

Arrival Rate

Let

\[ \lambda \]

denote the average arrival rate. If customers arrive at an average rate of \(\lambda=12\) per hour, then the expected number of arrivals during \(t\) hours is \(\lambda t\).

Service Rate

Let

\[ \mu \]

denote the average service rate of one server. The corresponding mean service time is

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{\mu}}. \]

Mean Service Time

If \(\mu=12\) customers per hour, then

\[ \frac{1}{\mu}=\frac{1}{12}\text{ hour}=5\text{ minutes}. \]

The Number in the System

Let \(N(t)\) denote the number of customers in the system at time \(t\). This count includes both customers waiting in line and customers currently receiving service.

The process \(\{N(t):t\ge0\}\) is often the main state process in a queueing model.

Queue Length Versus System Size

Queueing notation often distinguishes \(L\), the long-run mean number in the entire system, from \(L_q\), the long-run mean number waiting in the queue.

A customer currently in service is counted in \(L\) but not in \(L_q\).

Waiting Time Versus System Time

The quantity \(W\) denotes the mean time a customer spends in the entire system, while \(W_q\) denotes the mean time spent waiting before service.

\[ \boxed{ W=W_q+\text{mean service time} }. \]

Queue Discipline

A queue discipline specifies which waiting customer is served next.

  • first-come, first-served;
  • last-come, first-served;
  • priority service;
  • random selection;
  • processor sharing.

First-Come, First-Served

The most common introductory rule is first-come, first-served, abbreviated FCFS or FIFO. Customers are served in the same order in which they arrive.

One Server Versus Several Servers

A queue may have one server or several parallel servers. Examples include one cashier, several checkout lanes, one processor, or a pool of service agents.

The number of servers is a major structural feature of the model.

Kendall's Queueing Notation

A common notation summarizes arrival and service assumptions. For example,

\[ M/M/1 \]

denotes:

  • Markovian, or Poisson, arrivals;
  • Markovian, or exponential, service times;
  • one server.

The \(M/M/1\) Queue

The basic \(M/M/1\) model assumes:

  • Poisson arrivals with rate \(\lambda\);
  • independent exponential service times with rate \(\mu\);
  • one server;
  • unlimited waiting capacity;
  • no abandonment;
  • independent arrival and service processes.

Utilization

The traffic intensity or utilization is

\[ \boxed{ \rho=\frac{\lambda}{\mu} }. \]

In a stable \(M/M/1\) system, \(\rho\) is also the long-run fraction of time the server is busy.

Interpreting Utilization

If \(\lambda=8\) customers per hour and \(\mu=10\) customers per hour, then

\[ \rho=\frac{8}{10}=0.8. \]

The server is busy about \(80\%\) of the time in long-run equilibrium.

The Stability Condition

For a standard \(M/M/1\) queue to have a steady-state distribution,

\[ \boxed{\lambda<\mu} \]

or equivalently

\[ \boxed{\rho<1}. \]

The server must be able to process work faster on average than work arrives.

Why \(\lambda<\mu\) Matters

If \(\lambda\ge\mu\), arrivals occur at least as quickly as the server can process them on average. In the unlimited-capacity \(M/M/1\) model, the queue does not settle into a finite long-run equilibrium distribution.

Birth-Death Structure

The \(M/M/1\) queue can be viewed as a continuous-time birth-death process. When there are \(n\ge1\) customers:

\[ n\to n+1 \]

occurs at rate \(\lambda\), while

\[ n\to n-1 \]

occurs at rate \(\mu\).

What Happens at State Zero

When \(N(t)=0\), no customer is present to complete service. The only next transition is \(0\to1\) when the next arrival occurs.

Steady-State Probabilities

For a stable \(M/M/1\) queue,

\[ \boxed{ \pi_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n, \qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots }. \]

The long-run number in the system therefore has a geometric distribution on the nonnegative integers.

Probability the System Is Empty

\[ \boxed{ \pi_0=1-\rho }. \]

Therefore, the long-run probability that the system is empty is \(1-\rho\).

Probability the Server Is Busy

The server is busy whenever at least one customer is present:

\[ P(N\ge1)=1-\pi_0=\rho. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{P(\text{server busy})=\rho}. \]

Probability of at Least \(k\) Customers

For the steady-state \(M/M/1\) distribution,

\[ \boxed{ P(N\ge k)=\rho^k }. \]

Congestion probabilities rise sharply as \(\rho\) approaches \(1\).

Mean Number in the System

For a stable \(M/M/1\) queue,

\[ \boxed{ L=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho} = \frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda} }. \]

Mean Number Waiting in Queue

\[ \boxed{ L_q=\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho} = \frac{\lambda^2}{\mu(\mu-\lambda)} }. \]

Mean Time in the System

\[ \boxed{ W=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda} }. \]

This includes both waiting time and service time.

Mean Waiting Time in Queue

\[ \boxed{ W_q=\frac{\lambda}{\mu(\mu-\lambda)} }. \]

Since the mean service time is \(1/\mu\),

\[ W=W_q+\frac{1}{\mu}. \]

Little's Law

A fundamental relationship in queueing and flow systems is

\[ \boxed{ L=\lambda W }. \]

In words: average number in the system = arrival rate × average time in the system.

Little's Law for the Waiting Line

Applied only to the queue,

\[ \boxed{ L_q=\lambda W_q }. \]

Little's Law is far more general than the \(M/M/1\) formulas and applies to many stable flow systems.

Checking the \(M/M/1\) Formulas with Little's Law

Since

\[ W=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda}, \]

Little's Law gives

\[ L=\lambda W=\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}, \]

matching the direct \(M/M/1\) formula.

Example: Basic \(M/M/1\) System

Suppose \(\lambda=6\) customers per hour and \(\mu=10\) customers per hour. Then

\[ \rho=\frac{6}{10}=0.6. \]

The queue is stable because \(\rho<1\).

Example: Mean Number in the System

\[ L=\frac{0.6}{1-0.6} = \boxed{1.5}. \]

Example: Mean Time in the System

\[ W=\frac{1}{10-6} = \frac14\text{ hour} = \boxed{15\text{ minutes}}. \]

Example: Mean Waiting Time

The mean service time is

\[ \frac{1}{10}\text{ hour}=6\text{ minutes}. \]

Therefore,

\[ W_q=15-6=\boxed{9\text{ minutes}}. \]

Example: Mean Number Waiting

Little's Law for the queue gives

\[ L_q=\lambda W_q. \]

Since \(W_q=0.15\) hour,

\[ L_q=6(0.15)=\boxed{0.9}. \]

Congestion Increases Nonlinearly

As

\[ \rho\to1^-, \]

the quantities \(L,L_q,W,W_q\) grow without bound in the \(M/M/1\) model. Operating very close to full utilization can therefore create disproportionately large delays.

Why High Utilization Can Be Expensive

A stochastic service system needs slack to absorb random variation. When utilization becomes too high, even small fluctuations in arrivals and service times can create long queues.

Queueing theory therefore exposes a tradeoff between utilization and responsiveness.

Arrival Variability Matters

Two systems can have the same mean arrival rate but different waiting behavior if their arrival patterns have different variability.

Bursty, scheduled, correlated, or seasonal arrivals can require models other than a Poisson process.

Service Variability Matters

Two systems can have the same mean service time but different queueing performance if service-time variability differs.

Exponential service is only one model; deterministic or low-variance service can produce shorter waits under the same mean capacity.

The \(M/G/1\) Idea

The notation

\[ M/G/1 \]

describes Poisson arrivals, a general service-time distribution, and one server.

The \(M/M/c\) Idea

The notation

\[ M/M/c \]

describes Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, and \(c\) parallel servers. Multi-server systems require formulas different from the \(M/M/1\) case.

Capacity-Limited Queues

Some systems allow only a finite number of customers. If the system is full, new arrivals may be blocked or lost.

Such models change both the state space and the effective arrival rate.

Customer Abandonment

In many real queues, customers may leave before receiving service. This is called abandonment or reneging.

Balking

A customer may decide not to enter after observing congestion. This is called balking.

Balking makes the effective arrival behavior depend on the current system state.

Priority Queues

Some systems serve high-priority customers ahead of lower-priority customers. Priorities may be preemptive or nonpreemptive and can change waiting-time distributions substantially.

Networks of Queues

In many systems, customers move from one service station to another. Manufacturing lines, computer networks, hospital departments, and logistics systems can all be modeled as networks of interacting queues.

Queueing as a Flow System

\[ \text{arrivals} \longrightarrow \text{waiting} \longrightarrow \text{service} \longrightarrow \text{departures}. \]

Long-run conservation of flow is one reason Little's Law is so broadly applicable.

Throughput

The throughput is the long-run departure rate. In a stable, lossless single-class queue,

\[ \boxed{\text{throughput}=\lambda}. \]

If customers are blocked, lost, or abandon, effective throughput may be lower.

Queue Length Is Random

Even in steady state, the number of customers changes randomly. The quantity \(L\) is an expectation, not a constant queue length.

Waiting Time Is Random

Likewise, \(W\) is a mean. Some customers receive immediate service while others wait much longer.

Operational decisions often require probabilities and quantiles in addition to averages.

Service Levels

A service-level metric might ask for

\[ P(W_q\le5\text{ minutes}). \]

Such probabilities can be more operationally useful than mean waiting time alone.

Steady State Versus Transient Behavior

Standard \(M/M/1\) formulas describe long-run equilibrium. A system that has just opened, begins with a backlog, or has just experienced a surge can behave differently during a transient period.

Queueing Simulation

  1. Generate the next arrival time.
  2. Generate service times.
  3. Track whether the server is busy or idle.
  4. Place arriving customers in line when necessary.
  5. Process arrivals and departures in chronological order.
  6. Record waiting times, queue lengths, and utilization.

Discrete-Event Simulation

Queueing simulation is a major example of discrete-event simulation. The simulated clock jumps from one event time to the next rather than advancing through every instant.

What Simulation Can Estimate

  • mean queue length;
  • mean waiting time;
  • waiting-time quantiles;
  • server utilization;
  • probability of delay;
  • probability of overflow;
  • service-level performance;
  • effects of staffing or capacity changes.

Warm-Up Periods in Simulation

A simulation started from an empty system may initially understate steady-state congestion. Analysts often discard an initial warm-up period before estimating long-run performance.

Model Assumption: Poisson Arrivals

The \(M/M/1\) model assumes Poisson arrivals. Scheduled appointments, clustered demand, and daily cycles can violate this assumption.

Model Assumption: Exponential Service

The \(M/M/1\) model assumes independent exponential service times. Highly standardized or heavy-tailed service systems may behave differently.

Model Assumption: Independent Arrivals and Service

Basic queueing models assume arrival behavior does not directly alter service times and vice versa. Real congestion can violate this assumption.

Model Assumption: Infinite Waiting Capacity

The standard \(M/M/1\) model assumes arbitrarily many customers can wait. Physical spaces and computer buffers often have finite capacity.

Common Error: Confusing \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\)

\(\lambda\) measures incoming demand, while \(\mu\) measures service capacity. Reversing them changes the utilization calculation.

Common Error: Comparing Incompatible Units

If arrivals are measured per hour and service times are given in minutes, convert them before computing

\[ \rho=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}. \]

Common Error: Treating \(\rho=1\) as Stable

In the standard \(M/M/1\) model, \(\rho=1\) is not a stable steady-state operating point. Stability requires

\[ \boxed{\rho<1}. \]

Common Error: Confusing \(L\) and \(L_q\)

\(L\) includes the customer in service. \(L_q\) counts only customers waiting.

Common Error: Confusing \(W\) and \(W_q\)

\(W\) includes waiting and service time. \(W_q\) measures only the time before service begins.

Common Error: Applying \(M/M/1\) Formulas to Every Queue

The formulas

\[ L=\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda} \]

and

\[ W=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda} \]

depend on the \(M/M/1\) assumptions. They do not automatically apply to multi-server, finite-capacity, priority, or general-service systems.

Common Error: Assuming High Utilization Is Always Efficient

In stochastic systems, operating very close to \(100\%\) utilization can cause severe waiting. Efficiency must be considered together with delay and service quality.

A Reliable Procedure for \(M/M/1\) Problems

  1. Identify the arrival rate \(\lambda\).
  2. Identify the service rate \(\mu\).
  3. Convert both rates to the same time units.
  4. Compute \(\rho=\lambda/\mu\).
  5. Check the stability condition \(\rho<1\).
  6. Use the appropriate formula for \(L\), \(L_q\), \(W\), or \(W_q\).
  7. Use Little's Law as a consistency check.
  8. Interpret the result as a long-run average.

A Reliable Procedure for Little's Law

  1. Identify the relevant flow system.
  2. Determine the long-run effective arrival or throughput rate.
  3. Match system-wide \(L\) with \(W\), or queue-only \(L_q\) with \(W_q\).
  4. Use \(L=\lambda W\) or \(L_q=\lambda W_q\).
  5. Check units: rate times time should produce an average count.

Example: Full \(M/M/1\) Workflow

Customers arrive at rate \(\lambda=9\) per hour, and one server completes service at rate \(\mu=12\) per hour.

\[ \rho=\frac{9}{12}=0.75. \]

The system is stable.

\[ L=\frac{0.75}{1-0.75}=3. \] \[ W=\frac{1}{12-9}=\frac13\text{ hour}=20\text{ minutes}. \]

The mean service time is

\[ \frac1{12}\text{ hour}=5\text{ minutes}. \]

Therefore,

\[ W_q=20-5=15\text{ minutes}. \]

Finally,

\[ L_q=\lambda W_q=9\left(\frac14\right)=2.25. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \rho=0.75,\quad L=3,\quad L_q=2.25,\quad W=20\text{ min},\quad W_q=15\text{ min} }. \]

What to Remember

  • \(\lambda\) is the arrival rate.
  • \(\mu\) is the service rate of one server.
  • The mean service time is \(1/\mu\).
  • \(L\) is the mean number in the system.
  • \(L_q\) is the mean number waiting.
  • \(W\) is the mean total time in the system.
  • \(W_q\) is the mean waiting time before service.
  • An \(M/M/1\) queue has Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, and one server.
  • Utilization is \(\rho=\lambda/\mu\).
  • The \(M/M/1\) stability condition is \(\rho<1\).
  • In steady state, \(\pi_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n\).
  • The server is busy with probability \(\rho\).
  • \(L=\lambda/(\mu-\lambda)\).
  • \(W=1/(\mu-\lambda)\).
  • Little's Law gives \(L=\lambda W\).
  • The queue-only form is \(L_q=\lambda W_q\).
  • High stochastic utilization can produce rapidly increasing delay.
  • Multi-server, finite-capacity, priority, abandonment, and general-service systems require modified models.

The central operational principle is: random variability creates waiting whenever demand approaches service capacity too closely.
