Ethical Design and Interpretation

Ethical research is not defined only by consent forms or approval documents. It begins with a worthwhile question, uses a design capable of answering that question, distributes burdens and benefits fairly, protects participants and communities, and reports conclusions without exaggeration or concealment.

The Central Idea

Research asks people, organizations, animals, environments, or communities to bear costs so that knowledge can be produced. The design is ethical only when the expected knowledge has sufficient value, the methods can produce credible evidence, risks are proportionate and minimized, and affected parties are treated fairly.

\[ \boxed{ \text{ethical research} = \text{valuable question} + \text{credible design} + \text{proportionate protection} + \text{honest interpretation} } \]

A methodologically weak study can be unethical because it exposes units to burden without a realistic path to useful knowledge. A statistically strong study can also be unethical if it uses coercion, avoidable harm, unfair selection, invasive data practices, or misleading reporting.

Core Ethical Principles

Principle Operational Question
Respect for persons Can people make informed, voluntary choices, and are their preferences respected?
Beneficence Are potential benefits and useful knowledge promoted?
Nonmaleficence Are avoidable harms, burdens, and intrusions minimized?
Justice Are research burdens and expected benefits distributed fairly?
Scientific integrity Can the design answer the question, and will the full evidence be reported honestly?

These principles can conflict. A highly informative design may impose additional burden; broad inclusion may increase relevance while introducing safety concerns. Ethical design makes those tradeoffs explicit and justified.

Scientific and Social Value

A study should address a question whose answer could improve understanding, practice, policy, safety, or future research. Value depends on more than novelty.

Before collecting data, ask:

  • Is the question already answered adequately?
  • Would the proposed result change a decision or resolve meaningful uncertainty?
  • Is the target population or community likely to benefit from the knowledge?
  • Are the outcomes important enough to justify the burden?
  • Will the study produce interpretable evidence if it is completed?
  • Can results be disseminated regardless of direction?

Redundant research can still be valuable when it tests replicability, extends evidence to an excluded group, evaluates a new setting, or improves precision.

Scientific Validity Is an Ethical Requirement

An experiment with too little replication, an invalid measurement, a confounded comparison, or an analysis unrelated to the assignment may fail to answer its question. Participants then bear burden without producing reliable knowledge.

Ethical review therefore includes questions of:

  • clear estimands and outcomes;
  • appropriate controls and randomization;
  • adequate but not excessive sample size;
  • valid and reliable measurement;
  • feasible recruitment and retention;
  • prespecified analysis;
  • realistic implementation; and
  • credible internal and external validity.

Risk-Benefit Assessment

Ethical design compares anticipated harms and burdens with potential benefits and the value of the knowledge. The comparison is not a single numerical formula, but probability and magnitude remain useful organizing concepts.

\[ \text{expected burden} \approx \sum_j P(\text{harm}_j) \times \text{severity}(\text{harm}_j). \]

Risks may be physical, psychological, social, economic, legal, reputational, informational, environmental, or organizational. Burdens also include time, inconvenience, discomfort, repeated testing, travel, and opportunity cost.

Rare severe harms should not be dismissed merely because their expected numerical value is small. Reversibility, voluntariness, affected population, uncertainty, and available safeguards matter.

Minimize Risk Without Destroying the Question

Risk minimization may include:

  • using existing data when new exposure is unnecessary;
  • choosing the least invasive valid measurement;
  • reducing treatment dose or duration when scientifically adequate;
  • excluding conditions that create disproportionate danger;
  • adding safety monitoring and rapid response;
  • limiting collection to data needed for the research question;
  • using staged or pilot enrollment before full scale;
  • stopping when clear harm, benefit, or futility is established; and
  • providing appropriate follow-up, referrals, or support.

Removing so much information that the study cannot answer its question does not create an ethical design. It creates burden without sufficient value.

Informed Consent

Informed consent is an ongoing process through which a person receives understandable information, has a genuine opportunity to ask questions, and voluntarily decides whether to participate.

Appropriate consent generally addresses:

  • the purpose of the research;
  • the procedures and duration;
  • random assignment and possible conditions;
  • reasonably foreseeable risks and burdens;
  • potential benefits and the absence of guaranteed benefit;
  • alternatives to participation when relevant;
  • privacy, confidentiality, and data use;
  • compensation, costs, and treatment for research-related harm when applicable;
  • voluntary participation and the right to withdraw;
  • limits to withdrawal once data are aggregated or deidentified; and
  • contacts for questions, concerns, or injury.

A signed document does not by itself establish understanding or voluntariness.

Comprehension, Capacity, and Communication

Information should be presented in language, format, and pacing suited to the intended participants. Translation, interpretation, accessible media, teach-back, and opportunities to consult trusted advisers may be necessary.

Capacity concerns a person's ability to understand relevant information, appreciate consequences, reason about options, and communicate a choice. Capacity can be decision-specific and may change over time.

When a legally authorized representative or guardian is involved, the participant's own preferences, assent, discomfort, and resistance should still be respected to the extent possible.

Voluntariness, Coercion, and Undue Influence

A choice is voluntary when it is made without threats, improper pressure, or an offer so influential that it compromises reasonable judgment.

Power relationships deserve special attention:

  • employer and employee;
  • teacher and student;
  • clinician and patient;
  • military superior and subordinate;
  • service provider and dependent client; and
  • institution and person in custody.

Recruitment can be separated from authority figures, participation can be kept private when feasible, and equivalent alternatives can be offered so declining does not carry penalty.

Compensation and Reimbursement

Payment can reimburse expenses, compensate time and inconvenience, or encourage enrollment. It should not obscure risk, unfairly penalize withdrawal, or exploit financial vulnerability.

Prorated compensation generally avoids making full payment dependent on completing every study stage. Reimbursement for travel, childcare, or lost wages can improve access and justice without treating participation as a purchase of risk.

Fair Participant Selection

Justice requires examining who bears research burdens and who is expected to benefit from the resulting knowledge.

Ethical selection avoids:

  • recruiting a convenient or disadvantaged group merely because access is easy;
  • excluding groups without scientific or safety justification;
  • testing interventions in communities unlikely to receive later benefits;
  • placing high-risk conditions disproportionately on less powerful groups;
  • using eligibility rules that conceal meaningful heterogeneity; and
  • claiming broad applicability after systematically excluding relevant populations.

Contextual Vulnerability

Vulnerability is not a fixed label attached to a person. It can arise from limited decision-making capacity, dependency, economic constraint, illness, language, discrimination, crisis, institutional control, or lack of alternatives.

Additional safeguards should respond to the actual vulnerability:

  • independent consent support;
  • simplified and repeated communication;
  • privacy from authority figures;
  • reduced or staged risk;
  • community consultation;
  • participant advocates;
  • monitoring for undue influence; and
  • clear access to complaint and withdrawal processes.

Ethical Random Assignment

Random assignment is ethically appropriate only when each assigned condition is permissible and meaningful uncertainty remains about which is preferable for the relevant participants.

Randomization should not be used to:

  • assign a condition known to be unreasonably harmful;
  • withhold established effective care without adequate justification;
  • conceal resource allocation decisions that should be made transparently;
  • avoid responsibility for an unethical policy; or
  • make a scientifically weak comparison appear objective.

Participants should understand that treatment is assigned by chance and that personal preference does not determine the group.

Controls, Placebos, and Withholding Treatment

The control condition should answer the scientific question while protecting participants. Possible controls include standard care, usual practice, an active alternative, delayed treatment, placebo, or no intervention.

A placebo or no-treatment condition requires special scrutiny when effective treatment exists. Relevant questions include:

  • Would withholding treatment expose participants to serious or irreversible harm?
  • Is placebo scientifically necessary, or would an active control answer the question?
  • Can rescue treatment be provided?
  • Is the period without active treatment minimized?
  • Are participants fully informed of the possible assignment?
  • Does the target setting actually lack access to the standard treatment, and is that inequity being exploited?

Blinding, Deception, and Debriefing

Blinding treatment identity can be compatible with informed consent when participants understand the possible conditions and the reason assignment is masked.

Deception goes further by withholding or misrepresenting relevant study information. It requires strong scientific justification, minimal risk, lack of a feasible nondeceptive alternative, appropriate review, and a plan for timely debriefing when debriefing is safe and meaningful.

Debriefing should explain what was withheld, why it was necessary, what the study sought to learn, and what options participants have regarding their data when applicable.

Privacy and Confidentiality

Privacy concerns a person's control over access to themselves, their spaces, and their information. Confidentiality concerns how information already provided is stored, used, and disclosed.

A data-protection plan should address:

  • data minimization;
  • collection setting and method;
  • direct and indirect identifiers;
  • access roles and authentication;
  • encryption and secure transfer;
  • retention and deletion schedules;
  • linkage with other datasets;
  • secondary uses and future sharing;
  • reidentification risk;
  • breach response; and
  • limits imposed by law, safety, or mandatory reporting.

Deidentification Is Not Absolute Anonymity

Removing names reduces direct identification risk, but combinations of location, age, dates, rare characteristics, and linked public records may still identify a person.

Reidentification risk depends on the data, available external information, access controls, adversary capability, and future technology. Researchers should avoid promising anonymity that the data architecture cannot guarantee.

Digital Experiments and Platform Data

Online experiments can assign interfaces, messages, prices, rankings, or defaults to very large populations with little visible friction. Low physical risk does not imply no ethical concern.

Digital studies should consider:

  • whether users reasonably expect experimentation;
  • manipulation of emotion, attention, or vulnerability;
  • economic consequences and differential pricing;
  • effects on nonparticipants through networks;
  • children and other protected users;
  • automated discrimination or disparate impact;
  • the scale of small individual harms;
  • meaningful notice, consent, or opt-out where appropriate; and
  • retention and reuse of behavioral data.

An experiment can be operationally easy and ethically consequential.

Community and Group-Level Effects

Research may affect groups even when individual records are protected. Findings can stigmatize a community, influence resource allocation, reveal collective vulnerabilities, or create commercial value without shared benefit.

Community consultation, shared governance, locally relevant outcomes, benefit agreements, careful language, and return of aggregate findings can address some group-level concerns. Individual consent does not resolve every collective effect.

Animals, Environments, and Nonhuman Burdens

Experimental ethics also applies beyond human participants. Animal research often uses three organizing principles:

  • Replacement: use nonanimal alternatives when they can answer the question.
  • Reduction: use no more animals than needed for reliable evidence.
  • Refinement: minimize pain, distress, and lasting harm.

Field and environmental experiments should consider ecological disruption, spillover, reversibility, long-term monitoring, and effects on communities that depend on the environment.

Safety Monitoring

Ethical responsibility continues after enrollment. Monitoring plans should define:

  • which adverse events are collected;
  • who reviews accumulating safety data;
  • how events are graded and attributed;
  • which events require rapid reporting;
  • when treatment is paused or stopped;
  • how emergency unblinding occurs;
  • what care or referral follows harm; and
  • how participants receive new information that may affect continued consent.

Monitoring intensity should be proportionate to uncertainty, vulnerability, intervention risk, and study duration.

Stopping Rules and Interim Analysis

A study may stop early for clear benefit, harm, futility, infeasibility, or external evidence that changes the ethical balance.

Repeatedly examining outcomes and stopping whenever a favorable result appears inflates false-positive risk. Interim analyses should therefore use prespecified timing, decision boundaries, and independent review when appropriate.

Stopping too early for apparent benefit can also exaggerate effect size and leave limited safety information. Ethical stopping balances current participants, future users, statistical reliability, and remaining uncertainty.

Ethical Sample-Size Planning

Too few units may make the study incapable of detecting an important effect. Too many may expose unnecessary units or consume resources after the answer is already sufficiently precise.

Sample-size planning should consider:

  • the smallest effect important for the decision;
  • outcome variability and event frequency;
  • desired precision or statistical power;
  • number of treatment groups and comparisons;
  • clustering, blocking, repeated measures, and interference;
  • expected attrition and noncompliance;
  • interim monitoring plans; and
  • feasibility and burden per unit.

Preregistration and Prespecification

Preregistration records key study decisions before outcomes are examined. A prespecified plan may include:

  • the primary question and estimand;
  • eligibility and recruitment;
  • assignment and concealment;
  • primary and secondary outcomes;
  • sample-size rationale;
  • exclusion and missing-data rules;
  • statistical models and contrasts;
  • subgroup and interaction analyses;
  • interim analyses and stopping rules; and
  • sensitivity analyses.

Preregistration does not make a poor plan good, and legitimate deviations may be necessary. The ethical obligation is to distinguish prespecified decisions from changes made after seeing the data and to explain those changes.

Multiplicity and Selective Discovery

If many outcomes, subgroups, time points, transformations, and models are tested, some may appear favorable by chance.

If \(m\) independent tests each use significance level \(\alpha\), the probability of at least one false positive under all null hypotheses is

\[ 1-(1-\alpha)^m. \]

For \(m=20\) and \(\alpha=0.05\),

\[ 1-(0.95)^{20}\approx0.642. \]

The tests in real studies are often dependent, but the calculation shows why unreported multiplicity can mislead. Primary outcomes, correction methods, hierarchical testing, replication, and explicit exploratory labeling provide protection.

Researcher Degrees of Freedom

P-hacking describes searching among analytic choices until a favorable statistical result appears. HARKing means presenting a hypothesis formed after results are known as though it had been specified beforehand.

Exploration is scientifically valuable. The ethical problem is disguising exploration as confirmation. Reports should label analyses as confirmatory, prespecified secondary, exploratory, or post hoc and should preserve the full decision trail.

Selective Outcome and Result Reporting

Reporting only favorable outcomes, time points, subgroups, models, or complete cases distorts the evidence. Ethical reporting includes:

  • all prespecified primary outcomes;
  • important harms and unintended effects;
  • null, uncertain, and unfavorable results;
  • participant flow and missingness;
  • protocol deviations;
  • effect estimates with uncertainty;
  • absolute as well as relative effects when decision-relevant;
  • all material subgroup analyses; and
  • limitations that could change interpretation.

Publication Bias and the Missing Evidence

Studies with statistically significant or favorable results are often more likely to be submitted, accepted, publicized, or cited. If null and unfavorable studies remain invisible, the published record overstates effects.

Registration, results reporting, accessible protocols, data and code sharing when appropriate, replication, and inclusion of unpublished studies in evidence reviews can reduce the problem.

A participant's contribution should not disappear because the result was commercially inconvenient or statistically unexciting.

Conflicts of Interest

Financial, professional, institutional, ideological, or personal interests can influence question selection, comparator choice, design, analysis, interpretation, and publication.

Disclosure is necessary but may not be sufficient. Additional safeguards include:

  • independent protocol and statistical review;
  • separation of sponsor and analytic decisions;
  • contractual freedom to publish;
  • independent data access;
  • blinded outcome adjudication;
  • public registration and analysis plans; and
  • independent replication.

Data and Analysis Integrity

A credible evidence trail should preserve:

  • source data and provenance;
  • variable definitions and codebooks;
  • instrument and software versions;
  • randomization records;
  • data-cleaning decisions;
  • exclusion and correction logs;
  • analysis code and computational environment;
  • version history; and
  • clear responsibility for access and changes.

Fabrication, falsification, undisclosed deletion, and manipulation of records violate both scientific and ethical obligations.

Transparency and Responsible Data Sharing

Sharing protocols, code, metadata, and appropriately protected data can support verification and new discovery. Openness must be balanced against confidentiality, consent limitations, community interests, intellectual contribution, security, and reidentification risk.

Responsible options include controlled access, data enclaves, synthetic data, disclosure review, aggregate release, analysis code without row-level data, and governance that includes affected communities.

“Open” and “ethical” are related goals, not synonyms.

Interpret Effect Size, Not Only Significance

Statistical significance addresses compatibility with a model and null hypothesis. It does not determine whether an effect is large, useful, fair, affordable, or ethically acceptable.

Reports should present:

  • effect estimates in interpretable units;
  • confidence or uncertainty intervals;
  • baseline risk and absolute effects;
  • harms and benefits on comparable scales;
  • practical thresholds;
  • distributional and subgroup effects;
  • implementation burden and cost; and
  • remaining uncertainty.

Absolute and Relative Effects

Relative measures can sound dramatic when baseline risk is small. Suppose risk falls from \(2\%\) to \(1\%\).

\[ \text{relative risk} = \frac{1\%}{2\%} = 0.5, \]

a \(50\%\) relative reduction. The absolute risk difference is

\[ 1\%-2\%=-1 \text{ percentage point}. \]

Both are correct. Ethical communication presents the scale needed for an informed decision rather than selecting the one that sounds most persuasive.

Communicate Uncertainty Honestly

Uncertainty comes from more than sampling variation. It also comes from:

  • measurement error;
  • missing data and attrition;
  • model specification;
  • unmeasured confounding;
  • multiplicity and analytic flexibility;
  • treatment heterogeneity;
  • implementation variation;
  • limited external validity; and
  • uncertain causal assumptions.

A narrow confidence interval does not summarize every source of uncertainty. Interpretation should distinguish quantified random error from structural uncertainty that the interval does not include.

Match Causal Language to the Design

Terms such as causes, prevents, increases, and reduces imply counterfactual change. They require a design and assumptions that support causal inference.

Observational evidence may justify careful causal reasoning when confounding, selection, measurement, overlap, and sensitivity are addressed. It should not receive causal verbs merely because the association is statistically significant.

When causal identification is not justified, use language such as was associated with, co-occurred with, or predicted.

Subgroups, Heterogeneity, and Equity

An average benefit can conceal harm or no benefit in an important subgroup. Ethical interpretation asks who benefits, who is burdened, and whose data are too sparse for a confident conclusion.

Subgroup analysis should balance two risks:

  • ignoring real treatment-effect heterogeneity; and
  • overinterpreting noisy patterns from many small post hoc comparisons.

Prespecified effect modifiers, adequate representation, multiplicity awareness, transparent uncertainty, and replication support responsible subgroup conclusions.

A Null Result Is Not Proof of No Effect

Failure to reject a null hypothesis may reflect a small effect, an imprecise study, poor adherence, measurement error, diluted treatment, or genuine absence of a meaningful effect.

Interpretation should examine the interval of effects compatible with the data. If the interval includes both meaningful benefit and meaningful harm, the ethical conclusion is uncertainty—not equivalence.

Demonstrating equivalence or noninferiority requires a prespecified margin and an appropriate design, not merely a nonsignificant difference.

Negative and Unexpected Results Have Value

A well-designed study can be valuable even when the favored hypothesis fails. Null, negative, and unexpected findings can:

  • prevent adoption of ineffective practices;
  • identify harms or implementation barriers;
  • refine theory and measurement;
  • improve future sample-size planning;
  • reduce redundant exposure of future participants; and
  • correct an overly favorable evidence base.

Independent Ethical and Scientific Review

Many studies require review by an appropriate ethics committee, institutional board, community body, regulator, or organizational authority. The applicable process depends on jurisdiction, institution, population, intervention, and data.

Approval does not transfer all responsibility to the reviewing body. Researchers, sponsors, institutions, analysts, and publishers remain responsible for ongoing protection, protocol compliance, accurate reporting, and response to new information.

Example: A School Attendance Intervention

A district proposes sending behaviorally designed messages to families whose students have frequent absences. Schools would be randomly assigned to the new messages or usual communication.

Scientific Value

The study could improve attendance policy if the messages are effective and do not create unintended harm.

Risks and Burdens

Messages may create shame, disclose attendance concerns to unintended recipients, misunderstand disability or housing barriers, or strain family-school relationships.

Justice

Families facing structural barriers should not be framed as less responsible. Messages, language access, support services, and interpretation should account for different causes of absence.

Privacy

Contact information, attendance records, message content, and delivery channels require access controls and verification.

Interpretation

Even if average attendance improves, the report should examine adverse responses, subgroup effects, implementation, message reach, and whether gains reflect support or pressure.

Example: Stopping a Treatment Trial Early

An interim analysis suggests a new treatment substantially improves a short-term outcome. The trial team considers stopping and offering it to the control group.

Reason to Stop

Continuing may deny control participants an effective intervention.

Reason for Caution

Early estimates can be exaggerated, long-term benefit may be uncertain, and rare harms may not yet be visible.

Ethical Process

Use the prespecified boundary, independent safety review, totality of evidence, outcome importance, harm data, follow-up needs, and participant communication plan. The decision should not be made from an isolated favorable \(p\)-value.

An End-to-End Ethics Checklist

Before the Study

  1. Is the question valuable and not wastefully redundant?
  2. Can the design produce credible evidence?
  3. Are risks minimized and proportionate to value?
  4. Are recruitment and eligibility fair?
  5. Is consent understandable and voluntary?
  6. Are privacy, confidentiality, and data governance adequate?
  7. Are sample size, outcomes, analysis, and stopping rules specified?
  8. Are conflicts and independent oversight addressed?

During the Study

  1. Is treatment delivered and monitored as planned?
  2. Are harms, burdens, withdrawals, and deviations recorded?
  3. Do participants receive important new information?
  4. Is confidentiality maintained?
  5. Are interim decisions made under the approved plan?
  6. Are emerging inequities or subgroup harms addressed?

After the Study

  1. Are all prespecified outcomes and material harms reported?
  2. Are exploratory analyses labeled honestly?
  3. Are effect sizes, absolute risks, and uncertainty communicated?
  4. Do causal and external claims stay within the design?
  5. Are data, code, and materials shared as safely and appropriately as possible?
  6. Are participants and communities informed of results when promised or useful?
  7. Are negative findings disseminated rather than buried?

Common Errors

Treating Approval as the End of Ethics

Ethical responsibility continues through recruitment, conduct, monitoring, analysis, publication, data retention, and later use.

Equating Consent with Permission for Anything

Consent does not justify unnecessary risk, weak design, unfair selection, careless data handling, or uses outside what participants reasonably authorized.

Using Statistical Significance as the Ethical Decision Rule

Decisions require effect size, uncertainty, harms, baseline risk, equity, cost, feasibility, and external validity.

Reporting Only the Average Benefit

A favorable mean can conceal harms, unequal effects, nonresponders, burdens, and excluded populations.

Hiding Exploration

Exploratory analysis is legitimate. Presenting it as prespecified confirmation overstates evidence.

Guided Practice

Problem 1

A study recruits employees through their direct supervisors, who know who declines. Identify the primary ethical concern and one safeguard.

Show the solution

Voluntariness is threatened by the power relationship and lack of privacy. Recruitment can be handled by an independent team, with supervisors prevented from learning individual decisions and with no employment consequence for refusal.

Problem 2

Twenty independent null-hypothesis tests each use \(\alpha=0.05\). Under the simplified independence assumption, what is the probability of at least one false positive?

Show the solution

\[ 1-(1-0.05)^{20} = 1-(0.95)^{20} \approx0.642. \] The probability is about \(64.2\%\).

Problem 3

An intervention reduces risk from \(4\%\) to \(2\%\). State the relative risk, relative reduction, and absolute risk difference.

Show the solution

The relative risk is \(2\%/4\%=0.5\). The relative reduction is \(50\%\). The absolute risk difference is \(2\%-4\%=-2\) percentage points.

Problem 4

A trial finds no statistically significant difference, but its confidence interval includes both a meaningful benefit and a meaningful harm. What is the responsible interpretation?

Show the solution

The result is inconclusive. It does not establish equivalence or no effect. The interval shows that decision-relevant benefit and harm remain compatible with the data.

Problem 5

Researchers examine 30 outcomes and publish only the two with \(p<0.05\), without disclosing the other tests. What reporting problem occurs?

Show the solution

Selective outcome reporting and undisclosed multiplicity make chance findings appear confirmatory. All prespecified outcomes and the full testing context should be reported, with exploratory results labeled accordingly.

A Final Ethical Check

Before beginning or publishing a study, complete four statements:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &\text{The knowledge is worth seeking because }\ldots\\ &\text{The design can answer the question because }\ldots\\ &\text{The burdens and benefits are fair because }\ldots\\ &\text{The conclusion matches the evidence because }\ldots \end{aligned} } \]

If any statement cannot be completed honestly, the study or its interpretation requires revision.

Summary

  • Ethical research combines social value, scientific validity, respect, risk minimization, justice, privacy, and honest reporting.
  • Informed consent is an ongoing process of understandable and voluntary decision, not merely a signature.
  • Randomization, controls, placebos, blinding, deception, and stopping rules each require scientific and ethical justification.
  • Privacy and confidentiality require data minimization, access control, realistic disclosure of limits, and attention to reidentification and group harms.
  • Preregistration, prespecification, complete reporting, and disclosure of deviations protect against selective analysis and hidden multiplicity.
  • Interpretation should emphasize effect size, absolute risk, uncertainty, harms, heterogeneity, practical significance, and external validity—not significance alone.
  • Ethical responsibility continues from question selection through data stewardship, publication, replication, and application.

Section Complete

Experimental design and causal reasoning now form one connected workflow: distinguish observation from intervention, define treatments and outcomes, randomize and replicate, block and blind, identify confounding and bias, map causal pathways, define counterfactual effects, evaluate validity, and interpret the final evidence ethically.

These principles support the later work of estimation, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, modeling, and decision-making. Statistical calculations are meaningful only when the design and interpretation justify the quantity being calculated.
