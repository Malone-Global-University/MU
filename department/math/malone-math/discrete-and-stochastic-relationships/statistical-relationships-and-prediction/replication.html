Replication

Replication applies each treatment to multiple independent experimental units. Those repeated opportunities reveal natural variability, stabilize treatment comparisons, and show whether an observed difference is a recurring pattern or an accident of a few unusual units.

The Central Idea

Experimental outcomes vary even when conditions are carefully controlled. Two plants given the same fertilizer may grow by different amounts. Two students receiving the same instruction may earn different scores. Two manufactured parts produced under the same setting may have different lifetimes.

Replication means applying a treatment to more than one independent experimental unit. It provides repeated evidence about both the outcome level and the variation among units.

\[ \boxed{ \text{replicated treatments} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{measured variability} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{more stable comparisons} } \]

Without replication, a difference between treatments cannot be separated cleanly from a difference between the particular units that happened to receive them.

Why One Unit per Treatment Is Not Enough

Suppose one plant receives fertilizer (A) and one plant receives fertilizer (B). After four weeks, the first grows (8) centimeters and the second grows (5) centimeters.

\[ 8-5=3\text{ centimeters}. \]

The arithmetic difference is clear, but its cause is not. The plant receiving (A) may have had a stronger root system, better soil, more favorable light, or simply ordinary biological variation. Because treatment and individual plant are completely mixed together, the experiment provides no estimate of how much plants vary under the same treatment.

Replication assigns fertilizer (A) to several plants and fertilizer (B) to several others. The comparison then rests on treatment groups rather than on a single pair of possibly unusual plants.

What Counts as a Replicate?

A replicate is an independent experimental unit that receives a treatment. Independence here means that the unit represents a separate opportunity for experimental variation, not merely another reading from the same underlying unit.

If 20 independently assigned plants receive a fertilizer, the treatment has 20 experimental replicates. If one plant is measured 20 times, the treatment still has one experimental unit, not 20 independent replicates.

The correct replication count follows the unit of treatment assignment:

\[ \boxed{ \text{number of independent replicates} = \text{number of independently treated units} } \]

Repeated Measurements Are Not Independent Replication

Repeated measurements can improve measurement quality and reveal change over time, but measurements from the same unit share the unit's characteristics. They are usually correlated rather than independent.

Suppose a machine is run once under a new setting and its temperature is recorded every minute for 60 minutes. The study has 60 measurements but only one independently treated machine run. A disturbance affecting that run can influence all 60 readings.

Repeating the treatment on multiple independently prepared machine runs would create experimental replication. Repeated measurements within each run could then provide additional information about the time pattern.

Pseudoreplication

Pseudoreplication occurs when dependent observations or repeated measurements are analyzed as though they were independent treatment replicates. This inflates the apparent amount of evidence and can make an uncertain result look much more precise than it is.

Consider two aquariums. One receives treatment (A), and one receives treatment (B). Researchers measure 50 fish in each aquarium. There are 100 fish measurements, but treatment was assigned to only two aquariums.

Fish within an aquarium share water quality, temperature, feeding, and disease exposure. The aquarium is the experimental unit. With one aquarium per treatment, treatment is inseparable from aquarium. Measuring more fish within each aquarium does not create independent treatment replication.

Technical and Experimental Replicates

Different fields distinguish two useful forms of repetition.

Type What Is Repeated? Primary Purpose
Experimental or biological replicate The treatment is applied to another independent unit Measure unit-to-unit and treatment-response variability
Technical replicate The same sample or unit is measured repeatedly Measure or reduce instrument and measurement variability

Technical replicates can be valuable. Averaging several laboratory readings may produce a better measurement of one sample. They do not, however, replace independent experimental units when the goal is to estimate a treatment effect across units.

Three Roles of Replication

Revealing Variability

Multiple units receiving the same treatment show how much outcomes vary under that condition. This within-treatment variability is essential for deciding whether a between-treatment difference is unusually large.

Improving Precision

A group average based on many independent units is generally more stable than an average based on only a few. Unusually high and low outcomes have less influence on the result as information accumulates.

Detecting Treatment Effects

Replication improves the ability to distinguish a real treatment effect from background variation. Small or moderate effects may be hidden in noisy outcomes when treatment groups contain too few independent units.

Within-Treatment and Between-Treatment Variation

An experiment compares two kinds of variation:

  • within-treatment variation: differences among units that receive the same treatment; and
  • between-treatment variation: differences among treatment group summaries.

A treatment difference is easier to detect when it is large relative to the within-treatment variability. Informally,

\[ \text{evidence for a treatment difference} \quad\text{depends on}\quad \frac{\text{between-treatment difference}} {\text{background variability}}. \]

Replication supplies the information needed to estimate the denominator of that comparison.

Why Precision Improves with More Replication

When independent observations have standard deviation (sigma), the standard error of a sample mean has the form

\[ \operatorname{SE}(\overline{Y}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}. \]

The exact formula and assumptions depend on the design, but the square-root relationship communicates an important principle: precision improves as the number of independent replicates (n) increases.

If (sigma=12), then

\[ \begin{aligned} n=9:&\qquad \operatorname{SE}=\frac{12}{\sqrt9}=4,\\ n=36:&\qquad \operatorname{SE}=\frac{12}{\sqrt{36}}=2. \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying the independent sample size by four cuts this standard error in half. Replication improves precision, but with diminishing returns.

More Data Do Not Repair a Biased Design

Replication reduces random uncertainty. It does not automatically remove confounding, measurement bias, selection bias, or systematic procedural differences between treatment groups.

Repeating a biased measurement many times produces a precise estimate of the wrong quantity. Applying treatment (A) only in one location and treatment (B) only in another remains confounded by location even if thousands of observations are collected within each place.

\[ \boxed{ \text{replication improves precision, not design validity by itself} } \]

Strong experiments combine replication with random assignment, control, consistent measurement, and an analysis that respects the assignment structure.

Balanced and Unbalanced Replication

A balanced design uses the same number of independent units for each treatment. An unbalanced design uses different numbers.

Balance often simplifies analysis and provides efficient treatment comparisons when units have similar variability and cost. If 60 units are available for two equally important treatments, assigning 30 to each usually provides more precise comparison than assigning 50 to one and 10 to the other.

Unequal replication can still be appropriate. Treatments may differ in cost, variability, risk, availability, or informational value. The allocation should be planned rather than produced by avoidable loss or convenience.

Replication in Multifactor Experiments

In a multifactor experiment, replication applies to treatment combinations. Suppose an experiment uses two fertilizer levels and three watering levels, creating

\[ 2\times3=6 \]

treatment combinations. If each combination is independently applied to five plots, the experiment uses

\[ 6\times5=30 \]

experimental units. Replication at each combination helps estimate main effects, interactions, and background variation.

Merely adding more factor combinations does not create replication. Replication requires multiple independent units under the same treatment combination.

Replication in Cluster-Randomized Experiments

When intact groups receive treatments, the groups are the independent experimental units. Suppose eight schools are randomly assigned to two curricula, with four schools per curriculum. Each school contributes data from 200 students.

The experiment includes 1,600 student outcomes but only eight independently assigned clusters. There are four school-level replicates per curriculum.

Adding more students within the same schools improves knowledge about those schools, but it does not provide the same treatment-level information as adding independently assigned schools. Replication must be counted at the level where treatment is assigned.

Attrition and Lost Replication

Units may drop out, fail, become contaminated, miss measurement, or stop following the assigned condition. Such losses reduce usable replication and may create bias when loss is related to treatment or outcome.

A design should anticipate plausible loss without treating replacement as a casual afterthought. Researchers should specify:

  • how much attrition is expected;
  • whether additional units are needed at the start;
  • when a unit is considered missing or unusable;
  • whether and how a unit may be replaced; and
  • how missing outcomes will be analyzed and reported.

Selectively replacing only unfavorable outcomes would invalidate the treatment comparison.

Replication Reveals Heterogeneous Effects

A treatment need not affect every unit in the same way. Some units may benefit, some may show little change, and some may be harmed. Replication reveals the distribution of responses more clearly than a single outcome can.

With enough relevant replication, researchers can investigate whether effects differ across meaningful conditions or subgroups. Such analyses should be planned carefully, because dividing a small experiment into many subgroups can leave too little information in each one.

The average treatment effect remains useful, but it may not describe every unit's response.

Replication Within and Across Studies

The word replication is used at more than one scale.

  • Within-study replication uses multiple independent units under each treatment in one experiment.
  • Direct replication repeats a study as closely as practical to see whether a similar result appears again.
  • Conceptual replication tests the same underlying claim with different methods, settings, measures, or populations.

Within-study replication supports the original treatment comparison. Replication across independent studies tests whether the finding persists beyond one team, sample, procedure, or setting.

Replication, Repeatability, and Reproducibility

Field usage varies, but the following distinctions are useful:

Term Typical Meaning
Repeatability Obtaining consistent measurements under nearly the same conditions
Reproducibility Obtaining consistent results from the same data or method, often by another analyst or system
Replication Collecting new independent experimental evidence about the same treatment or claim

Because disciplines use these terms differently, a report should describe exactly what was repeated instead of relying on the label alone.

How Much Replication Is Enough?

There is no universal replication count. The required number of experimental units depends on:

  • the smallest effect worth detecting;
  • the expected outcome variability;
  • the desired precision;
  • the number of treatments and planned comparisons;
  • the assignment structure, including clusters or repeated measures;
  • expected attrition or unusable outcomes;
  • ethical limits; and
  • available time, cost, material, and personnel.

Sample-size planning should occur before data collection. Choosing a size only because it is convenient may waste resources on an experiment too small to answer its question or use more units than necessary.

Ethics and Efficient Replication

Too little replication may expose participants or consume resources without a realistic chance of producing a useful answer. Excessive replication may also be unethical when it subjects more people or animals to risk than the question requires.

Ethical design seeks enough independent information to answer the question at the intended precision while avoiding unnecessary exposure, cost, and waste. Better measurement, blocking, and careful control can sometimes improve precision without simply adding more units.

Example: Identify the True Replication

A researcher studies a new cleaning method in six hospitals. Three hospitals are randomly assigned to the new method, and three continue the standard method. Infection outcomes are recorded for 500 patients in each hospital.

What Is the Experimental Unit?

The hospital is the experimental unit because the cleaning method is assigned at the hospital level.

How Many Replicates Are There?

There are three independent hospital-level replicates per treatment, not 1,500 independent treatment replicates per group.

What Do the Patient Measurements Add?

Patient outcomes provide detailed information within each hospital and help estimate hospital-level rates. They do not remove the shared influences among patients in the same hospital or create additional randomized hospitals.

What Would Strengthen Treatment-Level Evidence?

Randomly assigning more hospitals would add independent treatment replication. Merely measuring more patients in the same six hospitals would not provide the same benefit.

Example: Compare Two Replication Plans

A laboratory has resources for 48 independently prepared samples and wants to compare two equally important treatments with similar cost and variability.

  • Plan 1 assigns 24 samples to each treatment.
  • Plan 2 assigns 40 samples to treatment (A) and 8 to treatment (B).

Plan 1 generally provides the more precise difference between the two treatment means because information is not constrained by one very small group. Plan 2 could still be justified if treatment (A) requires substantially more safety information or if the two groups differ greatly in variability or cost.

The correct allocation follows the purpose of the experiment, not a universal rule that all groups must always be equal.

A Replication Checklist

  1. Identify the treatment-assignment unit. What is independently treated?
  2. Count independent units. How many replicates receive each treatment?
  3. Separate measurement repetition. Which observations come from the same unit?
  4. Anticipate variability. What sources of outcome variation are expected?
  5. Set the target precision. What uncertainty is acceptable?
  6. Plan the allocation. Should treatment replication be balanced?
  7. Account for structure. Are units clustered, paired, or repeatedly measured?
  8. Plan for loss. How will attrition and unusable measurements be handled?
  9. Match the analysis. Will the statistical method respect the true replicates?

Common Errors

Counting Measurements Instead of Units

Ten measurements from one treated unit do not equal ten independently treated units. Replication follows assignment.

Treating Subsamples as Independent Replicates

Measuring many leaves from one plant, students from one classroom, or fish from one tank adds within-unit detail but does not multiply treatment replication.

Assuming More Replication Removes Bias

Replication reduces random uncertainty. Systematic confounding or measurement bias can persist regardless of sample size.

Using Replication Without Random Assignment

Many observations in self-selected treatment groups may yield a precise association without establishing causation.

Ignoring Diminishing Returns

Precision often improves with the square root of the independent sample size. Doubling the number of replicates does not usually cut uncertainty in half.

Guided Practice

Problem 1

One field receives fertilizer (A), and one field receives fertilizer (B). Yield is measured at 100 locations within each field. How many independent treatment replicates are there per fertilizer?

Show the solution

There is one independent field-level replicate per fertilizer. The 100 measurements describe variation within each field but do not separate the fertilizer effect from the field effect.

Problem 2

Twelve independently prepared samples receive each of three treatments. Every sample is measured twice. How many experimental replicates and how many total measurements are there?

Show the solution

There are (12\times3=36) experimental replicates. Measuring each twice produces (36\times2=72) total measurements. The second measurement is a technical or repeated measurement, not another independent treatment replicate.

Problem 3

If the standard error of a mean is approximately (10/\sqrt{n}), compute it for (n=25) and (n=100).

Show the solution

For (n=25), the standard error is (10/5=2). For (n=100), it is (10/10=1). Multiplying the sample size by four cuts the standard error in half.

Problem 4

A study has 20 classrooms per teaching method and 30 students per classroom. The method is assigned by classroom. What change adds more independent treatment replication: adding ten students to every classroom or adding five classrooms to every method?

Show the solution

Adding five independently assigned classrooms to every method adds treatment replication. Adding students within existing classrooms adds within-classroom information but not independently randomized units.

Problem 5

An experiment repeats a biased sensor reading 1,000 times. Does the large number of measurements eliminate the bias?

Show the solution

No. Repetition may estimate the sensor's biased reading very precisely, but it does not correct the systematic error. Calibration or an unbiased measurement process is needed.

A Final Replication Check

Ask one structural question before counting observations:

\[ \boxed{ \text{How many independent opportunities were there to apply each treatment?} } \]

That count—not the number of readings, subsamples, or people nested inside a treated cluster—identifies the treatment replication.

Summary

  • Replication applies a treatment to multiple independent experimental units.
  • Replication reveals within-treatment variability, improves precision, and increases the ability to detect treatment effects.
  • Repeated measurements and technical replicates do not replace independent experimental replicates.
  • Pseudoreplication treats dependent observations as independent and overstates the strength of evidence.
  • The replication count follows the level of treatment assignment, including in cluster-randomized experiments.
  • More replication reduces random uncertainty but does not repair bias or confounding.
  • Sample-size and allocation decisions should be planned around variability, precision, effect size, design structure, ethics, and resources.
