Cumulative Distributions

A probability mass function describes probability at discrete values, and a probability density function describes how continuous probability is spread across intervals. A cumulative distribution function gathers either kind of probability into one running total. At each number \(x\), it records the probability that the random variable has reached or fallen below \(x\).

The Central Idea

The cumulative distribution function, or CDF, of a random variable \(X\) is defined by

\[ \boxed{F_X(x)=P(X\le x)}. \]

The CDF tells us how much probability has accumulated at or below the threshold \(x\).

Unlike a PMF or PDF, the CDF is defined for every random variable, regardless of whether the distribution is discrete, continuous, or mixed.

What Cumulative Means

The word cumulative means that probability is collected from the left up to a specified point.

If

\[ F_X(4)=0.72, \]

then

\[ \boxed{P(X\le4)=0.72}. \]

Seventy-two percent of the total probability lies at or below \(4\).

The CDF Is a Function of the Threshold

In

\[ F_X(x)=P(X\le x), \]

the input \(x\) is a threshold. As the threshold moves to the right, more possible values of \(X\) are included in the event.

Therefore the accumulated probability can stay the same or increase, but it cannot decrease.

A CDF Is Always Between 0 and 1

Since a CDF value is itself a probability,

\[ \boxed{0\le F_X(x)\le1} \]

for every real number \(x\).

Values below \(0\) or above \(1\) cannot occur in a valid CDF.

A CDF Is Nondecreasing

If

\[ a then the event

\[ \{X\le a\} \]

is contained in

\[ \{X\le b\}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{F_X(a)\le F_X(b)}. \]

A valid CDF may remain flat over an interval, but it can never move downward.

The Extreme Limits of a CDF

Far to the left of the distribution, essentially no probability has yet accumulated:

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}F_X(x)=0}. \]

Far to the right, all probability has accumulated:

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to\infty}F_X(x)=1}. \]

These limits are fundamental requirements for every cumulative distribution function.

Right-Continuity

Every CDF is right-continuous. At any point \(x\),

\[ \boxed{\lim_{t\to x^+}F_X(t)=F_X(x)}. \]

This property matters especially for discrete distributions, where the CDF may jump at values having positive probability.

The Four Core CDF Properties

A valid cumulative distribution function satisfies these structural conditions:

  1. \(0\le F_X(x)\le1\) for all \(x\).
  2. \(F_X(x)\) is nondecreasing.
  3. \(F_X(x)\) is right-continuous.
  4. \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}F_X(x)=0\) and \(\lim_{x\to\infty}F_X(x)=1\).

These properties apply to discrete, continuous, and mixed probability distributions.

Building a CDF from a PMF

If \(X\) is discrete with PMF \(p_X(x)\), then

\[ \boxed{F_X(x)=\sum_{t\le x}p_X(t)}. \]

The CDF is obtained by adding all probability masses at values less than or equal to the chosen threshold.

Suppose \(X\) has the PMF

\(0\) \(0.10\)
\(1\) \(0.25\)
\(2\) \(0.40\)

Accumulating the Discrete Probabilities

The CDF values at the support points are

\[ F_X(0)=0.10, \] \[ F_X(1)=0.10+0.25=0.35, \] \[ F_X(2)=0.10+0.25+0.40=0.75, \]

and

\[ F_X(3)=1. \]

Each new support point adds its probability mass to the running total.

The Complete Discrete CDF

For the preceding distribution,

\[ F_X(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ 0.10, & 0\le x<1,\\ 0.35, & 1\le x<2,\\ 0.75, & 2\le x<3,\\ 1, & x\ge3. \end{cases} \]

Notice that the CDF is constant between possible values and jumps when a support point is reached.

Discrete CDFs Look Like Step Functions

A discrete CDF is typically a step function.

Between support points, no additional probability mass is encountered, so the cumulative probability remains constant. At a support point, the CDF jumps upward by the probability assigned to that value.

The Size of a Jump Equals Point Probability

For any random variable, the probability concentrated at a point \(x\) is the size of the CDF jump there:

\[ \boxed{P(X=x)=F_X(x)-F_X(x^-)} \]

where

\[ F_X(x^-)=\lim_{t\to x^-}F_X(t). \]

For a discrete distribution, these jumps recover the PMF.

Example: Recovering a PMF from a CDF

Suppose a CDF jumps from \(0.35\) immediately before \(x=2\) to \(0.75\) at \(x=2\).

Then

\[ P(X=2)=0.75-0.35. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P(X=2)=0.40}. \]

The jump size is exactly the probability mass at \(2\).

Building a CDF from a PDF

If \(X\) is continuous with probability density function \(f_X(x)\), then

\[ \boxed{F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(t)\,dt}. \]

The CDF is the total area under the density curve to the left of the threshold \(x\).

Why a Different Variable Appears Inside the Integral

In

\[ F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(t)\,dt, \]

the symbol \(t\) is a temporary integration variable. The input of the CDF remains \(x\).

Using a different symbol inside the integral helps distinguish the moving threshold \(x\) from the variable being integrated.

Example: Uniform Distribution on \([0,4]\)

Suppose

\[ f_X(x)= \begin{cases} \frac14, & 0\le x\le4,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

For \(x<0\), no probability has accumulated:

\[ F_X(x)=0. \]

For \(0\le x\le4\),

\[ F_X(x)=\int_0^x\frac14\,dt=\frac{x}{4}. \]

For \(x\ge4\), all probability has accumulated:

\[ F_X(x)=1. \]

The Uniform CDF

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ F_X(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ \frac{x}{4}, & 0\le x\le4,\\ 1, & x>4. \end{cases}} \]

Unlike the discrete step-function example, this CDF rises continuously across its support.

Finding Probabilities Directly from a CDF

By definition,

\[ \boxed{P(X\le b)=F_X(b)}. \]

The probability above a threshold follows from the complement rule:

\[ \boxed{P(X>b)=1-F_X(b)}. \]

These formulas work for both discrete and continuous random variables.

If a continuous CDF is differentiable at \(x\), then its density is

The CDF Unifies Discrete and Continuous Probability

PMFs and PDFs require different accumulation operations:

  • discrete probabilities are summed;
  • continuous probabilities are integrated.

The CDF packages the accumulated result into the same object:

\[ \boxed{F_X(x)=P(X\le x)}. \]

This is one reason the CDF is so fundamental in probability theory.

Flat Portions of a CDF

If a CDF is constant over an interval, then no probability is accumulating there.

For example, if

\[ F_X(x)=0.4 \]

for every \(x\) between \(2\) and \(5\), then

\[ P(2 The distribution places no probability in that interval.

Steep Portions of a Continuous CDF

Where a differentiable continuous CDF rises rapidly, its derivative is large.

Since

\[ f_X(x)=F_X'(x), \]

a steep CDF corresponds to a region of relatively high probability density.

Jumps Signal Point Mass

A jump in a CDF indicates positive probability concentrated at a single point.

If the CDF jumps by \(0.12\) at \(x=7\), then

\[ \boxed{P(X=7)=0.12}. \]

Purely continuous distributions have no jumps.

Continuous CDF Does Not Mean Constant Density

A continuous CDF may curve, flatten, or become steep depending on how the density changes.

Continuity of the CDF means it has no jumps. It does not mean the CDF is a straight line or that the underlying density is uniform.

A CDF Can Be Continuous Without Being Differentiable Everywhere

Some continuous distributions have CDFs that are not differentiable at every point.

Therefore the relationship

\[ f_X(x)=F_X'(x) \]

should be used where an ordinary density exists and the derivative is defined. The CDF itself remains the more general object.

Quantiles from a CDF

A CDF can also be used to locate thresholds associated with specified cumulative probabilities.

A value \(q\) satisfying

\[ F_X(q)\approx p \]

identifies a threshold near the \(p\)-level of the distribution.

This idea leads to medians, percentiles, and more formal definitions of quantiles.

The Median and the CDF

Informally, a median is a value near the point where half the probability lies at or below it and half lies at or above it.

For a continuous distribution with strictly increasing CDF, the median \(m\) satisfies

\[ \boxed{F_X(m)=0.5}. \]

Discrete distributions require slightly more care because the CDF may jump across \(0.5\).

Common Error: Confusing a CDF with a PDF

A CDF and a PDF are different functions.

The CDF gives cumulative probability:

\[ F_X(x)=P(X\le x). \]

A PDF gives density, and probabilities arise by integration.

For a continuous distribution,

\[ F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(t)\,dt. \]

Common Error: Assuming a CDF Must Be Strictly Increasing

A CDF must be nondecreasing, but it may remain constant.

Flat regions simply indicate that no probability is accumulating over those ranges.

Common Error: Allowing a CDF to Decrease

A function that decreases somewhere cannot be a valid CDF.

If \(a \[ P(X\le a)\le P(X\le b), \]

so

\[ F_X(a)\le F_X(b). \]

The standard definition is

Common Error: Ignoring Jumps in a Discrete CDF

A discrete CDF does not normally rise smoothly.

If the random variable has positive probability at \(x\), the CDF jumps there by

\[ P(X=x). \]

Those jumps are essential information, not graphing artifacts.

For a general random variable,

\[ F(b)-F(a) \]

gives

\[ P(a If the event instead includes \(a\), then any probability mass at \(a\) must also be included.

A Reliable Procedure for Reading a CDF

  1. Identify the threshold or interval in the probability question.
  2. Use \(F(x)=P(X\le x)\) for cumulative probabilities.
  3. Use \(1-F(x)\) for probabilities strictly above \(x\).
  4. Use \(F(b)-F(a)\) for \(P(a
  5. For discrete variables, check carefully whether endpoints are included.
  6. Read jump sizes as point probabilities.
  7. For continuous variables, endpoint inclusion does not affect probability.

A Reliable Procedure for Constructing a Discrete CDF

  1. List the possible values in increasing order.
  2. Record the PMF value at each support point.
  3. Add probabilities cumulatively from left to right.
  4. Keep the CDF constant between support points.
  5. At each support point, jump upward by its probability mass.
  6. Use \(0\) below the smallest support value and \(1\) at and beyond the upper end when the support is finite.

A Reliable Procedure for Constructing a Continuous CDF

  1. Identify the support of the density.
  2. Set the CDF equal to \(0\) below the support.
  3. Integrate the density from the left end of the support to \(x\).
  4. Simplify the resulting expression.
  5. Set the CDF equal to \(1\) after all probability has accumulated.
  6. Check that the result is nondecreasing and approaches \(0\) and \(1\) at the extremes.

What to Remember

The cumulative distribution function is

\[ \boxed{F_X(x)=P(X\le x)}. \]

For a discrete random variable,

\[ \boxed{F_X(x)=\sum_{t\le x}p_X(t)}. \]

For a continuous random variable with density,

\[ \boxed{F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(t)\,dt}. \]

The CDF is nondecreasing, right-continuous, approaches \(0\) on the far left, and approaches \(1\) on the far right.
