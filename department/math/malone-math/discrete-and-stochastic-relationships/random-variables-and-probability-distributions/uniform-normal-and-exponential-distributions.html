Uniform, Normal, and Exponential Distributions

Continuous probability distributions model quantities that can vary across intervals rather than only at separate points. The uniform distribution spreads probability evenly across a bounded interval. The normal distribution models symmetric bell-shaped variation around a mean. The exponential distribution models continuous waiting times when events occur at a constant average rate.

The Central Idea

Three continuous probability families appear repeatedly in probability, statistics, science, engineering, economics, and applied modeling:

  • uniform distributions for equally dense outcomes across a bounded interval;
  • normal distributions for symmetric variation concentrated near a mean;
  • exponential distributions for waiting times associated with constant-rate Poisson processes.

Each family is defined by a probability density function and characterized by one or more parameters.

The Continuous Uniform Distribution

A continuous uniform distribution assigns constant probability density across an interval

\[ [a,b]. \]

We write

\[ \boxed{X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(a,b)}. \]

Uniform Density Function

The density must have total area \(1\). Since the support has width

\[ b-a, \]

the constant height must be

\[ \frac{1}{b-a}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ f_X(x)= \begin{cases} \frac{1}{b-a}, & a\le x\le b,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}} \]

Why the Uniform Density Is Constant

Equal-length subintervals receive equal probabilities.

If two subintervals inside \([a,b]\) have the same width, then they have the same area under the density curve and therefore the same probability.

Uniform Interval Probabilities

If

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(a,b) \]

and

\[ a\le c then

\[ P(c\le X\le d) = \int_c^d\frac{1}{b-a}\,dx. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P(c\le X\le d)=\frac{d-c}{b-a} }. \]

Example: Uniform Probability

Let

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,10). \]

Find

\[ P(2\le X\le5). \]

The desired interval has width \(3\), while the full support has width \(10\).

\[ P(2\le X\le5) = \frac{3}{10}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P(2\le X\le5)=0.3}. \]

Uniform Mean

The continuous uniform distribution is symmetric about the midpoint of its support.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{a+b}{2} }. \]

Uniform Variance

For

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(a,b), \]

the variance is

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{(b-a)^2}{12} }. \]

The standard deviation is

\[ \boxed{ \sigma_X=\frac{b-a}{\sqrt{12}} }. \]

The Uniform CDF

The cumulative distribution function is

\[ \boxed{ F_X(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & xb. \end{cases}} \]

The CDF rises linearly because the density is constant.

The Normal Distribution

The normal distribution is a continuous distribution with a symmetric bell-shaped density.

It is determined by two parameters:

  • the mean \(\mu\), which controls location;
  • the standard deviation \(\sigma>0\), which controls spread.

We write

\[ \boxed{ X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2) }. \]

The Normal Density Function

The density of

\[ X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2) \]

is

\[ \boxed{ f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} }. \]

Although the formula is important, normal probabilities are usually calculated through standardization, tables, calculators, or software rather than by integrating this density directly.

Shape of the Normal Distribution

A normal density is:

  • symmetric about \(\mu\);
  • highest at the mean;
  • unimodal;
  • spread over the entire real line;
  • asymptotic to the horizontal axis in both tails.

The total area under the curve is \(1\).

Mean, Median, and Mode

Because a normal distribution is symmetric and unimodal,

\[ \boxed{ \text{mean}=\text{median}=\text{mode}=\mu }. \]

The center of the distribution occurs at the same location under all three measures.

Effect of the Mean

Changing \(\mu\) shifts the entire normal distribution horizontally without changing its shape.

Larger \(\mu\) moves the distribution to the right. Smaller \(\mu\) moves it to the left.

Effect of the Standard Deviation

Increasing \(\sigma\) spreads the distribution out and lowers its peak.

Decreasing \(\sigma\) concentrates the distribution more tightly around the mean and raises the peak.

The total area remains \(1\).

The Standard Normal Distribution

The standard normal distribution has

\[ \mu=0 \]

and

\[ \sigma=1. \]

We write

\[ \boxed{Z\sim N(0,1)}. \]

Standardization

If

\[ X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2), \]

then

\[ \boxed{ Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma} } \]

has the standard normal distribution.

This transformation converts values into numbers of standard deviations above or below the mean.

Interpreting a z-Score

A z-score tells how far a value lies from the mean in standard-deviation units.

  • \(z=0\): exactly at the mean;
  • \(z=1\): one standard deviation above the mean;
  • \(z=-2\): two standard deviations below the mean.

Example: Compute a z-Score

Suppose

\[ X\sim N(70,8^2) \]

and consider

\[ x=86. \]

Then

\[ z = \frac{86-70}{8} = \boxed{2}. \]

The value \(86\) lies two standard deviations above the mean.

Normal Probabilities

Normal probabilities are areas under the normal density curve.

For example,

\[ P(X\le x) \]

is the area to the left of \(x\).

Standardization converts this to

\[ P\left( Z\le \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \right). \]

The Standard Normal CDF

The standard normal cumulative distribution function is commonly denoted

\[ \Phi(z)=P(Z\le z). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P(X\le x) = \Phi\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right) }. \]

Normal Interval Probabilities

For

\[ a standardize both endpoints:

\[ z_a=\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}, \qquad z_b=\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ P(a\le X\le b) = \Phi(z_b)-\Phi(z_a) }. \]

Symmetry of the Standard Normal Distribution

The standard normal distribution is symmetric about \(0\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z) }. \]

This identity helps relate upper-tail and lower-tail probabilities.

The Empirical Rule

For a normal distribution, approximately:

  • \(68\%\) of values lie within \(1\) standard deviation of the mean;
  • \(95\%\) lie within \(2\) standard deviations;
  • \(99.7\%\) lie within \(3\) standard deviations.

This is often called the 68-95-99.7 rule.

Using the Empirical Rule

If

\[ X\sim N(100,15^2), \]

then approximately \(95\%\) of values lie between

\[ 100-2(15)=70 \]

and

\[ 100+2(15)=130. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{P(70\le X\le130)\approx0.95}. \]

Normal Quantiles

Sometimes the probability is known and the corresponding value must be found.

If

\[ P(Z\le z_p)=p, \]

then \(z_p\) is the standard normal \(p\)-quantile.

For a general normal variable,

\[ \boxed{ x_p=\mu+\sigma z_p }. \]

The Exponential Distribution

The exponential distribution models continuous waiting times associated with a constant-rate Poisson process.

If events occur at rate

\[ \lambda>0 \]

per unit time or exposure, then the waiting time \(T\) until the next event may be modeled as

\[ \boxed{ T\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda) }. \]

The Exponential Density

The exponential probability density function is

\[ \boxed{ f_T(t)= \begin{cases} \lambda e^{-\lambda t}, & t\ge0,\\ 0, & t<0. \end{cases}} \]

The density is largest near \(0\) and decreases as \(t\) increases.

The Exponential CDF

For \(t\ge0\),

\[ F_T(t) = \int_0^t\lambda e^{-\lambda u}\,du. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ F_T(t)=1-e^{-\lambda t} }. \]

Exponential Survival Probability

The probability of waiting longer than \(t\) is

\[ P(T>t) = 1-F_T(t). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P(T>t)=e^{-\lambda t} }. \]

This survival function is central to exponential waiting-time models.

Example: Waiting More Than Two Hours

Suppose

\[ T\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(0.5) \]

where time is measured in hours.

Then

\[ P(T>2) = e^{-0.5(2)} = e^{-1}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P(T>2)\approx0.3679}. \]

Exponential Mean

For

\[ T\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda), \]

the expected waiting time is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[T]=\frac1\lambda }. \]

Higher event rates correspond to shorter expected waiting times.

Exponential Variance

The variance is

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(T)=\frac{1}{\lambda^2} }. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \sigma_T=\frac1\lambda }. \]

The exponential distribution has equal mean and standard deviation.

Exponential Memorylessness

Like the geometric distribution, the exponential distribution is memoryless.

For \(s,t\ge0\),

\[ \boxed{ P(T>s+t\mid T>s)=P(T>t) }. \]

Given that no event has occurred by time \(s\), the remaining waiting-time distribution is the same as it was at time \(0\).

Why Exponential Memorylessness Occurs

In a constant-rate Poisson process, the future event rate does not depend on how long the process has already gone without an event.

The process has no probabilistic memory of the elapsed waiting time.

Poisson Counts and Exponential Waiting Times

The Poisson and exponential distributions describe two sides of the same constant-rate process.

  • Poisson: how many events occur in a fixed interval?
  • Exponential: how long until the next event occurs?

If the Poisson rate is \(\lambda\), then the corresponding exponential waiting-time rate is also \(\lambda\).

Example: Rate and Mean Waiting Time

Suppose events occur at an average rate of

\[ 4 \]

per hour.

Then

\[ \lambda=4 \]

and the expected waiting time is

\[ \mathbb{E}[T] = \frac14\text{ hour}. \]

Since one quarter of an hour is \(15\) minutes,

\[ \boxed{\mathbb{E}[T]=15\text{ minutes}}. \]

Recognizing a Uniform Model

Consider a uniform model when:

  • the variable is continuous;
  • it is restricted to a bounded interval;
  • equal-length subintervals should receive equal probability;
  • there is no reason to favor one location within the interval over another.

Recognizing a Normal Model

A normal model may be reasonable when:

  • the variable is continuous;
  • values cluster around a central mean;
  • deviations above and below the center are approximately symmetric;
  • extreme values become progressively less common;
  • the underlying mechanism aggregates many small influences.

Recognizing an Exponential Model

Consider an exponential model when:

  • the variable is a nonnegative waiting time;
  • events arise from a roughly constant-rate Poisson process;
  • the memoryless assumption is reasonable;
  • short waits are more common than long waits.

Common Error: Treating Density as Point Probability

For all three continuous distributions,

\[ P(X=x)=0 \]

at every exact point.

Probability is obtained from area over intervals, not from the density height at a single point.

Common Error: Forgetting the Uniform Support

A uniform density is zero outside

\[ [a,b]. \]

Probability assigned outside the support is therefore zero.

Common Error: Using the Wrong Uniform Height

The density height is not

\[ \frac1b \]

unless \(a=0\).

The correct height is

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{b-a}}. \]

Common Error: Confusing Variance and Standard Deviation in Normal Notation

In

\[ X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2), \]

the second parameter is the variance

\[ \sigma^2, \]

not the standard deviation \(\sigma\).

Common Error: Reversing the z-Score Formula

The correct standardization is

\[ \boxed{ z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} }. \]

Subtract the mean first, then divide by the standard deviation.

Common Error: Treating the Empirical Rule as Exact

The \(68\%-95\%-99.7\%\) values are useful approximations for normal distributions.

They are not exact probability identities.

Common Error: Treating \(\lambda\) as Mean Waiting Time

In an exponential distribution,

\[ \lambda \]

is the event rate.

The mean waiting time is

\[ \boxed{\frac1\lambda}. \]

Common Error: Using a Negative Exponential Waiting Time

Exponential waiting times are nonnegative:

\[ T\ge0. \]

The density is zero for negative values.

A Reliable Procedure for Uniform Problems

  1. Identify the interval \([a,b]\).
  2. Write the density height \(1/(b-a)\).
  3. Translate the event into a subinterval.
  4. Compute probability as subinterval width divided by total width.
  5. Use \((a+b)/2\) for the mean.
  6. Use \((b-a)^2/12\) for the variance.

A Reliable Procedure for Normal Problems

  1. Identify \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\).
  2. Translate the probability statement into an interval or tail.
  3. Standardize each boundary using \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\).
  4. Use the standard normal CDF, table, calculator, or software.
  5. Subtract CDF values for interval probabilities.
  6. Use symmetry or complements when they simplify the calculation.
  7. Interpret the probability in the original units.

A Reliable Procedure for Exponential Problems

  1. Identify the constant event rate \(\lambda\).
  2. Confirm that the waiting time is nonnegative.
  3. Use \(P(T>t)=e^{-\lambda t}\) for survival probabilities.
  4. Use \(P(T\le t)=1-e^{-\lambda t}\) for cumulative probabilities.
  5. Use \(1/\lambda\) for the mean waiting time.
  6. Use \(1/\lambda^2\) for the variance.
  7. Check that time units and rate units are compatible.

What to Remember

For

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(a,b), \]

the density is

\[ \boxed{ f_X(x)=\frac{1}{b-a} } \]

on \([a,b]\), with

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{a+b}{2} } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{(b-a)^2}{12} }. \]

For

\[ X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2), \]

standardization uses

\[ \boxed{ Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma} }. \]

For

\[ T\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda), \]

the survival probability is

\[ \boxed{ P(T>t)=e^{-\lambda t} }. \]

Its mean and variance are

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[T]=\frac1\lambda } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(T)=\frac1{\lambda^2} }. \]

The central modeling distinction is: uniform distributions spread density evenly across a bounded interval, normal distributions model symmetric bell-shaped variation, and exponential distributions model continuous waiting times in constant-rate processes.
