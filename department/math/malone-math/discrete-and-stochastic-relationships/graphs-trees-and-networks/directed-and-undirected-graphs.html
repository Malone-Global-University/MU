Directed and Undirected Graphs

Some relationships work equally in both directions, while others have a definite source and destination. Graph theory distinguishes these cases using undirected edges and directed edges. The choice determines how we interpret adjacency, degree, paths, and the structure of a network.

The Central Idea

An undirected graph represents relationships with no preferred direction.

A directed graph, or digraph, represents relationships that point from one vertex to another.

The distinction can be summarized by the edge notation

\[ \boxed{\{u,v\}\text{ for an undirected edge}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{(u,v)\text{ for a directed edge from }u\text{ to }v}. \]

Undirected Edges

In an undirected graph, an edge joins two vertices symmetrically.

If

\[ \{u,v\}\in E, \]

then \(u\) is connected to \(v\) and \(v\) is connected to \(u\).

Because sets are unordered,

\[ \boxed{\{u,v\}=\{v,u\}}. \]

Directed Edges

A directed edge has an orientation. It begins at one vertex and points to another.

The directed edge

\[ (u,v) \]

points from \(u\) to \(v\).

In general,

\[ \boxed{(u,v)\ne(v,u)}. \]

Tail and Head

For a directed edge

\[ (u,v), \]

the starting vertex \(u\) is called the tail, and the destination vertex \(v\) is called the head.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{u\longrightarrow v}. \]

Visual Comparison

An undirected edge is usually drawn as a line. A directed edge is usually drawn with an arrow.

Why Direction Matters

Direction changes the meaning of a connection.

If a road permits travel in both directions, an undirected edge may be appropriate. If a street permits travel only from \(A\) to \(B\), the directed edge

\[ (A,B) \]

records that restriction.

The reverse movement would require a separate directed edge

\[ (B,A). \]

Examples of Undirected Relationships

Undirected graphs are useful when the modeled relationship is naturally symmetric.

  • two rooms connected by an unrestricted doorway;
  • two computers linked by a symmetric physical connection;
  • two people recorded as mutual collaborators;
  • two locations joined by a two-way route;
  • two objects that share a specified property.

The modeling decision should match the actual relationship being studied.

Examples of Directed Relationships

Directed graphs are useful when the relationship has an origin and a destination.

  • a web page linking to another page;
  • a person following another account;
  • a one-way road;
  • a task that must precede another task;
  • a citation from one document to another;
  • a transition from one system state to another.

A First Undirected Graph

Let

\[ V=\{a,b,c,d\} \]

and

\[ E= \bigl\{ \{a,b\},\{a,c\},\{b,d\} \bigr\}. \]

Since the edges are undirected, \(\{a,b\}\) represents the same connection as \(\{b,a\}\).

A First Directed Graph

Now let

\[ V=\{a,b,c,d\} \]

and

\[ E= \bigl\{ (a,b),(a,c),(b,d) \bigr\}. \]

These edges mean

\[ a\to b,\qquad a\to c,\qquad b\to d. \]

No reverse directions are implied.

One Directed Edge Does Not Imply Its Reverse

If

\[ (a,b)\in E, \]

we may move along that edge from \(a\) to \(b\).

But unless

\[ (b,a)\in E, \]

the graph does not also contain an edge from \(b\) to \(a\).

Two Opposite Directed Edges

A directed graph may contain both

\[ (u,v) \]

and

\[ (v,u). \]

Together they represent two directed connections, one in each direction. They are still two distinct edges in a directed model.

Reciprocal Connections

When both

\[ (u,v)\in E \]

and

\[ (v,u)\in E, \]

the pair of vertices has a reciprocal or mutual directed relationship.

Reciprocal edges are common in communication and social-network models.

Adjacency in an Undirected Graph

In an undirected graph, vertices \(u\) and \(v\) are adjacent when

\[ \{u,v\}\in E. \]

The relationship is symmetric:

\[ u\text{ adjacent to }v \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v\text{ adjacent to }u. \]

Adjacency in a Directed Graph

Direction requires more precise language. In this lesson, if

\[ (u,v)\in E, \]

we say that \(v\) is an out-neighbor of \(u\), and \(u\) is an in-neighbor of \(v\).

This terminology avoids treating a one-way relationship as if it were symmetric.

Out-Neighborhood

The out-neighborhood of \(u\) is the set of vertices that receive a directed edge from \(u\).

\[ \boxed{N^+(u)=\{v:(u,v)\in E\}}. \]

If

\[ E=\{(a,b),(a,c),(c,a)\}, \]

then

\[ N^+(a)=\{b,c\}. \]

In-Neighborhood

The in-neighborhood of \(u\) is the set of vertices that send a directed edge into \(u\).

\[ \boxed{N^-(u)=\{v:(v,u)\in E\}}. \]

For

\[ E=\{(a,b),(a,c),(c,a)\}, \]

we have

\[ N^-(a)=\{c\}. \]

Degree in an Undirected Graph

In a simple undirected graph, the degree of a vertex counts all incident edges.

\[ \deg(v)=\text{number of edges incident with }v. \]

There is no need to distinguish incoming from outgoing edges because the edges have no direction.

Out-Degree

In a directed graph, the out-degree of \(v\), written \(\deg^+(v)\), counts directed edges whose tail is \(v\).

\[ \boxed{\deg^+(v)=\text{number of edges leaving }v}. \]

In a simple digraph without loops,

\[ \deg^+(v)=|N^+(v)|. \]

In-Degree

The in-degree of \(v\), written \(\deg^-(v)\), counts directed edges whose head is \(v\).

\[ \boxed{\deg^-(v)=\text{number of edges entering }v}. \]

In a simple digraph without loops,

\[ \deg^-(v)=|N^-(v)|. \]

Example: In-Degree and Out-Degree

Let

\[ E= \bigl\{ (a,b),(a,c),(b,c),(c,a),(d,c) \bigr\}. \]

For vertex \(a\), the outgoing edges are

\[ (a,b),\qquad(a,c), \]

so

\[ \deg^+(a)=2. \]

The only incoming edge is \((c,a)\), so

\[ \deg^-(a)=1. \]

Directed Degree Table

For

\[ E= \bigl\{ (a,b),(a,c),(b,c),(c,a),(d,c) \bigr\}, \]

the degrees are

Vertex In-degree \(\deg^-\) Out-degree \(\deg^+\)
\(a\) 1 2
\(b\) 1 1
\(c\) 3 1
\(d\) 0 1

Total Directed Degree at a Vertex

When useful, we may combine incoming and outgoing incidences:

\[ \boxed{\deg(v)=\deg^-(v)+\deg^+(v)}. \]

For vertex \(c\) in the previous example,

\[ \deg(c)=3+1=4. \]

When working with digraphs, however, keeping in-degree and out-degree separate usually preserves more information.

The Directed Degree-Sum Rules

Every directed edge contributes exactly \(1\) to one out-degree and exactly \(1\) to one in-degree.

Therefore, for a finite directed graph,

\[ \boxed{\sum_{v\in V}\deg^+(v)=|E|} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\sum_{v\in V}\deg^-(v)=|E|}. \]

Consequently,

\[ \sum_{v\in V}\left(\deg^-(v)+\deg^+(v)\right)=2|E|. \]

Example: Checking Directed Degrees

The example graph contains five directed edges.

Its out-degrees sum to

\[ 2+1+1+1=5, \]

while its in-degrees sum to

\[ 1+1+3+0=5. \]

Both totals equal

\[ |E|=5. \]

Sources and Sinks

In a directed graph, a vertex with no incoming edges has

\[ \deg^-(v)=0. \]

Such a vertex is often called a source when it has outgoing edges.

A vertex with no outgoing edges has

\[ \deg^+(v)=0. \]

Such a vertex is often called a sink when it has incoming edges.

Example: Source and Sink

Let

\[ E=\{(p,q),(p,r),(q,s),(r,s)\}. \]

Vertex \(p\) has

\[ \deg^-(p)=0,\qquad \deg^+(p)=2, \]

so \(p\) is a source.

Vertex \(s\) has

\[ \deg^-(s)=2,\qquad \deg^+(s)=0, \]

so \(s\) is a sink.

Directed Adjacency Lists

A directed adjacency list usually records outgoing neighbors.

For

\[ E=\{(a,b),(a,c),(b,c),(c,a)\}, \]

the outgoing adjacency list is

  • \(a:\ b,c\)
  • \(b:\ c\)
  • \(c:\ a\)

Each row records where a directed edge can go from that vertex.

Directed Adjacency Matrices

For a directed graph, define

\[ A_{ij}= \begin{cases} 1,&\text{if }(v_i,v_j)\in E,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

The row identifies the tail, and the column identifies the head.

Unlike the adjacency matrix of a simple undirected graph, a directed adjacency matrix does not have to be symmetric.

Example: Directed Adjacency Matrix

Let

\[ E=\{(a,b),(a,c),(b,c),(c,a)\}. \]

Using vertex order \(a,b,c\),

\[ A= \begin{pmatrix} 0&1&1\\ 0&0&1\\ 1&0&0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The entry in row \(a\), column \(b\) is \(1\) because \(a\to b\). The entry in row \(b\), column \(a\) is \(0\) because \(b\to a\) is absent.

Degrees from a Directed Adjacency Matrix

In a simple directed graph without loops:

  • the sum of row \(i\) gives \(\deg^+(v_i)\);
  • the sum of column \(i\) gives \(\deg^-(v_i)\).

For

\[ A= \begin{pmatrix} 0&1&1\\ 0&0&1\\ 1&0&0 \end{pmatrix}, \]

the first row sums to \(2\), so \(\deg^+(a)=2\). The first column sums to \(1\), so \(\deg^-(a)=1\).

Symmetry in an Undirected Adjacency Matrix

In a simple undirected graph,

\[ \{v_i,v_j\}\in E \]

implies the same connection when the endpoints are reversed.

Therefore,

\[ A_{ij}=A_{ji}. \]

The adjacency matrix is symmetric:

\[ \boxed{A=A^{T}}. \]

Direction Can Change Reachability

Suppose a directed graph contains

\[ a\to b\to c. \]

Following the arrows allows movement from \(a\) to \(c\) through \(b\).

The same edges do not necessarily allow movement from \(c\) back to \(a\).

This asymmetry becomes important when we study directed paths and connectivity.

Converting Direction into an Undirected View

Sometimes we care only whether two vertices have some directed connection, not which way it points.

In that case we can form an underlying undirected graph by replacing directed connections between distinct vertices with undirected edges.

For example,

\[ (a,b),\qquad(b,c),\qquad(c,a) \]

become

\[ \{a,b\},\qquad\{b,c\},\qquad\{a,c\}. \]

Direction Contains Additional Information

Converting a directed graph to an undirected graph can discard information.

The two directed graphs

\[ a\to b \]

and

\[ a\leftarrow b \]

have the same underlying undirected edge

\[ \{a,b\}. \]

Once the arrow is removed, the original direction cannot be recovered from the undirected graph alone.

Choosing the Correct Graph Type

Before constructing a graph, ask what the edge is supposed to mean.

If the relationship is symmetric, an undirected graph may be appropriate. If the relationship has a source and destination, use a directed graph.

A technically correct graph with the wrong edge type can still be a poor model of the real system.

Example: Transportation

Suppose intersections are vertices.

A two-way street between intersections \(A\) and \(B\) can be modeled by

\[ \{A,B\}. \]

A one-way street from \(C\) to \(D\) should instead be modeled by

\[ (C,D). \]

Using one notation for both would conceal an operational difference in the network.

Example: Dependency Network

Suppose task \(A\) must be completed before task \(B\), and task \(B\) before task \(C\).

A natural directed model is

\[ A\to B\to C. \]

Replacing these arrows with undirected edges would lose the precedence information.

Example: Communication Network

If a communication link allows messages in both directions, it may be modeled as an undirected edge.

If messages can flow only from one node to another, a directed edge is more appropriate.

The mathematical form should reflect the actual permitted flow.

Common Error: Reversing an Ordered Edge

In a directed graph,

\[ (u,v) \]

and

\[ (v,u) \]

are different edges.

Never reverse a directed edge unless the reverse edge is actually present.

Common Error: Using Set Notation for Direction

The notation

\[ \{u,v\} \]

does not encode direction because

\[ \{u,v\}=\{v,u\}. \]

Use an ordered pair such as \((u,v)\), or an arrow \(u\to v\), when direction matters.

Common Error: Counting In-Degree as Out-Degree

If

\[ a\to b, \]

then this edge contributes \(1\) to

\[ \deg^+(a) \]

and \(1\) to

\[ \deg^-(b). \]

It does not contribute to \(\deg^-(a)\) or \(\deg^+(b)\).

Common Error: Assuming Directed Matrices Are Symmetric

If \(A_{ij}=1\) in a directed adjacency matrix, then

\[ v_i\to v_j. \]

This does not imply

\[ v_j\to v_i. \]

Therefore \(A_{ji}\) may be \(0\).

Common Error: Losing Information during Conversion

Turning a directed graph into an undirected graph can be useful, but it removes orientation.

Do not use the resulting undirected graph to infer which directions were present in the original network.

A Reliable Procedure for Classifying a Graph

  1. Identify what each vertex represents.
  2. Identify what each edge represents.
  3. Ask whether the relationship is symmetric.
  4. If direction is irrelevant, use undirected edges.
  5. If source and destination matter, use directed edges.
  6. For a digraph, distinguish incoming from outgoing neighbors.
  7. Compute in-degree and out-degree separately.
  8. Check that total in-degree and total out-degree each equal \(|E|\).

Example: Full Directed-Graph Workflow

Let

\[ V=\{p,q,r,s\} \]

and

\[ E= \bigl\{ (p,q),(p,r),(q,r),(r,p),(r,s) \bigr\}. \]

The out-neighborhood of \(r\) is

\[ N^+(r)=\{p,s\}, \]

while the in-neighborhood is

\[ N^-(r)=\{p,q\}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \deg^+(r)=2,\qquad \deg^-(r)=2. \]

Across all vertices, the in-degrees sum to \(5\), the out-degrees sum to \(5\), and

\[ |E|=5. \]

\[ \boxed{\text{Use directed edges.}} \]

Practice 2

The ordered pair

\[ (a,b) \]

represents a directed edge whose tail is \(a\) and whose head is \(b\):

\[ \boxed{a\to b}. \]

No. Directed edges are ordered:

\[ \boxed{(a,b)\not\Rightarrow(b,a)}. \]

Practice 4

The edges leaving \(a\) are \((a,b)\) and \((a,c)\). Therefore,

\[ \boxed{N^+(a)=\{b,c\}}. \]

Practice 5

The edges entering \(c\) are

\[ (a,c),\qquad(b,c),\qquad(d,c). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{N^-(c)=\{a,b,d\}}. \]

Practice 6

Three edges enter \(c\), and one edge leaves \(c\):

\[ \boxed{\deg^-(c)=3,\qquad \deg^+(c)=1}. \]

Practice 7

Vertex \(p\) has no incoming edges and has outgoing edges, so it is a source.

Vertex \(s\) has incoming edges and no outgoing edges, so it is a sink.

\[ \boxed{p\text{ is a source},\qquad s\text{ is a sink}.} \]

Practice 8

Every directed edge contributes once to the in-degree total and once to the out-degree total. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\sum\deg^-(v)=7,\qquad \sum\deg^+(v)=7}. \]

Practice 9

\[ \boxed{ A= \begin{pmatrix} 0&1&1\\ 0&0&0\\ 0&1&0 \end{pmatrix} }. \]

Practice 10

The arrow \(x\to y\) permits the directed relationship from \(x\) to \(y\). It does not create the reverse edge.

Travel from \(y\) to \(x\) along a directed edge would require

\[ \boxed{y\to x} \]

to be present separately.

What to Remember

  • An undirected edge has no preferred orientation.
  • \(\{u,v\}=\{v,u\}\) for an undirected edge.
  • A directed edge \((u,v)\) points from \(u\) to \(v\).
  • In general, \((u,v)\ne(v,u)\).
  • \(\deg^+(v)\) counts edges leaving \(v\).
  • \(\deg^-(v)\) counts edges entering \(v\).
  • For a finite digraph, total out-degree and total in-degree both equal \(|E|\).
  • Directed adjacency matrices need not be symmetric.
  • The graph type should reflect whether the modeled relationship is symmetric or directional.
