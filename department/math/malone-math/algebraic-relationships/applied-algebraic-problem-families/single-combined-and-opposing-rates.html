Single, Combined, and Opposing Work Rates

When several workers, machines, pipes, pumps, or processes act on the same task, the quantities that combine are their work rates. Productive rates add, opposing rates subtract, and the resulting net rate determines how quickly the job is completed or undone. The essential discipline is to convert every completion time into a rate before combining anything.

The Central Idea

If one contributor completes work at rate \(r_1\) and another contributes at rate \(r_2\), their combined productive rate is

\[ \boxed{r_{\text{combined}}=r_1+r_2}. \]

If one process undoes work at rate \(r_2\), the net rate is

\[ \boxed{r_{\text{net}}=r_1-r_2}. \]

For one complete job,

\[ \boxed{1=r_{\text{net}}t}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{t=\frac{1}{r_{\text{net}}}}. \]

Review: Completion Time Becomes Rate

If worker A completes a job in \(a\) hours, then

\[ r_A=\frac1a. \]

If worker B completes the same job in \(b\) hours, then

\[ r_B=\frac1b. \]

These rates, not the completion times \(a\) and \(b\), are the quantities that combine.

Two Productive Workers

Suppose worker A completes one job in \(6\) hours and worker B completes it in \(4\) hours.

Their rates are

\[ \frac16 \]

and

\[ \frac14 \]

job per hour.

Together,

\[ r_{\text{combined}} = \frac16+\frac14 = \frac{2}{12}+\frac{3}{12} = \frac{5}{12}. \]

Thus they complete

\[ \boxed{\frac{5}{12}\text{ of the job per hour}}. \]

Find the Combined Completion Time

Continue with the combined rate

\[ \frac{5}{12}. \]

For one complete job,

\[ 1=\frac{5}{12}t. \]

Therefore,

\[ t=\frac{12}{5}=2.4. \]

Thus the job takes

\[ \boxed{2.4\text{ hours}} \]

when both workers operate together.

Why the Combined Time Is Shorter

Worker A alone takes \(6\) hours, and worker B alone takes \(4\) hours.

Together they take

\[ 2.4\text{ hours}. \]

This makes sense because the combined productive rate

\[ \frac{5}{12} \]

is greater than either individual rate.

For two positive contributors working independently,

\[ \boxed{ t_{\text{combined}} < \min(a,b) }. \]

The General Two-Worker Formula

If one worker takes \(a\) hours and another takes \(b\) hours, then

\[ r_{\text{combined}} = \frac1a+\frac1b. \]

Combine:

\[ \frac1a+\frac1b = \frac{a+b}{ab}. \]

Therefore, the combined completion time is

\[ \boxed{ t=\frac{ab}{a+b} }. \]

This formula assumes both contributors work productively and continuously on the same job at constant rates.

Example: Use the General Formula

One machine takes \(8\) hours and another takes \(12\) hours.

Then

\[ t= \frac{8(12)}{8+12} = \frac{96}{20} = 4.8. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{4.8\text{ hours}}. \]

Three Productive Contributors

If three contributors have rates

\[ r_1,\qquad r_2,\qquad r_3, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ r_{\text{combined}} = r_1+r_2+r_3 }. \]

If their individual completion times are \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\), then

\[ \boxed{ r_{\text{combined}} = \frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c }. \]

Example: Three Workers

Three workers can individually complete a job in \(6\), \(8\), and \(12\) hours.

Their combined rate is

\[ \frac16+\frac18+\frac1{12}. \]

Using denominator \(24\),

\[ \frac4{24}+\frac3{24}+\frac2{24} = \frac9{24} = \frac38. \]

Therefore, the combined completion time is

\[ t=\frac{1}{3/8} = \frac83. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{2\frac23\text{ hours}}. \]

Work Tables for Combined Rates

A table keeps the contributors separate before their work is combined.

\[ \begin{array}{c|c|c|c} \text{contributor} & \text{rate} & \text{time} & \text{work}\\ \hline A & \frac1a & t & \frac{t}{a}\\ B & \frac1b & t & \frac{t}{b} \end{array} \]

If both work for the same interval \(t\), then

\[ \frac{t}{a}+\frac{t}{b}=1. \]

Factoring \(t\) gives

\[ t\left(\frac1a+\frac1b\right)=1. \]

Combined Work as Fractions of the Job

Suppose worker A takes \(5\) hours and B takes \(10\) hours.

In \(2\) hours, A completes

\[ \frac25, \]

while B completes

\[ \frac2{10}=\frac15. \]

Together they complete

\[ \frac25+\frac15=\frac35. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\frac35\text{ of the job}} \]

is finished after \(2\) hours.

Unknown Combined Time

A worker takes \(9\) hours and another takes \(6\) hours. Let \(t\) be the time required together.

Their completed work is

\[ \frac{t}{9} \]

and

\[ \frac{t}{6}. \]

Since the whole job equals \(1\),

\[ \frac{t}{9}+\frac{t}{6}=1. \]

Multiply by \(18\):

\[ 2t+3t=18. \] \[ 5t=18. \] \[ t=\frac{18}{5}=3.6. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{3.6\text{ hours}}. \]

Unknown Individual Completion Time

Worker A completes a job in \(6\) hours. Working together, A and B complete the job in \(4\) hours. Find B's completion time.

Let B's time be \(x\) hours.

Then

\[ \frac16+\frac1x=\frac14. \]

Subtract:

\[ \frac1x = \frac14-\frac16 = \frac{3}{12}-\frac{2}{12} = \frac1{12}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=12\text{ hours}}. \]

Unknown Rate from a Combined Rate

If the combined rate is known, an unknown individual rate can be found by subtraction.

In general,

\[ r_A+r_B=r_{\text{combined}}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ r_B=r_{\text{combined}}-r_A }. \]

Once \(r_B\) is known, its completion time is

\[ \boxed{T_B=\frac1{r_B}}. \]

Example: Find the Missing Machine Time

Machine A completes a job in \(10\) hours. Together, A and B complete it in \(6\) hours.

A's rate is

\[ \frac1{10}. \]

The combined rate is

\[ \frac16. \]

Therefore,

\[ r_B= \frac16-\frac1{10} = \frac5{30}-\frac3{30} = \frac1{15}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{B\text{ takes }15\text{ hours alone}}. \]

Comparing Contributions

If two workers operate for the same amount of time, their completed work is proportional to their rates.

Suppose A's rate is

\[ \frac14 \]

and B's rate is

\[ \frac16. \]

Their rate ratio is

\[ \frac{1/4}{1/6} = \frac32. \]

Therefore, over equal working times, A completes

\[ \boxed{\frac32} \]

as much work as B.

Contribution Shares in a Completed Job

If A and B work together from start to finish, each contributor's fraction of the completed job equals its rate divided by the combined rate.

If

\[ r_A=\frac14, \qquad r_B=\frac16, \]

then

\[ r_{\text{combined}} = \frac5{12}. \]

A's share is

\[ \frac{1/4}{5/12} = \frac35, \]

and B's share is

\[ \frac{1/6}{5/12} = \frac25. \]

Their shares add to \(1\).

Opposing Work Rates

Some processes undo or remove work performed by another process.

Examples include:

  • a drain emptying a tank while a pipe fills it;
  • a leak removing fluid while a pump adds it;
  • a process producing material while another removes material;
  • a repair process operating while damage continues to accumulate.

In such cases, opposing rates subtract.

Net Work Rate

If a productive process has rate \(r_p\) and an opposing process has rate \(r_o\), then

\[ \boxed{ r_{\text{net}}=r_p-r_o }. \]

The sign of the net rate determines the direction of change.

  • If \(r_{\text{net}}>0\), net work is completed.
  • If \(r_{\text{net}}=0\), there is no net progress.
  • If \(r_{\text{net}}<0\), the opposing process dominates.

Example: Fill and Drain

A pipe can fill a tank in \(6\) hours. A drain can empty the same tank in \(9\) hours.

The filling rate is

\[ \frac16. \]

The draining rate is

\[ \frac19. \]

With both open,

\[ r_{\text{net}} = \frac16-\frac19 = \frac3{18}-\frac2{18} = \frac1{18}. \]

Therefore, the tank fills in

\[ \boxed{18\text{ hours}}. \]

Why the Net Completion Time Can Be Much Longer

The fill pipe alone needs \(6\) hours, but with the drain open the tank needs \(18\) hours.

The drain continuously removes part of the progress.

The net rate

\[ \frac1{18} \]

is much smaller than the fill rate

\[ \frac16. \]

A small positive net rate produces a large completion time.

When the Opposing Process Is Faster

Suppose a pipe fills a tank in \(10\) hours while a drain empties it in \(8\) hours.

The net rate is

\[ \frac1{10}-\frac18 = \frac4{40}-\frac5{40} = -\frac1{40}. \]

The negative rate means the tank cannot fill from empty while both operate under this model.

Instead, the amount in the tank decreases at a net rate of

\[ \boxed{\frac1{40}\text{ tank per hour}}. \]

No Net Progress

If a filling process and an opposing process have equal rates,

\[ r_p=r_o, \]

then

\[ r_{\text{net}}=0. \]

The system remains at the same work level while both rates continue unchanged.

A completion time based on

\[ \frac1{r_{\text{net}}} \]

does not exist because division by zero is undefined.

General Opposing-Rate Formula

If one process completes a job in \(a\) hours and another reverses one full job in \(b\) hours, with \(a<b\) so the productive process is faster, then

\[ r_{\text{net}} = \frac1a-\frac1b. \]

Combine:

\[ \frac1a-\frac1b = \frac{b-a}{ab}. \]

Therefore, the net completion time is

\[ \boxed{ t=\frac{ab}{b-a} }. \]

Example: Use the Opposing-Rate Formula

A fill pipe fills a tank in \(4\) hours, while a drain empties it in \(12\) hours.

Then

\[ t= \frac{4(12)}{12-4} = \frac{48}{8} = 6. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{6\text{ hours}}. \]

Several Productive and Opposing Processes

With several processes, assign positive signs to productive rates and negative signs to opposing rates.

For example,

\[ \boxed{ r_{\text{net}} = r_1+r_2-r_3-r_4 }. \]

The same principle extends to any number of constant independent rates.

Example: Two Fill Pipes and One Drain

Two pipes fill a tank in \(6\) hours and \(8\) hours. A drain empties it in \(24\) hours.

The net rate is

\[ \frac16+\frac18-\frac1{24}. \]

Using denominator \(24\),

\[ \frac4{24}+\frac3{24}-\frac1{24} = \frac6{24} = \frac14. \]

Therefore, the tank fills in

\[ \boxed{4\text{ hours}}. \]

Unknown Opposing Rate

A pipe fills a tank in \(5\) hours. With a drain open, the tank fills in \(8\) hours. Find the drain's emptying time.

Let the drain rate be \(r_d\).

Then

\[ \frac15-r_d=\frac18. \]

Therefore,

\[ r_d= \frac15-\frac18 = \frac8{40}-\frac5{40} = \frac3{40}. \]

The drain's emptying time is

\[ \frac{1}{3/40} = \frac{40}{3}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{13\frac13\text{ hours}}. \]

Unknown Productive Rate

A drain empties a tank in \(15\) hours. With a fill pipe open, the tank fills from empty in \(10\) hours. Find the fill pipe's rate.

Let \(r_f\) be the fill rate.

\[ r_f-\frac1{15}=\frac1{10}. \]

Therefore,

\[ r_f = \frac1{10}+\frac1{15} = \frac3{30}+\frac2{30} = \frac16. \]

Thus the fill pipe alone would fill the tank in

\[ \boxed{6\text{ hours}}. \]

Combined Rate from Measured Output

Work problems do not always begin with completion times.

Suppose two machines together produce \(180\) units per hour, and one machine produces \(75\) units per hour.

The second machine's rate is

\[ 180-75=105. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{105\text{ units per hour}}. \]

The same additive logic applies whether work is measured in whole jobs, components, gallons, or another consistent unit.

Production with Loss

A process produces \(120\) units per hour while defects or losses remove \(15\) units per hour.

The net production rate is

\[ 120-15=105. \]

Over \(8\) hours, net production is

\[ 105(8)=840. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{840\text{ net units}}. \]

Combined Rates Must Use the Same Work Unit

Rates may be added or subtracted only when they refer to the same kind of work.

For example,

\[ 30\text{ units/hour} \]

cannot be added directly to

\[ \frac14\text{ job/hour} \]

unless the size of one job is defined in units.

Normalize the work units before combining the rates.

Combined Rates Must Use the Same Time Unit

Suppose one machine works at

\[ 20\text{ units/min} \]

and another at

\[ 900\text{ units/hr}. \]

Convert the second rate:

\[ 900\text{ units/hr} = 15\text{ units/min}. \]

Therefore, the combined rate is

\[ 20+15=35. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{35\text{ units/min}}. \]

Combined Work and Direct Variation

Once the combined rate is known and constant, total work varies directly with time:

\[ \boxed{ W=r_{\text{combined}}t }. \]

If

\[ r_{\text{combined}}=\frac{7}{20}, \]

then after \(2\) hours,

\[ W= \frac{7}{20}(2) = \frac7{10}. \]

The combined-rate problem reduces to an ordinary work-rate problem after the rates have been consolidated.

Net Rate Is an Algebraic Sum

A useful general viewpoint is to assign a sign to every contributor.

Productive contributors receive positive rates, while opposing contributors receive negative rates.

Then

\[ \boxed{ r_{\text{net}}=\sum_i r_i }. \]

This notation treats addition and subtraction as one unified algebraic operation.

Checking a Combined-Rate Answer

Suppose two workers take \(6\) and \(4\) hours alone, and the calculated combined time is \(2.4\) hours.

In \(2.4\) hours, A completes

\[ \frac{2.4}{6}=0.4, \]

while B completes

\[ \frac{2.4}{4}=0.6. \]

Together,

\[ 0.4+0.6=1. \]

The whole job is recovered.

Checking an Opposing-Rate Answer

Suppose a fill pipe takes \(6\) hours and a drain takes \(9\) hours, with a calculated net fill time of \(18\) hours.

In \(18\) hours, the fill pipe contributes

\[ \frac{18}{6}=3 \]

tank-equivalents of inflow.

The drain removes

\[ \frac{18}{9}=2. \]

Net work:

\[ 3-2=1. \]

The result is verified.

Common Error: Adding Completion Times

If two workers take \(6\) and \(4\) hours, adding

\[ 6+4=10 \]

does not give their combined completion time.

Convert first:

\[ \frac16+\frac14=\frac5{12}. \]

Then invert the combined rate:

\[ \boxed{\frac{12}{5}=2.4\text{ hours}}. \]

Common Error: Averaging Completion Times

The average of \(6\) hours and \(4\) hours is

\[ 5\text{ hours}, \]

but two workers acting together should complete the job faster than either one acting alone.

A \(5\)-hour result fails this basic reasonableness check.

Common Error: Adding Rates When One Process Opposes Another

A fill pipe and a drain do not both contribute positively to the same quantity.

If the fill rate is

\[ \frac16 \]

and the drain rate is

\[ \frac19, \]

the net rate is

\[ \boxed{ \frac16-\frac19 }. \]

Common Error: Subtracting in the Wrong Order

If the tank is supposed to fill, use

\[ r_{\text{fill}}-r_{\text{drain}}. \]

Reversing the order changes the sign and therefore changes the physical interpretation.

Define the positive direction of work before writing the net-rate equation.

Common Error: Ignoring an Impossible Net Rate

If the drain rate exceeds the fill rate, the tank cannot fill from empty while both processes remain active.

A negative net rate is not merely an arithmetic inconvenience. It describes a system moving in the opposite direction from the requested completion.

Common Error: Mixing Work Units

Rates such as

\[ \frac14\text{ job/hr} \]

and

\[ 30\text{ parts/hr} \]

cannot be added until “one job” is defined in parts.

The work quantities must refer to the same whole.

Common Error: Mixing Time Units

A rate in jobs per hour cannot be combined directly with a rate in jobs per minute.

Convert both to a common time unit before adding or subtracting.

Common Error: Forgetting to Invert the Final Rate

If a combined rate is

\[ \frac{3}{10}\text{ job/hr}, \]

the completion time is not

\[ \frac{3}{10}\text{ hr}. \]

Instead,

\[ t=\frac{1}{3/10} = \boxed{\frac{10}{3}\text{ hr}}. \]

A Reliable Procedure for Combined Work Rates

  1. Define one complete job as \(1\).
  2. Convert each completion time \(T_i\) to rate \(1/T_i\).
  3. Make all work and time units consistent.
  4. Add the productive rates.
  5. Set \(1=r_{\text{combined}}t\) for one whole job.
  6. Solve for \(t\).
  7. Verify that each contributor's work fractions add to \(1\).
  8. Check that the combined time is shorter than every positive individual time.

A Reliable Procedure for Opposing Work Rates

  1. Choose which direction of work is positive.
  2. Convert all completion or reversal times into rates.
  3. Assign positive signs to productive rates.
  4. Assign negative signs to opposing rates.
  5. Add algebraically to obtain the net rate.
  6. Determine whether the net rate is positive, zero, or negative.
  7. If completion is possible, use \(1=r_{\text{net}}t\).
  8. Reconstruct the productive and opposing work to verify the result.

A Reliable Procedure for Unknown Individual Rates

  1. Write the known combined or net rate.
  2. Write all known individual rates.
  3. Represent the unknown contributor by a rate variable.
  4. Solve the rate equation.
  5. If the problem asks for completion time, take the reciprocal.
  6. Reject nonphysical rates or times.
  7. Substitute back into the combined-rate equation.

What to Remember

Productive work rates add:

\[ \boxed{ r_{\text{combined}}=r_1+r_2+\cdots }. \]

Opposing work rates subtract:

\[ \boxed{ r_{\text{net}} = r_{\text{productive}}-r_{\text{opposing}} }. \]

If completion times are given, convert them first:

\[ \boxed{r=\frac1T}. \]

For one complete job,

\[ \boxed{1=r_{\text{net}}t}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{t=\frac1{r_{\text{net}}}} \]

whenever the net rate is positive.

The most important habit is to combine rates rather than completion times, and to assign signs according to whether each process contributes to or opposes the desired work.
