Concentration, Dilution, and Replacement

A mixture can change even when no new type of ingredient is introduced. Adding pure solvent dilutes a solution, adding pure solute strengthens it, removing part of a well-mixed solution removes every component in the same proportion, and replacing part of a mixture repeatedly creates a geometric pattern. These problems are governed by conservation: track how much of the component remains, how much is added or removed, and how the total quantity changes.

The Central Idea

If a mixture has total quantity \(Q\) and component concentration \(c\), then the amount of the component is

\[ \boxed{A=cQ}. \]

Concentration changes when either the component amount \(A\), the total quantity \(Q\), or both change.

The fundamental concentration relationship is

\[ \boxed{c=\frac{A}{Q}}. \]

Dilution and replacement problems are solved by tracking \(A\) and \(Q\) separately.

Three Basic Ways Concentration Can Change

Concentration can change by:

  1. adding material;
  2. removing material;
  3. removing and replacing material.

The key question is always:

How much of the component is present after the operation?

Dilution by Adding Pure Solvent

Suppose a solution contains \(A\) units of solute in total quantity \(Q\).

If \(x\) units of pure solvent are added, the solute amount remains

\[ A, \]

while total quantity becomes

\[ Q+x. \]

Therefore, the new concentration is

\[ \boxed{ c_{\text{new}}=\frac{A}{Q+x} }. \]

Example: Dilute a Solution

A \(20\)-liter solution is \(40\%\) solute. How much water must be added to reduce the concentration to \(25\%\)?

Initial solute:

\[ A=0.40(20)=8. \]

Let \(x\) liters of water be added.

Since no solute is added,

\[ 8=0.25(20+x). \]

Solve:

\[ 8=5+0.25x. \] \[ 3=0.25x. \] \[ x=12. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{12\text{ liters of water}}. \]

The Dilution Formula

When dilution changes only the total quantity and does not change the amount of solute, then

\[ c_1Q_1=c_2Q_2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ c_1Q_1=c_2Q_2 }. \]

This formula is useful only when the component amount is unchanged between the initial and final states.

Example: Use the Dilution Formula

A \(15\)-liter solution is \(60\%\) solute. To what total volume must it be diluted to obtain a \(36\%\) solution?

Use

\[ 0.60(15)=0.36Q_2. \] \[ 9=0.36Q_2. \] \[ Q_2=25. \]

The final total volume is

\[ \boxed{25\text{ liters}}. \]

Since the original volume was \(15\) liters, the amount of solvent added is

\[ 25-15=10. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{10\text{ liters of solvent}}. \]

Strengthening by Adding Pure Solute

Suppose a mixture initially contains component amount \(A\) in total quantity \(Q\).

Adding \(x\) units of pure component increases both the component amount and total quantity by \(x\):

\[ A_{\text{new}}=A+x, \] \[ Q_{\text{new}}=Q+x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ c_{\text{new}} = \frac{A+x}{Q+x} }. \]

Example: Add Pure Solute

A \(30\)-kg mixture is \(20\%\) component A. How much pure component A must be added to raise the concentration to \(35\%\)?

Initial component amount:

\[ 0.20(30)=6. \]

Let \(x\) kilograms of pure component be added.

\[ 6+x=0.35(30+x). \]

Expand:

\[ 6+x=10.5+0.35x. \] \[ 0.65x=4.5. \] \[ x=\frac{90}{13}\approx6.923. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{6.92\text{ kg approximately}}. \]

Removing Pure Component

If pure component is removed directly, then both the component amount and total quantity decrease by the removed amount.

If \(x\) units of pure component are removed,

\[ A_{\text{new}}=A-x, \] \[ Q_{\text{new}}=Q-x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ c_{\text{new}} = \frac{A-x}{Q-x} }. \]

This model differs from removing a portion of a well-mixed solution.

Removing Part of a Well-Mixed Solution

If a solution is thoroughly mixed, every removed portion has the same concentration as the whole mixture at that moment.

Suppose the mixture has concentration \(c\), and \(x\) units are removed.

The removed component amount is

\[ cx. \]

Therefore,

\[ A_{\text{new}}=A-cx. \]

Removing a Well-Mixed Portion Does Not Change Concentration

Suppose a mixture has quantity \(Q\), component amount \(cQ\), and concentration \(c\).

Remove \(x\) units.

Remaining component:

\[ cQ-cx=c(Q-x). \]

Remaining total quantity:

\[ Q-x. \]

Therefore, the remaining concentration is

\[ \frac{c(Q-x)}{Q-x}=c. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\text{removing a well-mixed portion alone does not change concentration}}. \]

Example: Remove Part of a Solution

A \(25\)-liter solution is \(40\%\) solute. Five liters are removed.

Solute removed:

\[ 0.40(5)=2. \]

Initial solute:

\[ 0.40(25)=10. \]

Remaining solute:

\[ 10-2=8. \]

Remaining solution:

\[ 25-5=20. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac8{20}=0.40. \]

The concentration remains

\[ \boxed{40\%}. \]

Replacement Problems

A replacement problem removes part of a well-mixed mixture and then replaces the removed amount with another material.

A common case is:

  1. remove \(x\) units of mixture;
  2. replace them with \(x\) units of pure solvent.

The total quantity returns to its original value, but the component amount decreases.

One-Step Replacement with Pure Solvent

Begin with total quantity \(Q\) and concentration \(c\).

Remove \(x\) units.

The fraction of the mixture remaining is

\[ \frac{Q-x}{Q} = 1-\frac{x}{Q}. \]

Therefore, the fraction of the original component remaining is also

\[ \boxed{ 1-\frac{x}{Q} }. \]

One-Step Replacement Formula

The original component amount is

\[ cQ. \]

After removing \(x\) units of well-mixed solution, the remaining component amount is

\[ cQ\left(1-\frac{x}{Q}\right). \]

Replacing with pure solvent adds no component.

Since the total quantity returns to \(Q\), the new concentration is

\[ \boxed{ c_{\text{new}} = c\left(1-\frac{x}{Q}\right) }. \]

Example: One Replacement

A \(20\)-liter solution is \(50\%\) solute. Four liters are removed and replaced with pure water.

The fraction retained is

\[ 1-\frac4{20} = \frac45. \]

Therefore, the new concentration is

\[ 0.50\left(\frac45\right) = 0.40. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{40\%}. \]

The Same Example by Component Accounting

Initial solute:

\[ 0.50(20)=10. \]

Four liters of \(50\%\) solution contain

\[ 0.50(4)=2 \]

liters of solute.

Remaining solute:

\[ 10-2=8. \]

Replacing with \(4\) liters of water restores the total to \(20\) liters.

Therefore,

\[ \frac8{20}=0.40. \]

The two methods agree.

Repeated Replacement

If the same fraction of a mixture is removed and replaced repeatedly, the amount of the original component decreases geometrically.

If the retained fraction each time is

\[ r=1-\frac{x}{Q}, \]

then after \(n\) identical replacements,

\[ \boxed{ c_n=c_0r^n }. \]

Repeated Replacement Formula

For total quantity \(Q\), replacement amount \(x\), initial concentration \(c_0\), and \(n\) repeated replacements with pure solvent,

\[ \boxed{ c_n = c_0 \left( 1-\frac{x}{Q} \right)^n }. \]

This formula assumes the mixture is thoroughly mixed before every removal.

Example: Two Replacements

A \(30\)-liter solution is \(60\%\) solute. Six liters are removed and replaced with water. The mixture is stirred, then the same process is repeated.

Retained fraction each time:

\[ 1-\frac6{30} = \frac45. \]

After two replacements:

\[ c_2 = 0.60\left(\frac45\right)^2. \] \[ c_2 = 0.60\left(\frac{16}{25}\right) = 0.384. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{38.4\%}. \]

Why Repeated Replacement Is Geometric

Each replacement removes the same fraction of whatever component is present at that moment.

The component amount is repeatedly multiplied by the same retained factor:

\[ A_1=A_0r, \] \[ A_2=A_1r=A_0r^2, \] \[ A_3=A_0r^3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A_n=A_0r^n}. \]

Replacement with a Nonzero-Concentration Fluid

Replacement material need not be pure solvent.

Suppose a fraction

\[ f=\frac{x}{Q} \]

of the mixture is removed and replaced with material having concentration \(c_r\).

The new concentration is

\[ \boxed{ c_{\text{new}} = c(1-f)+c_rf }. \]

Deriving the General Replacement Formula

After removing fraction \(f\), fraction

\[ 1-f \]

of the original mixture remains.

Its component contribution is

\[ c(1-f). \]

The replacement contributes

\[ c_rf. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ c_{\text{new}} = c(1-f)+c_rf }. \]

Example: Replace with Another Solution

A \(20\)-liter solution is \(50\%\) solute. Four liters are removed and replaced with a \(10\%\) solution.

The replaced fraction is

\[ f=\frac4{20}=0.20. \]

Therefore,

\[ c_{\text{new}} = 0.50(0.80)+0.10(0.20). \] \[ c_{\text{new}} = 0.40+0.02 = 0.42. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{42\%}. \]

Repeated Replacement toward a New Concentration

If each replacement uses a fluid with concentration \(c_r\), the mixture moves toward \(c_r\).

Let

\[ r=1-\frac{x}{Q}. \]

Then after \(n\) replacements,

\[ \boxed{ c_n = c_r+(c_0-c_r)r^n }. \]

When \(c_r=0\), this reduces to the pure-solvent formula.

Why the General Repeated Formula Works

The difference between the current concentration and the replacement concentration is multiplied by the retained fraction each cycle.

Thus,

\[ c_{n+1}-c_r = r(c_n-c_r). \]

Repeating gives

\[ c_n-c_r = (c_0-c_r)r^n. \]

Therefore,

\[ c_n = c_r+(c_0-c_r)r^n. \]

Unknown Replacement Amount

Suppose one replacement with pure solvent changes concentration from \(c_0\) to \(c_1\) in a container of volume \(Q\).

Then

\[ c_1 = c_0\left(1-\frac{x}{Q}\right). \]

Solve for \(x\):

\[ \frac{c_1}{c_0} = 1-\frac{x}{Q}. \] \[ \frac{x}{Q} = 1-\frac{c_1}{c_0}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ x = Q\left(1-\frac{c_1}{c_0}\right) }. \]

Example: Find the Replacement Amount

A \(40\)-liter solution is \(50\%\) solute. How much must be removed and replaced with pure water to reduce the concentration to \(35\%\) in one step?

Use

\[ 0.35 = 0.50\left(1-\frac{x}{40}\right). \]

Divide by \(0.50\):

\[ 0.70 = 1-\frac{x}{40}. \] \[ \frac{x}{40}=0.30. \] \[ x=12. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{12\text{ liters}}. \]

Unknown Number of Replacements

If repeated pure-solvent replacement gives

\[ c_n = c_0r^n, \]

then the number of replacement cycles can be found from

\[ \frac{c_n}{c_0}=r^n. \]

Taking logarithms,

\[ \boxed{ n = \frac{\ln(c_n/c_0)}{\ln r} }. \]

The final practical answer may need to be rounded up to the next whole replacement cycle.

Example: How Many Replacements?

A \(20\)-liter solution begins at \(80\%\) concentration. Each cycle removes \(5\) liters and replaces them with water. How many complete cycles are needed to reduce the concentration below \(30\%\)?

The retained fraction is

\[ r=1-\frac5{20}=\frac34. \]

We want

\[ 0.80\left(\frac34\right)^n<0.30. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \left(\frac34\right)^n<0.375. \]

Testing powers:

\[ \left(\frac34\right)^3\approx0.4219, \] \[ \left(\frac34\right)^4\approx0.3164. \]

Therefore,

\[ 0.80\left(\frac34\right)^4\approx0.2531<0.30. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{4\text{ replacement cycles}}. \]

Replacement versus Simple Dilution

Dilution by adding solvent increases total quantity.

Replacement removes mixture first and then restores the original total quantity.

These are different operations.

In dilution,

\[ Q_{\text{final}}>Q_{\text{initial}}. \]

In equal-volume replacement,

\[ Q_{\text{final}}=Q_{\text{initial}}. \]

Replacement versus Removing Pure Solute

Removing a well-mixed portion removes all components proportionally.

Removing pure solute selectively removes only the component.

The algebra and final concentration differ greatly.

Always identify what exactly is being removed.

Sequential Operations

Some problems involve several different operations.

For example:

  1. remove part of a mixture;
  2. add pure solvent;
  3. add concentrated solution;
  4. remove another portion.

Update the component amount and total quantity after every step.

Example: Sequential Operations

A \(20\)-liter solution is \(40\%\) solute. Remove \(5\) liters, then add \(8\) liters of pure water.

Initial solute:

\[ 0.40(20)=8. \]

Removed solute:

\[ 0.40(5)=2. \]

Remaining solute:

\[ 8-2=6. \]

Remaining solution after removal:

\[ 15\text{ L}. \]

Add \(8\) liters of water:

\[ Q_{\text{final}}=23. \]

Therefore,

\[ c_f=\frac6{23}\approx0.2609. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{26.09\%\text{ approximately}}. \]

Unknown Added Solvent after Removal

Suppose a mixture is partially removed and then an unknown amount of pure solvent is added.

First compute the remaining component amount after removal.

Then let the final quantity be

\[ Q_{\text{remaining}}+x. \]

The target concentration equation becomes

\[ \boxed{ A_{\text{remaining}} = c_f(Q_{\text{remaining}}+x) }. \]

Replacement Tables

A replacement table can track the state before and after each step.

\[ \begin{array}{c|c|c|c} \text{stage} & \text{total quantity} & \text{concentration} & \text{component}\\ \hline \text{initial} & Q & c_0 & c_0Q\\ \text{after removal} & Q-x & c_0 & c_0(Q-x)\\ \text{after replacement} & Q & c_1 & c_1Q \end{array} \]

This layout is especially useful before introducing a shortcut formula.

Repeated Replacement as Exponential Decay

The repeated replacement model

\[ c_n=c_0r^n \]

is a discrete exponential decay model when

\[ 0<r<1. \]

Each replacement cycle multiplies the current concentration by the same retained fraction.

This connects mixture problems to exponential models studied elsewhere in algebra.

Limiting Behavior

For repeated replacement with pure solvent,

\[ c_n=c_0r^n, \qquad 0<r<1. \]

As the number of replacements grows,

\[ r^n\to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{c_n\to0}. \]

The concentration approaches zero, although a finite number of replacement cycles does not generally remove every last trace.

Replacement toward a Nonzero Limit

If replacement fluid has concentration \(c_r\), then

\[ c_n = c_r+(c_0-c_r)r^n. \]

Since

\[ r^n\to0, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{c_n\to c_r}. \]

The mixture approaches the concentration of the replacement fluid.

Checking Dilution Problems

Adding pure solvent should lower concentration.

The component amount should remain unchanged.

Therefore, verify both:

\[ A_{\text{before}}=A_{\text{after}} \]

and

\[ c_{\text{after}}<c_{\text{before}}. \]

Checking Pure-Solute Addition

Adding pure component should increase concentration toward \(100\%\).

The final concentration should satisfy

\[ c_{\text{initial}} < c_{\text{final}} < 1 \]

for a positive finite addition to a non-pure initial mixture.

Checking Replacement Problems

After replacement with pure solvent, the total quantity should be restored to its original value.

The component amount should decrease by exactly the component removed.

The final concentration should be lower than the original concentration.

Checking Repeated Replacement

If each cycle retains fraction \(r\), then after \(n\) cycles the remaining original component fraction should be

\[ r^n. \]

Because

\[ 0<r<1, \]

the concentration should decrease monotonically toward the replacement concentration.

Common Error: Using the Dilution Formula When Solute Changes

The formula

\[ c_1Q_1=c_2Q_2 \]

assumes the component amount is unchanged.

It cannot be used when pure solute is added, when mixture is removed, or when the component amount otherwise changes.

Common Error: Treating Removed Mixture as Pure Solvent

A removed portion of a well-mixed solution contains solute in the same proportion as the original mixture.

Removing \(x\) liters from a \(30\%\) solution removes

\[ \boxed{0.30x} \]

liters of solute.

Common Error: Assuming Removal Alone Changes Concentration

Removing part of a well-mixed solution reduces both solute and total solution proportionally.

Therefore, removal alone leaves concentration unchanged.

Common Error: Replacing before Mixing

Repeated replacement formulas assume the mixture is thoroughly mixed before each removal.

Without remixing, the removed portion may not have the same concentration as the entire container.

The standard formula would then not apply.

Common Error: Subtracting the Same Absolute Component Amount Repeatedly

In repeated replacement, each cycle removes the same fraction of the component currently present, not the same absolute component amount.

That is why the process is multiplicative:

\[ \boxed{A_n=A_0r^n}. \]

Common Error: Forgetting the Total Quantity after Addition

If \(x\) liters are added to \(Q\) liters, the final quantity is

\[ \boxed{Q+x}. \]

Using the original denominator \(Q\) after addition produces an incorrect concentration.

Common Error: Forgetting the Total Quantity after Removal

If \(x\) units are removed from a mixture of quantity \(Q\), the remaining total is

\[ \boxed{Q-x}. \]

The denominator must reflect the new total unless the removed quantity is replaced.

Common Error: Using Percentage Points as Multipliers

A concentration change from \(40\%\) to \(30\%\) is a decrease of \(10\) percentage points.

But the retained concentration factor is

\[ \frac{30}{40}=0.75, \]

not \(0.90\).

Distinguish percentage-point differences from multiplicative percent changes.

A Reliable Procedure for Dilution Problems

  1. Compute the initial component amount \(A=cQ\).
  2. Identify what is being added.
  3. If pure solvent is added, keep \(A\) unchanged.
  4. Write the new total quantity.
  5. Use \(c=A/Q\) or \(c_1Q_1=c_2Q_2\) when valid.
  6. Solve for the unknown addition or final quantity.
  7. Check that concentration moves in the expected direction.

A Reliable Procedure for Removal Problems

  1. Assume the mixture is well mixed unless stated otherwise.
  2. Compute the current concentration.
  3. Multiply concentration by the removed quantity to find component removed.
  4. Subtract both total quantity and component amount.
  5. Recompute concentration if further operations follow.
  6. Remember that removal alone does not change concentration.

A Reliable Procedure for Replacement Problems

  1. Identify the total quantity \(Q\).
  2. Identify the removed and replaced quantity \(x\).
  3. Compute the retained fraction \(1-x/Q\).
  4. Multiply the current component amount or concentration by that retained fraction.
  5. Add the contribution of the replacement fluid if its concentration is nonzero.
  6. Repeat the process or use a power formula for identical repeated cycles.
  7. Verify that total quantity returns to \(Q\) after each equal replacement.

What to Remember

Concentration is

\[ \boxed{c=\frac{A}{Q}}. \]

Dilution by pure solvent keeps the component amount constant:

\[ \boxed{c_1Q_1=c_2Q_2}. \]

Removing a well-mixed portion alone does not change concentration.

For one replacement with pure solvent,

\[ \boxed{ c_{\text{new}} = c_0\left(1-\frac{x}{Q}\right) }. \]

For \(n\) identical replacements,

\[ \boxed{ c_n = c_0 \left( 1-\frac{x}{Q} \right)^n }. \]

The most important habit is to track component amount and total quantity separately after every operation.
