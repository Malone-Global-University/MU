Simple and Compound Interest

Interest models describe how money changes over time. Simple interest adds the same amount during each time period, while compound interest repeatedly applies growth to an amount that already includes earlier interest. The difference is fundamental: simple interest produces linear growth, whereas compound interest produces exponential growth. Algebra lets us calculate future value, present value, interest earned, rates, and time under both models.

The Central Idea

Interest depends on three basic quantities:

  • the amount of money invested or borrowed;
  • the interest rate;
  • the amount of time.

The original amount is called the principal.

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal.

Compound interest is calculated on the accumulated balance.

Principal, Interest, and Amount

Let

  • \(P\) = principal;
  • \(I\) = interest earned or charged;
  • \(A\) = accumulated amount or future value.

These quantities satisfy

\[ \boxed{A=P+I}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{I=A-P}. \]

Interest Rates as Decimals

Interest formulas use rates in decimal form.

For example,

\[ 6\%=0.06, \] \[ 4.5\%=0.045, \]

and

\[ 0.8\%=0.008. \]

Divide a percent by \(100\) before substituting it into a formula.

Simple Interest

Simple interest is calculated on the original principal throughout the entire investment or loan.

If \(P\) is principal, \(r\) is the annual interest rate as a decimal, and \(t\) is time in years, then

\[ \boxed{I=Prt}. \]

Simple-Interest Amount Formula

Since

\[ A=P+I \]

and

\[ I=Prt, \]

we have

\[ A=P+Prt. \]

Factor \(P\):

\[ \boxed{A=P(1+rt)}. \]

Example: Simple Interest Earned

Find the simple interest on \(\$2{,}500\) invested at \(6\%\) annually for \(4\) years.

\[ I=Prt. \] \[ I=2500(0.06)(4). \] \[ I=600. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$600\text{ interest}}. \]

Example: Simple-Interest Amount

Continue the previous example.

\[ A=P+I. \] \[ A=2500+600. \] \[ A=3100. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$3{,}100}. \]

Simple Interest Produces Linear Growth

For fixed \(P\) and \(r\),

\[ A=P(1+rt). \]

Rewrite:

\[ A=P+(Pr)t. \]

This is linear in \(t\).

The starting value is \(P\), and the amount added each year is

\[ \boxed{Pr}. \]

Example: Annual Increase under Simple Interest

A principal of \(\$4{,}000\) earns \(5\%\) simple interest.

The annual interest is

\[ Pr=4000(0.05)=200. \]

Therefore, the balance increases by

\[ \boxed{\$200\text{ each year}}. \]

Solving Simple Interest for Principal

From

\[ I=Prt, \]

solve for \(P\):

\[ \boxed{ P=\frac{I}{rt} }. \]

Example: Find the Principal

An investment earns \(\$540\) in simple interest at \(4.5\%\) for \(3\) years.

\[ P= \frac{540}{0.045(3)}. \] \[ P= \frac{540}{0.135}. \] \[ P=4000. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$4{,}000}. \]

Solving Simple Interest for Rate

From

\[ I=Prt, \]

solve for \(r\):

\[ \boxed{ r=\frac{I}{Pt} }. \]

Example: Find a Simple Interest Rate

A principal of \(\$5{,}000\) earns \(\$750\) in simple interest over \(5\) years.

\[ r= \frac{750}{5000(5)}. \] \[ r=0.03. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{3\%}. \]

Solving Simple Interest for Time

From

\[ I=Prt, \]

solve for \(t\):

\[ \boxed{ t=\frac{I}{Pr} }. \]

Example: Find Simple-Interest Time

How long does \(\$3{,}000\) need to earn \(\$720\) at \(6\%\) simple interest?

\[ t= \frac{720}{3000(0.06)}. \] \[ t= \frac{720}{180}=4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{4\text{ years}}. \]

Time Units Must Match the Rate

If \(r\) is an annual interest rate, then \(t\) should be measured in years.

For example,

\[ 9\text{ months} = \frac9{12}\text{ year} = 0.75\text{ year}. \]

Convert time units before using an annual-rate formula.

Example: Simple Interest for Months

Find the simple interest on \(\$8{,}000\) at \(7\%\) for \(9\) months.

Convert time:

\[ t=\frac9{12}=0.75. \]

Then

\[ I=8000(0.07)(0.75). \] \[ I=420. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$420}. \]

Compound Interest

Under compound interest, interest is periodically added to the balance.

Future interest is then calculated on that larger balance.

This produces repeated multiplicative growth rather than repeated additive growth.

One Compounding Period

Suppose the periodic interest rate is \(i\).

After one period,

\[ A=P+Pi. \]

Factor:

\[ \boxed{A=P(1+i)}. \]

Repeated Compounding

After two periods,

\[ A=P(1+i)^2. \]

After three periods,

\[ A=P(1+i)^3. \]

After \(N\) periods,

\[ \boxed{ A=P(1+i)^N }. \]

Nominal Annual Rate with \(n\) Compounding Periods

Suppose the nominal annual interest rate is \(r\), compounded \(n\) times per year.

The periodic rate is

\[ \boxed{\frac{r}{n}}. \]

Over \(t\) years, the number of compounding periods is

\[ \boxed{nt}. \]

The Compound-Interest Formula

Combining the periodic rate and the number of periods gives

\[ \boxed{ A = P \left( 1+\frac{r}{n} \right)^{nt} }. \]

Here:

  • \(P\) = principal;
  • \(r\) = nominal annual rate as a decimal;
  • \(n\) = compounding periods per year;
  • \(t\) = years;
  • \(A\) = accumulated amount.

Common Compounding Frequencies

Typical values of \(n\) include:

\[ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{compounding frequency} & n\\ \hline \text{annual} & 1\\ \text{semiannual} & 2\\ \text{quarterly} & 4\\ \text{monthly} & 12\\ \text{daily} & 365 \end{array} \]

Use the frequency stated in the problem.

Example: Annual Compounding

Invest \(\$5{,}000\) at \(6\%\) compounded annually for \(4\) years.

\[ A= 5000(1.06)^4. \] \[ A\approx6312.38. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$6{,}312.38\text{ approximately}}. \]

Example: Quarterly Compounding

Invest \(\$5{,}000\) at a nominal annual rate of \(6\%\), compounded quarterly for \(4\) years.

Here,

\[ n=4. \]

Periodic rate:

\[ \frac{0.06}{4}=0.015. \]

Number of periods:

\[ 4(4)=16. \]

Therefore,

\[ A= 5000(1.015)^{16}. \] \[ A\approx6344.93. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\$6{,}344.93\text{ approximately}}. \]

Interest Earned under Compounding

Once the final amount is known, compound interest earned is

\[ \boxed{I=A-P}. \]

In the quarterly example,

\[ I\approx6344.93-5000. \] \[ I\approx1344.93. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$1{,}344.93\text{ interest approximately}}. \]

Compound Growth Is Exponential

In

\[ A=P(1+i)^N, \]

the number of periods \(N\) appears in the exponent.

Each period multiplies the current balance by the same growth factor

\[ \boxed{1+i}. \]

This is the defining structure of exponential growth.

Simple versus Compound Interest

Simple interest:

\[ A=P(1+rt). \]

Compound interest:

\[ A= P \left( 1+\frac{r}{n} \right)^{nt}. \]

Simple interest grows by equal additions.

Compound interest grows by equal multiplicative factors.

Example: Compare Simple and Compound Interest

Compare \(\$10{,}000\) at \(5\%\) for \(10\) years.

Simple interest:

\[ A_s=10000(1+0.05(10)). \] \[ A_s=15000. \]

Annual compounding:

\[ A_c=10000(1.05)^{10}. \] \[ A_c\approx16288.95. \]

Therefore, annual compounding produces approximately

\[ 16288.95-15000=1288.95 \]

more than simple interest over the same period.

Present Value

Sometimes the future amount \(A\) is known and the required initial principal \(P\) is unknown.

Start with

\[ A= P \left( 1+\frac{r}{n} \right)^{nt}. \]

Divide:

\[ \boxed{ P = \frac{A}{ \left( 1+\frac{r}{n} \right)^{nt} } }. \]

Example: Find Present Value

How much must be invested now to have \(\$10{,}000\) in \(5\) years at \(4\%\) compounded annually?

\[ P= \frac{10000}{(1.04)^5}. \] \[ P\approx8219.27. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$8{,}219.27\text{ approximately}}. \]

Solving Compound Interest for Rate

With annual compounding,

\[ A=P(1+r)^t. \]

Divide by \(P\):

\[ \frac{A}{P}=(1+r)^t. \]

Take the \(t\)-th root:

\[ 1+r= \left( \frac{A}{P} \right)^{1/t}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ r= \left( \frac{A}{P} \right)^{1/t}-1 }. \]

Example: Find an Annual Compound Rate

An investment grows from \(\$4{,}000\) to \(\$5{,}324\) in \(3\) years with annual compounding.

\[ r= \left( \frac{5324}{4000} \right)^{1/3}-1. \]

Since

\[ \frac{5324}{4000}=1.331, \]

and

\[ 1.331=1.1^3, \]

we obtain

\[ r=0.10. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{10\%}. \]

Solving Compound Interest for Time

With annual compounding,

\[ A=P(1+r)^t. \]

Divide:

\[ \frac{A}{P}=(1+r)^t. \]

Take logarithms:

\[ \ln\left(\frac{A}{P}\right) = t\ln(1+r). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ t= \frac{ \ln(A/P) }{ \ln(1+r) } }. \]

Example: Find Compound-Growth Time

How long does \(\$5{,}000\) take to grow to \(\$8{,}000\) at \(6\%\) compounded annually?

\[ t= \frac{\ln(8000/5000)}{\ln(1.06)}. \] \[ t\approx8.06. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{8.06\text{ years approximately}}. \]

Compounding More Frequently

For the same nominal annual rate \(r\), increasing the compounding frequency generally increases the accumulated amount.

Annual compounding uses

\[ n=1, \]

while monthly compounding uses

\[ n=12. \]

The increase becomes smaller as compounding becomes more frequent.

Effective Annual Rate

A nominal annual rate does not by itself describe the exact one-year growth when interest is compounded more than once per year.

The effective annual rate is

\[ \boxed{ r_{\text{eff}} = \left( 1+\frac{r}{n} \right)^n-1 }. \]

Example: Effective Annual Rate

Find the effective annual rate for a nominal \(6\%\) rate compounded monthly.

\[ r_{\text{eff}} = \left( 1+\frac{0.06}{12} \right)^{12}-1. \] \[ r_{\text{eff}} = (1.005)^{12}-1. \] \[ r_{\text{eff}}\approx0.061678. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{6.17\%\text{ approximately}}. \]

Nominal versus Effective Rate

A nominal rate is the stated annual rate used to determine the periodic rate.

An effective annual rate is the actual one-year percentage growth after compounding.

When compounding occurs more than once per year at a positive rate,

\[ r_{\text{eff}}>r. \]

Continuous Compounding

As compounding becomes increasingly frequent, the compound-interest model approaches continuous compounding.

The continuously compounded amount is

\[ \boxed{ A=Pe^{rt} }. \]

Here \(e\) is the natural exponential base.

Example: Continuous Compounding

Invest \(\$7{,}500\) at \(5\%\) continuously compounded for \(6\) years.

\[ A=7500e^{0.05(6)}. \] \[ A=7500e^{0.30}. \] \[ A\approx10123.94. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$10{,}123.94\text{ approximately}}. \]

Present Value under Continuous Compounding

From

\[ A=Pe^{rt}, \]

solve for \(P\):

\[ \boxed{ P=Ae^{-rt} }. \]

Example: Continuous Present Value

How much must be invested now to reach \(\$20{,}000\) in \(8\) years at \(4\%\) continuously compounded?

\[ P=20000e^{-0.04(8)}. \] \[ P=20000e^{-0.32}. \] \[ P\approx14522.98. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$14{,}522.98\text{ approximately}}. \]

Solving Continuous Compounding for Time

Start with

\[ A=Pe^{rt}. \]

Divide:

\[ \frac{A}{P}=e^{rt}. \]

Take natural logarithms:

\[ \ln\left(\frac{A}{P}\right)=rt. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ t= \frac{ \ln(A/P) }{r} }. \]

Solving Continuous Compounding for Rate

From

\[ \ln\left(\frac{A}{P}\right)=rt, \]

solve for \(r\):

\[ \boxed{ r= \frac{ \ln(A/P) }{t} }. \]

Doubling Time under Continuous Compounding

If an amount doubles,

\[ A=2P. \]

Using

\[ 2P=Pe^{rt}, \]

cancel \(P\):

\[ 2=e^{rt}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ t=\frac{\ln2}{r} }. \]

Example: Continuous Doubling Time

Find the doubling time at \(7\%\) continuous compounding.

\[ t= \frac{\ln2}{0.07}. \] \[ t\approx9.90. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{9.90\text{ years approximately}}. \]

Growth Factor View

Compound-interest models are easiest to understand through growth factors.

For periodic compounding, the growth factor per period is

\[ \boxed{ 1+\frac{r}{n} }. \]

After \(nt\) periods, that factor has been applied \(nt\) times.

Interest Rate versus Growth Factor

If the periodic rate is \(2\%\), the growth factor is

\[ 1+0.02=1.02. \]

A growth factor of \(1.02\) means the new balance is \(102\%\) of the previous balance after each period.

Compounding Frequency and Periodic Rate

A nominal annual rate of \(12\%\) compounded monthly does not mean \(12\%\) is applied every month.

The monthly periodic rate is

\[ \frac{0.12}{12}=0.01. \]

Therefore, the monthly growth factor is

\[ \boxed{1.01}. \]

Number of Periods

A nominal annual rate compounded monthly for \(3\) years uses

\[ 12(3)=36 \]

compounding periods.

The exponent must count periods, not years alone.

Example: Monthly Compounding

Find the value of \(\$6{,}000\) at \(4.8\%\) compounded monthly for \(5\) years.

Here,

\[ \frac{r}{n} = \frac{0.048}{12} = 0.004, \]

and

\[ nt=12(5)=60. \]

Therefore,

\[ A=6000(1.004)^{60}. \] \[ A\approx7623.84. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\$7{,}623.84\text{ approximately}}. \]

Comparing Compounding Frequencies

Suppose \(\$1{,}000\) earns a nominal \(8\%\) for one year.

Annual compounding:

\[ 1000(1.08)=1080. \]

Quarterly compounding:

\[ 1000\left(1+\frac{0.08}{4}\right)^4. \]

Monthly compounding:

\[ 1000\left(1+\frac{0.08}{12}\right)^{12}. \]

Continuous compounding:

\[ 1000e^{0.08}. \]

More frequent compounding increases the amount, but the increments become progressively smaller.

Simple Interest as a Rate Problem

Simple interest can be viewed as a constant-dollar rate problem.

The annual dollar interest rate is

\[ Pr. \]

Therefore,

\[ I=(Pr)t. \]

This is structurally similar to other rate-times-time models studied earlier.

Compound Interest as a Recursive Process

If \(A_k\) is the balance after period \(k\), then

\[ A_{k+1}=A_k(1+i). \]

Starting with

\[ A_0=P, \]

repeated substitution gives

\[ A_k=P(1+i)^k. \]

Compound interest is therefore both an exponential model and a recurrence.

Checking Simple Interest

Under simple interest, equal time intervals should produce equal dollar interest amounts.

If a \(\$2{,}000\) principal earns \(5\%\) simple interest, each year adds

\[ 2000(0.05)=100. \]

After \(3\) years, interest should be

\[ 3(100)=300. \]

Checking Compound Interest

Under compound interest at a positive rate, the dollar amount of interest added each period generally increases because the balance grows.

A result showing the same dollar interest every period would describe simple interest rather than compound interest.

Checking with Magnitude

At a positive interest rate and positive time,

\[ A>P. \]

If a compound-interest calculation produces an accumulated value smaller than the principal without withdrawals or negative rates, recheck the setup.

Common Error: Using Percent Instead of Decimal

A rate of \(6\%\) should be entered as

\[ 0.06, \]

not

\[ 6. \]

Common Error: Confusing Interest with Final Amount

In simple interest,

\[ I=Prt \]

gives only the interest.

The final amount is

\[ \boxed{A=P+I}. \]

Common Error: Forgetting to Divide the Rate by \(n\)

A nominal annual rate compounded monthly must be divided by \(12\) before being applied each month.

The periodic rate is

\[ \boxed{\frac{r}{12}}. \]

Common Error: Forgetting to Multiply Time by \(n\)

Monthly compounding for \(5\) years means

\[ 12(5)=60 \]

periods.

The exponent is \(60\), not \(5\).

Common Error: Applying Simple Interest to a Compound Problem

The formulas

\[ A=P(1+rt) \]

and

\[ A= P \left( 1+\frac{r}{n} \right)^{nt} \]

describe fundamentally different growth rules.

Read the problem carefully for words such as “simple,” “compounded monthly,” or “compounded continuously.”

Common Error: Treating Nominal Rate as Effective Rate

A stated nominal rate of \(6\%\) compounded monthly produces an effective annual rate slightly greater than \(6\%\).

Do not treat those two percentages as interchangeable.

Common Error: Rounding Too Early

Compound-interest calculations may involve many repeated multiplications.

Rounding intermediate values too aggressively can noticeably change the final result.

Keep extra calculator digits until the final monetary answer.

Common Error: Ignoring Time Units

An annual rate paired with \(18\) months should use

\[ t=1.5\text{ years} \]

unless the formula has been rewritten in monthly terms.

A Reliable Procedure for Simple Interest

  1. Identify principal \(P\).
  2. Convert the annual rate to decimal form.
  3. Convert time to years if needed.
  4. Use \(I=Prt\).
  5. If final amount is requested, compute \(A=P+I\).
  6. Check units and reasonableness.

A Reliable Procedure for Periodic Compound Interest

  1. Identify \(P\), \(r\), \(n\), and \(t\).
  2. Convert \(r\) to decimal form.
  3. Compute the periodic rate \(r/n\).
  4. Compute the number of periods \(nt\).
  5. Use \(A=P(1+r/n)^{nt}\).
  6. Subtract principal if interest earned is requested.
  7. Round only at the end.

A Reliable Procedure for Unknowns in Compound Interest

  1. Write the correct compound-growth equation.
  2. Isolate the unknown algebraically when possible.
  3. Use roots when solving for a growth rate.
  4. Use logarithms when the unknown appears in an exponent.
  5. Check that the resulting rate or time is positive and contextually reasonable.
  6. Substitute back into the original model.

A Reliable Procedure for Continuous Compounding

  1. Use \(A=Pe^{rt}\).
  2. Convert the annual rate to decimal form.
  3. Use time in years.
  4. Apply logarithms when solving for \(r\) or \(t\).
  5. Check the result against the starting principal and expected growth direction.

What to Remember

Simple interest is

\[ \boxed{I=Prt}. \]

Its accumulated amount is

\[ \boxed{A=P(1+rt)}. \]

Periodic compound interest is

\[ \boxed{ A= P \left( 1+\frac{r}{n} \right)^{nt} }. \]

Continuous compounding is

\[ \boxed{A=Pe^{rt}}. \]

Simple interest is linear in time; compound interest is exponential.

The most important habit is to identify the interest model and compounding frequency before choosing the formula.
