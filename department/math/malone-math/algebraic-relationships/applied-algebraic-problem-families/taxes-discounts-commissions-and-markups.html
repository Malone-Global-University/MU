Taxes, Discounts, Commissions, and Markups

Many everyday financial problems are percentage transformations. A tax adds a percentage of a base price, a discount subtracts a percentage of the original price, a commission pays a percentage of sales, and a markup raises cost by a chosen percentage. The arithmetic is usually simple once the correct reference amount is identified. Most errors come from using the wrong base, adding percentages that should be multiplied, or confusing markup with profit margin.

The Central Idea

A percentage adjustment starts from a reference amount.

If the original amount is \(A\) and the rate is \(r\), then the percentage amount is

\[ \boxed{rA}. \]

An increase by rate \(r\) produces

\[ \boxed{A(1+r)}, \]

while a decrease by rate \(r\) produces

\[ \boxed{A(1-r)}. \]

Percentages Must Be Written as Decimals

Convert the stated percentage before substitution:

\[ 8\%=0.08, \] \[ 15\%=0.15, \]

and

\[ 2.5\%=0.025. \]

Sales Tax

If an item has pre-tax price \(P\) and tax rate \(t\), then the tax amount is

\[ \boxed{T=tP}. \]

The total price is

\[ P+T. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P_{\text{total}}=P(1+t) }. \]

Example: Find Sales Tax

An item costs \(\$80\) before tax. The tax rate is \(7.5\%\).

\[ T=0.075(80). \] \[ T=6. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$6\text{ tax}}. \]

Example: Find the Total after Tax

Continue the previous example.

\[ P_{\text{total}}=80(1.075). \] \[ P_{\text{total}}=86. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$86}. \]

Reverse Tax Problems

Sometimes the after-tax price is known and the pre-tax price is unknown.

If

\[ A=P(1+t), \]

then

\[ \boxed{ P=\frac{A}{1+t} }. \]

Example: Recover the Pre-Tax Price

An after-tax total is \(\$107\) with a \(7\%\) tax rate.

\[ P=\frac{107}{1.07}. \] \[ P=100. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$100\text{ before tax}}. \]

Discounts

If an item has original price \(P\) and discount rate \(d\), then the discount amount is

\[ \boxed{D=dP}. \]

The sale price is

\[ P-D. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P_{\text{sale}}=P(1-d) }. \]

Example: Find a Discount

A \(\$120\) item is discounted by \(25\%\).

\[ D=0.25(120)=30. \]

Therefore, the discount is

\[ \boxed{\$30}. \]

Example: Find the Sale Price

Using the multiplier directly,

\[ P_{\text{sale}}=120(0.75). \] \[ P_{\text{sale}}=90. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$90}. \]

Reverse Discount Problems

If a sale price \(S\) results from discount rate \(d\), then

\[ S=P(1-d). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P=\frac{S}{1-d} }. \]

Example: Recover the Original Price

After a \(20\%\) discount, an item costs \(\$64\).

\[ 64=P(0.80). \] \[ P=\frac{64}{0.80}. \] \[ P=80. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$80\text{ original price}}. \]

Discount Followed by Tax

If a discount is applied first and tax is then applied to the discounted price, the multipliers are applied in sequence.

With original price \(P\), discount rate \(d\), and tax rate \(t\),

\[ \boxed{ P_{\text{final}} = P(1-d)(1+t) }. \]

Example: Discount Then Tax

An item costs \(\$200\), is discounted \(15\%\), and then taxed at \(8\%\).

Discount multiplier:

\[ 0.85. \]

Tax multiplier:

\[ 1.08. \]

Therefore,

\[ P_{\text{final}}=200(0.85)(1.08). \] \[ P_{\text{final}}=183.60. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\$183.60}. \]

Successive Discounts

Two discounts are applied multiplicatively, not additively.

If discounts \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) are applied in sequence,

\[ \boxed{ P_{\text{final}} = P(1-d_1)(1-d_2) }. \]

Example: Two Discounts

A product receives a \(20\%\) discount followed by another \(10\%\) discount.

The final multiplier is

\[ 0.80(0.90)=0.72. \]

Therefore, the final price is

\[ \boxed{72\%\text{ of the original}}. \]

The equivalent single discount is

\[ \boxed{28\%}, \]

not \(30\%\).

Equivalent Single Discount

If two discounts \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) are applied,

\[ 1-d_{\text{eq}} = (1-d_1)(1-d_2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ d_{\text{eq}} = 1-(1-d_1)(1-d_2) }. \]

Successive Increases

Repeated increases also multiply.

If an amount increases by \(r_1\) and then by \(r_2\),

\[ \boxed{ A_{\text{final}} = A(1+r_1)(1+r_2) }. \]

Equal Increase and Decrease Do Not Cancel

Suppose a price rises by \(20\%\) and then falls by \(20\%\).

The combined multiplier is

\[ 1.20(0.80)=0.96. \]

Therefore, the final price is

\[ \boxed{96\%\text{ of the original}}. \]

Commission

A commission is often calculated as a percentage of sales.

If sales are \(S\) and the commission rate is \(c\), then commission is

\[ \boxed{C=cS}. \]

Example: Straight Commission

A salesperson earns \(6\%\) commission on \(\$18{,}000\) in sales.

\[ C=0.06(18000). \] \[ C=1080. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$1{,}080}. \]

Base Pay plus Commission

Some compensation models combine fixed pay \(B\) with commission.

If sales are \(S\) and commission rate is \(c\),

\[ \boxed{ E=B+cS }. \]

Example: Base plus Commission

A salesperson earns \(\$500\) base pay plus \(4\%\) of sales. If sales are \(\$20{,}000\),

\[ E=500+0.04(20000). \] \[ E=500+800. \] \[ E=1300. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$1{,}300}. \]

Find Required Sales for a Target Commission

If

\[ C=cS, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ S=\frac{C}{c} }. \]

Example: Required Sales

A salesperson earns \(5\%\) commission and wants \(\$2{,}000\) in commission.

\[ S=\frac{2000}{0.05}. \] \[ S=40000. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$40{,}000\text{ in sales}}. \]

Target Earnings with Base plus Commission

If

\[ E=B+cS, \]

and target earnings are \(E_t\), then

\[ E_t=B+cS. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ S=\frac{E_t-B}{c} }. \]

Example: Required Sales for Target Earnings

A salesperson earns \(\$600\) base pay plus \(3\%\) commission. How much must be sold to earn \(\$1{,}500\)?

\[ 1500=600+0.03S. \] \[ 900=0.03S. \] \[ S=30000. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$30{,}000\text{ in sales}}. \]

Tiered Commission

Some commission plans change rates after a threshold.

For example:

  • \(4\%\) on the first \(\$10{,}000\) of sales;
  • \(6\%\) on sales above \(\$10{,}000\).

Such models are piecewise rather than one single linear formula.

Example: Tiered Commission

Suppose sales are \(\$16{,}000\).

First tier:

\[ 0.04(10000)=400. \]

Remaining sales:

\[ 16000-10000=6000. \]

Second-tier commission:

\[ 0.06(6000)=360. \]

Total commission:

\[ 400+360=760. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$760}. \]

Markup

Markup is commonly defined as an amount added to cost.

If cost is \(C\) and markup rate on cost is \(m\), then markup amount is

\[ \boxed{M=mC}. \]

Selling price is

\[ C+M. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ S=C(1+m) }. \]

Example: Markup on Cost

An item costs a retailer \(\$80\). It is marked up \(35\%\) on cost.

\[ S=80(1.35). \] \[ S=108. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$108}. \]

Reverse Markup

If selling price \(S\) and markup rate \(m\) on cost are known,

\[ S=C(1+m). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ C=\frac{S}{1+m} }. \]

Example: Recover Cost from Markup

A product sells for \(\$156\) after a \(30\%\) markup on cost.

\[ C=\frac{156}{1.30}. \] \[ C=120. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$120\text{ cost}}. \]

Markup Rate

If cost is \(C\) and selling price is \(S\), then markup amount is

\[ S-C. \]

Markup rate on cost is

\[ \boxed{ m=\frac{S-C}{C} }. \]

Example: Find Markup Rate

An item costs \(\$75\) and sells for \(\$96\).

Markup:

\[ 96-75=21. \]

Markup rate:

\[ \frac{21}{75}=0.28. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{28\%\text{ markup on cost}}. \]

Markup versus Profit Margin

Markup rate and profit margin use different reference amounts.

Markup on cost:

\[ \boxed{ \text{markup rate} = \frac{S-C}{C} }. \]

Gross margin as a fraction of selling price:

\[ \boxed{ \text{margin rate} = \frac{S-C}{S} }. \]

They are not generally equal.

Example: Markup versus Margin

An item costs \(\$80\) and sells for \(\$100\).

Difference:

\[ 100-80=20. \]

Markup on cost:

\[ \frac{20}{80}=0.25. \]

Therefore, markup is

\[ \boxed{25\%}. \]

Margin on selling price:

\[ \frac{20}{100}=0.20. \]

Therefore, margin is

\[ \boxed{20\%}. \]

Convert Markup Rate to Margin Rate

If selling price is

\[ S=C(1+m), \]

then gross profit is

\[ S-C=mC. \]

Margin rate is

\[ \frac{mC}{C(1+m)}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{margin} = \frac{m}{1+m} }. \]

Convert Margin Rate to Markup Rate

If margin rate is \(g\), then

\[ g=\frac{S-C}{S}. \]

The corresponding markup rate on cost is

\[ \boxed{ m=\frac{g}{1-g} }. \]

Example: Margin to Markup

A seller wants a \(25\%\) gross margin on selling price.

Then

\[ m= \frac{0.25}{1-0.25}. \] \[ m= \frac{0.25}{0.75} = \frac13. \]

Therefore, the equivalent markup on cost is

\[ \boxed{33\frac13\%}. \]

Markdown after Markup

A retailer may first mark up cost and later discount the listed price.

If cost is \(C\), markup rate is \(m\), and later discount rate is \(d\), then

\[ \boxed{ S_{\text{final}} = C(1+m)(1-d) }. \]

Example: Markup Then Discount

An item costs \(\$100\), is marked up \(50\%\), then discounted \(20\%\).

\[ S=100(1.50)(0.80). \] \[ S=120. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$120}. \]

Even after the discount, the selling price remains above cost.

Net Percent Change from Multipliers

If the final multiplier is \(M\), then the net percent change relative to the original is

\[ \boxed{M-1}. \]

If

\[ M>1, \]

the result is a net increase.

If

\[ M<1, \]

the result is a net decrease.

Example: Net Change after Markup and Discount

A \(40\%\) markup followed by a \(25\%\) discount gives multiplier

\[ 1.40(0.75)=1.05. \]

Therefore, the final price is

\[ \boxed{5\%\text{ above cost}}. \]

Reverse Successive-Change Problems

If the final amount \(F\) is known after several percentage adjustments, divide by the combined multiplier.

For discount \(d\) followed by tax \(t\),

\[ F=P(1-d)(1+t). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P= \frac{F}{ (1-d)(1+t) } }. \]

Example: Reverse Discount and Tax

After a \(20\%\) discount and \(5\%\) tax, an item costs \(\$84\). Find the original price.

\[ 84=P(0.80)(1.05). \] \[ 84=0.84P. \] \[ P=100. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$100}. \]

Reference Amount Matters

Percentages are always percentages of something.

Tax is usually a percentage of taxable price.

Discount is usually a percentage of the current listed price.

Commission is usually a percentage of sales.

Markup is often a percentage of cost.

Margin is a percentage of selling price.

Percent of Cost versus Percent of Selling Price

Suppose profit amount is

\[ S-C. \]

Dividing by cost answers:

“What percent of cost is the profit?”

Dividing by selling price answers:

“What percent of selling price is the profit?”

Those are different questions.

Percentage Points versus Percent Change

Suppose a commission rate rises from \(4\%\) to \(5\%\).

The increase is

\[ 1 \]

percentage point.

Relative to the original \(4\%\), the percent increase in the rate is

\[ \frac{5-4}{4}=0.25. \]

Therefore, the commission rate increased by

\[ \boxed{25\%} \]

relative to its original level.

Combining Flat Amounts with Percentages

Some problems combine percentage adjustments with fixed-dollar amounts.

For example, a bill may include a base fee \(F\) plus a percentage charge \(rA\):

\[ \boxed{ T=F+rA }. \]

Do not force every applied problem into a pure multiplier model.

Example: Flat Fee plus Percentage

A service charges \(\$25\) plus \(3\%\) of transaction value.

For a \(\$2{,}000\) transaction,

\[ T=25+0.03(2000). \] \[ T=25+60. \] \[ T=85. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\$85}. \]

Piecewise Percentage Rules

Some tax, commission, fee, or discount systems use different rates on different ranges.

Such systems are modeled piecewise.

The amount in each tier is calculated separately, then the pieces are added.

This is the same interval logic used in staggered work-rate problems.

Checking a Tax Result

A positive tax should make the after-tax price greater than the pre-tax price:

\[ P(1+t)>P \]

when

\[ t>0. \]

Checking a Discount Result

A positive discount less than \(100\%\) should satisfy

\[ 0<P(1-d)<P. \]

Checking a Commission Result

For straight commission with

\[ 0<c<1, \]

commission should be less than total sales:

\[ 0<cS<S. \]

Checking a Markup Result

With positive markup on cost,

\[ S=C(1+m) \]

should satisfy

\[ S>C. \]

Common Error: Using the Percent as a Whole Number

A \(7\%\) tax rate is

\[ 0.07, \]

not

\[ 7. \]

Common Error: Adding Successive Percentages

A \(20\%\) discount followed by a \(10\%\) discount does not usually equal a \(30\%\) discount.

Use multipliers:

\[ 0.80(0.90)=0.72. \]

The equivalent discount is

\[ \boxed{28\%}. \]

Common Error: Subtracting Tax after a Discount

Tax is an increase, so its multiplier is

\[ 1+t. \]

A discount uses

\[ 1-d. \]

Keep the signs conceptually tied to whether the adjustment raises or lowers the current amount.

Common Error: Using the Final Amount as the Original Base

If an item is discounted \(20\%\), the discount is \(20\%\) of the pre-discount price, not \(20\%\) of the sale price.

Reverse problems therefore require division by the multiplier.

Common Error: Confusing Markup with Margin

Markup uses cost in the denominator:

\[ \frac{S-C}{C}. \]

Margin uses selling price:

\[ \frac{S-C}{S}. \]

Always identify the stated reference amount.

Common Error: Applying a Tier Rate to the Entire Amount

In a tiered commission model, a higher rate may apply only to the amount above a threshold.

Do not apply the higher rate retroactively to the entire sales total unless the plan explicitly says so.

Common Error: Ignoring Order

When flat fees and percentage changes are mixed, order can matter.

For example,

\[ (P+F)(1+t) \]

differs from

\[ P(1+t)+F. \]

Translate the stated sequence carefully.

A Reliable Procedure for Taxes and Discounts

  1. Identify the reference price.
  2. Convert the percentage to decimal form.
  3. Use \(1+r\) for an increase.
  4. Use \(1-r\) for a decrease.
  5. Apply successive adjustments in the stated order.
  6. For reverse problems, divide by the multiplier.
  7. Check that the result moves in the expected direction.

A Reliable Procedure for Commission Problems

  1. Identify the sales amount.
  2. Identify the commission rate.
  3. Write \(C=cS\) for straight commission.
  4. Add base pay if the plan includes it.
  5. Split tiered plans into separate ranges.
  6. For target earnings, solve the resulting equation for sales.

A Reliable Procedure for Markup Problems

  1. Identify cost \(C\).
  2. Determine whether the stated percent is markup on cost or margin on selling price.
  3. For markup on cost, use \(S=C(1+m)\).
  4. For reverse markup, divide by \(1+m\).
  5. For margin, use \((S-C)/S\).
  6. Check that the selling price is consistent with the stated percentage basis.

What to Remember

An increase by rate \(r\) uses multiplier

\[ \boxed{1+r}. \]

A decrease by rate \(r\) uses multiplier

\[ \boxed{1-r}. \]

Sales tax:

\[ \boxed{ P_{\text{total}}=P(1+t) }. \]

Discount:

\[ \boxed{ P_{\text{sale}}=P(1-d) }. \]

Straight commission:

\[ \boxed{C=cS}. \]

Markup on cost:

\[ \boxed{ S=C(1+m) }. \]

The most important habit is to identify the correct reference amount before calculating a percentage. Successive percentage changes should usually be handled with multipliers rather than by adding or subtracting the rates.
