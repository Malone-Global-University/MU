Supply, Demand, and Market Equilibrium

Supply-and-demand problems are algebraic intersection problems. A demand relationship describes how much buyers are willing to purchase at different prices, while a supply relationship describes how much sellers are willing to offer. Market equilibrium occurs where the two relationships agree. Algebra lets us find the equilibrium price and quantity, evaluate shortages and surpluses at other prices, and study how changes in the equations move the equilibrium.

The Central Idea

Let \(p\) represent price and \(q\) represent quantity.

A demand equation gives a relationship between price and quantity demanded.

A supply equation gives a relationship between price and quantity supplied.

Market equilibrium occurs when

\[ \boxed{q_d=q_s}. \]

Equivalently, if both equations express price as a function of quantity, equilibrium occurs when

\[ \boxed{p_d(q)=p_s(q)}. \]

Quantity Demanded

Quantity demanded is the amount buyers are willing to purchase at a particular price under the assumptions of the model.

A common linear form is

\[ \boxed{q_d=a-bp}, \]

where

\[ b>0. \]

As price rises, the modeled quantity demanded falls.

Quantity Supplied

Quantity supplied is the amount sellers are willing to offer at a particular price under the assumptions of the model.

A common linear form is

\[ \boxed{q_s=c+dp}, \]

where

\[ d>0. \]

As price rises, the modeled quantity supplied rises.

Example: Evaluate Demand

Suppose demand is

\[ q_d=500-10p. \]

At price

\[ p=20, \]

quantity demanded is

\[ q_d=500-10(20). \] \[ q_d=300. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{300\text{ units}}. \]

Example: Evaluate Supply

Suppose supply is

\[ q_s=100+5p. \]

At price

\[ p=20, \]

quantity supplied is

\[ q_s=100+5(20). \] \[ q_s=200. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{200\text{ units}}. \]

Equilibrium as Equality

If

\[ q_d=500-10p \]

and

\[ q_s=100+5p, \]

equilibrium requires

\[ 500-10p=100+5p. \]

Example: Solve for Equilibrium Price

Continue with

\[ 500-10p=100+5p. \]

Subtract \(100\):

\[ 400-10p=5p. \]

Add \(10p\):

\[ 400=15p. \] \[ p=\frac{400}{15}=\frac{80}{3}. \]

Therefore, the equilibrium price is

\[ \boxed{\$26.67\text{ approximately}}. \]

Find the Equilibrium Quantity

Substitute

\[ p=\frac{80}{3} \]

into either equation.

Using demand:

\[ q=500-10\left(\frac{80}{3}\right). \] \[ q= \frac{1500}{3}-\frac{800}{3} = \frac{700}{3}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{233.33\text{ units approximately}}. \]

Check the Equilibrium

Using supply:

\[ q_s = 100+5\left(\frac{80}{3}\right). \] \[ q_s= \frac{300}{3}+\frac{400}{3} = \frac{700}{3}. \]

Demand and supply agree:

\[ \boxed{ q_d=q_s=\frac{700}{3} }. \]

Equilibrium as an Intersection

Graphically, equilibrium is the intersection of the supply and demand relationships.

Algebraically, an intersection is found by setting the two expressions equal and solving.

This is the same general method used to find break-even points, crossing times, and intersections of function graphs.

Inverse Demand

A demand equation may instead express price as a function of quantity:

\[ \boxed{p_d=a-bq}, \]

with

\[ b>0. \]

This form is often called an inverse demand relationship.

Inverse Supply

Supply may also be written as price as a function of quantity:

\[ \boxed{p_s=c+dq}, \]

with

\[ d>0. \]

Example: Equilibrium from Inverse Equations

Suppose

\[ p_d=80-0.2q \]

and

\[ p_s=20+0.1q. \]

Set them equal:

\[ 80-0.2q=20+0.1q. \] \[ 60=0.3q. \] \[ q=200. \]

Substitute:

\[ p=80-0.2(200)=40. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{q^*=200,\qquad p^*=\$40}. \]

Notation for Equilibrium

Equilibrium values are often marked with a superscript star:

\[ \boxed{p^*} \]

for equilibrium price and

\[ \boxed{q^*} \]

for equilibrium quantity.

Shortage

A shortage occurs in the model when quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied:

\[ \boxed{q_d>q_s}. \]

The amount of shortage is

\[ \boxed{q_d-q_s}. \]

Example: Calculate a Shortage

Let

\[ q_d=500-10p \]

and

\[ q_s=100+5p. \]

At

\[ p=20, \]

we found

\[ q_d=300 \]

and

\[ q_s=200. \]

Therefore, shortage is

\[ 300-200=100. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{100\text{ units}}. \]

Surplus

A surplus occurs in the model when quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded:

\[ \boxed{q_s>q_d}. \]

The amount of surplus is

\[ \boxed{q_s-q_d}. \]

Example: Calculate a Surplus

Using

\[ q_d=500-10p \]

and

\[ q_s=100+5p, \]

let

\[ p=40. \]

Demand:

\[ q_d=500-10(40)=100. \]

Supply:

\[ q_s=100+5(40)=300. \]

Therefore, surplus is

\[ 300-100=200. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{200\text{ units}}. \]

Below Equilibrium Price

In the standard linear model, a price below equilibrium generally produces

\[ q_d>q_s. \]

Therefore, the model indicates a shortage.

Above Equilibrium Price

In the standard linear model, a price above equilibrium generally produces

\[ q_s>q_d. \]

Therefore, the model indicates a surplus.

A Shortage Function

Given demand and supply as functions of price, define

\[ D(p)=q_d(p)-q_s(p). \]

Then:

  • \(D(p)>0\) indicates shortage;
  • \(D(p)=0\) indicates equilibrium;
  • \(D(p)<0\) indicates surplus.

Equilibrium is therefore another zero-finding problem.

Example: Build the Excess-Demand Function

If

\[ q_d=500-10p \]

and

\[ q_s=100+5p, \]

then

\[ D(p) = (500-10p)-(100+5p). \] \[ \boxed{D(p)=400-15p}. \]

Equilibrium occurs where

\[ D(p)=0. \]

Constructing Demand from Two Points

Suppose demand is linear and two price-quantity pairs are known.

For points

\[ (p_1,q_1) \]

and

\[ (p_2,q_2), \]

the slope of quantity with respect to price is

\[ \boxed{ m_d= \frac{q_2-q_1}{p_2-p_1} }. \]

A downward-sloping demand relationship has

\[ m_d<0. \]

Example: Build a Demand Equation from Data

At price \(\$10\), quantity demanded is \(400\). At price \(\$30\), quantity demanded is \(200\).

Slope:

\[ m= \frac{200-400}{30-10} = \frac{-200}{20} = -10. \]

Use

\[ q=-10p+b. \]

Substitute

\[ (p,q)=(10,400): \] \[ 400=-100+b. \] \[ b=500. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{q_d=500-10p}. \]

Constructing Supply from Two Points

For two supply observations,

\[ (p_1,q_1) \]

and

\[ (p_2,q_2), \]

use the same slope formula:

\[ \boxed{ m_s= \frac{q_2-q_1}{p_2-p_1} }. \]

In a standard upward-sloping supply model,

\[ m_s>0. \]

Example: Build a Supply Equation from Data

At price \(\$10\), quantity supplied is \(150\). At price \(\$30\), quantity supplied is \(250\).

Slope:

\[ m= \frac{250-150}{30-10} = \frac{100}{20} = 5. \]

Write

\[ q=5p+b. \]

Substitute

\[ (10,150): \] \[ 150=50+b. \] \[ b=100. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{q_s=100+5p}. \]

Finding Equilibrium from Data-Built Models

Once the demand and supply equations have been constructed,

\[ q_d=500-10p \]

and

\[ q_s=100+5p, \]

solve

\[ q_d=q_s. \]

The equilibrium follows from the same intersection method already developed.

Demand Intercepts

For a demand equation

\[ q_d=a-bp, \]

setting

\[ p=0 \]

gives

\[ q_d=a. \]

This is the quantity intercept of the linear model.

The Price Intercept of Demand

Set

\[ q_d=0. \]

Then

\[ 0=a-bp. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ p=\frac{a}{b} }. \]

This is the modeled price at which quantity demanded reaches zero.

Supply Intercepts

In

\[ q_s=c+dp, \]

setting \(p=0\) gives

\[ q_s=c. \]

Depending on the application, a negative intercept may be an algebraic artifact outside the economically meaningful domain rather than a literal negative quantity.

Domain Restrictions

Market models usually require

\[ p\ge0 \]

and

\[ q\ge0. \]

A linear formula may produce negative values outside its intended range.

Such values should not automatically be interpreted as meaningful market quantities.

Example: Restrict a Demand Model

Suppose

\[ q_d=500-10p. \]

Nonnegative quantity requires

\[ 500-10p\ge0. \] \[ p\le50. \]

Therefore, a natural nonnegative domain is

\[ \boxed{0\le p\le50}. \]

Movement along a Demand Relationship

If the demand equation itself remains unchanged but price changes, the model moves to a different point on the same demand relationship.

Algebraically, the coefficients stay fixed while a new value of \(p\) is substituted.

Movement along a Supply Relationship

Likewise, if the supply equation remains unchanged and price changes, a new quantity supplied is found by substituting the new price into the same supply equation.

Shifts in Demand

A demand shift changes the demand equation itself.

For example,

\[ q_d=500-10p \]

might become

\[ q_d=560-10p. \]

At every given price, modeled quantity demanded is now \(60\) units higher.

Shifts in Supply

A supply shift changes the supply equation itself.

For example,

\[ q_s=100+5p \]

might become

\[ q_s=160+5p. \]

At every given price, modeled quantity supplied is now \(60\) units higher.

Equilibrium after a Demand Shift

Suppose supply remains

\[ q_s=100+5p, \]

but demand changes to

\[ q_d=560-10p. \]

Set equal:

\[ 560-10p=100+5p. \] \[ 460=15p. \] \[ p=\frac{92}{3}\approx30.67. \]

Equilibrium quantity is

\[ q=100+5\left(\frac{92}{3}\right). \] \[ q=\frac{760}{3}\approx253.33. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ p^*\approx\$30.67,\qquad q^*\approx253.33 }. \]

Compare the Two Equilibria

Before the demand shift,

\[ p^*\approx26.67, \qquad q^*\approx233.33. \]

After the increase in demand,

\[ p^*\approx30.67, \qquad q^*\approx253.33. \]

In this particular linear model, the demand increase raises both equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity.

Equilibrium after a Supply Shift

Suppose demand remains

\[ q_d=500-10p, \]

while supply increases to

\[ q_s=160+5p. \]

Solve:

\[ 500-10p=160+5p. \] \[ 340=15p. \] \[ p=\frac{68}{3}\approx22.67. \]

Then

\[ q=160+5\left(\frac{68}{3}\right) = \frac{820}{3} \approx273.33. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ p^*\approx\$22.67,\qquad q^*\approx273.33 }. \]

Compare a Supply Shift

Relative to the original equilibrium,

\[ p^*\approx26.67, \qquad q^*\approx233.33, \]

the increased-supply model produces

\[ p^*\approx22.67, \qquad q^*\approx273.33. \]

In this example, increased supply lowers equilibrium price and raises equilibrium quantity.

Parameterizing a Demand Shift

A shift can be represented by a parameter.

For example,

\[ \boxed{ q_d=(a+k)-bp }. \]

The parameter \(k\) measures an additive shift in quantity demanded at every price.

Parameterizing a Supply Shift

Similarly,

\[ \boxed{ q_s=(c+h)+dp }. \]

The parameter \(h\) measures an additive shift in quantity supplied at every price.

General Linear Equilibrium Formula

Let

\[ q_d=a-bp \]

and

\[ q_s=c+dp, \]

where

\[ b>0,\qquad d>0. \]

At equilibrium,

\[ a-bp=c+dp. \]

Therefore,

\[ a-c=(b+d)p. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ p^*=\frac{a-c}{b+d} }. \]

General Equilibrium Quantity

Substitute

\[ p^*=\frac{a-c}{b+d} \]

into demand:

\[ q^* = a- b \left( \frac{a-c}{b+d} \right). \]

Simplifying gives

\[ \boxed{ q^*= \frac{ad+bc}{b+d} }. \]

What the General Formula Shows

In

\[ p^*=\frac{a-c}{b+d}, \]

equilibrium price depends on both intercept information and both slopes.

A larger demand intercept \(a\) tends to raise equilibrium price, while a larger supply intercept \(c\) tends to lower it, holding the other parameters fixed.

Unknown Demand Parameter

Suppose demand is

\[ q_d=a-8p \]

and supply is

\[ q_s=40+4p. \]

If equilibrium price is known to be

\[ p^*=20, \]

then

\[ a-8(20)=40+4(20). \] \[ a-160=120. \] \[ a=280. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a=280}. \]

Unknown Supply Parameter

Suppose demand is

\[ q_d=600-12p \]

and supply is

\[ q_s=c+6p. \]

If equilibrium occurs at price

\[ p=25, \]

then

\[ 600-12(25)=c+6(25). \] \[ 300=c+150. \] \[ c=150. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{c=150}. \]

Unknown Slope from Equilibrium Information

Suppose

\[ q_d=500-10p \]

and

\[ q_s=100+dp. \]

If equilibrium price is

\[ 25, \]

then

\[ 500-10(25)=100+25d. \] \[ 250=100+25d. \] \[ 150=25d. \] \[ d=6. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{d=6}. \]

Price as the Independent Variable

In equations such as

\[ q_d=500-10p, \]

price is treated algebraically as the input and quantity as the output.

This is convenient for evaluating quantities at a specified market price.

Quantity as the Independent Variable

In inverse equations such as

\[ p_d=80-0.2q, \]

quantity is the input and price is the output.

Both representations can describe the same linear relationship after algebraic rearrangement.

Converting Demand to Inverse Demand

Suppose

\[ q=500-10p. \]

Solve for \(p\):

\[ 10p=500-q. \] \[ p=50-0.1q. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{p=50-0.1q}. \]

Converting Supply to Inverse Supply

Suppose

\[ q=100+5p. \]

Solve for \(p\):

\[ 5p=q-100. \] \[ p=0.2q-20. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{p=0.2q-20}. \]

Checking the Same Equilibrium in Inverse Form

Set

\[ 50-0.1q=0.2q-20. \] \[ 70=0.3q. \] \[ q=\frac{700}{3}. \]

Then

\[ p=50-0.1\left(\frac{700}{3}\right) = \frac{80}{3}. \]

This matches the equilibrium found from the original quantity equations.

Supply and Demand Tables

A table can help compare the two relationships at several prices.

\[ \begin{array}{c|c|c|c} p & q_d & q_s & \text{market condition}\\ \hline 20 & 300 & 200 & \text{shortage}\\ \frac{80}{3} & \frac{700}{3} & \frac{700}{3} & \text{equilibrium}\\ 40 & 100 & 300 & \text{surplus} \end{array} \]

Market Equilibrium Is a Model Outcome

The algebraic equilibrium is the point where the stated supply and demand equations intersect.

Real markets may include taxes, regulation, inventories, contracts, strategic behavior, changing expectations, and many other effects.

This lesson studies the algebraic structure of the simplified model rather than claiming that every market behaves exactly this way.

Checking Equilibrium by Substitution

After finding \(p^*\), compute both

\[ q_d(p^*) \]

and

\[ q_s(p^*). \]

They should be equal.

If they differ, recheck the algebra.

Checking with Direction

In the standard model, if price is moved below equilibrium while the curves are unchanged, demand should exceed supply.

If price is moved above equilibrium, supply should exceed demand.

This provides a quick qualitative check.

Checking the Domain

A mathematically valid intersection can still lie outside the meaningful domain.

For example, a negative equilibrium price may signal that the chosen linear equations are not appropriate over the stated range.

Applied solutions should satisfy

\[ p^*\ge0 \]

and ordinarily

\[ q^*\ge0. \]

Common Error: Setting Price Equal to Quantity

Equilibrium does not mean

\[ p=q. \]

It means quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, or equivalently the supply and demand price functions agree at the same quantity.

Common Error: Adding Supply and Demand

Equilibrium is found by setting the two relationships equal.

It is not found by adding

\[ q_d+q_s. \]

Common Error: Confusing Shortage with Surplus

Shortage means

\[ q_d>q_s. \]

Surplus means

\[ q_s>q_d. \]

Subtract the smaller quantity from the larger one to report the magnitude.

Common Error: Ignoring Which Variable Is on Which Axis

Some equations write quantity as a function of price, while others write price as a function of quantity.

Before solving, identify what each variable represents and which quantity the equation outputs.

Common Error: Treating a Shift as a Movement along the Same Curve

Changing price within an unchanged equation moves to another point on the same relationship.

Changing an intercept or slope changes the relationship itself.

Common Error: Accepting Negative Quantities

A linear model may produce

\[ q<0 \]

outside its useful range.

Negative physical market quantity is ordinarily not meaningful, so restrict the model's domain.

Common Error: Forgetting Units

A price may be measured in dollars per item, while quantity may be measured in units, thousands of units, or another scale.

Interpret coefficients using the units stated in the problem.

A Reliable Procedure for Market Equilibrium

  1. Identify the demand equation.
  2. Identify the supply equation.
  3. Confirm that both are written using compatible variables.
  4. Set quantity demanded equal to quantity supplied.
  5. Solve for equilibrium price or quantity.
  6. Substitute back to find the other equilibrium value.
  7. Verify that both equations give the same result.
  8. Check that price and quantity lie in the meaningful domain.

A Reliable Procedure for Shortage and Surplus

  1. Substitute the stated price into demand.
  2. Substitute the same price into supply.
  3. Compare the two quantities.
  4. If \(q_d>q_s\), compute shortage \(q_d-q_s\).
  5. If \(q_s>q_d\), compute surplus \(q_s-q_d\).
  6. If they are equal, the price is an equilibrium price.

A Reliable Procedure for Shifts

  1. Write the original supply and demand equations.
  2. Identify which equation changes.
  3. Write the new equation explicitly.
  4. Resolve the equilibrium using the new pair of equations.
  5. Compare old and new \(p^*\) and \(q^*\).
  6. Describe the change only for the model actually given.

What to Remember

A common linear demand model is

\[ \boxed{q_d=a-bp}, \]

while a common linear supply model is

\[ \boxed{q_s=c+dp}. \]

Equilibrium occurs where

\[ \boxed{q_d=q_s}. \]

For the general linear pair,

\[ \boxed{ p^*=\frac{a-c}{b+d} }. \]

A shortage occurs when

\[ q_d>q_s, \]

and a surplus occurs when

\[ q_s>q_d. \]

The most important habit is to treat equilibrium as an intersection problem and to distinguish changes in price along a fixed relationship from changes in the relationship itself.

Where We Go Next

Supply-and-demand models use equations to describe how price and quantity relate. Many everyday financial problems instead adjust a base amount by percentages: taxes are added, discounts are subtracted, commissions are earned, and markups transform cost into selling price.
