Arithmetic and Geometric Sums

Arithmetic and geometric sequences generate two of the most important families of finite series. Arithmetic sums accumulate constant differences, while geometric sums accumulate repeated multiplicative change. Each family has a formula that replaces long addition with a compact calculation.

The Central Idea

Consider the arithmetic series

\[ 4+7+10+13+16. \]

Because the terms have a constant difference, we can use the arithmetic-sum formula

\[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n)}. \]

Here,

\[ n=5,\qquad a_1=4,\qquad a_5=16, \]

so

\[ S_5=\frac52(4+16)=\boxed{50}. \]

Now consider the geometric series

\[ 3+6+12+24+48. \]

Its terms have common ratio \(2\), so we can use the finite geometric-sum formula

\[ \boxed{S_n=a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}}. \]

Therefore,

\[ S_5=3\frac{1-2^5}{1-2}=\boxed{93}. \]

Arithmetic Series

An arithmetic series is the sum of terms from an arithmetic sequence.

If

\[ a_k=a_1+(k-1)d, \]

then the first \(n\) terms form the finite series

\[ \boxed{S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k}. \]

Geometric Series

A geometric series is the sum of terms from a geometric sequence.

If

\[ a_k=a_1r^{k-1}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_1r^{k-1}}. \]

The Arithmetic-Sum Formula

A finite arithmetic series can be summed by

\[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n)}. \]

This says

\[ \boxed{ \text{sum} = \text{number of terms} \times \text{average of first and last terms} }. \]

Why the Arithmetic Formula Works

Write the series forward:

\[ S_n=a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots+a_{n-1}+a_n. \]

Write the same series in reverse:

\[ S_n=a_n+a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}+\cdots+a_2+a_1. \]

Adding the two equations term by term produces \(n\) identical pairs, each equal to \(a_1+a_n\):

\[ 2S_n=n(a_1+a_n). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n)}. \]

The Average-Term View

Arithmetic terms are evenly spaced numerically, so their average is the midpoint of the first and last terms:

\[ \text{average term}=\frac{a_1+a_n}{2}. \]

Multiplying by the number of terms gives

\[ \boxed{S_n=n\left(\frac{a_1+a_n}{2}\right)}. \]

Arithmetic Sum Using the Common Difference

Since

\[ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d, \]

substitute this expression into the arithmetic-sum formula:

\[ S_n=\frac{n}{2}\left[a_1+a_1+(n-1)d\right]. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}\left[2a_1+(n-1)d\right]}. \]

Two Equivalent Arithmetic-Sum Formulas

The standard forms are

\[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n)} \]

and

\[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}\left[2a_1+(n-1)d\right]}. \]

Choose the form that best matches the information given.

Example: Arithmetic Sum from First and Last Terms

Find

\[ 5+9+13+\cdots+41. \]

Here,

\[ a_1=5,\qquad d=4,\qquad a_n=41. \]

First find the number of terms:

\[ 41=5+4(n-1). \] \[ 36=4(n-1), \] \[ n=10. \]

Then

\[ S_{10}=\frac{10}{2}(5+41)=\boxed{230}. \]

Example: Arithmetic Sum from \(a_1\), \(d\), and \(n\)

Suppose

\[ a_1=12,\qquad d=3,\qquad n=20. \]

Then

\[ S_{20} = \frac{20}{2}\left[2(12)+19(3)\right]. \] \[ =10(24+57) \] \[ =\boxed{810}. \]

Sum of the First \(n\) Positive Integers

The sequence

\[ 1,2,3,\ldots,n \]

is arithmetic. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{1+2+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}. \]

Sum of the First \(n\) Odd Positive Integers

The first \(n\) odd positive integers are

\[ 1,3,5,\ldots,2n-1. \]

Use the arithmetic-sum formula:

\[ S_n=\frac{n}{2}[1+(2n-1)]. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2}. \]

Sum of the First \(n\) Even Positive Integers

The first \(n\) even positive integers are

\[ 2,4,6,\ldots,2n. \]

Therefore,

\[ S_n=\frac{n}{2}(2+2n) \] \[ =\boxed{n(n+1)}. \]

Arithmetic Sums in Sigma Notation

If

\[ a_k=a_1+(k-1)d, \]

then

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n}[a_1+(k-1)d] \]

is an arithmetic series. It may be evaluated by expansion, sigma properties, or an arithmetic-sum formula.

Example: Arithmetic Sum from Sigma Notation

Evaluate

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{15}(4k+1). \]

The first and last terms are

\[ a_1=5 \]

and

\[ a_{15}=61. \]

Therefore,

\[ S_{15}=\frac{15}{2}(5+61)=\boxed{495}. \]

The Finite Geometric-Sum Formula

For

\[ a_1+a_1r+a_1r^2+\cdots+a_1r^{n-1}, \]

with \(r\neq1\),

\[ \boxed{S_n=a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}}. \]

An equivalent form is

\[ \boxed{S_n=a_1\frac{r^n-1}{r-1}}. \]

Deriving the Geometric-Sum Formula

Begin with

\[ S_n=a_1+a_1r+a_1r^2+\cdots+a_1r^{n-1}. \]

Multiply by \(r\):

\[ rS_n=a_1r+a_1r^2+\cdots+a_1r^{n-1}+a_1r^n. \]

Subtract:

\[ S_n-rS_n=a_1-a_1r^n. \]

Factor:

\[ S_n(1-r)=a_1(1-r^n). \]

For \(r\neq1\),

\[ \boxed{S_n=a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}}. \]

Why \(r=1\) Is a Special Case

If \(r=1\), every term equals \(a_1\):

\[ a_1+a_1+\cdots+a_1. \]

With \(n\) terms,

\[ \boxed{S_n=na_1}. \]

The standard geometric formula cannot be used directly because its denominator becomes zero.

Choosing a Geometric-Sum Form

The forms

\[ a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r} \]

and

\[ a_1\frac{r^n-1}{r-1} \]

are algebraically equivalent.

Evaluate

\[ 2+6+18+54+162. \]

Here,

\[ a_1=2,\qquad r=3,\qquad n=5. \]

Therefore,

\[ S_5=2\frac{3^5-1}{3-1} \] \[ =2\frac{242}{2} \] \[ =\boxed{242}. \]

Example: Geometric Sum with a Fractional Ratio

Evaluate

\[ 64+32+16+8+4. \]

Here,

\[ a_1=64,\qquad r=\frac12,\qquad n=5. \]

Then

\[ S_5 = 64\frac{1-(1/2)^5}{1-1/2} \] \[ =\boxed{124}. \]

Example: Geometric Sum with a Negative Ratio

Evaluate

\[ 3-6+12-24+48. \]

Here,

\[ a_1=3,\qquad r=-2,\qquad n=5. \]

Therefore,

\[ S_5=3\frac{1-(-2)^5}{1-(-2)} \] \[ =3\frac{33}{3} \] \[ =\boxed{33}. \]

Geometric Sums in Sigma Notation

A geometric series may be written

\[ \boxed{\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_1r^{k-1}} \]

or, after shifting the index,

\[ \boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_1r^k}. \]

Both expressions contain the same terms.

Example: Geometric Sum from Sigma Notation

Evaluate

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{6}5(2)^k. \]

The index values \(0\) through \(6\) give seven terms, with first term \(5\) and ratio \(2\).

\[ S=5\frac{2^7-1}{2-1} \] \[ =5(127) \] \[ =\boxed{635}. \]

Counting Terms Carefully

If an index runs from \(m\) through \(n\) in unit steps, the number of terms is

\[ \boxed{n-m+1}. \]

Thus

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{6}5(2)^k \]

contains

\[ 6-0+1=7 \]

terms, not six.

Finding the Number of Arithmetic Terms

If \(a_1\), \(d\), and \(a_n\) are known, use

\[ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d \]

to solve for \(n\) before using the sum formula.

Finding the Number of Geometric Terms

If \(a_1\), \(r\), and \(a_n\) are known, use

\[ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} \]

to solve for \(n\).

Familiar powers may make the result immediate. Otherwise, logarithms may be needed.

Example: Finding the Number of Geometric Terms

Consider

\[ 3+6+12+\cdots+384. \]

Since

\[ 384=3(2)^{n-1}, \]

we have

\[ 128=2^{n-1}=2^7. \]

Therefore,

\[ n=8. \]

The sum is then

\[ S_8=3\frac{2^8-1}{2-1}=\boxed{765}. \]

Arithmetic Accumulation Produces Quadratic Structure

An arithmetic sequence has a linear explicit formula. Summing its first \(n\) terms introduces a factor of \(n\), so the partial-sum formula is generally quadratic in \(n\).

For example,

\[ 1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2. \]

Accumulating constant first differences therefore produces quadratic partial-sum structure.

Geometric Accumulation Retains Exponential Structure

For a geometric sequence,

\[ a_n=a_1r^{n-1}. \]

Its finite sum contains

\[ r^n, \]

so the accumulated total retains exponential structure:

\[ \boxed{S_n=a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}}. \]

Application: Increasing Weekly Deposits

Suppose a person deposits \(20\) dollars during the first week and increases the weekly deposit by \(5\) dollars each week for \(12\) weeks.

Then

\[ a_1=20,\qquad d=5,\qquad n=12. \]

The final weekly deposit is

\[ a_{12}=20+11(5)=75. \]

The total deposited is

\[ S_{12}=\frac{12}{2}(20+75) \] \[ =\boxed{570}. \]

Application: Repeated Doubling

Suppose a process contributes

\[ 1,2,4,8,\ldots \]

units over ten stages.

Here,

\[ a_1=1,\qquad r=2,\qquad n=10. \]

Therefore,

\[ S_{10}=\frac{2^{10}-1}{2-1}=\boxed{1023}. \]

Application: Rows of Seats

Suppose an auditorium has \(18\) seats in the first row and each successive row has \(2\) more seats. There are \(25\) rows.

The last row has

\[ a_{25}=18+24(2)=66. \]

Total seating is

\[ S_{25}=\frac{25}{2}(18+66) \] \[ =\boxed{1050}. \]

Application: Repeated Percentage Change

Suppose an amount of \(100\) occurs in the first period and each later amount is \(90\%\) of the preceding one.

Then

\[ a_1=100,\qquad r=0.9. \]

The total over eight periods is

\[ \boxed{ S_8=100\frac{1-(0.9)^8}{1-0.9} }. \]

Application: Rebound Distances

Suppose a ball rebounds to \(60\%\) of its previous height. The rebound heights form a geometric sequence.

A total-distance problem may count some rebound heights twice: once upward and once downward. The geometric sequence provides the distances, but the physical interpretation determines how those distances enter the series.

Common Error: Using the Wrong Family

Before choosing a formula, inspect the terms.

The series

\[ 2+5+8+11+\cdots \]

is arithmetic because its differences are constant.

The series

\[ 2+6+18+54+\cdots \]

is geometric because its ratios are constant.

Common Error: Confusing \(a_n\) with \(S_n\)

The symbol \(a_n\) represents a term. The symbol \(S_n\) represents an accumulated sum.

For

\[ 2+4+6+8, \]

we have

\[ a_4=8 \]

but

\[ S_4=20. \]

Common Error: Forgetting to Find \(n\)

In

\[ 5+9+13+\cdots+41, \]

the value \(41\) is the last term, not the number of terms.

We must solve

\[ 41=5+4(n-1) \]

to obtain

\[ n=10. \]

Common Error: Using \(r^{n-1}\) in the Sum Formula

The \(n\)th term formula is

\[ a_n=a_1r^{n-1}. \]

But the finite geometric-sum formula contains \(r^n\):

\[ \boxed{S_n=a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}}. \]

Common Error: Ignoring the \(r=1\) Case

If \(r=1\), the standard fraction would divide by zero.

Instead, every term equals \(a_1\), so

\[ \boxed{S_n=na_1}. \]

Common Error: Losing a Negative Ratio

For

\[ 4-8+16-32, \]

the common ratio is

\[ \boxed{r=-2}, \]

not \(2\).

The negative sign determines the alternating pattern and must remain inside powers such as \((-2)^n\).

A Reliable Procedure for Arithmetic Sums

  1. Verify that the terms form an arithmetic sequence.
  2. Identify \(a_1\).
  3. Identify \(d\) if needed.
  4. Identify or solve for \(n\).
  5. Find \(a_n\) if needed.
  6. Choose the sum formula that matches the known information.
  7. Substitute carefully.
  8. Check whether the total is reasonable.

A Reliable Procedure for Geometric Sums

  1. Verify that the terms form a geometric sequence.
  2. Identify \(a_1\).
  3. Identify the common ratio \(r\).
  4. Count or solve for the number of terms \(n\).
  5. If \(r=1\), use \(S_n=na_1\).
  6. If \(r\neq1\), use a finite geometric-sum formula.
  7. Keep negative and fractional ratios inside parentheses when exponentiating.
  8. Check the result against direct addition when practical.

Example: Full Arithmetic-Sum Workflow

Find

\[ 14+19+24+\cdots+99. \]

First,

\[ a_1=14,\qquad d=5,\qquad a_n=99. \]

Find \(n\):

\[ 99=14+5(n-1). \] \[ 85=5(n-1), \] \[ n=18. \]

Then

\[ S_{18}=\frac{18}{2}(14+99) \] \[ =9(113) \] \[ =\boxed{1017}. \]

Example: Full Geometric-Sum Workflow

Find

\[ 5+15+45+\cdots+3645. \]

Here,

\[ a_1=5,\qquad r=3. \]

Find \(n\):

\[ 3645=5(3)^{n-1}. \] \[ 729=3^{n-1}=3^6, \]

so

\[ n=7. \]

Therefore,

\[ S_7=5\frac{3^7-1}{3-1} \] \[ =5\frac{2186}{2} \] \[ =\boxed{5465}. \]

What to Remember

For a finite arithmetic series,

\[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n)} \]

or equivalently,

\[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}[2a_1+(n-1)d]}. \]

For a finite geometric series with \(r\neq1\),

\[ \boxed{S_n=a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}} \]

or equivalently,

\[ \boxed{S_n=a_1\frac{r^n-1}{r-1}}. \]

If \(r=1\),

\[ \boxed{S_n=na_1}. \]
  • Arithmetic series come from constant differences.
  • Geometric series come from constant ratios.
  • The number of terms \(n\) must be known or determined.
  • The arithmetic formula uses the average of the first and last terms.
  • The geometric formula comes from scaling and subtracting aligned sums.
  • Arithmetic accumulation typically produces quadratic partial-sum structure.
  • Geometric accumulation retains exponential structure.
  • Negative and fractional ratios must be handled carefully.

The central comparison is

\[ \boxed{ \text{constant additive change} \longrightarrow \text{arithmetic sum} } \]

versus

\[ \boxed{ \text{constant multiplicative change} \longrightarrow \text{geometric sum}. } \]
