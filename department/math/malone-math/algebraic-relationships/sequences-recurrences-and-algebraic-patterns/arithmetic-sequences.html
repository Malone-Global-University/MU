Arithmetic Sequences

An arithmetic sequence changes by a constant amount from one term to the next. That constant change, called the common difference, gives arithmetic sequences both a recursive rule and a direct explicit formula. Because the index changes in equal steps, arithmetic sequences are the discrete counterpart of linear functions.

The Central Idea

Consider the sequence

\[ 5,\ 8,\ 11,\ 14,\ 17,\ldots \]

Each term is \(3\) greater than the previous term:

\[ 8-5=3, \] \[ 11-8=3, \] \[ 14-11=3. \]

Because the difference between consecutive terms is constant, this is an arithmetic sequence.

The constant difference is

\[ \boxed{d=3}. \]

What Is an Arithmetic Sequence?

An arithmetic sequence is a sequence in which the difference between consecutive terms is constant.

If the common difference is \(d\), then

\[ a_n-a_{n-1}=d. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{a_n=a_{n-1}+d}. \]

This is the recursive structure of every arithmetic sequence.

The Common Difference

The constant amount added from one term to the next is called the common difference.

It is usually represented by

\[ d. \]

To find it, subtract any term from the term immediately after it:

\[ \boxed{d=a_n-a_{n-1}}. \]

For an arithmetic sequence, this calculation gives the same result for every pair of consecutive terms.

Example: Finding the Common Difference

Consider

\[ 12,\ 19,\ 26,\ 33,\ 40,\ldots \]

Subtract consecutive terms:

\[ 19-12=7, \] \[ 26-19=7, \] \[ 33-26=7. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{d=7}. \]

The Common Difference Can Be Negative

An arithmetic sequence does not have to increase.

Consider

\[ 20,\ 16,\ 12,\ 8,\ 4,\ldots \]

Then

\[ 16-20=-4, \] \[ 12-16=-4. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{d=-4}. \]

A negative common difference produces a decreasing arithmetic sequence.

The Common Difference Can Be Zero

A constant sequence is also arithmetic.

For

\[ 6,\ 6,\ 6,\ 6,\ldots \]

we have

\[ 6-6=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{d=0}. \]

Every term remains unchanged.

Recognizing an Arithmetic Sequence

To determine whether a listed sequence is arithmetic:

  1. subtract each term from the term after it;
  2. compare the differences;
  3. if every difference is the same, the sequence is arithmetic.

For example,

\[ 4,\ 9,\ 14,\ 19,\ldots \]

has differences

\[ 5,\ 5,\ 5,\ldots \]

so it is arithmetic.

Example: A Sequence That Is Not Arithmetic

Consider

\[ 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 16,\ldots \]

The differences are

\[ 4-2=2, \] \[ 8-4=4, \] \[ 16-8=8. \]

The differences are not constant.

Therefore, the sequence is

\[ \boxed{\text{not arithmetic}}. \]

Later we will recognize this as a geometric sequence.

The Recursive Definition

An arithmetic sequence is naturally described recursively.

If the first term is \(a_1\) and the common difference is \(d\), then

\[ \boxed{ a_1=\text{given},\qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+d\quad(n\geq2) }. \]

The initial condition supplies the starting point, and the recurrence adds the same amount at every step.

Example: Writing a Recursive Definition

Consider

\[ 7,\ 12,\ 17,\ 22,\ldots \]

The first term is

\[ a_1=7, \]

and the common difference is

\[ d=5. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ a_1=7,\qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+5\quad(n\geq2) }. \]

Building the Explicit Formula

Suppose the first term is \(a_1\) and the common difference is \(d\).

Then

\[ a_2=a_1+d. \]

The third term is

\[ a_3=a_1+2d. \]

The fourth term is

\[ a_4=a_1+3d. \]

The \(n\)th term is reached after \(n-1\) equal steps.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a_n=a_1+(n-1)d}. \]

The Explicit Formula

The standard explicit formula for an arithmetic sequence indexed from \(1\) is

\[ \boxed{a_n=a_1+(n-1)d}. \]

Here:

  • \(a_n\) is the \(n\)th term;
  • \(a_1\) is the first term;
  • \(n\) is the index;
  • \(d\) is the common difference.

Why the Formula Uses \(n-1\)

The first term already exists before any common differences are added.

To reach \(a_2\), add \(d\) once.

To reach \(a_3\), add \(d\) twice.

To reach \(a_n\), add \(d\)

\[ n-1 \]

times.

That is why the formula is

\[ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d \]

rather than \(a_1+nd\).

Example: Writing an Explicit Formula

Consider

\[ 10,\ 14,\ 18,\ 22,\ldots \]

We have

\[ a_1=10 \]

and

\[ d=4. \]

Substitute into

\[ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d. \]

Then

\[ a_n=10+4(n-1). \]

Simplify:

\[ a_n=10+4n-4 \] \[ \boxed{a_n=4n+6}. \]

Equivalent Explicit Forms

The formulas

\[ a_n=10+4(n-1) \]

and

\[ a_n=4n+6 \]

define the same sequence.

The first form makes the first term and common difference visible.

The second resembles the familiar slope-intercept form of a linear function.

Both representations are useful.

Finding a Distant Term

Suppose

\[ a_1=6,\qquad d=8. \]

Find \(a_{25}\).

Use

\[ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d. \]

Then

\[ a_{25}=6+(25-1)(8) \] \[ =6+24(8) \] \[ =6+192 \] \[ =\boxed{198}. \]

Finding the Index of a Known Term

Suppose

\[ a_n=5+3(n-1). \]

At what index does the value \(47\) occur?

Set

\[ 47=5+3(n-1). \]

Subtract \(5\):

\[ 42=3(n-1). \]

Divide by \(3\):

\[ 14=n-1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{n=15}. \]

Determining Whether a Value Is a Term

Let

\[ a_n=4n+1,\qquad n\geq1. \]

Is \(30\) a term of the sequence?

Solve

\[ 4n+1=30. \]

Then

\[ 4n=29 \]

and

\[ n=\frac{29}{4}. \]

Since the allowed indices are positive integers, \(30\) is not a term.

\[ \boxed{30\notin\{a_n:n\geq1\}}. \]

Finding a Missing Term

Suppose

\[ 8,\ 13,\ \square,\ 23,\ 28 \]

is arithmetic.

The common difference is

\[ 13-8=5. \]

Therefore, the missing term is

\[ 13+5=\boxed{18}. \]

Finding Several Missing Terms

Suppose

\[ 4,\ \square,\ \square,\ 19 \]

is arithmetic.

There are three equal steps from \(4\) to \(19\).

The total change is

\[ 19-4=15. \]

Therefore,

\[ d=\frac{15}{3}=5. \]

The sequence is

\[ \boxed{4,\ 9,\ 14,\ 19}. \]

Arithmetic Means

A number inserted between two values so that the resulting terms form an arithmetic sequence is called an arithmetic mean.

To insert one arithmetic mean between \(6\) and \(14\), find the midpoint:

\[ \frac{6+14}{2}=10. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{6,\ 10,\ 14} \]

is an arithmetic sequence.

Several Arithmetic Means

Suppose we want to insert three arithmetic means between \(2\) and \(22\).

The completed sequence has five terms:

\[ 2,\ \square,\ \square,\ \square,\ 22. \]

There are four equal steps.

Therefore,

\[ d=\frac{22-2}{4}=5. \]

The sequence is

\[ \boxed{2,\ 7,\ 12,\ 17,\ 22}. \]

Finding the Common Difference from Two Terms

Suppose

\[ a_4=17 \]

and

\[ a_{10}=41. \]

From index \(4\) to index \(10\) there are

\[ 10-4=6 \]

equal steps.

The total change in value is

\[ 41-17=24. \]

Therefore,

\[ d=\frac{24}{6} \] \[ =\boxed{4}. \]

A General Two-Term Formula

For any two terms \(a_m\) and \(a_n\) of an arithmetic sequence,

\[ a_n-a_m=(n-m)d. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a_n=a_m+(n-m)d}. \]

This form is useful when a term other than \(a_1\) is already known.

Example: Using a Known Later Term

Suppose

\[ a_6=25 \]

and

\[ d=3. \]

Find \(a_{14}\).

Use

\[ a_{14}=a_6+(14-6)d. \]

Then

\[ a_{14}=25+8(3) \] \[ =25+24 \] \[ =\boxed{49}. \]

Finding the First Term from Another Term

Suppose

\[ a_8=31 \]

and

\[ d=4. \]

Use

\[ a_8=a_1+(8-1)d. \]

Then

\[ 31=a_1+7(4). \]

Therefore,

\[ 31=a_1+28 \]

and

\[ \boxed{a_1=3}. \]

Finding an Explicit Formula from Two Terms

Suppose

\[ a_3=11 \]

and

\[ a_7=27. \]

First find the common difference:

\[ d=\frac{27-11}{7-3} \] \[ =\frac{16}{4} \] \[ =4. \]

Now use

\[ a_3=a_1+2d. \]

Thus,

\[ 11=a_1+8, \]

so

\[ a_1=3. \]

Therefore,

\[ a_n=3+4(n-1) \] \[ \boxed{a_n=4n-1}. \]

Arithmetic Sequences and Linear Functions

The explicit formula

\[ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d \]

can be expanded:

\[ a_n=a_1+dn-d. \]

Rearranging gives

\[ \boxed{a_n=dn+(a_1-d)}. \]

This has the same algebraic form as

\[ y=mx+b. \]

The common difference \(d\) plays the role of slope.

The Common Difference Is a Discrete Rate of Change

In an arithmetic sequence,

\[ \frac{a_n-a_{n-1}}{n-(n-1)} = \frac{a_n-a_{n-1}}{1} = d. \]

Thus the common difference is the rate of change in the sequence value for each one-unit increase in the index.

This is the discrete analogue of constant slope.

A Sequence Table

Consider

\[ a_n=3n+2. \]
Index \(n\) Term \(a_n\)
\(1\) \(5\)
\(2\) \(8\)
\(3\) \(11\)
\(4\) \(14\)
\(5\) \(17\)

The input increases by \(1\), while the output increases by \(3\).

The Discrete Graph

The sequence

\[ a_n=3n+2 \]

produces the points

\[ (1,5),\ (2,8),\ (3,11),\ (4,14),\ldots \]

These points lie on the line

\[ y=3x+2. \]

But the sequence itself contains only the specified indexed points.

The line describes a continuous extension; the sequence remains discrete.

Constant First Differences

Arithmetic sequences are characterized by constant first differences.

Consider

\[ 4,\ 10,\ 16,\ 22,\ 28. \]

The first differences are

\[ 6,\ 6,\ 6,\ 6. \]

Since the first differences are constant, the sequence is arithmetic.

Later, finite differences will help us identify polynomial sequences whose first differences are not constant.

Indexing from Zero

If an arithmetic sequence begins at \(n=0\), the standard formula changes slightly.

If \(a_0\) is the starting term, then

\[ \boxed{a_n=a_0+nd}. \]

This occurs because moving from index \(0\) to index \(n\) requires exactly \(n\) steps.

Example: Arithmetic Sequence Beginning at Zero

Let

\[ a_0=7 \]

and

\[ d=4. \]

Then

\[ a_n=7+4n. \]

The sequence begins

\[ a_0=7, \] \[ a_1=11, \] \[ a_2=15, \] \[ a_3=19. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{7,\ 11,\ 15,\ 19,\ldots}. \]

A More General Indexing Formula

Suppose the sequence begins at some index \(k\), with known value \(a_k\).

Then

\[ \boxed{a_n=a_k+(n-k)d}. \]

This formula simply counts how many equal index steps separate \(k\) and \(n\).

The familiar formula

\[ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d \]

is the special case \(k=1\).

Arithmetic Sequences in Applications

Arithmetic sequences appear whenever a quantity changes by the same amount at each discrete step.

Examples include:

  • adding the same amount to an account every month;
  • increasing production by a fixed number of units each cycle;
  • moving a fixed distance at each step;
  • reducing inventory by a constant number each period;
  • arranging objects in rows whose sizes increase by a fixed amount;
  • following a linear depreciation schedule over discrete periods.

Example: A Savings Pattern

Suppose a person begins a savings schedule by depositing \(50\) dollars in the first month and increases the monthly deposit by \(10\) dollars each month.

The monthly deposit amounts form the arithmetic sequence

\[ 50,\ 60,\ 70,\ 80,\ldots \]

Here,

\[ a_1=50 \]

and

\[ d=10. \]

The deposit in month \(n\) is

\[ a_n=50+10(n-1). \]

Example: Fixed Decrease

A storage tank contains \(900\) liters at the first recorded time and loses \(35\) liters during each equal time interval.

The recorded amounts are

\[ 900,\ 865,\ 830,\ 795,\ldots \]

so

\[ d=-35. \]

The \(n\)th recorded amount is

\[ a_n=900-35(n-1). \]

Common Error: Using a Ratio Instead of a Difference

Arithmetic sequences are identified by constant differences, not constant ratios.

For

\[ 3,\ 6,\ 9,\ 12,\ldots \]

the differences are

\[ 3,\ 3,\ 3. \]

The ratios are not constant:

\[ \frac63=2, \] \[ \frac96=\frac32. \]

The sequence is arithmetic because of the constant difference.

Common Error: Reversing the Subtraction

The common difference is found by subtracting the earlier term from the later term:

\[ d=a_n-a_{n-1}. \]

For

\[ 20,\ 15,\ 10,\ 5,\ldots \]

we have

\[ d=15-20=-5. \]

Writing \(20-15=5\) would reverse the direction of change.

Common Error: Using \(nd\) Instead of \((n-1)d\)

Suppose

\[ a_1=5,\qquad d=3. \]

The correct formula is

\[ a_n=5+3(n-1). \]

If we incorrectly use

\[ 5+3n, \]

then at \(n=1\) we get

\[ 8 \]

instead of the required first term \(5\).

Common Error: Assuming Every Linear-Looking List Is Arithmetic

A visual impression is not enough.

Consider

\[ 2,\ 5,\ 9,\ 12,\ldots \]

The differences are

\[ 3,\ 4,\ 3. \]

Since they are not constant, the sequence is not arithmetic.

Always calculate the differences.

Common Error: Forgetting the Starting Index

The formula

\[ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d \]

assumes indexing begins at \(1\).

If indexing begins at \(0\), use

\[ a_n=a_0+nd. \]

More generally, if the known term is \(a_k\), use

\[ a_n=a_k+(n-k)d. \]

A Reliable Procedure for Analyzing an Arithmetic Sequence

  1. Identify the indexing convention.
  2. Calculate consecutive differences.
  3. Verify that the difference is constant.
  4. Record the common difference \(d\).
  5. Identify a known term such as \(a_1\).
  6. Write the recursive rule if needed.
  7. Write the explicit formula if needed.
  8. Substitute known information to solve for missing quantities.
  9. Check the result against the original sequence.

Example: Full Arithmetic-Sequence Workflow

Consider

\[ 18,\ 13,\ 8,\ 3,\ -2,\ldots \]

First find the common difference:

\[ 13-18=-5. \]

Thus,

\[ d=-5. \]

The recursive definition is

\[ a_1=18,\qquad a_n=a_{n-1}-5. \]

The explicit definition is

\[ a_n=18-5(n-1). \]

Simplify:

\[ a_n=18-5n+5 \] \[ \boxed{a_n=23-5n}. \]

Example: Recovering a Sequence from Two Terms

Suppose

\[ a_5=24 \]

and

\[ a_{12}=52. \]

First find the common difference:

\[ d=\frac{52-24}{12-5} \] \[ =\frac{28}{7} \] \[ =4. \]

Now use

\[ a_5=a_1+4d. \]

Therefore,

\[ 24=a_1+16 \]

and

\[ a_1=8. \]

The explicit rule is

\[ a_n=8+4(n-1) \] \[ \boxed{a_n=4n+4}. \]

What to Remember

An arithmetic sequence changes by a constant difference:

\[ \boxed{d=a_n-a_{n-1}}. \]

Its recursive form is

\[ \boxed{ a_1=\text{given},\qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+d }. \]

Its standard explicit form is

\[ \boxed{a_n=a_1+(n-1)d}. \]

More generally,

\[ \boxed{a_n=a_k+(n-k)d}. \]
  • The common difference may be positive, negative, or zero.
  • Constant first differences identify arithmetic sequences.
  • The common difference is a discrete rate of change.
  • Arithmetic sequences correspond to linear relationships on discrete domains.
  • The starting index determines the correct explicit formula.
  • Known terms can be used to recover \(d\), \(a_1\), or the general term.
