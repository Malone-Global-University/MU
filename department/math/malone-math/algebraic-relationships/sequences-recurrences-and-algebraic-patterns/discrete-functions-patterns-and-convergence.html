Discrete Functions, Patterns, and Introductory Convergence

Sequences are discrete functions: each allowed index is paired with one value. Their long-run behavior may reveal growth, decay, oscillation, boundedness, periodicity, or convergence toward a limiting value. This lesson brings together the algebraic, graphical, and recursive viewpoints developed throughout the section and introduces the first language of convergence.

The Central Idea

Consider the sequence

\[ 1,\ \frac12,\ \frac13,\ \frac14,\ \frac15,\ldots \]

Its explicit rule is

\[ a_n=\frac1n. \]

As \(n\) increases, the terms become smaller and move closer to \(0\).

We express this long-run behavior by writing

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=0 }. \]

The notation means that the sequence values can be made as close to \(0\) as desired by taking sufficiently large indices.

A Sequence Is a Discrete Function

A sequence assigns one output to each allowed index.

We may write

\[ n\longmapsto a_n. \]

If the index set is

\[ \{1,2,3,\ldots\}, \]

then the sequence is a function whose domain is the positive integers.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{sequence} = \text{function on a discrete domain} }. \]

Discrete Does Not Mean Random

A discrete function may follow a perfectly regular algebraic rule.

For example,

\[ a_n=3n+2 \]

is linear in structure even though it is defined only at integer indices.

Likewise,

\[ b_n=2^n \]

is exponential in structure while still being discrete.

Explicit, Recursive, Tabular, and Graphical Views

A sequence may be represented in several equivalent ways.

For example, the sequence

\[ 3,\ 6,\ 12,\ 24,\ldots \]

has explicit form

\[ a_n=3\cdot2^{n-1}, \]

recursive form

\[ a_1=3,\qquad a_n=2a_{n-1}, \]

and ordered pairs

\[ (1,3),\ (2,6),\ (3,12),\ (4,24),\ldots \]

Each representation emphasizes different structure.

Long-Run Behavior

Once a sequence is defined, we can ask what happens as the index becomes large.

  • approaching a finite value;
  • growing without bound;
  • decreasing without bound;
  • oscillating;
  • repeating periodically;
  • remaining bounded without approaching one value.

Increasing Sequences

A sequence is increasing when later terms are greater than earlier terms.

\[ a_{n+1}>a_n. \]

For example,

\[ a_n=n^2 \]

gives

\[ 1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ldots \]

and is increasing for positive integer indices.

Decreasing Sequences

A sequence is decreasing when

\[ a_{n+1} For example,

\[ a_n=\frac1n \]

gives

\[ 1,\ \frac12,\ \frac13,\ \frac14,\ldots \]

and decreases as \(n\) increases.

Monotonic Sequences

A sequence that consistently moves in one direction is called monotonic.

  • increasing;
  • decreasing;
  • nondecreasing;
  • nonincreasing.

Monotonicity becomes especially important when studying convergence.

Nondecreasing and Nonincreasing

A sequence is nondecreasing if

\[ a_{n+1}\geq a_n. \]

It is nonincreasing if

\[ a_{n+1}\leq a_n. \]

Equality is allowed.

Bounded Above

A sequence is bounded above if there is some number \(M\) such that

\[ a_n\leq M \]

for every allowed index \(n\).

Bounded Below

A sequence is bounded below if there is some number \(m\) such that

\[ a_n\geq m \]

for every allowed index.

Bounded Sequences

A sequence is bounded if it is bounded both above and below.

Equivalently, there is some \(M>0\) such that

\[ \boxed{|a_n|\leq M} \]

for every term.

Example: A Bounded Sequence

Consider

\[ a_n=\frac1n. \]

For \(n\geq1\),

\[ 0 Therefore, the sequence is bounded.

Example: An Unbounded Sequence

Consider

\[ a_n=n^2. \]

The values eventually exceed every fixed real number.

Therefore, the sequence is not bounded above.

Oscillating Sequences

A sequence may alternate or move back and forth instead of changing in one direction.

For example,

\[ a_n=(-1)^n \]

gives

\[ -1,\ 1,\ -1,\ 1,\ldots \]

and oscillates between two values.

Periodic Sequences

A sequence is periodic if its values repeat after a fixed number of indices.

If

\[ a_{n+p}=a_n \]

for a positive integer \(p\), then \(p\) is a period.

For \(a_n=(-1)^n\),

\[ a_{n+2}=a_n, \]

so the sequence has period \(2\).

Bounded Does Not Mean Convergent

The sequence

\[ (-1)^n \]

is bounded because

\[ |(-1)^n|=1. \]

But its terms do not approach one single number.

\[ \boxed{ \text{bounded} \not\Rightarrow \text{convergent} }. \]

What Is Convergence?

A sequence converges to a number \(L\) if its terms become arbitrarily close to \(L\) as the index becomes sufficiently large.

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L }. \]

The number \(L\) is called the limit of the sequence.

Convergence Is About the Tail

A limit describes what happens eventually.

For example,

\[ a_n=\frac{100}{n} \]

begins with large values but still satisfies

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{100}{n}=0 }. \]

Getting Closer Does Not Mean Reaching the Limit

The sequence

\[ a_n=\frac1n \]

never equals \(0\) for a positive integer \(n\).

Nevertheless,

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n=0. \]

A sequence does not need to reach its limit exactly.

Example: A Convergent Geometric Sequence

Consider

\[ a_n=\left(\frac12\right)^n. \]

The terms are

\[ \frac12,\ \frac14,\ \frac18,\ \frac1{16},\ldots \]

and become arbitrarily close to \(0\).

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty} \left(\frac12\right)^n =0 }. \]

Geometric Sequences and Long-Run Behavior

For

\[ a_n=a_1r^{n-1}, \]

if

\[ |r|<1, \]

then

\[ r^{n-1}\to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a_n\to0}. \]

Geometric Growth When \(r>1\)

If

\[ r>1 \]

and \(a_1>0\), then

\[ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} \]

grows without bound.

It does not converge to a finite real number.

Negative Ratios with Magnitude Less Than One

Consider

\[ a_n=\left(-\frac12\right)^n. \]

The signs alternate, but the magnitudes shrink toward \(0\).

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty} \left(-\frac12\right)^n =0 }. \]

The Case \(r=-1\)

If

\[ a_n=(-1)^n, \]

then the magnitude remains \(1\) and the sign alternates forever.

The sequence is bounded but divergent.

Divergence

A sequence that does not converge to a finite limit is called divergent.

  • growth without bound;
  • decrease without bound;
  • persistent oscillation;
  • more complicated nonsettling behavior.

Growth to Positive Infinity

For

\[ a_n=n, \]

the values grow beyond every fixed real bound.

\[ \boxed{a_n\to+\infty}. \]

This describes unbounded growth, not convergence to an ordinary real number.

Decrease to Negative Infinity

For

\[ a_n=-n^2, \]

the terms become arbitrarily negative.

\[ \boxed{a_n\to-\infty}. \]

Convergence from a Graph

A discrete graph can suggest convergence when its points move closer to a horizontal level.

For

\[ a_n=\frac1n, \]

the points

\[ \left(n,\frac1n\right) \]

move closer to the horizontal axis.

Convergence from a Table

Consider

\[ a_n=2+\frac1n. \]

The values suggest

\[ \boxed{a_n\to2}. \]

Algebraic Limit Intuition

For

\[ a_n=2+\frac1n, \]

since

\[ \frac1n\to0, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty} \left(2+\frac1n\right) =2 }. \]

Fixed Points and Recurrence Limits

Suppose

\[ a_{n+1}=f(a_n). \]

If \(a_n\to L\) and \(f\) is continuous at \(L\), then

\[ \boxed{L=f(L)}. \]

Thus a limit of such an iteration must be a fixed point of the update rule.

Example: Solving for a Possible Recurrence Limit

Let

\[ a_{n+1}=\frac12a_n+3. \]

A possible limit \(L\) satisfies

\[ L=\frac12L+3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{L=6}. \]

Finding a Fixed Point Does Not Prove Convergence

Solving

\[ L=f(L) \]

identifies a possible limit.

It does not by itself prove that the sequence approaches that value.

Example: A Fixed Point That Is Not Attracting

Consider

\[ a_{n+1}=2a_n. \]

The fixed-point equation gives

\[ L=0. \]

But with

\[ a_0=1, \]

the sequence

\[ 1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ldots \]

moves away from \(0\).

Attracting Behavior

A fixed point is informally called attracting when nearby starting values move toward it under iteration.

For

\[ a_{n+1}=\frac12a_n+3, \]

the fixed point \(6\) attracts many starting values.

Monotone and Bounded Behavior

A major theorem for real sequences says that every monotone bounded sequence converges.

\[ \boxed{ \text{monotone} + \text{bounded} \longrightarrow \text{convergent} }. \]

Intuitively, if a sequence keeps moving in one direction but cannot move indefinitely, it must approach some limiting level.

Example: Increasing and Bounded

Consider

\[ a_n=1-\frac1n. \]

The terms are

\[ 0,\ \frac12,\ \frac23,\ \frac34,\ldots \]

They increase and remain below \(1\).

The sequence converges to

\[ \boxed{1}. \]

Example: Decreasing and Bounded

The sequence

\[ a_n=2+\frac1n \]

decreases and remains above \(2\).

It converges to

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

Convergence and Partial Sums

An infinite series

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_k \]

is studied through its partial sums

\[ S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k. \]

The infinite series converges when the sequence

\[ S_1,S_2,S_3,\ldots \]

converges to a finite limit.

Example: Geometric Partial Sums

Consider

\[ 1+\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots. \]

The partial sums satisfy

\[ S_n= \frac{1-\left(\frac12\right)^n}{1-\frac12} = 2\left[ 1-\left(\frac12\right)^n \right]. \]

Since

\[ \left(\frac12\right)^n\to0, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{S_n\to2}. \]

A First Infinite Geometric-Series Formula

If

\[ |r|<1, \]

then

\[ a_1+a_1r+a_1r^2+\cdots \]

converges to

\[ \boxed{ \frac{a_1}{1-r} }. \]

More detailed study of infinite series belongs to later analysis.

Convergence Is Not the Same as Small Terms

A sequence may have small terms without converging.

For example,

\[ a_n=\frac12(-1)^n \]

stays between

\[ -\frac12 \]

and

\[ \frac12, \]

but oscillates forever.

Eventually Constant Sequences

Some sequences eventually stop changing.

For example,

\[ 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ldots \]

converges to

\[ \boxed{7}. \]

Finite Changes Do Not Affect the Limit

Convergence depends on the long-run tail of a sequence.

Changing finitely many early terms does not change the limit.

For example,

\[ 1000,\ -50,\ 8,\ \frac14,\ \frac15,\ \frac16,\ldots \]

still approaches

\[ \boxed{0}. \]

Patterns Can Be Local or Global

A sequence may show one pattern over a short interval and another in the long run.

Long-run analysis therefore requires more than inspecting only the first few terms.

Graphical Pattern Recognition Has Limits

A graph can suggest behavior, but scale matters.

A slowly changing sequence may look flat even when it is still changing. Algebraic analysis should support visual interpretation.

Tables Have Limits Too

A table can display only finitely many values.

Long-run behavior concerns arbitrarily large indices.

Numerical evidence can suggest convergence but does not by itself establish what happens forever.

Common Error: Treating a Large Index as Infinity

The notation

\[ n\to\infty \]

does not mean that \(n\) becomes an actual number called infinity.

It means that we examine what happens as \(n\) grows without bound.

Common Error: Assuming Bounded Means Convergent

The sequence

\[ (-1)^n \]

is bounded but alternates forever.

Boundedness alone is not sufficient for convergence.

Common Error: Assuming Monotonic Means Convergent

The sequence

\[ a_n=n \]

is increasing but unbounded above.

Therefore, it does not converge to a finite real limit.

Common Error: Assuming a Fixed Point Is Automatically the Limit

Solving

\[ L=f(L) \]

gives only a candidate fixed point.

The recurrence must still be analyzed to determine whether the sequence approaches it.

Common Error: Thinking a Sequence Must Reach Its Limit

The sequence

\[ \frac1n \]

converges to \(0\) without ever equaling \(0\).

Common Error: Confusing Sequence Convergence with Series Convergence

The sequence

\[ a_n=\frac1n \]

converges to \(0\).

But the infinite series

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n \]

diverges.

A sequence of terms and the series formed from those terms are different mathematical objects.

A Reliable Procedure for Classifying a Sequence Pattern

  1. Identify the domain and starting index.
  2. Inspect explicit or recursive structure.
  3. Check first differences.
  4. Check ratios where appropriate.
  5. Build higher finite differences if polynomial structure is suspected.
  6. Examine signs, repetition, and oscillation.
  7. Use a table or graph to support the algebraic analysis.

A Reliable Procedure for Introductory Convergence Analysis

  1. Inspect several terms.
  2. Check whether the sequence is increasing, decreasing, or oscillating.
  3. Determine whether it appears bounded.
  4. Use the explicit rule or recurrence to identify a candidate limit.
  5. For recurrences, solve the fixed-point equation when appropriate.
  6. Check whether the sequence actually moves toward that candidate.
  7. Distinguish finite-limit convergence from unbounded growth.
  8. Support numerical or graphical evidence with algebra whenever possible.

Example: Full Explicit-Sequence Analysis

Consider

\[ a_n=3+\frac2n. \]

Since

\[ \frac2n\to0, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty} \left(3+\frac2n\right)=3 }. \]

Example: Full Recurrence Analysis

Let

\[ a_0=10, \qquad a_{n+1}=\frac12a_n+2. \]

The first few terms are

\[ 10,\ 7,\ 5.5,\ 4.75,\ 4.375,\ldots \]

A possible limit \(L\) satisfies

\[ L=\frac12L+2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{L=4}. \]

The computed terms move toward \(4\), supporting

\[ \boxed{a_n\to4}. \]

What to Remember

A sequence is a function on a discrete index set:

\[ \boxed{n\longmapsto a_n}. \]

Convergence to \(L\) is written

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L }. \]
  • Increasing and decreasing behavior describe direction of change.
  • Boundedness describes whether terms remain within fixed limits.
  • Boundedness alone does not guarantee convergence.
  • Monotonicity alone does not guarantee convergence.
  • Monotone and bounded real sequences converge.
  • Geometric sequences with \(|r|<1\) converge to \(0\).
  • A sequence may approach a limit without ever reaching it.
  • Fixed-point equations identify possible recurrence limits but do not by themselves prove convergence.
  • Graphs and tables suggest long-run behavior; algebra provides stronger support.
  • Infinite series are studied through convergence of their partial-sum sequences.

The main conceptual transition is

\[ \boxed{ \text{individual discrete terms} \longrightarrow \text{long-run behavior} }. \]
