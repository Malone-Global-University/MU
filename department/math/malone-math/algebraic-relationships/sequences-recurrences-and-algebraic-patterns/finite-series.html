Finite Series

A sequence lists terms; a series adds them. Finite series turn indexed values into accumulated totals and give us a language for combining a limited number of terms. They connect sequence notation, sigma notation, partial sums, and many practical situations in which repeated contributions must be combined.

The Central Idea

Consider the sequence

\[ 2,\ 5,\ 8,\ 11,\ 14. \]

These five values are terms of a finite sequence.

If we add them,

\[ 2+5+8+11+14, \]

we obtain a finite series.

Its value is

\[ 2+5+8+11+14=\boxed{40}. \]

The distinction is simple but important:

\[ \boxed{ \text{sequence}=\text{terms} } \]

while

\[ \boxed{ \text{series}=\text{sum of terms}. } \]

What Is a Finite Series?

A finite series is the sum of a finite number of terms.

If

\[ a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n \]

are the terms of a finite sequence, then the corresponding finite series is

\[ a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n. \]

In sigma notation,

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k }. \]

Sequence Versus Series

The notation and punctuation help distinguish the two ideas.

A sequence may be written

\[ 3,\ 6,\ 9,\ 12. \]

A series formed from those same terms is

\[ 3+6+9+12. \]

The first is an ordered list.

The second is an addition problem whose value is

\[ \boxed{30}. \]

A Series Has a Value

A finite sequence consists of separate indexed values.

A finite series combines those values into one total.

For example,

\[ 1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16 \]

is a sequence, while

\[ 1+4+9+16 \]

is a series whose value is

\[ \boxed{30}. \]

Finite Series in Sigma Notation

Sigma notation compresses a finite series.

For example,

\[ 2+4+6+8+10 \]

can be written

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{5}2k }. \]

The sigma symbol tells us to generate and add the terms associated with the specified index values.

Expanding a Finite Series

Expand

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{4}(3k-1). \]

Substitute \(k=1,2,3,4\):

\[ (3(1)-1)+(3(2)-1)+(3(3)-1)+(3(4)-1). \]

Simplify:

\[ 2+5+8+11. \]

Therefore,

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{4}(3k-1)=\boxed{26}. \]

The First Partial Sum

Given a sequence

\[ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots, \]

the first partial sum is

\[ S_1=a_1. \]

The second partial sum is

\[ S_2=a_1+a_2. \]

The third is

\[ S_3=a_1+a_2+a_3. \]

In general,

\[ \boxed{ S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k }. \]

Partial Sums Form a New Sequence

Suppose the original sequence is

\[ 2,\ 4,\ 6,\ 8,\ldots \]

Then

\[ S_1=2, \] \[ S_2=2+4=6, \] \[ S_3=2+4+6=12, \] \[ S_4=2+4+6+8=20. \]

Thus the sequence of partial sums is

\[ \boxed{2,\ 6,\ 12,\ 20,\ldots}. \]

Terms Versus Partial Sums

The term \(a_n\) and the partial sum \(S_n\) are different quantities.

For

\[ 2,\ 4,\ 6,\ 8,\ldots, \]

we have

\[ a_4=8, \]

but

\[ S_4=2+4+6+8=20. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a_4\neq S_4}. \]

Recursive Structure of Partial Sums

Partial sums satisfy

\[ S_n=S_{n-1}+a_n. \]

Each accumulated total is obtained by taking the previous total and adding the next sequence term.

If

\[ S_0=0, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ S_n=S_{n-1}+a_n }. \]

This is accumulation written recursively.

Recovering Terms from Partial Sums

Since

\[ S_n=S_{n-1}+a_n, \]

subtract \(S_{n-1}\):

\[ \boxed{ a_n=S_n-S_{n-1} }. \]

Thus the original sequence terms are the first differences of the partial sum sequence.

Example: Recovering Terms from Cumulative Totals

Suppose the partial sums are

\[ 5,\ 12,\ 21,\ 32. \]

Then

\[ a_1=S_1=5, \] \[ a_2=S_2-S_1=12-5=7, \] \[ a_3=S_3-S_2=21-12=9, \] \[ a_4=S_4-S_3=32-21=11. \]

Therefore, the original sequence is

\[ \boxed{5,\ 7,\ 9,\ 11}. \]

Finite Series as Accumulation

A finite series can be viewed as an accumulation process.

Each term contributes something to a running total:

\[ 0 \longrightarrow a_1 \longrightarrow a_1+a_2 \longrightarrow a_1+a_2+a_3 \longrightarrow\cdots \]

The final partial sum is the total value of the finite series.

Series from Arithmetic Sequences

If the terms form an arithmetic sequence, then the corresponding sum is an arithmetic series.

For example,

\[ 4,\ 7,\ 10,\ 13,\ 16 \]

is arithmetic.

Therefore,

\[ 4+7+10+13+16 \]

is a finite arithmetic series.

Series from Geometric Sequences

If the terms form a geometric sequence, then their sum is a geometric series.

For example,

\[ 3,\ 6,\ 12,\ 24,\ 48 \]

is geometric.

Therefore,

\[ 3+6+12+24+48 \]

is a finite geometric series.

Not Every Finite Series Is Arithmetic or Geometric

Consider

\[ 1+4+9+16+25. \]

These are square numbers.

The terms do not have constant first differences and do not have a constant ratio.

The expression is still a perfectly valid finite series:

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{5}k^2. \]

A Finite Series Always Has a Finite Number of Terms

The word finite refers to the number of terms being added.

For example,

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{1000000}k \]

contains one million terms.

That is a very large number of terms, but it is still finite because the upper bound is a finite integer.

Finite Does Not Mean Small

A finite series may have very many terms or a very large total.

For example,

\[ 10^{20}+10^{20} \]

contains only two terms, so it is finite.

Its size has nothing to do with whether the series contains finitely or infinitely many terms.

Number of Terms from the Bounds

In

\[ \sum_{k=m}^{n}a_k, \]

where the index increases by \(1\), the number of terms is

\[ \boxed{n-m+1}. \]

Thus

\[ \sum_{k=4}^{10}a_k \]

contains

\[ 10-4+1=\boxed{7} \]

terms.

Changing the Starting Index

Consider

\[ 3+6+9+12. \]

One representation is

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{4}3k. \]

Another is

\[ \sum_{j=0}^{3}3(j+1). \]

The index labels differ, but the generated terms and final total are the same.

Splitting a Finite Series

A finite sum may be divided into adjacent parts.

For example,

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{8}a_k \]

can be written as

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{3}a_k + \sum_{k=4}^{8}a_k }. \]

This can be useful when different portions of the sum have different structures.

Combining Finite Series

If two sums have matching bounds, then

\[ \sum_{k=m}^{n}a_k + \sum_{k=m}^{n}b_k \]

can be combined:

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=m}^{n}(a_k+b_k) }. \]

The addition is performed term by term across matching indices.

Scaling a Finite Series

A constant factor may be moved outside a finite sum:

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=m}^{n}ca_k = c\sum_{k=m}^{n}a_k }. \]

For example,

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{5}4k = 4\sum_{k=1}^{5}k. \]

Since

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{5}k=15, \]

the total is

\[ 4(15)=\boxed{60}. \]

Adding a Constant Term

Consider

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_k+c). \]

Split the sum:

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k + \sum_{k=1}^{n}c. \]

Since \(c\) appears \(n\) times,

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_k+c) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k+nc }. \]

Example: Evaluating a Linear Finite Series

Evaluate

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{6}(2k+1). \]

Split:

\[ 2\sum_{k=1}^{6}k + \sum_{k=1}^{6}1. \]

Use

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{6}k=\frac{6(7)}{2}=21. \]

Therefore,

\[ 2(21)+6 \] \[ =42+6 \] \[ =\boxed{48}. \]

Known Finite-Sum Formulas

Several formulas are especially useful:

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{n}1=n } \] \[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{n}k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} } \] \[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{n}k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} } \] \[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{n}k^3 = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2 }. \]

These formulas allow large finite series to be evaluated without writing every term.

Example: Sum of Squares

Evaluate

\[ 1^2+2^2+3^2+\cdots+10^2. \]

Write

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{10}k^2. \]

Use

\[ \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{10(11)(21)}{6} \] \[ =\boxed{385}. \]

Finite Series and Area-Like Accumulation

A finite sum may represent repeated contributions of many kinds.

For example, if rectangular strips have areas

\[ A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_n, \]

then their total area is

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n}A_k. \]

This basic accumulation idea later becomes central to numerical integration and calculus.

Finite Series and Total Distance

Suppose an object travels distances

\[ d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n \]

during successive time intervals.

The total distance traveled is

\[ \boxed{ D=\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_k }. \]

A series converts local contributions into a global total.

Finite Series and Total Cost

Suppose purchases have costs

\[ c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_n. \]

The total cost is

\[ \boxed{ C=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_k }. \]

If each cost depends on an index or quantity, sigma notation can combine those relationships compactly.

Finite Series and Repeated Payments

Suppose payments are

\[ P_1,P_2,\ldots,P_n. \]

Their total is

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n}P_k. \]

If the payments increase arithmetically or geometrically, specialized finite-series formulas can make the total much easier to compute.

Finite Series and Probability

Probabilities of mutually exclusive outcomes may be added.

If

\[ p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n \]

are probabilities of disjoint events, then the probability of one of them occurring is

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n}p_k. \]

Sigma notation becomes especially useful when many outcomes must be combined.

Weighted Finite Series

Sometimes each term has a weight.

A weighted sum has the form

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{n}w_ka_k }. \]

Here \(w_k\) determines how strongly the value \(a_k\) contributes to the total.

Weighted sums appear throughout probability, statistics, economics, and numerical analysis.

Average as a Finite Series

The arithmetic mean of

\[ a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n \]

is

\[ \boxed{ \bar a = \frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k }. \]

The sum accumulates the values, and division by \(n\) distributes that total evenly across the number of terms.

Telescoping Series

Some finite series simplify because neighboring terms cancel.

Consider

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{4} \left( \frac1k-\frac1{k+1} \right). \]

Expand:

\[ \left(1-\frac12\right) + \left(\frac12-\frac13\right) + \left(\frac13-\frac14\right) + \left(\frac14-\frac15\right). \]

The interior terms cancel, leaving

\[ 1-\frac15 \] \[ =\boxed{\frac45}. \]

Why Telescoping Works

In a telescoping series, terms are arranged so that one contribution is canceled by a later contribution.

Symbolically,

\[ (b_1-b_2)+(b_2-b_3)+\cdots+(b_n-b_{n+1}) \]

simplifies to

\[ \boxed{b_1-b_{n+1}}. \]

Most of the intermediate terms disappear.

Finite Series and Difference Sequences

Suppose

\[ a_k=b_{k+1}-b_k. \]

Then

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k = \sum_{k=1}^{n}(b_{k+1}-b_k). \]

This telescopes to

\[ \boxed{ b_{n+1}-b_1 }. \]

Summation can therefore reverse a finite-difference process.

Summation and Finite Differences

Finite differences measure discrete change:

\[ \Delta b_k=b_{k+1}-b_k. \]

Summing those changes gives

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta b_k = b_{n+1}-b_1. \]

This is a discrete version of a broader principle:

\[ \boxed{ \text{accumulating changes recovers total change}. } \]

Finite Series as Discrete Integration

In calculus, integration accumulates infinitesimal contributions.

In discrete mathematics, finite summation accumulates discrete contributions.

This analogy can be summarized as

\[ \boxed{ \text{finite difference} \leftrightarrow \text{derivative-like change} } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \text{finite sum} \leftrightarrow \text{integral-like accumulation}. } \]

The concepts are not identical, but the structural connection is important.

Finite Versus Infinite Series

Compare

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{10}\frac1{2^k} \]

with

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{2^k}. \]

The first has exactly ten terms.

The second indicates an infinite process and requires the idea of a limit to assign a value.

Finite series require only ordinary finite addition.

Every Finite Real Series Has a Defined Sum

If a finite list contains ordinary real numbers, then their sum is well-defined.

There is no convergence question for

\[ a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n \]

because the addition stops after finitely many terms.

Convergence becomes an issue only when infinitely many terms are involved.

Common Error: Calling a Sequence a Series

The expression

\[ 2,\ 4,\ 6,\ 8 \]

is a sequence.

The expression

\[ 2+4+6+8 \]

is a series.

The presence of addition is essential to the meaning of the word series.

Common Error: Confusing \(a_n\) with \(S_n\)

If

\[ a_n=3n, \]

then

\[ a_4=12. \]

But

\[ S_4=3+6+9+12=30. \]

A term and a partial sum are different mathematical objects.

Common Error: Forgetting the First Index

The sum

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{4}(k+1) \]

begins with

\[ k=0, \]

so its first term is

\[ 0+1=1. \]

Ignoring the lower bound changes the series.

Common Error: Omitting the Last Term

The upper bound is included.

Therefore,

\[ \sum_{k=2}^{5}k \]

means

\[ 2+3+4+5, \]

not \(2+3+4\).

Common Error: Adding the Number of Terms Instead of the Terms

Suppose

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{4}(2k). \]

There are four terms, but the sum is not \(4\).

The terms are

\[ 2,\ 4,\ 6,\ 8, \]

whose sum is

\[ \boxed{20}. \]

Common Error: Assuming Every Series Needs a Special Formula

A short finite series can always be evaluated by ordinary addition.

Specialized formulas are conveniences, not requirements.

For example,

\[ 3+7+11 \]

can simply be calculated as

\[ \boxed{21}. \]

A Reliable Procedure for Evaluating a Finite Series

  1. Identify the lower and upper bounds.
  2. Determine how many terms are present.
  3. Expand several terms to verify the pattern.
  4. Decide whether direct addition is practical.
  5. Look for arithmetic, geometric, telescoping, or polynomial structure.
  6. Use summation properties or a known formula when helpful.
  7. Evaluate carefully.
  8. Check that all intended terms were included exactly once.

A Reliable Procedure for Building Partial Sums

  1. Start with \(S_0=0\) when convenient.
  2. Add the first term to obtain \(S_1\).
  3. Add the second term to obtain \(S_2\).
  4. Continue using \(S_n=S_{n-1}+a_n\).
  5. Record each accumulated total.
  6. Use differences \(S_n-S_{n-1}\) to recover terms if needed.

Example: Full Finite-Series Workflow

Evaluate

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{8}(3k+2). \]

Split the sum:

\[ 3\sum_{k=1}^{8}k + 2\sum_{k=1}^{8}1. \]

Use

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{8}k = \frac{8(9)}{2} = 36. \]

Therefore,

\[ 3(36)+2(8) \] \[ =108+16 \] \[ =\boxed{124}. \]

Example: Partial-Sum Workflow

Let

\[ a_n=2n-1. \]

The first five terms are

\[ 1,\ 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 9. \]

Their partial sums are

\[ S_1=1, \] \[ S_2=4, \] \[ S_3=9, \] \[ S_4=16, \] \[ S_5=25. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{S_n=n^2} \]

for this sequence.

The sum of the first \(n\) odd positive integers is a perfect square.

What to Remember

A sequence lists terms:

\[ a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n. \]

A finite series adds them:

\[ \boxed{ a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k }. \]

Partial sums are defined by

\[ \boxed{ S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k }. \]

They satisfy

\[ \boxed{ S_n=S_{n-1}+a_n } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}. } \]
  • A finite series contains finitely many added terms.
  • Finite refers to the number of terms, not the size of the total.
  • Sigma notation compactly represents finite series.
  • Partial sums form a new sequence of accumulated totals.
  • Arithmetic and geometric sequences produce important special series.
  • Telescoping series simplify through cancellation.
  • Summation reverses certain finite-difference processes.
  • Finite summation is a basic form of discrete accumulation.
