Compound and Absolute-Value Inequalities

Compound inequalities combine more than one comparison. Absolute-value inequalities describe distance constraints, which naturally become intervals or unions of intervals.

The Central Idea

Some inequalities describe one boundary:

\[ x<5. \]

Others describe two boundaries at once:

\[ 2And some describe values lying in either of two separated regions:

\[ x<-3 \quad\text{or}\quad x>3. \]

These are compound inequalities.

Absolute-value inequalities often produce exactly these two kinds of solution sets:

  • inside a distance produces an interval;
  • outside a distance produces two rays.

What Is a Compound Inequality?

A compound inequality combines two or more inequalities using logical words such as and or or.

For example,

\[ x>2 \quad\text{and}\quad x<7 \]

means that both conditions must be true.

This can be written compactly as

\[ 2By contrast,

\[ x<-2 \quad\text{or}\quad x>7 \]

means that either condition is sufficient.

“And” Means Intersection

For an and compound inequality, a number must satisfy both conditions.

Consider

\[ x\ge1 \quad\text{and}\quad x<6. \]

The numbers satisfying both are those from \(1\) up to but not including \(6\):

\[ 1\le x<6. \]

In interval notation:

\[ [1,6). \]

This shared region is the intersection of the two solution sets.

“Or” Means Union

For an or compound inequality, a number may satisfy either condition.

Consider

\[ x\le-2 \quad\text{or}\quad x>4. \]

The solution set contains two separate regions:

\[ (-\infty,-2] \cup (4,\infty). \]

The symbol

\[ \cup \]

denotes the union of the two sets.

Solving a Three-Part Inequality

Consider

\[ 2<3x+5<17. \]

We can perform the same operation on all three parts.

Subtract \(5\):

\[ -3<3x<12. \]

Divide by \(3\):

Negative Division in a Chained Inequality

Consider

\[ -6<-2x\le10. \]

Divide all three parts by \(-2\).

Because the divisor is negative, both inequality directions reverse:

\[ 3>x\ge-5. \]

Rewrite in increasing order:

\[ -5\le x<3. \]

Interval notation:

\[ [-5,3). \]

Why Both Directions Reverse

In

\[ amultiplying by a negative number reflects all three values across zero.

The order becomes reversed:

\[ -ac<-bc<-aa \]

when written from smallest to largest.

So every inequality symbol affected by multiplication or division by a negative quantity must reverse.

Solving an “And” Compound Inequality Separately

Consider

\[ 2x-1\ge5 \quad\text{and}\quad 3x+2<14. \]

Solve the first:

\[ 2x\ge6, \] \[ x\ge3. \]

Solve the second:

\[ 3x<12, \] \[ x<4. \]

Both must hold, so

\[ 3\le x<4. \]

Interval notation:

\[ [3,4). \]

Solving an “Or” Compound Inequality Separately

Consider

\[ 2x+1<-5 \quad\text{or}\quad 3x-2\ge10. \]

First branch:

\[ 2x<-6, \] \[ x<-3. \]

Second branch:

\[ 3x\ge12, \] \[ x\ge4. \]

Therefore,

\[ x<-3 \quad\text{or}\quad x\ge4. \]

Interval notation:

\[ (-\infty,-3) \cup [4,\infty). \]

When an “And” Compound Has No Solution

Consider

\[ x>5 \quad\text{and}\quad x<2. \]

No real number can satisfy both conditions.

Therefore,

\[ \varnothing \]

is the solution set.

When an “Or” Compound Gives All Real Numbers

Consider

\[ x<5 \quad\text{or}\quad x\ge2. \]

Every real number satisfies at least one of these conditions.

Therefore, the union is

\[ \mathbb{R}. \]

Absolute Value as Distance

The expression

\[ |x-a| \]

is the distance between \(x\) and \(a\) on the real number line.

This gives a geometric meaning to absolute-value inequalities.

For example,

\[ |x-3|<5 \]

means:

The distance from \(x\) to \(3\) is less than \(5\).

So \(x\) must lie within 5 units of \(3\).

Less Than Means Inside

Consider

\[ |x|<4. \]

The numbers whose distance from zero is less than \(4\) lie between \(-4\) and \(4\):

\[ -4So

\[ |x|<4 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad -4Interval notation:

\[ (-4,4). \]

Less Than or Equal Means Inside Including the Boundary

Consider

\[ |x|\le4. \]

The distance from zero may be at most \(4\).

Therefore,

\[ -4\le x\le4. \]

Interval notation:

\[ [-4,4]. \]

Greater Than Means Outside

Consider

\[ |x|>4. \]

The distance from zero must exceed \(4\).

So \(x\) lies outside the interval from \(-4\) to \(4\):

\[ x<-4 \quad\text{or}\quad x>4. \]

Thus,

\[ |x|>4 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x<-4 \ \text{or}\ x>4. \]

Greater Than or Equal Means Outside Including the Boundary

For

\[ |x|\ge4, \]

we include the boundary points:

\[ x\le-4 \quad\text{or}\quad x\ge4. \]

Interval notation:

\[ (-\infty,-4] \cup [4,\infty). \]

For \(k>0\):

A Memory Aid That Reflects the Geometry

For positive \(k\):

  • less than means inside the two boundary points;
  • greater than means outside the two boundary points.

This is more reliable than memorizing isolated sign patterns because it comes directly from distance.

Testing a Compound Solution

Suppose we obtain

\[ -2Choose a value inside, such as \(x=0\), and verify it satisfies the original inequality.

Then test the boundary points:

  • \(-2\) should be excluded;
  • \(5\) should be included.

Boundary testing is especially useful for catching strict-versus-inclusive errors.

Testing an “Or” Solution

Suppose the solution is

\[ x<-3 \quad\text{or}\quad x\ge4. \]

Useful test values include:

  • a value in the left region, such as \(-5\);
  • a value in the gap, such as \(0\);
  • a value in the right region, such as \(5\);
  • the boundary values \(-3\) and \(4\).

This checks both rays, the excluded middle region, and endpoint behavior.

Application: Tolerance

Suppose a machine part should be \(20\) mm long with a tolerance of at most \(0.1\) mm.

If \(x\) is the actual length, then

\[ |x-20|\le0.1. \]

Convert to a compound inequality:

\[ -0.1\le x-20\le0.1. \]

Add \(20\):

\[ 19.9\le x\le20.1. \]

The acceptable lengths form a closed interval.

Application: Distance from a Target

Suppose a temperature must differ from \(72^\circ\) by more than \(5^\circ\).

If \(T\) is the temperature, then

\[ |T-72|>5. \]

Therefore,

\[ T<67 \]

or

\[ T>77. \]

This produces two separate allowable regions.

Application: Range Requirement

Suppose a score must be at least \(70\) but no more than \(90\).

If \(s\) is the score, then

\[ 70\le s\le90. \]

This is an and compound inequality because both bounds must be satisfied.

A Reliable Strategy for Compound Inequalities

  1. Identify whether the connector is and or or.
  2. Solve each inequality while preserving order.
  3. Reverse inequality directions when multiplying or dividing by a negative quantity.
  4. For and, keep only the overlap.
  5. For or, combine all allowed regions.
  6. Express the result with inequality notation, interval notation, or a number-line graph.

A Reliable Strategy for Absolute-Value Inequalities

  1. Isolate the absolute-value expression.
  2. Inspect the comparison value.
  3. If it is positive, use the inside or outside pattern.
  4. If it is zero or negative, analyze the special case directly.
  5. Solve the resulting compound inequality or branches.
  6. Write the final solution set clearly.
  7. Check boundaries and representative interior or exterior values when useful.

Common Errors

Confusing “And” with “Or”

And means both conditions must hold. Or means either condition is enough.

Using the Wrong Absolute-Value Pattern

For positive \(k\):

\[ |A|means inside, while

\[ |A|>k \]

means outside.

Forgetting to Isolate the Absolute Value

Do not convert to a compound inequality until the absolute-value expression stands alone.

Forgetting to Reverse Both Signs in a Chained Inequality

Dividing all three parts by a negative number reverses both inequality directions.

Using Intersection for an “Or” Statement

An “or” compound inequality uses union, not overlap.

Ignoring Special Cases When the Comparison Value Is Nonpositive

Absolute value is never negative, so cases involving \(0\) or negative bounds should be analyzed before applying memorized formulas.

Key Ideas

  • Compound inequalities combine multiple conditions using logical connectors such as and and or.
  • And corresponds to intersection: every condition must hold.
  • Or corresponds to union: at least one condition must hold.
  • Chained inequalities are compact forms of and statements.
  • When a chained inequality is multiplied or divided by a negative quantity, every affected inequality direction reverses.
  • Absolute value measures distance from a center.
  • For positive bounds, absolute-value inequalities with less than describe inside intervals.
  • For positive bounds, absolute-value inequalities with greater than describe outside regions.
  • Strict inequalities produce open boundaries; inclusive inequalities produce closed boundaries.
  • Absolute-value inequalities with zero or negative comparison values require special-case reasoning.
  • Intervals can often be rewritten in center-radius absolute-value form.
  • Compound and absolute-value inequalities model ranges, tolerances, minimums, maximums, and distance constraints.

Where This Leads

Compound and absolute-value inequalities show that solution sets may be single intervals, unions of intervals, all real numbers, or the empty set.

This prepares the way for polynomial and rational inequalities, sign charts, systems of inequalities, feasible regions, optimization, and more advanced constraint-based reasoning.
