Polynomial and Rational Inequalities

Polynomial and rational inequalities are usually solved by studying where an expression is positive, negative, zero, or undefined. The central tool is interval sign analysis.

The Central Idea

Consider

\[ (x-2)(x+3)>0. \]

We are not asking where the product equals a single value. We are asking where the product is positive.

The factors change sign at

\[ x=2 \]

and

\[ x=-3. \]

These values divide the number line into intervals:

\[ (-\infty,-3),\qquad (-3,2),\qquad (2,\infty). \]

On each interval, the sign of every factor remains constant, so the sign of the whole product remains constant.

This is the key strategy:

Find the critical points, divide the number line into intervals, determine the sign on each interval, and keep the intervals that satisfy the inequality.

Why Equation Methods Are Not Enough

For an equation such as

\[ (x-2)(x+3)=0, \]

the zero-product property immediately gives

\[ x=2 \]

or

\[ x=-3. \]

But for

\[ (x-2)(x+3)>0, \]

the zeros are only boundary points.

The actual solution consists of intervals where the product has the correct sign.

Polynomial Inequalities

A polynomial inequality compares a polynomial expression with zero or another expression.

Examples include

\[ x^2-5x+6>0, \] \[ x^3-4x\le0, \]

and

\[ 2x^2+3x<5. \]

A useful first step is usually to move all terms to one side so that the other side is zero.

Put the Inequality in Zero Form

Consider

\[ x^2-3x<4. \]

Move everything to one side:

\[ x^2-3x-4<0. \]

Factor:

\[ (x-4)(x+1)<0. \]

Now the important boundary points are visible:

\[ x=-1,\qquad x=4. \]

Critical Points

A critical point for sign analysis is a value where the expression is zero or undefined.

For a polynomial, critical points come from its real zeros.

For

\[ (x-4)(x+1), \]

the critical points are

\[ x=-1 \]

and

\[ x=4. \]

They divide the number line into the intervals

\[ (-\infty,-1), \qquad (-1,4), \qquad (4,\infty). \]

To solve

\[ (x-4)(x+1)<0, \]

\[ x<-1, \]

\[ (0-4)(0+1)=(-4)(1)<0. \]

\[ x>4, \]

use \(x=5\):

\[ (5-4)(5+1)=(1)(6)>0. \]

The expression is negative only between the roots.

The solution

The endpoints are excluded because the inequality is strict:

\[ (x-4)(x+1)<0. \]

At \(x=-1\) or \(x=4\), the product equals zero, not a negative number.

Inclusive Polynomial Inequality

Now solve

\[ (x-4)(x+1)\le0. \]

The sign analysis is unchanged, but the zeros now satisfy the inequality because equality is allowed.

Therefore,

\[ -1\le x\le4. \]

Interval notation:

\[ [-1,4]. \]

Positive Outside the Roots

Consider

\[ (x-2)(x+3)>0. \]

The critical points are

\[ -3,\qquad 2. \]

Testing the three intervals shows the product is positive on the outside intervals:

\[ x<-3 \]

or

\[ x>2. \]

So the solution is

\[ (-\infty,-3)\cup(2,\infty). \]

Negative Between Simple Roots

For a quadratic with two distinct real roots and a positive leading coefficient, the sign pattern is often

\[ +\quad-\quad+. \]

That is, the polynomial is positive outside the roots and negative between them.

This pattern comes from the factor signs, not from a rule that should be memorized without explanation.

\[ -(x-2)(x+3)>0. \]

The negative sign reverses the sign of the entire product.

So the expression is positive where

Repeated Roots

Consider

\[ (x-2)^2(x+1)>0. \]

The critical points are

\[ x=-1 \]

and

\[ x=2. \]

The factor

\[ (x-2)^2 \]

is nonnegative and has even multiplicity.

As \(x\) passes through \(2\), this factor does not change sign.

So the sign of the full expression does not switch at \(x=2\).

Odd and Even Multiplicity

A zero of odd multiplicity usually causes the sign to change as the graph crosses the axis.

A zero of even multiplicity usually leaves the sign unchanged because the graph touches the axis and turns back.

For example:

\[ (x-1)^1 \]

changes sign at \(x=1\), while

\[ (x-1)^2 \]

does not.

This connection links algebraic multiplicity with graphical behavior.

Example with a Repeated Root

Solve

\[ (x-2)^2(x+1)\le0. \]

The factor

\[ (x-2)^2 \]

is always nonnegative.

Therefore, the sign is controlled primarily by

\[ x+1, \]

except at \(x=2\), where the entire product is zero.

The product is negative for

\[ x<-1, \]

and zero at

\[ x=-1 \]

and

\[ x=2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(-\infty,-1]\cup\{2\}}. \]

Higher-Degree Polynomial Inequality

Solve

\[ x(x-2)(x+4)>0. \]

The critical points are

\[ -4,\qquad 0,\qquad 2. \]

These divide the number line into four intervals:

\[ (-\infty,-4), \] \[ (-4,0), \] \[ (0,2), \]

and

\[ (2,\infty). \]

Testing one point in each interval gives the sign pattern

\[ -\quad+\quad-\quad+. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(-4,0)\cup(2,\infty)}. \]

Rational Inequalities

A rational inequality contains a rational expression, often with a variable in the denominator.

For example,

\[ \frac{x-2}{x+1}>0. \]

Rational inequalities use the same interval sign logic as polynomial inequalities, but with one important addition:

Denominator zeros are always excluded because the expression is undefined there.

Zeros Versus Undefined Points

Consider

\[ \frac{x-2}{x+1}. \]

The numerator is zero at

\[ x=2. \]

The denominator is zero at

\[ x=-1. \]

These two points play different roles:

  • \(x=2\) is a zero of the rational expression and may be included in a non-strict inequality.
  • \(x=-1\) is not in the domain and can never be included.

Basic Rational Inequality

Solve

\[ \frac{x-2}{x+1}>0. \]

Critical points:

\[ x=-1,\qquad x=2. \]

Test the intervals.

For \(x=-2\):

\[ \frac{-4}{-1}>0. \]

For \(x=0\):

\[ \frac{-2}{1}<0. \]

For \(x=3\):

\[ \frac{1}{4}>0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(-\infty,-1)\cup(2,\infty)}. \]

The point \(-1\) is excluded because the denominator is zero, and \(2\) is excluded because the inequality is strict.

Inclusive Rational Inequality

Solve

\[ \frac{x-2}{x+1}\ge0. \]

The positive intervals are the same, but now the zero at \(x=2\) may be included.

The denominator zero at \(x=-1\) remains excluded.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{(-\infty,-1)\cup[2,\infty)}. \]

Always Move Everything to One Side

Consider

\[ \frac{1}{x}>2. \]

It is dangerous to multiply both sides by \(x\) immediately because the sign of \(x\) is unknown.

Instead, move everything to one side:

\[ \frac{1}{x}-2>0. \]

Combine:

\[ \frac{1-2x}{x}>0. \]

Now use sign analysis.

Why Blind Cross-Multiplication Can Fail

In an equation, multiplying by a known nonzero denominator can preserve equivalence.

In an inequality, multiplying by a variable denominator is more delicate because its sign may be positive or negative.

If the denominator is negative, the inequality direction should reverse.

If its sign is unknown, direct multiplication can accidentally merge different cases.

Sign analysis avoids that problem.

Worked Rational Example with Unknown-Sign Denominator

Solve

\[ \frac{1}{x}>2. \]

Rewrite:

\[ \frac{1-2x}{x}>0. \]

Critical points:

\[ x=0 \]

and

\[ x=\frac12. \]

Test intervals.

For \(x=-1\):

\[ \frac{1-2(-1)}{-1} = \frac{3}{-1}<0. \]

For \(x=\frac14\):

\[ \frac{1-\frac12}{\frac14}>0. \]

For \(x=1\):

\[ \frac{1-2}{1}<0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\left(0,\frac12\right)}. \]

Rational Inequality with Several Factors

Solve

\[ \frac{(x-3)(x+2)}{x-1}\le0. \]

Critical points:

\[ x=-2,\qquad x=1,\qquad x=3. \]

The points \(-2\) and \(3\) are zeros.

The point \(1\) is excluded because it makes the denominator zero.

Testing the intervals gives the sign pattern

\[ -\quad+\quad-\quad+. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(-\infty,-2]\cup(1,3]}. \]

Canceling Factors Requires Domain Memory

Consider

\[ \frac{(x-2)(x+1)}{x-2}>0. \]

The original expression requires

\[ x\ne2. \]

For allowed values, it simplifies to

\[ x+1>0. \]

So

\[ x>-1. \]

But \(x=2\) must still be excluded.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(-1,2)\cup(2,\infty)}. \]

Cancellation does not erase the original domain restriction.

Holes Matter Even When the Sign Does Not Change

In the previous example,

\[ \frac{(x-2)(x+1)}{x-2}, \]

the factor \(x-2\) cancels algebraically, so the sign does not switch at \(x=2\).

But the original rational expression remains undefined there.

This creates a hole in the solution set even though the sign on both sides of \(2\) is the same.

Multiplicity in Rational Inequalities

Multiplicity matters for both numerator and denominator factors.

At a factor of even multiplicity, the sign does not change.

At a factor of odd multiplicity, the sign usually does change.

However, denominator zeros remain excluded regardless of multiplicity.

Example with Repeated Denominator Factor

Consider

\[ \frac{x-1}{(x+2)^2}>0. \]

The denominator is positive for every

\[ x\ne-2. \]

So the sign is controlled by \(x-1\).

The expression is positive when

\[ x>1. \]

The excluded point \(x=-2\) does not affect this positive interval because it lies outside it.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(1,\infty)}. \]

Example Where a Hole Splits an Interval

Consider

\[ \frac{x+3}{(x-1)^2}\ge0. \]

The denominator is positive except at

\[ x=1, \]

where the expression is undefined.

The numerator is nonnegative when

\[ x\ge-3. \]

So the solution would appear to be

\[ [-3,\infty), \]

except \(x=1\) must be removed.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{[-3,1)\cup(1,\infty)}. \]

Graphical Interpretation of Polynomial Inequalities

Solving

\[ f(x)>0 \]

means finding where the graph of

\[ y=f(x) \]

lies above the \(x\)-axis.

Similarly,

\[ f(x)<0 \]

asks where the graph lies below the \(x\)-axis.

The roots are the boundary points where the graph meets the axis.

Graphical Interpretation of Rational Inequalities

For a rational function, sign analysis still asks whether the graph lies above or below the \(x\)-axis.

But denominator zeros may create:

  • vertical asymptotes;
  • holes;
  • or other domain exclusions.

These undefined points must be removed from the solution set even if nearby signs match.

Critical Points Organize the Entire Problem

For polynomial inequalities, critical points are real zeros.

For rational inequalities, critical points include both:

  • zeros of the numerator;
  • zeros of the denominator.

Once these points are placed in order on the number line, every remaining interval has a constant sign.

Why One Test Point Is Enough

Between two consecutive critical points, none of the factors crosses zero or becomes undefined.

Therefore, the sign of each factor remains constant throughout that interval.

So testing one point tells us the sign of the whole interval.

This is why interval testing is efficient rather than guesswork.

Using Factor Signs Without Numerical Substitution

For

\[ (x-2)(x+5), \]

we can reason directly.

If

\[ x<-5, \]

both factors are negative, so the product is positive.

If

\[ -5one factor is positive and the other negative, so the product is negative.

If

\[ x>2, \]

both factors are positive, so the product is positive.

With practice, a sign chart can replace repeated arithmetic substitution.

Do Not Solve Factors Separately with the Same Inequality

From

\[ (x-2)(x+3)>0, \]

it is incorrect to say

\[ x-2>0 \]

and

\[ x+3>0 \]

as the only possibility.

A product is also positive when both factors are negative.

That second possibility produces

\[ x<-3. \]

Sign analysis automatically captures all sign combinations.

Product Sign Logic

For two nonzero factors:

First Factor Second Factor Product
Positive Positive Positive
Positive Negative Negative
Negative Positive Negative
Negative Negative Positive

Rational inequalities use the same sign logic because division has the same sign rules as multiplication.

Quotient Sign Logic

For nonzero numerator and denominator:

  • same signs produce a positive quotient;
  • opposite signs produce a negative quotient.

The difference is that denominator zeros are forbidden rather than ordinary zero boundaries.

Suppose profit is modeled by

\[ P(x)=(x-20)(100-x). \]

To determine when profit is positive, solve

\[ (x-20)(100-x)>0. \]

The critical points are

\[ 20 \]

and

\[ 100. \]

Testing shows the product is positive between them:

Application: Rational Constraint

Suppose a model contains

\[ R(x)=\frac{x-5}{x-10} \]

and requires

\[ R(x)\ge0. \]

The critical points are

\[ x=5 \]

and

\[ x=10. \]

The numerator zero may be included because equality is allowed.

The denominator zero must always be excluded.

Sign analysis determines which remaining intervals satisfy the model.

A Reliable Polynomial-Inequality Workflow

  1. Move all terms to one side.
  2. Factor the polynomial when possible.
  3. Find all real zeros.
  4. Place the zeros in order on a number line.
  5. Determine the sign on each interval.
  6. Select the intervals that satisfy the inequality.
  7. Include zeros only when equality is allowed.

A Reliable Rational-Inequality Workflow

  1. Move everything to one side and combine into one rational expression.
  2. Factor numerator and denominator when useful.
  3. Find numerator zeros and denominator zeros.
  4. Mark denominator zeros as permanently excluded.
  5. Use all critical points to divide the number line.
  6. Determine the sign on each interval.
  7. Include numerator zeros only when equality is allowed.
  8. Never include denominator zeros.

Common Errors

Using the Zero-Product Property as Though the Inequality Were an Equation

The zeros locate boundaries but do not by themselves give the full solution set.

Multiplying a Rational Inequality by an Unknown-Sign Denominator

This may require reversing the inequality in some cases and not others. Sign analysis is safer.

Including Denominator Zeros

An undefined value can never belong to the solution set.

Excluding Numerator Zeros from an Inclusive Inequality

If the inequality uses \(\le\) or \(\ge\), numerator zeros may be included.

Ignoring Multiplicity

Even-multiplicity factors often do not cause a sign change.

Forgetting Original Restrictions After Cancellation

A canceled denominator factor can leave a hole that remains excluded.

Testing Only the Critical Points

The solution usually consists of intervals between critical points, so interval signs must be analyzed.

Key Ideas

  • Polynomial and rational inequalities are usually solved by sign analysis across intervals.
  • Move the inequality to one side so the problem becomes a comparison with zero.
  • Polynomial zeros and rational zeros or undefined points form the critical points.
  • Critical points divide the number line into intervals where the sign is constant.
  • One test point is enough to determine the sign on an interval.
  • Odd-multiplicity factors usually change sign; even-multiplicity factors usually do not.
  • For inclusive inequalities, polynomial zeros and rational numerator zeros may be included.
  • Rational denominator zeros are always excluded.
  • Cancellation does not erase original domain restrictions.
  • Blindly multiplying a rational inequality by an unknown-sign denominator can produce errors.
  • Graphically, positive intervals lie above the \(x\)-axis and negative intervals lie below it.
  • Sign charts organize the relationship among factors, zeros, undefined points, and solution intervals.

Where This Leads

Polynomial and rational inequalities extend inequality solving from single linear boundaries to solution sets shaped by several critical points.

The same sign-analysis ideas lead naturally to graph-based inequality solving, systems of inequalities, feasible regions, optimization, and later analysis of where functions are positive, negative, increasing, decreasing, or undefined.
