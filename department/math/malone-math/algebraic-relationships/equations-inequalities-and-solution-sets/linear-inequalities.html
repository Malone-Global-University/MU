Linear Inequalities

Linear inequalities are solved much like linear equations, but their answers are usually intervals rather than single values. The central task is to preserve the ordering relationship while transforming the inequality.

The Central Idea

An equation asks when two expressions are equal.

An inequality asks when one expression is less than, greater than, or possibly equal to another.

For example,

\[ x+3\lt 8 \]

asks for every value of \(x\) that makes the left side smaller than \(8\).

Subtract \(3\) from both sides:

\[ x\lt5. \]

The solution is not one number. It is every real number less than \(5\).

So solving an inequality means describing an entire solution set.

Inequality Symbols

Symbol Meaning
\(\lt\) Less than
\(\gt\) Greater than
\(\le\) Less than or equal to
\(\ge\) Greater than or equal to

The strict symbols \(\lt\) and \(\gt\) exclude the boundary value.

The inclusive symbols \(\le\) and \(\ge\) include it.

One-Step Inequalities

Consider

\[ x+4\gt9. \]

Subtract \(4\) from both sides:

\[ x\gt5. \]

Every number greater than \(5\) satisfies the original inequality.

Addition and Subtraction Preserve Direction

Adding or subtracting the same quantity from both sides does not reverse the inequality.

For example,

\[ x-7\le2 \]

becomes

\[ x\le9 \]

after adding \(7\) to both sides.

Likewise, from

\[ x+6\ge1, \]

subtract \(6\):

\[ x\ge-5. \]

Multiplication by a Positive Number Preserves Direction

Consider

\[ \frac{x}{3}\lt4. \]

Multiply both sides by the positive number \(3\):

\[ x\lt12. \]

Because the multiplier is positive, the order direction remains unchanged.

The Crucial Rule: Negative Multiplication Reverses Order

Suppose

\[ 2\lt5. \]

Multiply both sides by \(-1\):

\[ -2\gt-5. \]

The direction reverses.

Therefore, whenever both sides of an inequality are multiplied or divided by a negative quantity, the inequality symbol must reverse:

\[ \lt\ \longleftrightarrow\ \gt \]

and

\[ \le\ \longleftrightarrow\ \ge. \]

Why the Sign Reverses

Negative multiplication reflects numbers across zero on the number line.

For example,

\[ 3\lt7. \]

After multiplying by \(-1\):

\[ -3\gt-7. \]

The point that was farther right becomes farther left after reflection.

The sign reversal is therefore not an arbitrary rule. It preserves the original ordering relationship after reflection.

One-Step Negative-Coefficient Example

Solve

\[ -4x\lt20. \]

Divide both sides by \(-4\).

Because the divisor is negative, reverse the inequality:

\[ x\gt-5. \]

This reversal is essential.

Another Negative Example

Solve

\[ -3x\ge12. \]

Divide by \(-3\) and reverse the direction:

\[ x\le-4. \]

Two-Step Inequalities

Solve

\[ 3x+5\lt20. \]

Subtract \(5\):

\[ 3x\lt15. \]

Divide by \(3\):

\[ x\lt5. \]

This looks almost identical to solving a linear equation.

Two-Step Inequality with a Negative Coefficient

Solve

\[ -2x+3\gt11. \]

Subtract \(3\):

\[ -2x\gt8. \]

Divide by \(-2\) and reverse the inequality:

\[ x\lt-4. \]

Multistep Inequalities

Consider

\[ 4x+7-2x\le19. \]

Combine like terms:

\[ 2x+7\le19. \]

Subtract \(7\):

\[ 2x\le12. \]

Divide by \(2\):

\[ x\le6. \]

Distribution in Inequalities

Solve

\[ 3(x-2)\gt12. \]

We could divide by \(3\) first:

\[ x-2\gt4, \]

then add \(2\):

\[ x\gt6. \]

Or distribute first:

\[ 3x-6\gt12, \]

then solve.

Both methods preserve the same solution set.

Negative Distribution

Consider

\[ -2(x+3)\le10. \]

One approach is to divide by \(-2\) first.

Reverse the inequality:

\[ x+3\ge-5. \]

Subtract \(3\):

\[ x\ge-8. \]

This avoids distributing a negative coefficient and can reduce sign errors.

Variables on Both Sides

Solve

\[ 5x+2\gt3x+10. \]

Subtract \(3x\):

\[ 2x+2\gt10. \]

Subtract \(2\):

\[ 2x\gt8. \]

Divide by \(2\):

\[ x\gt4. \]

Choose the Variable Side Strategically

Consider

\[ 2x+7\lt5x-8. \]

Subtract \(2x\):

\[ 7\lt3x-8. \]

Add \(8\):

\[ 15\lt3x. \]

Divide by \(3\):

\[ 5\lt x. \]

More conventionally, write

\[ x\gt5. \]

Choosing a route that keeps the variable coefficient positive can make sign handling easier.

Variables on Both Sides with a Negative Final Coefficient

Solve

\[ 7x+4\le3x-12. \]

Subtract \(7x\):

\[ 4\le-4x-12. \]

Add \(12\):

\[ 16\le-4x. \]

Divide by \(-4\) and reverse the inequality:

\[ -4\ge x. \]

So

\[ x\le-4. \]

Fractions in Linear Inequalities

Solve

\[ \frac{x}{3}+\frac12\gt2. \]

Subtract \(\frac12\):

\[ \frac{x}{3}\gt\frac32. \]

Multiply by \(3\):

\[ x\gt\frac92. \]

Clearing Positive Denominators

For

\[ \frac{x}{4}+\frac{x}{2}\le9, \]

multiply every term by the positive number \(4\):

\[ x+2x\le36. \]

Then

\[ 3x\le36, \]

so

\[ x\le12. \]

Because \(4\) is positive, the inequality direction stays unchanged.

Be Careful with Unknown-Sign Multipliers

Suppose

\[ ax\lt b. \]

We cannot automatically divide by \(a\) without knowing its sign.

If

\[ a\gt0, \]

then

\[ x\lt\frac{b}{a}. \]

If

\[ a\lt0, \]

then the inequality reverses:

\[ x\gt\frac{b}{a}. \]

If

\[ a=0, \]

the equation must be analyzed separately.

Parameter inequalities naturally require case analysis.

Solution Sets on the Number Line

The solution

\[ x\lt5 \]

contains every real number to the left of \(5\).

The boundary value \(5\) is excluded.

For

\[ x\le5, \]

the same leftward region is included, but now \(5\) belongs to the solution set.

Inequalities naturally describe regions rather than isolated points.

Open and Closed Boundaries

On a number line:

  • \(x\lt a\) or \(x\gt a\) uses an open boundary because \(a\) is excluded.
  • \(x\le a\) or \(x\ge a\) uses a closed boundary because \(a\) is included.

This same distinction appears in interval notation.

Interval Notation

The inequality

\[ x\lt5 \]

can be written as

\[ (-\infty,5). \]

The inequality

\[ x\le5 \]

becomes

\[ (-\infty,5]. \]

Similarly,

\[ x\gt2 \]

becomes

\[ (2,\infty), \]

and

\[ x\ge2 \]

becomes

\[ [2,\infty). \]

Why Infinity Always Uses Parentheses

The symbols

\[ \infty \]

and

\[ -\infty \]

do not represent ordinary endpoint numbers that can be included.

Therefore, intervals extending without bound always use parentheses at infinity:

\[ (-\infty,4] \]

or

\[ (7,\infty). \]

Testing a Solution

Suppose we solve

\[ 2x+1\lt9 \]

and obtain

\[ x\lt4. \]

Choose a value inside the solution set, such as \(x=0\):

\[ 2(0)+1=1, \]

and

\[ 1\lt9. \]

Choose a value outside, such as \(x=5\):

\[ 2(5)+1=11, \]

and

\[ 11\not\lt9. \]

Testing values can help catch algebraic mistakes.

Boundary Testing

For

\[ x\le4, \]

the boundary value \(4\) should satisfy the original inequality.

For

\[ x\lt4, \]

the boundary value should make the two sides equal but should not count as a solution.

Boundary testing is especially useful when checking strict versus inclusive symbols.

Inequalities That Become Identities

Consider

\[ 2(x+3)\le2x+6. \]

Expand:

\[ 2x+6\le2x+6. \]

Subtract \(2x\):

\[ 6\le6. \]

This is always true.

Therefore, every real number is a solution:

\[ \mathbb{R}. \]

Inequalities That Become Contradictions

Consider

\[ 3(x+2)\gt3x+8. \]

Expand:

\[ 3x+6\gt3x+8. \]

Subtract \(3x\):

\[ 6\gt8. \]

This is false.

Therefore, the solution set is

\[ \varnothing. \]

Strict Identity Cases

Consider

\[ 4x+5\lt4x+5. \]

Subtract \(4x\):

\[ 5\lt5. \]

This is never true.

So

\[ \varnothing \]

is the solution set.

By contrast,

\[ 4x+5\le4x+5 \]

is true for every real \(x\).

Three Possible Outcomes

A linear inequality may produce:

Outcome Example Final Form Solution Set
A proper interval \(x\lt4\) \((-\infty,4)\)
All real numbers \(3\le3\) \(\mathbb{R}\)
No solution \(7\lt2\) \(\varnothing\)

Equivalent Inequalities

Two inequalities are equivalent when they have exactly the same solution set.

For example,

\[ 2x+4\lt10 \]

is equivalent to

\[ 2x\lt6, \]

which is equivalent to

\[ x\lt3. \]

We may write

\[ 2x+4\lt10 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2x\lt6 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\lt3. \]

Negative Multiplication Still Preserves Equivalence When Reversed

Consider

\[ -2x\lt8. \]

Dividing by \(-2\) and reversing the inequality gives

\[ x\gt-4. \]

These are equivalent inequalities:

\[ -2x\lt8 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\gt-4. \]

The sign reversal is what preserves equivalence.

Graphical Interpretation

Solving

\[ 2x+1\lt7 \]

means finding where the graph

\[ y=2x+1 \]

lies below the horizontal line

\[ y=7. \]

The two graphs are equal when

\[ 2x+1=7, \]

so

\[ x=3. \]

For values less than \(3\), the line \(y=2x+1\) lies below \(7\).

Thus,

\[ x\lt3. \]

Contextual Inequalities

Suppose a venue can hold at most \(250\) people.

If \(n\) is attendance, then

\[ n\le250. \]

The inequality expresses a capacity constraint rather than a single target value.

Many real-world restrictions naturally produce inequalities:

  • budgets;
  • maximum weights;
  • minimum scores;
  • speed limits;
  • temperature ranges;
  • production capacities;
  • error tolerances.

Application: Budget Constraint

Suppose tickets cost \$12 each and you have at most \$90.

If \(n\) is the number of tickets, then

\[ 12n\le90. \]

Divide by \(12\):

\[ n\le7.5. \]

But \(n\) counts whole tickets, so the contextual solution is

\[ n\le7 \]

for nonnegative integers \(n\).

The algebraic solution must be interpreted within the domain of the model.

Application: Minimum Requirement

Suppose a course requires an average of at least \(70\).

If \(a\) is the average, then

\[ a\ge70. \]

The phrase at least corresponds to \(\ge\).

The phrase at most corresponds to \(\le\).

Language and Inequality Symbols

Phrase Typical Symbol
Less than \(\lt\)
More than / greater than \(\gt\)
At most / no more than \(\le\)
At least / no less than \(\ge\)
Below Usually \(\lt\)
Above Usually \(\gt\)

Worked Example: Multistep Inequality

Solve

\[ 5x-7\ge18. \]

Add \(7\):

\[ 5x\ge25. \]

Divide by \(5\):

\[ x\ge5. \]

Interval notation:

\[ [5,\infty). \]

Worked Example: Negative Coefficient

Solve

\[ -6x+4\lt22. \]

Subtract \(4\):

\[ -6x\lt18. \]

Divide by \(-6\) and reverse the inequality:

\[ x\gt-3. \]

Interval notation:

\[ (-3,\infty). \]

Worked Example: Variables on Both Sides

Solve

\[ 8x+3\le5x+18. \]

Subtract \(5x\):

\[ 3x+3\le18. \]

Subtract \(3\):

\[ 3x\le15. \]

Divide by \(3\):

\[ x\le5. \]

Interval notation:

\[ (-\infty,5]. \]

Worked Example: Identity

Solve

\[ 4(x+2)-3\ge4x+5. \]

Expand:

\[ 4x+8-3\ge4x+5. \]

Simplify:

\[ 4x+5\ge4x+5. \]

Subtract \(4x\):

\[ 5\ge5. \]

This is always true.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. \]

Worked Example: Contradiction

Solve

\[ 2(x+4)\lt2x+3. \]

Expand:

\[ 2x+8\lt2x+3. \]

Subtract \(2x\):

\[ 8\lt3. \]

This is false.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\varnothing}. \]

A Reliable Solving Strategy

  1. Simplify each side.
  2. Collect variable terms on one side.
  3. Collect constants on the other side.
  4. Isolate the variable.
  5. Reverse the inequality whenever multiplying or dividing by a negative quantity.
  6. Interpret the result as a solution set.
  7. Write interval notation or graph the region when useful.
  8. Apply contextual restrictions such as integer counts or physical bounds.

Common Errors

Forgetting to Reverse the Inequality

From

\[ -2x\lt6, \]

the correct result is

\[ x\gt-3. \]

Reversing the Sign During Addition or Subtraction

Adding or subtracting the same quantity does not reverse the inequality.

Reversing Because a Negative Number Appears Somewhere

The symbol reverses only when the entire inequality is multiplied or divided by a negative quantity.

Using Brackets with Infinity

Infinity always uses parentheses in interval notation.

Ignoring Whether the Endpoint Is Included

The symbols \(\le\) and \(\ge\) include the boundary; \(\lt\) and \(\gt\) do not.

Forgetting Context

If a variable counts people, objects, or tickets, the real-number interval may need to be restricted to appropriate integers.

Key Ideas

  • Linear inequalities usually describe intervals or regions rather than single values.
  • Addition and subtraction preserve the direction of an inequality.
  • Multiplication or division by a positive quantity preserves the direction.
  • Multiplication or division by a negative quantity reverses the inequality.
  • The reversal rule reflects the order-reversing effect of multiplying by a negative number on the number line.
  • Variables can appear on both sides and can be collected just as in equations.
  • Strict inequalities exclude boundary values; inclusive inequalities include them.
  • Solutions can be represented symbolically, with interval notation, or as regions on a number line.
  • An inequality may be true for every value, false for every value, or true only on a proper subset of the domain.
  • Unknown-sign symbolic divisors require case analysis.
  • Testing interior, exterior, and boundary values can help verify a solution set.
  • Real-world inequalities often represent limits, minimums, maximums, capacities, and allowable ranges.

Where This Leads

Linear inequalities extend equation solving from individual solution values to ordered regions of the number line.

This prepares the way for compound inequalities, absolute-value inequalities, polynomial and rational inequalities, systems of inequalities, feasible regions, optimization, and the broader study of mathematical constraints.
