Higher-Degree Polynomial Equations

Polynomial equations of degree \(3\) and above often cannot be solved by a single universal elementary method. Instead, they are attacked by recognizing structure: factor out common terms, use special forms, make substitutions, test known roots, divide out factors, and reduce the remaining equation to a lower degree.

The Central Idea

A polynomial equation of degree \(n\) has the form

\[ P(x)=0, \]

where the highest exponent appearing in \(P(x)\) is \(n\).

Examples include

\[ x^3-4x=0, \] \[ x^4-5x^2+4=0, \]

and

\[ 2x^5-3x^4-8x+12=0. \]

The main strategy is usually

\[ \boxed{\text{reduce a difficult equation to simpler equations}}. \]

Degree Matters

Quadratic equations have degree \(2\) and can always be solved by the quadratic formula.

Higher-degree equations are different.

Some factor easily. Some contain useful substitutions. Some require a known or tested root before synthetic division can reduce the degree.

The structure of the polynomial therefore determines the practical method.

Start by Setting the Equation Equal to Zero

Factoring methods work most naturally when the equation is written as

\[ P(x)=0. \]

For example,

\[ x^3+2x^2=8x \]

should first become

\[ x^3+2x^2-8x=0. \]

Then the full polynomial structure is visible.

Always Check for a GCF First

Consider

\[ x^3-9x=0. \]

Factor the GCF:

\[ x(x^2-9)=0. \]

Then factor the difference of squares:

\[ x(x-3)(x+3)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=0,\ 3,\ -3}. \]

The Zero-Product Principle Still Drives the Solution

If

\[ A(x)B(x)C(x)=0, \]

then at least one factor must equal zero:

\[ A(x)=0, \] \[ B(x)=0, \]

or

\[ C(x)=0. \]

Higher-degree solving therefore often becomes a factoring problem first and a lower-degree equation problem second.

Example: Cubic Factoring

Solve

\[ x^3-4x^2-5x=0. \]

Factor \(x\):

\[ x(x^2-4x-5)=0. \]

Factor the quadratic:

\[ x(x-5)(x+1)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=0,\ 5,\ -1}. \]

Grouping Can Solve Cubics

Consider

\[ x^3+2x^2-9x-18=0. \]

Group:

\[ (x^3+2x^2)+(-9x-18)=0. \]

Factor each group:

\[ x^2(x+2)-9(x+2)=0. \]

Factor the shared binomial:

\[ (x+2)(x^2-9)=0. \]

Then

\[ (x+2)(x-3)(x+3)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=-2,\ 3,\ -3}. \]

Special Forms Still Matter

Higher-degree equations can contain familiar factoring patterns.

Solve

\[ x^4-16=0. \]

Use a difference of squares:

\[ (x^2-4)(x^2+4)=0. \]

Then

\[ (x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)=0. \]

Over the real numbers, the real solutions are

\[ \boxed{x=\pm2}. \]

Over the complex numbers, \(x^2+4=0\) also gives

\[ \boxed{x=\pm2i}. \]

Equations That Are Quadratic in Form

Some higher-degree equations are really quadratic equations in disguise.

Consider

\[ x^4-5x^2+4=0. \]

The powers are

\[ x^4=(x^2)^2 \]

and

\[ x^2. \]

This suggests the substitution

\[ \boxed{u=x^2}. \]

Use the Substitution

Replace

\[ x^4 \]

with

\[ u^2 \]

and

\[ x^2 \]

with

\[ u. \]

Then

\[ u^2-5u+4=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (u-1)(u-4)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ u=1 \]

or

\[ u=4. \]

Substitute Back

Since

\[ u=x^2, \]

solve

\[ x^2=1 \]

and

\[ x^2=4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=\pm1,\ \pm2}. \]

Recognizing Quadratic-in-Form Structure

Equations such as

\[ x^6-7x^3+6=0 \]

can also be quadratic in form because

\[ x^6=(x^3)^2. \]

The substitution

\[ u=x^3 \]

reduces the equation to

\[ u^2-7u+6=0. \]

Example: Substitution with Cubic Powers

Solve

\[ x^6-7x^3+6=0. \]

Let

\[ u=x^3. \]

Then

\[ u^2-7u+6=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (u-1)(u-6)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ x^3=1 \]

or

\[ x^3=6. \]

The real solutions are

\[ \boxed{x=1,\ \sqrt[3]{6}}. \]

Known Roots and the Factor Theorem

If

\[ P(c)=0, \]

then

\[ x-c \]

is a factor of \(P(x)\).

This lets us reduce the degree of a higher-degree polynomial by synthetic division.

Example: A Known Root

Suppose

\[ P(x)=x^3-4x^2+x+6 \]

and we know

\[ x=2 \]

is a root.

Therefore,

\[ x-2 \]

is a factor.

Use synthetic division with coefficients

\[ 1,\ -4,\ 1,\ 6. \]

Reduce the Degree

Synthetic division by \(2\) gives

\[ 1,\ -2,\ -3,\ 0. \]

Therefore,

\[ P(x)=(x-2)(x^2-2x-3). \]

Factor the quadratic:

\[ x^2-2x-3=(x-3)(x+1). \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{x=2,\ 3,\ -1}. \]

Testing a Candidate Root

Sometimes a possible root is suggested by inspection, a graph, or another method.

To test whether \(c\) is a root, calculate

\[ P(c). \]

If

\[ P(c)=0, \]

then synthetic division by

\[ x-c \]

reduces the degree.

Example: Test and Divide

Solve

\[ x^3-6x^2+11x-6=0. \]

Test \(x=1\):

\[ 1-6+11-6=0. \]

Therefore, \(x-1\) is a factor.

Synthetic division gives

\[ x^2-5x+6. \]

Factor:

\[ x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=1,\ 2,\ 3}. \]

Repeated Degree Reduction

A higher-degree polynomial can sometimes be reduced one linear factor at a time.

For example, a quartic

\[ P(x) \]

with known factor \(x-a\) becomes

\[ P(x)=(x-a)Q_3(x), \]

where \(Q_3\) is cubic.

If another root of \(Q_3\) is found, division reduces the problem to a quadratic, which can then be solved by familiar methods.

Example: Quartic Reduced to Quadratic

Solve

\[ x^4-5x^3+5x^2+5x-6=0. \]

Test \(x=1\):

\[ 1-5+5+5-6=0. \]

Therefore, \(x-1\) is a factor.

Synthetic division gives

\[ x^3-4x^2+x+6. \]

Continue the Quartic Example

Test \(x=2\) in the cubic:

\[ 8-16+2+6=0. \]

Therefore, divide by \(x-2\).

The quotient is

\[ x^2-2x-3. \]

Factor:

\[ (x-3)(x+1). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{x=1,\ 2,\ 3,\ -1}. \]

Use the Simplest Available Structure First

Suppose we encounter

\[ x^5-4x^3=0. \]

Synthetic division is unnecessary.

Factor directly:

\[ x^3(x^2-4)=0. \]

Then

\[ x^3(x-2)(x+2)=0. \]

The solutions are

\[ \boxed{x=0,\ 2,\ -2}. \]

Recognizing visible factors is more efficient than using a heavier method.

Substitution Can Reveal Hidden Factoring

Consider

\[ x^8-10x^4+9=0. \]

Let

\[ u=x^4. \]

Then

\[ u^2-10u+9=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (u-1)(u-9)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ x^4=1 \]

or

\[ x^4=9. \]

Finish the Real Solutions

From

\[ x^4=1, \]

the real solutions are

\[ x=\pm1. \]

From

\[ x^4=9, \]

the real solutions are

\[ x=\pm\sqrt3. \]

Therefore, the real solution set is

\[ \boxed{x=\pm1,\ \pm\sqrt3}. \]

Be Clear About the Number System

A higher-degree equation may have additional complex solutions beyond the real solutions found by a real-variable method.

For example,

\[ x^4=1 \]

has real solutions

\[ \pm1, \]

but over the complex numbers it also has

\[ \pm i. \]

Always note whether a problem asks for real solutions or all complex solutions.

Solving a Biquadratic Equation

A polynomial involving only

\[ x^4,\quad x^2,\quad 1 \]

is often called biquadratic.

It can frequently be solved by

\[ u=x^2. \]

This transforms a degree-\(4\) equation into a quadratic equation in \(u\).

Example: Biquadratic with No Real Solutions from One Branch

Solve over the real numbers:

\[ x^4+3x^2-4=0. \]

Let

\[ u=x^2. \]

Then

\[ u^2+3u-4=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (u+4)(u-1)=0. \]

So

\[ u=-4 \]

or

\[ u=1. \]

Interpret the Substitution Correctly

Since

\[ u=x^2, \]

the branch

\[ x^2=-4 \]

has no real solutions.

The branch

\[ x^2=1 \]

gives

\[ x=\pm1. \]

Therefore, over the real numbers,

\[ \boxed{x=\pm1}. \]

Over the complex numbers, the other branch also gives

\[ x=\pm2i. \]

Known Factor Versus Known Root

These statements are equivalent:

\[ x-c\text{ is a factor} \]

and

\[ c\text{ is a root}. \]

Therefore, if a problem gives either one, we immediately obtain the other.

This allows synthetic division to reduce the degree.

A Root Does Not Solve the Whole Polynomial Automatically

Finding one root of a quartic does not mean the problem is finished.

It means one linear factor has been found.

The remaining quotient must still be solved.

A complete solution requires every factor or every remaining equation to be addressed.

Verification by Substitution

If \(r\) is claimed to solve

\[ P(x)=0, \]

substitute \(r\) into \(P\).

A correct solution satisfies

\[ \boxed{P(r)=0}. \]

This works for real or complex roots.

Verification by Rebuilding the Factorization

If the roots are found through factoring, multiply the factors to recover the original polynomial.

For example,

\[ (x-1)(x-2)(x-3) \]

expands to

\[ x^3-6x^2+11x-6. \]

This verifies the complete factorization.

Common Error: Forgetting to Set the Equation Equal to Zero

The zero-product principle cannot be used directly on

\[ x^3-4x=12. \]

First rewrite:

\[ x^3-4x-12=0. \]

Only then can factor-based solving be applied.

Common Error: Missing the GCF

Consider

\[ 3x^4-12x^2=0. \]

The first step should be

\[ 3x^2(x^2-4)=0, \]

not an unnecessarily complicated root test.

Visible structure should be used before numerical searching.

Common Error: Forgetting to Substitute Back

If

\[ u=x^2 \]

and the reduced equation gives

\[ u=9, \]

then \(x\) is not simply \(9\).

We must return to

\[ x^2=9, \]

which gives

\[ \boxed{x=\pm3}. \]

Common Error: Keeping an Invalid Real Branch

If a real-number problem leads to

\[ x^2=-5, \]

that branch has no real solutions.

If complex solutions are allowed, it gives

\[ x=\pm i\sqrt5. \]

The number system matters.

Common Error: Stopping After Finding One Factor

Suppose

\[ P(x)=(x-2)(x^2-5x+6). \]

Finding

\[ x=2 \]

is only part of the solution.

The quadratic still factors:

\[ x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3). \]

Complete solving requires continuing through the remaining factor.

Common Error: Assuming Every Higher-Degree Polynomial Factors Nicely

Not every cubic, quartic, or higher-degree polynomial has an obvious factorization over the integers.

Some equations require more advanced methods, numerical approximation, or additional theorems.

The methods in this lesson are powerful structural tools, but they are not a guarantee that every higher-degree equation will simplify immediately.

A Practical Solving Strategy

  1. Move all terms to one side.
  2. Factor out the GCF.
  3. Look for special products or grouping.
  4. Check whether the equation is quadratic in form.
  5. Use substitution when powers follow a repeated pattern.
  6. Use a known or tested root to obtain a linear factor.
  7. Use synthetic or polynomial division to reduce the degree.
  8. Solve the remaining lower-degree equation.
  9. Repeat until all required roots are found.
  10. Verify solutions and respect the requested number system.

What to Remember

Higher-degree polynomial equations are usually solved by recognizing structure rather than by one universal elementary formula.

The main tools are:

  • GCF factoring;
  • special-product factoring;
  • factoring by grouping;
  • substitution for equations that are quadratic in form;
  • the Factor Theorem;
  • synthetic division;
  • degree reduction;
  • quadratic methods for the remaining factors.

The guiding principle is

\[ \boxed{\text{reduce the degree until familiar methods apply}}. \]

Always distinguish real solutions from all complex solutions, and verify roots by substitution or by rebuilding the factorization.
