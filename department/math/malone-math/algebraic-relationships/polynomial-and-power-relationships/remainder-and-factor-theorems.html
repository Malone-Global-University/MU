Remainder and Factor Theorems

Division, evaluation, and factoring are tightly connected. When a polynomial \(P(x)\) is divided by \(x-c\), the remainder is exactly \(P(c)\). If that value is zero, then \(x-c\) divides the polynomial exactly and is therefore a factor. These two results turn synthetic division into a powerful tool for testing factors and identifying zeros.

The Central Idea

Suppose a polynomial \(P(x)\) is divided by the linear divisor

\[ x-c. \]

Polynomial division gives

\[ P(x)=(x-c)Q(x)+R. \]

Because the divisor has degree \(1\), the remainder must have degree less than \(1\). Therefore, the remainder is a constant.

Now substitute

\[ x=c. \]

Then

\[ P(c)=(c-c)Q(c)+R. \]

Since

\[ c-c=0, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{P(c)=R}. \]

This is the Remainder Theorem.

The Remainder Theorem

The Remainder Theorem states:

If a polynomial \(P(x)\) is divided by

\[ x-c, \]

then the remainder is

\[ \boxed{P(c)}. \]

This means polynomial evaluation and polynomial division are connected by the same number.

Why the Theorem Is Useful

Without performing full polynomial division, we can determine the remainder from

\[ P(c). \]

For example, if

\[ P(x)=x^3-4x+7, \]

then the remainder when dividing by

\[ x-2 \]

is

\[ P(2). \]

Compute:

\[ P(2)=2^3-4(2)+7. \] \[ P(2)=8-8+7. \] \[ \boxed{P(2)=7}. \]

Therefore, the remainder is

\[ \boxed{7}. \]

Example: Remainder from Direct Evaluation

Find the remainder when

\[ P(x)=2x^4-3x^2+5x-1 \]

is divided by

\[ x+1. \]

Rewrite

\[ x+1=x-(-1). \]

Therefore, use

\[ c=-1. \]

Evaluate:

\[ P(-1) = 2(-1)^4-3(-1)^2+5(-1)-1. \]

Thus,

\[ 2-3-5-1=-7. \]

Therefore, the remainder is

\[ \boxed{-7}. \]

Remainders from Synthetic Division

Synthetic division produces the same remainder.

Suppose we divide

\[ P(x)=x^3+2x^2-5x+4 \]

by

\[ x-3. \]

Use the synthetic number

\[ 3 \]

with coefficients

\[ 1,\quad2,\quad-5,\quad4. \]

Synthetic division gives:

\[ 1,\quad5,\quad10,\quad34. \]

The final number is the remainder:

\[ \boxed{34}. \]

Check with Direct Evaluation

Evaluate

\[ P(3). \]

We get

\[ 3^3+2(3)^2-5(3)+4. \] \[ 27+18-15+4. \] \[ \boxed{34}. \]

The synthetic remainder and the direct evaluation agree.

Synthetic Division as Efficient Evaluation

Because the final synthetic value equals \(P(c)\), synthetic division can be used simply to evaluate a polynomial.

This is especially useful when the polynomial has high degree or many terms.

Instead of repeatedly computing powers such as

\[ c^5,\quad c^4,\quad c^3, \]

synthetic evaluation uses repeated multiplication and addition.

Example: Efficient Evaluation

Evaluate

\[ P(2) \]

for

\[ P(x)=3x^5-4x^4+2x^3-7x+6. \]

Include the missing \(x^2\) coefficient:

\[ 3,\quad-4,\quad2,\quad0,\quad-7,\quad6. \]

Use synthetic evaluation with \(2\).

Bring down \(3\).

Multiply and add:

\[ 3(2)=6,\qquad -4+6=2, \] \[ 2(2)=4,\qquad2+4=6, \] \[ 6(2)=12,\qquad0+12=12, \] \[ 12(2)=24,\qquad-7+24=17, \] \[ 17(2)=34,\qquad6+34=40. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P(2)=40}. \]

The Factor Theorem

The Remainder Theorem immediately leads to another important result.

If dividing \(P(x)\) by

\[ x-c \]

produces remainder zero, then the division is exact.

That means

\[ x-c \]

is a factor of \(P(x)\).

Since the remainder equals \(P(c)\), we obtain the Factor Theorem.

Factor Theorem Statement

The Factor Theorem states:

\[ \boxed{ x-c\text{ is a factor of }P(x) \iff P(c)=0 }. \]

The symbol

\[ \iff \]

means “if and only if.”

Thus, the relationship works in both directions.

From Factor to Zero

If

\[ x-c \]

is a factor of \(P(x)\), then

\[ P(x)=(x-c)Q(x) \]

for some polynomial \(Q(x)\).

Substitute \(x=c\):

\[ P(c)=(c-c)Q(c). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P(c)=0}. \]

From Zero to Factor

If

\[ P(c)=0, \]

then the Remainder Theorem says that the remainder from dividing by

\[ x-c \]

is zero.

Therefore, the division is exact and

\[ \boxed{x-c\text{ is a factor of }P(x)}. \]

Example: Test a Possible Factor

Determine whether

\[ x-2 \]

is a factor of

\[ P(x)=x^3-3x^2-4x+12. \]

Evaluate

\[ P(2). \]

We get

\[ 8-12-8+12. \] \[ \boxed{P(2)=0}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x-2\text{ is a factor of }P(x)}. \]

Find the Remaining Factor

Once the Factor Theorem confirms that

\[ x-2 \]

is a factor, synthetic division can find the quotient.

Use \(2\) with coefficients

\[ 1,\quad-3,\quad-4,\quad12. \]

Synthetic division gives

\[ 1,\quad-1,\quad-6,\quad0. \]

Therefore,

\[ Q(x)=x^2-x-6. \]

So

\[ P(x)=(x-2)(x^2-x-6). \]

Continue the Factorization

The quadratic factor

\[ x^2-x-6 \]

factors as

\[ (x-3)(x+2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ x^3-3x^2-4x+12 = (x-2)(x-3)(x+2) }. \]

The Factor Theorem can therefore become the first step in a larger factorization.

Testing a Nonfactor

Determine whether

\[ x-4 \]

is a factor of

\[ P(x)=x^3-3x^2-4x+12. \]

Evaluate

\[ P(4). \]

Then

\[ 64-48-16+12=12. \]

Since

\[ P(4)\neq0, \]

we conclude

\[ \boxed{x-4\text{ is not a factor}}. \]

The remainder from division by \(x-4\) is \(12\).

The Sign Connection

If the possible factor is

\[ x+5, \]

write it as

\[ x-(-5). \]

Therefore, test

\[ \boxed{P(-5)}. \]

The sign in the factor and the number substituted into the polynomial are opposites.

Example with a Positive Binomial Factor

Determine whether

\[ x+3 \]

is a factor of

\[ P(x)=x^3+4x^2-3x-18. \]

Test

\[ P(-3). \]

Compute:

\[ (-3)^3+4(-3)^2-3(-3)-18. \] \[ -27+36+9-18. \] \[ \boxed{0}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x+3\text{ is a factor}}. \]

Factors and Zeros

If

\[ P(c)=0, \]

then \(c\) is called a zero of the polynomial function.

By the Factor Theorem,

\[ P(c)=0 \]

is equivalent to saying

\[ x-c \]

is a factor.

Thus:

\[ \boxed{ c\text{ is a zero} \iff x-c\text{ is a factor} }. \]

Zeros and Graphs

If

\[ P(c)=0, \]

then the point

\[ (c,0) \]

lies on the graph of

\[ y=P(x). \]

Therefore, a real zero is an \(x\)-intercept of the polynomial graph.

This gives a three-way connection:

\[ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \iff x-c\text{ is a factor} \iff (c,0)\text{ lies on the graph} }. \]

Example: Factor, Zero, and Intercept

Suppose

\[ P(x)=(x-4)(x+1). \]

Then

\[ P(4)=0 \]

and

\[ P(-1)=0. \]

Therefore, the zeros are

\[ 4 \]

and

\[ -1. \]

The graph has \(x\)-intercepts at

\[ \boxed{(4,0)} \]

and

\[ \boxed{(-1,0)}. \]

Factor Testing with Synthetic Division

Suppose we want to test whether \(x-3\) is a factor of

\[ P(x)=2x^4-x^3-13x^2+4x+12. \]

Use synthetic division with

\[ c=3. \]

Coefficients:

\[ 2,\quad-1,\quad-13,\quad4,\quad12. \]

Bring down \(2\).

Multiply and add:

\[ 2(3)=6,\qquad -1+6=5, \] \[ 5(3)=15,\qquad -13+15=2, \] \[ 2(3)=6,\qquad4+6=10, \] \[ 10(3)=30,\qquad12+30=42. \]

The remainder is

\[ \boxed{42}. \]

Therefore, \(x-3\) is not a factor.

Factor Testing Without Full Quotient Interpretation

If the only question is whether \(x-c\) is a factor, we care only about whether the synthetic remainder is zero.

We do not need to interpret the quotient unless the remainder is zero and we want to continue factoring.

This can save substantial work when testing several possible factors.

Using the Theorem to Find an Unknown Coefficient

The Factor Theorem can also determine unknown coefficients.

Suppose

\[ P(x)=x^3+kx^2-4x+8 \]

and we are told that

\[ x-2 \]

is a factor.

Then

\[ P(2)=0. \]

Solve for the Unknown Coefficient

Substitute \(x=2\):

\[ 2^3+k(2)^2-4(2)+8=0. \]

Simplify:

\[ 8+4k-8+8=0. \] \[ 4k+8=0. \] \[ 4k=-8. \] \[ \boxed{k=-2}. \]

Thus the factor condition determines the missing coefficient.

Using a Known Remainder to Find an Unknown

Suppose

\[ P(x)=2x^3+ax^2-5x+1 \]

leaves remainder \(7\) when divided by

\[ x-2. \]

By the Remainder Theorem,

\[ P(2)=7. \]

Therefore,

\[ 2(8)+4a-10+1=7. \] \[ 16+4a-9=7. \] \[ 4a+7=7. \] \[ \boxed{a=0}. \]

Remainder Theorem as a Shortcut

Suppose the problem asks only:

“What is the remainder when \(P(x)\) is divided by \(x-4\)?”

Full long division is unnecessary.

Simply calculate

\[ \boxed{P(4)}. \]

Choosing the method that matches the question is an important part of efficient algebra.

Factor Theorem as a Decision Test

Suppose the question asks:

“Is \(x+2\) a factor of \(P(x)\)?”

The most direct test is

\[ \boxed{P(-2)}. \]

If the result is zero, the factor works.

If the result is not zero, the factor does not work.

Several Candidate Factors

Suppose we want to determine which of

\[ x-1,\qquad x-2,\qquad x+2 \]

are factors of

\[ P(x)=x^3-x^2-4x+4. \]

Test the corresponding values:

\[ P(1)=1-1-4+4=0, \] \[ P(2)=8-4-8+4=0, \] \[ P(-2)=-8-4+8+4=0. \]

Therefore, all three are factors:

\[ \boxed{ P(x)=(x-1)(x-2)(x+2) }. \]

Verification by Expansion

Multiply the last two factors:

\[ (x-2)(x+2)=x^2-4. \]

Then

\[ (x-1)(x^2-4) \]

gives

\[ x^3-x^2-4x+4. \]

The original polynomial is recovered.

Known Factors Reduce the Polynomial Degree

If a degree-\(5\) polynomial has known factor

\[ x-c, \]

synthetic division produces a degree-\(4\) quotient.

Finding one factor therefore reduces the remaining problem to a lower-degree polynomial.

Repeating this process can eventually reduce a higher-degree polynomial to quadratic or linear factors.

Example: Reducing Degree

Suppose

\[ P(x)=x^4-5x^3+5x^2+5x-6 \]

and we know that \(x-1\) is a factor.

Synthetic division by \(x-1\) uses coefficients

\[ 1,\ -5,\ 5,\ 5,\ -6. \]

The result is

\[ 1,\ -4,\ 1,\ 6,\ 0. \]

Therefore,

\[ P(x)=(x-1)(x^3-4x^2+x+6). \]

A degree-\(4\) problem has become a degree-\(3\) problem.

The Theorems and Polynomial Structure

The Remainder and Factor Theorems connect several ideas that were previously studied separately:

  • function evaluation;
  • polynomial division;
  • synthetic division;
  • factoring;
  • zeros;
  • graph intercepts.

This is an important example of mathematical structure: one relationship appears in several different representations.

Equivalent Statements

For a polynomial \(P(x)\) and number \(c\), the following statements are equivalent:

  1. \(P(c)=0\);
  2. the remainder from division by \(x-c\) is \(0\);
  3. \(x-c\) divides \(P(x)\) exactly;
  4. \(x-c\) is a factor of \(P(x)\);
  5. \(c\) is a zero of \(P\);
  6. if \(c\) is real, \((c,0)\) is an \(x\)-intercept of the graph.

Moving fluently among these statements is more powerful than memorizing them separately.

Common Error: Using the Wrong Sign

If the factor is

\[ x+4, \]

the value to test is

\[ \boxed{-4}, \]

not \(4\).

The factor must be written

\[ x-c. \]

Since

\[ x+4=x-(-4), \]

we have

\[ c=-4. \]

Common Error: Confusing Zero with the Remainder Requirement

A nonzero remainder does not mean the division procedure failed.

It simply means the divisor is not a factor.

If

\[ P(c)=5, \]

then the remainder from division by

\[ x-c \]

is

\[ 5. \]

The division is valid; it is just not exact.

Common Error: Forgetting Missing Coefficients

When using synthetic division to evaluate

\[ P(x)=x^4-3x+2, \]

the coefficient list is

\[ \boxed{1,\ 0,\ 0,\ -3,\ 2}. \]

Missing \(x^3\) and \(x^2\) terms require zero placeholders.

Common Error: Assuming One Successful Test Finds All Factors

Showing that

\[ x-2 \]

is a factor identifies only one factor.

The quotient may contain additional factors.

Continue factoring or testing the reduced polynomial when the problem asks for complete factorization.

Common Error: Treating Every \(x\)-Intercept the Same Way

The Factor Theorem tells us that a real zero corresponds to a real \(x\)-intercept.

Later we will see that repeated factors can affect how the graph behaves at that intercept.

The graph may cross the axis or touch and turn depending on multiplicity.

That distinction belongs to the later lesson on Zeros, Roots, Factors, and Multiplicity.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding a Remainder

  1. Write the divisor in the form \(x-c\).
  2. Identify \(c\).
  3. Evaluate \(P(c)\) directly or synthetically.
  4. The result is the remainder.

A Reliable Procedure for Testing a Factor

  1. Write the possible factor in the form \(x-c\).
  2. Identify \(c\).
  3. Compute \(P(c)\).
  4. If \(P(c)=0\), then \(x-c\) is a factor.
  5. If \(P(c)\neq0\), then \(x-c\) is not a factor.
  6. If the factor works and further factorization is needed, use synthetic division to find the quotient.

Example: Complete Factor-Test Workflow

Determine whether

\[ x+2 \]

is a factor of

\[ P(x)=x^3+x^2-4x-4. \]

Since

\[ x+2=x-(-2), \]

evaluate

\[ P(-2). \]

We get

\[ -8+4+8-4=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ x+2 \]

is a factor.

Continue the Complete Workflow

Use synthetic division with \(-2\):

\[ 1,\quad1,\quad-4,\quad-4. \]

The synthetic result is

\[ 1,\quad-1,\quad-2,\quad0. \]

Therefore,

\[ P(x)=(x+2)(x^2-x-2). \]

Factor the quadratic:

\[ x^2-x-2=(x-2)(x+1). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ P(x)=(x+2)(x-2)(x+1) }. \]

What to Remember

The Remainder Theorem states:

\[ \boxed{ \text{remainder from dividing }P(x)\text{ by }x-c = P(c) }. \]

The Factor Theorem states:

\[ \boxed{ x-c\text{ is a factor of }P(x) \iff P(c)=0 }. \]

These results connect:

  • evaluation;
  • division;
  • synthetic division;
  • factors;
  • zeros;
  • graph intercepts.

A nonzero value \(P(c)\) is the remainder. A zero value means exact divisibility by \(x-c\).
