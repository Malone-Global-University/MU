Polynomial Addition, Subtraction, and Multiplication

Polynomials can be combined using the same arithmetic ideas already used with numbers and algebraic expressions. Addition and subtraction operate coefficient by coefficient on like powers, while multiplication combines every term of one polynomial with every term of the other. Standard form gives us the structure needed to carry out these operations reliably.

The Central Idea

A polynomial is a structured sum of power terms. Because each term is associated with a particular power of the variable, polynomial arithmetic can be organized around those powers.

Consider

\[ P(x)=3x^3-2x+5 \]

and

\[ Q(x)=x^3+4x^2-7. \]

Written with missing powers made explicit,

\[ P(x)=3x^3+0x^2-2x+5 \]

and

\[ Q(x)=x^3+4x^2+0x-7. \]

Addition and subtraction combine coefficients attached to the same power. Multiplication is broader: each term in one polynomial multiplies every term in the other.

Polynomials Are Closed Under These Operations

If \(P(x)\) and \(Q(x)\) are polynomials, then

\[ P(x)+Q(x), \] \[ P(x)-Q(x), \]

and

\[ P(x)Q(x) \]

are also polynomials.

We say that polynomials are closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication.

These operations never require us to introduce negative powers, fractional powers, or variables in denominators. We remain inside the polynomial family.

Like Terms Are the Key to Addition and Subtraction

Two terms are like terms when they have the same variable part with the same exponents.

For example,

\[ 3x^4 \]

and

\[ -7x^4 \]

are like terms because both contain \(x^4\).

Their coefficients can be combined:

\[ 3x^4-7x^4=(3-7)x^4=-4x^4. \]

By contrast,

\[ 3x^4 \]

and

\[ 3x^3 \]

are not like terms. Their powers differ, so they cannot be combined by addition or subtraction.

Why Like Terms Combine

Combining like terms is an application of the distributive property.

Consider

\[ 5x^2+3x^2. \]

Both terms contain the common factor \(x^2\), so

\[ 5x^2+3x^2=(5+3)x^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{5x^2+3x^2=8x^2}. \]

The power does not change because we are adding coefficients, not multiplying the powers.

Adding Polynomials

To add polynomials, combine all like terms.

Suppose

\[ P(x)=3x^3+2x^2-5x+4 \]

and

\[ Q(x)=2x^3-6x^2+7x-9. \]

Then

\[ P(x)+Q(x) \]

is

\[ (3x^3+2x^2-5x+4) +(2x^3-6x^2+7x-9). \]

Group like powers:

\[ (3x^3+2x^3) +(2x^2-6x^2) +(-5x+7x) +(4-9). \]

Combine the coefficients:

\[ 5x^3-4x^2+2x-5. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P(x)+Q(x)=5x^3-4x^2+2x-5}. \]

Addition as Coefficient Arithmetic

The previous example can be viewed entirely through coefficients.

The coefficient lists are

\[ P:\quad(3,2,-5,4) \]

and

\[ Q:\quad(2,-6,7,-9). \]

Add corresponding entries:

\[ (3+2,\ 2-6,\ -5+7,\ 4-9). \]

This gives

\[ (5,-4,2,-5), \]

corresponding to

\[ \boxed{5x^3-4x^2+2x-5}. \]

This coefficient-by-coefficient viewpoint is one reason standard form is so useful.

Adding Polynomials with Missing Powers

Missing powers represent zero coefficients.

Suppose

\[ P(x)=4x^5-3x^2+1 \]

and

\[ Q(x)=2x^4+7x^2-5x+6. \]

Exposing the missing powers gives

\[ P(x)=4x^5+0x^4+0x^3-3x^2+0x+1 \]

and

\[ Q(x)=0x^5+2x^4+0x^3+7x^2-5x+6. \]

Add corresponding coefficients:

\[ \boxed{ P(x)+Q(x) = 4x^5+2x^4+4x^2-5x+7 }. \]

Vertical Addition

Polynomials may also be arranged vertically. Like powers should occupy the same column.

\[ \begin{array}{rrrrrrrr} & 3x^4 & -2x^3 & +5x^2 & & -7 \\ + & 2x^4 & & -3x^2 & +4x & +1 \\ \hline & 5x^4 & -2x^3 & +2x^2 & +4x & -6 \end{array} \]

The vertical method is especially useful when polynomials contain many terms because the columns make the coefficient alignment visible.

Commutativity of Polynomial Addition

Because ordinary number addition is commutative, polynomial addition is also commutative:

\[ \boxed{P(x)+Q(x)=Q(x)+P(x)}. \]

For example,

\[ (x^2+3x)+(2x^2-5) \]

and

\[ (2x^2-5)+(x^2+3x) \]

both simplify to

\[ 3x^2+3x-5. \]

Associativity of Polynomial Addition

Polynomial addition is also associative:

\[ \boxed{(P+Q)+R=P+(Q+R)}. \]

This means that when several polynomials are being added, we may regroup them without changing the result.

The familiar laws of arithmetic continue to operate on these larger algebraic objects.

Subtracting Polynomials

Polynomial subtraction is most reliable when we treat subtraction as addition of an additive inverse.

Suppose

\[ P(x)=5x^3-2x^2+4x-1 \]

and

\[ Q(x)=2x^3+3x^2-x+7. \]

We want

\[ P(x)-Q(x). \]

Write the subtraction explicitly:

\[ (5x^3-2x^2+4x-1) -(2x^3+3x^2-x+7). \]

Distribute the Negative Sign First

Subtracting the entire polynomial \(Q(x)\) means changing the sign of every term in \(Q(x)\).

\[ -(2x^3+3x^2-x+7) = -2x^3-3x^2+x-7. \]

Therefore,

\[ P(x)-Q(x) = 5x^3-2x^2+4x-1 -2x^3-3x^2+x-7. \]

Combine like terms:

\[ \boxed{ P(x)-Q(x) = 3x^3-5x^2+5x-8 }. \]

Subtraction as Addition of the Additive Inverse

The same operation can be written

\[ P(x)-Q(x)=P(x)+[-Q(x)]. \]

If

\[ Q(x)=2x^3+3x^2-x+7, \]

then its additive inverse is

\[ -Q(x)=-2x^3-3x^2+x-7. \]

Thus subtraction is not a completely separate operation. It is addition after replacing the second polynomial by its opposite.

Why Parentheses Matter in Subtraction

Consider

\[ (3x^2+4x-5)-(x^2-2x+7). \]

The negative sign applies to the entire second polynomial:

\[ 3x^2+4x-5-x^2+2x-7. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{2x^2+6x-12}. \]

A common mistake is to change only the sign of the first term in the second polynomial. Parentheses help show that every term is being subtracted.

Vertical Subtraction

Vertical subtraction works well when signs are handled carefully.

Suppose

\[ (4x^3+2x^2-5x+8)-(x^3-3x^2+2x-6). \]

First replace the second polynomial by its additive inverse:

\[ -x^3+3x^2-2x+6. \]

Then add by columns:

\[ \begin{array}{rrrrrr} & 4x^3 & +2x^2 & -5x & +8 \\ + & -x^3 & +3x^2 & -2x & +6 \\ \hline & 3x^3 & +5x^2 & -7x & +14 \end{array} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{3x^3+5x^2-7x+14}. \]

Addition and Subtraction Do Not Usually Change Exponents

When like terms are added or subtracted, their coefficients change but their common power remains the same.

For example,

\[ 8x^5-3x^5=5x^5. \]

We do not add or subtract the exponents because the terms are not being multiplied or divided.

The expression

\[ x^2+x^2 \]

is

\[ \boxed{2x^2}, \]

not \(x^4\).

The Degree of a Sum

If \(P\) and \(Q\) are nonzero polynomials, the degree of their sum cannot exceed the greater of their two degrees:

\[ \deg(P+Q)\le \max\{\deg(P),\deg(Q)\}. \]

Frequently the degree equals that maximum, but leading terms can cancel.

For example,

\[ (3x^4+2x)-(3x^4-5) \]

simplifies to

\[ 2x+5. \]

The degree drops from \(4\) to \(1\) because the \(x^4\) terms cancel.

The Degree of a Difference

The same principle applies to subtraction:

\[ \deg(P-Q)\le \max\{\deg(P),\deg(Q)\}, \]

provided the result is not the zero polynomial.

If

\[ P(x)=Q(x), \]

then

\[ P(x)-Q(x)=0, \]

producing the zero polynomial, whose degree is left undefined in our current convention.

Multiplying Polynomials

Polynomial multiplication is built from two familiar ideas:

  1. multiply coefficients;
  2. add exponents when multiplying equal bases.

For monomials,

\[ (3x^2)(4x^5) \]

becomes

\[ (3)(4)x^{2+5}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(3x^2)(4x^5)=12x^7}. \]

Multiplying Signed Monomials

The ordinary sign rules still apply.

\[ (-2x^3)(5x^4) = (-2)(5)x^{3+4} = \boxed{-10x^7}. \]

Likewise,

\[ (-3x^2)(-4x^5) = 12x^7. \]

The coefficient arithmetic and exponent arithmetic can be handled separately and then recombined.

Multiplying a Monomial by a Polynomial

To multiply a monomial by a polynomial, multiply the monomial by every term in the polynomial.

Consider

\[ 3x^2(2x^3-5x+4). \]

Apply the distributive property:

\[ (3x^2)(2x^3) +(3x^2)(-5x) +(3x^2)(4). \]

Multiply each pair:

\[ 6x^5-15x^3+12x^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ 3x^2(2x^3-5x+4) = 6x^5-15x^3+12x^2 }. \]

Every Term Must Be Multiplied

In

\[ -2x(4x^3+x^2-6x+5), \]

the factor \(-2x\) multiplies all four terms:

\[ (-2x)(4x^3) +(-2x)(x^2) +(-2x)(-6x) +(-2x)(5). \]

This gives

\[ \boxed{-8x^4-2x^3+12x^2-10x}. \]

Missing even one term changes the polynomial and makes the product incorrect.

Multiplying Two Polynomials

When both factors contain several terms, every term in the first polynomial must multiply every term in the second.

Consider

\[ (x^2+2x+3)(x+4). \]

Multiply the first polynomial by \(x\):

\[ x(x^2+2x+3) = x^3+2x^2+3x. \]

Multiply the first polynomial by \(4\):

\[ 4(x^2+2x+3) = 4x^2+8x+12. \]

Add the partial products:

\[ x^3+2x^2+3x +4x^2+8x+12. \]

Combine like terms:

\[ \boxed{x^3+6x^2+11x+12}. \]

The Partial-Product Method

The previous multiplication can be organized vertically, much like multidigit multiplication.

\[ \begin{array}{rrrrr} & x^2 & +2x & +3 \\ \times & & x & +4 \\ \hline & 4x^2 & +8x & +12 \\ & x^3 & +2x^2 & +3x \\ \hline & x^3 & +6x^2 & +11x & +12 \end{array} \]

Each row is a partial product. Once the rows are aligned by powers, like terms can be added.

This method scales well when the factors contain several terms.

A Term-by-Term View

The same product

\[ (x^2+2x+3)(x+4) \]

can be expanded by listing all pairwise products:

\[ x^2(x)+x^2(4) +2x(x)+2x(4) +3(x)+3(4). \]

This becomes

\[ x^3+4x^2+2x^2+8x+3x+12. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{x^3+6x^2+11x+12}. \]

The essential rule is simple: every term in one factor pairs with every term in the other.

Multiplying Two Binomials

Consider

\[ (2x+3)(x-5). \]

Multiply \(2x\) by both terms in the second factor:

\[ 2x(x)+2x(-5) = 2x^2-10x. \]

Multiply \(3\) by both terms:

\[ 3(x)+3(-5) = 3x-15. \]

Add:

\[ 2x^2-10x+3x-15. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(2x+3)(x-5)=2x^2-7x-15}. \]

Binomial multiplication has several useful shortcuts and recurring patterns. Those will be developed systematically in the next lesson on Distribution and Special Products.

Multiplying Polynomials with Missing Powers

Missing powers do not need to be inserted before ordinary multiplication, but keeping the degree structure visible can prevent errors.

Consider

\[ (x^3+2)(x^2-x+4). \]

Multiply \(x^3\) by every term:

\[ x^5-x^4+4x^3. \]

Multiply \(2\) by every term:

\[ 2x^2-2x+8. \]

Add:

\[ \boxed{x^5-x^4+4x^3+2x^2-2x+8}. \]

Multiplying Longer Polynomials

Consider

\[ (x^2+x-2)(2x^2-3x+1). \]

Multiply by \(2x^2\):

\[ 2x^4+2x^3-4x^2. \]

Multiply by \(-3x\):

\[ -3x^3-3x^2+6x. \]

Multiply by \(1\):

\[ x^2+x-2. \]

Add the partial products:

\[ 2x^4 +(2x^3-3x^3) +(-4x^2-3x^2+x^2) +(6x+x) -2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ (x^2+x-2)(2x^2-3x+1) = 2x^4-x^3-6x^2+7x-2 }. \]

Degree of a Product

For nonzero polynomials with real coefficients,

\[ \boxed{ \deg(PQ)=\deg(P)+\deg(Q) }. \]

If

\[ \deg(P)=m \]

and

\[ \deg(Q)=n, \]

then the leading terms have the form

\[ a_mx^m \]

and

\[ b_nx^n. \]

Their product is

\[ a_mb_nx^{m+n}. \]

Because \(a_m\neq0\) and \(b_n\neq0\), the coefficient \(a_mb_n\) is also nonzero, so the product has degree \(m+n\).

Example: Predict the Product Degree

Suppose

\[ P(x)=3x^4-2x+1 \]

and

\[ Q(x)=-5x^3+x^2+7. \]

We know

\[ \deg(P)=4 \]

and

\[ \deg(Q)=3. \]

Therefore, before multiplying anything else,

\[ \boxed{\deg(PQ)=7}. \]

This prediction is a useful error check during expansion.

Leading Coefficient of a Product

The leading coefficient of a product is the product of the leading coefficients.

If

\[ P(x)=3x^4+\cdots \]

and

\[ Q(x)=-5x^3+\cdots, \]

then

\[ P(x)Q(x) \]

begins with

\[ (3x^4)(-5x^3)=-15x^7. \]

Thus the product has

\[ \boxed{\text{leading coefficient }-15} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\text{degree }7}. \]

A Fast Structural Check Before Expanding

Before multiplying two nonzero polynomials, inspect their leading terms.

For

\[ (2x^5+\cdots)(-3x^4+\cdots), \]

the product must begin with

\[ -6x^9. \]

If a completed expansion has degree \(8\), degree \(10\), or a positive leading coefficient, something went wrong.

Structural predictions provide a quick independent check without repeating the entire calculation.

Constant Terms Also Multiply Predictably

The constant term of a product comes from multiplying the constant terms of the factors.

If

\[ P(x)=\cdots+a_0 \]

and

\[ Q(x)=\cdots+b_0, \]

then the constant term of \(P(x)Q(x)\) is

\[ \boxed{a_0b_0}. \]

For example, in

\[ (x^2+3x-4)(2x^3+x+5), \]

the constant term of the product must be

\[ (-4)(5)=\boxed{-20}. \]

Why the Constant-Term Rule Works

Any product involving a positive power of \(x\) still contains a positive power of \(x\).

The only way to produce an \(x^0\) term from polynomial multiplication is therefore

\[ a_0x^0\cdot b_0x^0. \]

Since

\[ x^0x^0=x^0=1, \]

the resulting constant coefficient is simply

\[ a_0b_0. \]

Polynomial Multiplication as Coefficient Convolution

The coefficient-list viewpoint can also describe multiplication.

Suppose

\[ P(x)=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2 \]

and

\[ Q(x)=b_0+b_1x+b_2x^2. \]

When the polynomials are multiplied, terms whose exponents add to the same value become like terms.

The constant coefficient is

\[ c_0=a_0b_0. \]

The coefficient of \(x\) is

\[ c_1=a_0b_1+a_1b_0. \]

The coefficient of \(x^2\) is

\[ c_2=a_0b_2+a_1b_1+a_2b_0. \]

This systematic combining of shifted coefficient products is called convolution.

The General Coefficient Rule for Multiplication

If

\[ P(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_ix^i \]

and

\[ Q(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_jx^j, \]

then

\[ P(x)Q(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{m+n}c_kx^k, \]

where

\[ \boxed{ c_k=\sum_{i+j=k}a_ib_j }. \]

This compact formula says exactly what ordinary polynomial multiplication does: multiply coefficient pairs whose exponents add to \(k\), then add those products to obtain the coefficient of \(x^k\).

You do not need this notation to perform elementary multiplication, but it exposes the algorithm underneath the familiar procedure.

Example: See the Convolution

Let

\[ P(x)=2+3x \]

and

\[ Q(x)=4+5x. \]

The coefficient lists, written from \(x^0\) upward, are

\[ (2,3) \]

and

\[ (4,5). \]

The product coefficients are

\[ c_0=(2)(4)=8, \] \[ c_1=(2)(5)+(3)(4)=22, \]

and

\[ c_2=(3)(5)=15. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P(x)Q(x)=8+22x+15x^2}. \]

Rewritten in descending standard form,

\[ \boxed{15x^2+22x+8}. \]

Why Computer Algebra Likes Coefficient Lists

A computer does not need to "see" a polynomial the way a person does. It can store a polynomial as a sequence of coefficients.

For example,

\[ 4x^3-2x+7 \]

can be stored as

\[ (4,0,-2,7) \]

when coefficients are listed from highest degree to constant term.

Addition and subtraction then become element-by-element arithmetic. Multiplication becomes a systematic combination of coefficient products.

The same algebra learned by hand therefore connects directly to the algorithms used in symbolic and numerical computation.

Multiplication and Function Values

If

\[ R(x)=P(x)Q(x), \]

then evaluating \(R\) at any number \(a\) gives

\[ \boxed{R(a)=P(a)Q(a)}. \]

This gives us another way to check a multiplication.

Suppose

\[ (x+2)(x+3)=x^2+5x+6. \]

At \(x=4\), the factored form gives

\[ (4+2)(4+3)=6\cdot7=42. \]

The expanded form gives

\[ 4^2+5(4)+6=16+20+6=42. \]

Agreement at a chosen test value does not by itself prove two formulas are identical, but it is an efficient way to catch many arithmetic errors.

Checking Addition by Evaluation

If

\[ R(x)=P(x)+Q(x), \]

then

\[ R(a)=P(a)+Q(a). \]

For example, let

\[ P(x)=x^2+2x \]

and

\[ Q(x)=3x^2-5. \]

Their sum is

\[ R(x)=4x^2+2x-5. \]

At \(x=2\),

\[ P(2)=8, \qquad Q(2)=7, \]

so

\[ P(2)+Q(2)=15. \]

The simplified sum gives

\[ R(2)=4(4)+4-5=15. \]

Checking Subtraction by Evaluation

Similarly, if

\[ R(x)=P(x)-Q(x), \]

then

\[ R(a)=P(a)-Q(a). \]

This can be especially useful for detecting a missed sign when subtracting a polynomial.

Several Variables

The same operations work with multivariable polynomials.

For example,

\[ (3x^2y+2xy^2)+(5x^2y-7xy^2) \]

becomes

\[ (3+5)x^2y+(2-7)xy^2, \]

so

\[ \boxed{8x^2y-5xy^2}. \]

The terms combine because \(x^2y\) matches \(x^2y\), and \(xy^2\) matches \(xy^2\).

Multiplication with Several Variables

When multiplying monomials, add exponents on matching variable bases.

\[ (3x^2y^4)(-2x^3y) \]

gives

\[ (3)(-2)x^{2+3}y^{4+1}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{-6x^5y^5}. \]

Each variable keeps its own exponent bookkeeping.

A Geometric Interpretation of Multiplication

Polynomial multiplication can represent area.

Suppose a rectangle has side lengths

\[ x+3 \]

and

\[ x+5. \]

Its area is

\[ A=(x+3)(x+5). \]

Dividing the rectangle according to the two parts of each side produces four smaller areas:

\[ x^2,\qquad 5x,\qquad 3x,\qquad15. \]

Adding them gives

\[ A=x^2+8x+15. \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{(x+3)(x+5)=x^2+8x+15}. \]

The next lesson will develop this distributive structure more deeply and use it to recognize recurring special products.

Multiplication Is Commutative

Polynomial multiplication inherits the commutative property:

\[ \boxed{P(x)Q(x)=Q(x)P(x)}. \]

For example,

\[ (x+2)(x^2-3) \]

and

\[ (x^2-3)(x+2) \]

both produce

\[ x^3+2x^2-3x-6. \]

Multiplication Is Associative

Polynomial multiplication is associative:

\[ \boxed{(PQ)R=P(QR)}. \]

This means a product of several polynomial factors may be grouped in whichever order is most convenient.

The grouping can change the amount of work, but not the final polynomial.

Multiplication Distributes Over Addition

Polynomials also obey the distributive property:

\[ \boxed{P(Q+R)=PQ+PR}. \]

This law is the engine behind polynomial multiplication.

When we say that every term in one polynomial must multiply every term in another, we are repeatedly applying distribution.

The next lesson will focus specifically on this structure and the useful patterns it creates.

Common Error: Adding Unlike Terms

The expression

\[ 3x^2+4x \]

cannot be simplified to

\[ 7x^3. \]

The terms have different variable parts, so they are not like terms.

Unless another operation is being performed, the correct expression remains

\[ \boxed{3x^2+4x}. \]

Common Error: Adding Exponents During Addition

Exponents are added when powers with the same base are multiplied, not when like terms are added.

\[ x^3+x^3=2x^3, \]

while

\[ x^3\cdot x^3=x^6. \]

The operation determines which rule applies.

Common Error: Losing the Subtraction Sign

In

\[ (2x^2+5x-1)-(x^2-3x+4), \]

the entire second polynomial changes sign:

\[ 2x^2+5x-1-x^2+3x-4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2+8x-5}. \]

The safest habit is to rewrite subtraction as addition of the additive inverse before combining any terms.

Common Error: Multiplying Only the First Terms

For

\[ (x+2)(x+5), \]

multiplying only \(x\cdot x\) produces only one part of the product.

All four pairings are needed:

\[ x(x),\qquad x(5),\qquad2(x),\qquad2(5). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2+7x+10}. \]

Common Error: Multiplying Coefficients but Forgetting Exponents

Consider

\[ (2x^3)(5x^4). \]

The coefficients multiply:

\[ 2\cdot5=10, \]

and the powers multiply:

\[ x^3x^4=x^{3+4}=x^7. \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{10x^7}. \]

Common Error: Failing to Combine Partial Products

An expansion is not finished merely because every multiplication has been performed.

For example,

\[ (x+2)(x+3) = x^2+3x+2x+6. \]

The terms \(3x\) and \(2x\) are like terms, so standard form is

\[ \boxed{x^2+5x+6}. \]

Multiplication creates partial products; simplification combines them.

A Reliable Procedure for Polynomial Addition

  1. Write both polynomials in standard form.
  2. Identify corresponding powers.
  3. Treat missing powers as having coefficient \(0\).
  4. Add the coefficients of like terms.
  5. Write the result in standard form.
  6. Check whether leading terms canceled and changed the degree.

A Reliable Procedure for Polynomial Subtraction

  1. Place parentheses around the polynomial being subtracted.
  2. Replace subtraction with addition of its additive inverse.
  3. Change the sign of every term in the second polynomial.
  4. Combine like terms.
  5. Write the result in standard form.
  6. Check signs and degree.

A Reliable Procedure for Polynomial Multiplication

  1. Predict the degree from the degrees of the factors.
  2. Predict the leading term from the leading terms of the factors.
  3. Multiply every term in one factor by every term in the other.
  4. Multiply coefficients and add exponents on equal variable bases.
  5. Collect all partial products.
  6. Combine like terms.
  7. Write the result in standard form.
  8. Compare the result with your predicted degree and leading term.

Example: A Complete Addition Workflow

Add

\[ (5x^4-2x^2+7) +(3x^3+6x^2-4x-9). \]

Align the powers:

\[ 5x^4+0x^3-2x^2+0x+7 \] \[ 0x^4+3x^3+6x^2-4x-9. \]

Add corresponding coefficients:

\[ \boxed{5x^4+3x^3+4x^2-4x-2}. \]

Example: A Complete Subtraction Workflow

Subtract

\[ (2x^4-3x^3+x-6) -(5x^4+x^3-2x^2-4). \]

Change every sign in the second polynomial:

\[ 2x^4-3x^3+x-6 -5x^4-x^3+2x^2+4. \]

Combine like terms:

\[ \boxed{-3x^4-4x^3+2x^2+x-2}. \]

Example: A Complete Multiplication Workflow

Multiply

\[ (2x^2-x+3)(x^2+4x-2). \]

First predict the leading term:

\[ (2x^2)(x^2)=2x^4. \]

Now form the partial products:

\[ 2x^2(x^2+4x-2) = 2x^4+8x^3-4x^2, \] \[ -x(x^2+4x-2) = -x^3-4x^2+2x, \] \[ 3(x^2+4x-2) = 3x^2+12x-6. \]

Combine:

\[ 2x^4 +(8x^3-x^3) +(-4x^2-4x^2+3x^2) +(2x+12x) -6. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ 2x^4+7x^3-5x^2+14x-6 }. \]

The result has degree \(4\) and leading term \(2x^4\), exactly as predicted.

What to Remember

Polynomial arithmetic is organized by powers and coefficients.

  • Addition: add coefficients of like powers.
  • Subtraction: add the additive inverse of the second polynomial, then combine like terms.
  • Multiplication: multiply every term in one factor by every term in the other, then combine like terms.

For nonzero polynomials,

\[ \boxed{\deg(PQ)=\deg(P)+\deg(Q)}. \]

The leading coefficient of a product is the product of the leading coefficients, while the constant term of a product is the product of the constant terms.

Addition and subtraction work almost directly on coefficient lists. Multiplication combines shifted coefficient products, a process that can be described formally as convolution.
