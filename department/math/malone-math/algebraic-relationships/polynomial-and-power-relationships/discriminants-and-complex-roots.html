Discriminants and Complex Roots

The quadratic formula contains a built-in classifier. The expression \(b^2-4ac\), called the discriminant, tells us whether a quadratic has two distinct real roots, one repeated real root, or two nonreal complex roots. Its arithmetic structure can also distinguish rational from irrational real solutions.

The Central Idea

Recall the quadratic formula:

\[ x= \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}. \]

The expression under the radical,

\[ \boxed{b^2-4ac}, \]

determines the nature of the roots.

We define

\[ \boxed{D=b^2-4ac}, \]

where \(D\) is called the discriminant.

Why the Discriminant Matters

The quadratic formula may be written

\[ x= \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{D}}{2a}. \]

Therefore, the kind of number represented by

\[ \sqrt{D} \]

determines the kind of roots the quadratic has.

Three Main Cases

Discriminant Root Type Graph Meaning
\(D>0\) Two distinct real roots Two real \(x\)-intercepts
\(D=0\) One repeated real root Parabola touches the \(x\)-axis once
\(D<0\) Two nonreal complex roots No real \(x\)-intercepts

Case 1: Positive Discriminant

If

\[ D>0, \]

then

\[ \sqrt{D} \]

is a positive real number.

The plus and minus cases therefore produce two different real roots.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{D>0\Rightarrow\text{two distinct real roots}}. \]

Example: Positive Discriminant

Consider

\[ x^2-5x+6=0. \]

Here,

\[ a=1,\qquad b=-5,\qquad c=6. \]

Compute:

\[ D=(-5)^2-4(1)(6). \] \[ D=25-24=1. \]

Since

\[ D>0, \]

there are two distinct real roots.

Case 2: Zero Discriminant

If

\[ D=0, \]

then

\[ \sqrt{D}=0. \]

The quadratic formula becomes

\[ x=\frac{-b}{2a}. \]

The plus and minus cases collapse to the same value.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{D=0\Rightarrow\text{one repeated real root}}. \]

Example: Zero Discriminant

Consider

\[ x^2-6x+9=0. \]

Here,

\[ a=1,\qquad b=-6,\qquad c=9. \]

Compute:

\[ D=(-6)^2-4(1)(9). \] \[ D=36-36=0. \]

Therefore, there is one repeated real root:

\[ \boxed{x=3}. \]

Repeated Root and Perfect Square

The quadratic

\[ x^2-6x+9 \]

factors as

\[ (x-3)^2. \]

The repeated factor corresponds directly to the repeated root.

This is the algebraic reason the graph touches the axis instead of crossing at that point.

Case 3: Negative Discriminant

If

\[ D<0, \]

then

\[ \sqrt{D} \]

is not a real number.

To solve the equation, we extend the number system to include complex numbers.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{D<0\Rightarrow\text{two nonreal complex roots}}. \]

The Imaginary Unit

Define

\[ \boxed{i=\sqrt{-1}}. \]

Therefore,

\[ i^2=-1. \]

This allows square roots of negative numbers to be rewritten using real radicals and \(i\).

Square Roots of Negative Numbers

For \(k>0\),

\[ \sqrt{-k} = \sqrt{k}\sqrt{-1}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\sqrt{-k}=i\sqrt{k}}. \]

For example,

\[ \sqrt{-9}=3i \]

and

\[ \sqrt{-20}=2i\sqrt5. \]

Complex Numbers

A complex number has the form

\[ \boxed{a+bi}, \]

where \(a\) and \(b\) are real numbers.

The number \(a\) is the real part.

The number \(b\) is the coefficient of the imaginary part.

If \(b\neq0\), the number is nonreal complex.

Example: Negative Discriminant

Solve

\[ x^2+4x+8=0. \]

Here,

\[ a=1,\qquad b=4,\qquad c=8. \]

Compute:

\[ D=4^2-4(1)(8). \] \[ D=16-32=-16. \]

Since \(D<0\), the roots are complex.

Finish the Complex-Root Example

Apply the quadratic formula:

\[ x= \frac{-4\pm\sqrt{-16}}{2}. \]

Since

\[ \sqrt{-16}=4i, \]

we obtain

\[ x= \frac{-4\pm4i}{2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=-2\pm2i}. \]

Complex Conjugate Roots

The roots

\[ -2+2i \]

and

\[ -2-2i \]

are complex conjugates.

They have the same real part and opposite imaginary parts.

Quadratic polynomials with real coefficients produce nonreal complex roots in conjugate pairs.

Why Conjugate Pairs Appear

In the quadratic formula,

\[ \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{D}}{2a}, \]

if \(D<0\), then

\[ \sqrt{D}=i\sqrt{|D|}. \]

Therefore, the two solutions have the form

\[ \frac{-b}{2a} \pm \frac{\sqrt{|D|}}{2a}i. \]

The real parts match, while the imaginary parts differ only by sign.

Positive Discriminant: Rational or Irrational?

A positive discriminant guarantees two real roots, but those roots may be rational or irrational.

The key question is whether

\[ D \]

is a perfect square.

Positive Perfect-Square Discriminant

If

\[ D>0 \]

and \(D\) is a perfect square, then

\[ \sqrt{D} \]

is rational.

If \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are integers, the resulting roots are rational.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ D>0\text{ and perfect square} \Rightarrow \text{two rational real roots} }. \]

Example: Rational Real Roots

Consider

\[ 2x^2-7x+3=0. \]

Compute:

\[ D=(-7)^2-4(2)(3). \] \[ D=49-24=25. \]

Since \(25\) is a positive perfect square, the roots are rational.

Indeed,

\[ x= \frac{7\pm5}{4}, \]

giving

\[ \boxed{x=3,\ \frac12}. \]

Positive Non-Square Discriminant

If

\[ D>0 \]

but \(D\) is not a perfect square, then

\[ \sqrt{D} \]

is irrational.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ D>0\text{ and not a perfect square} \Rightarrow \text{two irrational real roots} }. \]

Example: Irrational Real Roots

Consider

\[ x^2-2x-1=0. \]

Compute:

\[ D=(-2)^2-4(1)(-1). \] \[ D=4+4=8. \]

Since \(8>0\) but is not a perfect square, the roots are real and irrational.

The solutions are

\[ \boxed{x=1\pm\sqrt2}. \]

A More Detailed Classification

Discriminant Number of Roots Type
\(D>0\), perfect square Two Distinct rational real roots
\(D>0\), not a perfect square Two Distinct irrational real roots
\(D=0\) One repeated Rational real root when coefficients are rational
\(D<0\) Two Nonreal complex conjugate roots

Graph Interpretation

The discriminant also describes how the parabola relates to the \(x\)-axis.

  • \(D>0\): the parabola crosses the axis twice.
  • \(D=0\): the parabola touches the axis once.
  • \(D<0\): the parabola does not meet the axis.

The discriminant therefore connects symbolic algebra to graph geometry.

Why \(D=0\) Means the Vertex Is on the Axis

When

\[ D=0, \]

the two quadratic-formula roots collapse to

\[ x=-\frac{b}{2a}. \]

This is exactly the \(x\)-coordinate of the vertex.

Therefore, the repeated root occurs at the axis of symmetry, and the vertex lies on the \(x\)-axis.

Why \(D<0\) Means No Real \(x\)-Intercepts

A real \(x\)-intercept would require some real \(x\) satisfying

\[ f(x)=0. \]

But if \(D<0\), the quadratic formula produces no real solutions.

Therefore, the graph cannot intersect the real \(x\)-axis.

Example: Classify Without Solving

Consider

\[ 3x^2+4x+7=0. \]

Compute:

\[ D=4^2-4(3)(7). \] \[ D=16-84=-68. \]

Since

\[ D<0, \]

we can immediately conclude:

\[ \boxed{\text{two nonreal complex roots}} \]

without solving the equation completely.

Example: Determine Root Type Only

Consider

\[ 4x^2-12x+9=0. \]

Compute:

\[ D=(-12)^2-4(4)(9). \] \[ D=144-144=0. \]

Therefore, the equation has

\[ \boxed{\text{one repeated real root}}. \]

No further solving is required if classification is the only goal.

Example: Predict Rationality

Consider

\[ 5x^2+x-1=0. \]

Compute:

\[ D=1^2-4(5)(-1)=21. \]

Since \(21>0\) but is not a perfect square, the equation has

\[ \boxed{\text{two irrational real roots}}. \]

Complex Roots from the Quadratic Formula

Solve

\[ x^2-6x+13=0. \]

Compute:

\[ D=(-6)^2-4(1)(13). \] \[ D=36-52=-16. \]

Then

\[ x= \frac{6\pm\sqrt{-16}}{2}. \] \[ x= \frac{6\pm4i}{2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=3\pm2i}. \]

Verify a Complex Root by Substitution

Let

\[ P(x)=x^2-6x+13. \]

Test

\[ x=3+2i. \]

Compute

\[ (3+2i)^2=9+12i+4i^2. \]

Since

\[ i^2=-1, \]

we get

\[ (3+2i)^2=5+12i. \]

Then

\[ P(3+2i) = (5+12i)-6(3+2i)+13. \] \[ =5+12i-18-12i+13=0. \]

The root is verified.

Why Real-Coefficient Quadratics Produce Conjugates

Suppose

\[ a,\ b,\ c \]

are real and

\[ D<0. \]

Then the roots have the form

\[ p+qi \]

and

\[ p-qi. \]

Their imaginary parts cancel when the corresponding linear factors are multiplied, leaving real polynomial coefficients.

Build a Quadratic from Complex Conjugate Roots

Suppose the roots are

\[ 2+3i \]

and

\[ 2-3i. \]

The corresponding factors are

\[ x-(2+3i) \]

and

\[ x-(2-3i). \]

Rewrite:

\[ (x-2-3i)(x-2+3i). \]

Multiply the Conjugate Factors

Let

\[ u=x-2. \]

Then

\[ (u-3i)(u+3i)=u^2-(3i)^2. \]

Since

\[ (3i)^2=-9, \]

we get

\[ u^2+9. \]

Therefore,

\[ (x-2)^2+9. \]

Expand:

\[ \boxed{x^2-4x+13}. \]

Complex Roots and Factorization

Over the real numbers,

\[ x^2-4x+13 \]

does not factor into real linear factors.

Over the complex numbers, it factors as

\[ \boxed{ [x-(2+3i)][x-(2-3i)] }. \]

The number system determines what kinds of factors are available.

Real Versus Complex Factorization

Consider

\[ x^2+4. \]

Over the real numbers, this has no real linear factors.

But since

\[ x^2=-4 \]

gives

\[ x=\pm2i, \]

over the complex numbers,

\[ \boxed{x^2+4=(x-2i)(x+2i)}. \]

The Discriminant as a Decision Tool

Before solving a quadratic, computing \(D\) can answer several questions:

  • Will the roots be real or complex?
  • Will there be one real root or two?
  • If the coefficients are integers, are the real roots likely rational or irrational?
  • Will the graph cross, touch, or miss the \(x\)-axis?

This makes the discriminant useful even when the exact roots are not required.

Common Error: Forgetting the Sign of \(b\)

For

\[ 2x^2-7x+4=0, \]

we have

\[ b=-7, \]

so

\[ b^2=(-7)^2=49. \]

The coefficient should be substituted with its sign before squaring.

Common Error: Misreading \(-4ac\)

Suppose

\[ a=2,\qquad c=-3. \]

Then

\[ -4ac=-4(2)(-3)=24. \]

The product is positive because two negatives are involved.

Parentheses help preserve the signs.

Common Error: Saying \(D<0\) Means No Solutions

A negative discriminant means no real solutions.

It does not mean the equation has no solutions at all.

Over the complex numbers, a quadratic with \(D<0\) has two nonreal complex roots.

Common Error: Treating \(\sqrt{-k}\) as \(-\sqrt{k}\)

For example,

\[ \sqrt{-25} \]

is not

\[ -5. \]

Since

\[ i=\sqrt{-1}, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{\sqrt{-25}=5i}. \]

Common Error: Forgetting the Conjugate Pair

If one root of a real-coefficient quadratic is

\[ 4+3i, \]

the other must be

\[ \boxed{4-3i}. \]

The two roots come from the \(\pm\) in the quadratic formula.

A Reliable Procedure for Classifying Roots

  1. Write the quadratic in standard form \(ax^2+bx+c=0\).
  2. Identify \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) with signs.
  3. Compute \(D=b^2-4ac\).
  4. If \(D>0\), expect two distinct real roots.
  5. If \(D=0\), expect one repeated real root.
  6. If \(D<0\), expect two nonreal complex conjugate roots.
  7. If \(D>0\), inspect whether \(D\) is a perfect square to distinguish rational from irrational roots.

A Reliable Procedure for Solving Complex Quadratic Roots

  1. Compute the discriminant.
  2. If it is negative, write \(D=-k\) with \(k>0\).
  3. Rewrite \(\sqrt{-k}=i\sqrt{k}\).
  4. Simplify the radical.
  5. Substitute into the quadratic formula.
  6. Simplify both conjugate solutions.

What to Remember

The discriminant is

\[ \boxed{D=b^2-4ac}. \]

It classifies quadratic roots:

  • \(D>0\): two distinct real roots;
  • \(D=0\): one repeated real root;
  • \(D<0\): two nonreal complex conjugate roots.

When \(D>0\), a perfect-square discriminant gives rational roots, while a non-square discriminant gives irrational roots when the coefficients are rational.

The imaginary unit

\[ \boxed{i=\sqrt{-1}} \]

allows negative discriminants to be handled naturally.

Real-coefficient quadratics with nonreal roots produce those roots in conjugate pairs.
