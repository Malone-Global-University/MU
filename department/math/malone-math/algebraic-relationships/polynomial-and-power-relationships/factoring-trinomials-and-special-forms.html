Factoring Trinomials and Special Forms

Many polynomials can be factored by recognizing how multiplication created them. Quadratic trinomials often come from multiplying two binomials, while perfect-square trinomials and differences of squares come from familiar special products. Factoring these expressions means reading those multiplication patterns in reverse.

The Central Idea

Factoring reverses multiplication.

If

\[ (x+2)(x+5)=x^2+7x+10, \]

then reading the relationship backward gives

\[ \boxed{x^2+7x+10=(x+2)(x+5)}. \]

Factoring a trinomial therefore means identifying which binomials could have produced it.

Always Check for a GCF First

Before using any trinomial or special-form method, inspect the entire polynomial for a greatest common factor.

Consider

\[ 6x^2+18x+12. \]

The GCF is \(6\):

\[ 6(x^2+3x+2). \]

Now factor the trinomial:

\[ x^2+3x+2=(x+1)(x+2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{6x^2+18x+12=6(x+1)(x+2)}. \]

Factoring out the GCF first makes the remaining structure easier to see.

Quadratic Trinomials

A quadratic trinomial has the form

\[ ax^2+bx+c, \]

where \(a\neq0\).

When \(a=1\), the trinomial has the simpler form

\[ x^2+bx+c. \]

We will begin with this monic case.

Factoring \(x^2+bx+c\)

Suppose

\[ x^2+bx+c=(x+m)(x+n). \]

Expanding the right side gives

\[ x^2+(m+n)x+mn. \]

Therefore, to factor

\[ x^2+bx+c, \]

we need two numbers \(m\) and \(n\) such that

\[ \boxed{m+n=b} \]

and

\[ \boxed{mn=c}. \]

Example: Positive Constant Term

Factor

\[ x^2+7x+12. \]

We need two numbers that multiply to \(12\) and add to \(7\).

The numbers are

\[ 3 \]

and

\[ 4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2+7x+12=(x+3)(x+4)}. \]

Sign Patterns for Monic Trinomials

The signs of \(b\) and \(c\) give useful information.

If

\[ x^2+bx+c \]

has \(c>0\), then the two factor numbers have the same sign.

  • If \(b>0\), both are positive.
  • If \(b<0\), both are negative.

If \(c<0\), the two factor numbers have opposite signs.

Example: Both Signs Negative

Factor

\[ x^2-9x+20. \]

We need two numbers whose product is \(20\) and whose sum is \(-9\).

The numbers are

\[ -4 \]

and

\[ -5. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2-9x+20=(x-4)(x-5)}. \]

Example: Opposite Signs

Factor

\[ x^2+x-12. \]

We need two numbers whose product is \(-12\) and whose sum is \(1\).

The numbers are

\[ 4 \]

and

\[ -3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2+x-12=(x+4)(x-3)}. \]

Use Factor Pairs Systematically

When the correct pair is not immediately obvious, list factor pairs.

To factor

\[ x^2+11x+24, \]

consider the positive factor pairs of \(24\):

\[ 1\cdot24, \] \[ 2\cdot12, \] \[ 3\cdot8, \] \[ 4\cdot6. \]

The pair whose sum is \(11\) is

\[ 3+8=11. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2+11x+24=(x+3)(x+8)}. \]

Prime Trinomials over the Integers

Not every quadratic trinomial factors into binomials with integer coefficients.

Consider

\[ x^2+x+1. \]

We would need two integers whose product is \(1\) and whose sum is \(1\).

The possible integer pairs are

\[ 1,\ 1 \]

and

\[ -1,\ -1. \]

Their sums are \(2\) and \(-2\), not \(1\).

Thus this polynomial is not factorable into linear factors with integer coefficients.

At this stage we may call it prime over the integers.

When the Leading Coefficient Is Not 1

Now consider a trinomial of the form

\[ ax^2+bx+c \]

with \(a\neq1\).

For example,

\[ 2x^2+7x+3. \]

We cannot simply search for two numbers whose product is \(3\) and sum is \(7\), because the leading coefficient changes the multiplication structure.

The AC Method

A reliable method for factoring

\[ ax^2+bx+c \]

is to multiply

\[ a\cdot c. \]

Then find two numbers whose product is

\[ ac \]

and whose sum is

\[ b. \]

These numbers are used to split the middle term, after which factoring by grouping can be applied.

Example: AC Method

Factor

\[ 2x^2+7x+3. \]

Here,

\[ a=2,\qquad b=7,\qquad c=3. \]

Compute

\[ ac=2\cdot3=6. \]

We need two numbers whose product is \(6\) and sum is \(7\):

\[ 6 \]

and

\[ 1. \]

Split the Middle Term

Rewrite

\[ 7x \]

as

\[ 6x+x. \]

Then

\[ 2x^2+7x+3 = 2x^2+6x+x+3. \]

Group:

\[ (2x^2+6x)+(x+3). \]

Factor each group:

\[ 2x(x+3)+1(x+3). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{2x^2+7x+3=(2x+1)(x+3)}. \]

Example: Non-Monic Trinomial with a Negative Term

Factor

\[ 3x^2+x-2. \]

Here,

\[ ac=3(-2)=-6. \]

We need two numbers whose product is \(-6\) and sum is \(1\).

The numbers are

\[ 3 \]

and

\[ -2. \]

Split the middle term:

\[ 3x^2+3x-2x-2. \]

Group:

\[ 3x(x+1)-2(x+1). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{3x^2+x-2=(3x-2)(x+1)}. \]

Example: Larger Coefficients

Factor

\[ 6x^2+11x+3. \]

Compute

\[ ac=6\cdot3=18. \]

We need two numbers whose product is \(18\) and sum is \(11\).

The numbers are

\[ 9 \]

and

\[ 2. \]

Split:

\[ 6x^2+9x+2x+3. \]

Group:

\[ 3x(2x+3)+1(2x+3). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{6x^2+11x+3=(3x+1)(2x+3)}. \]

Example: Negative Constant Term

Factor

\[ 8x^2-2x-3. \]

Compute

\[ ac=8(-3)=-24. \]

We need two numbers whose product is \(-24\) and sum is \(-2\).

The numbers are

\[ 4 \]

and

\[ -6. \]

Split:

\[ 8x^2+4x-6x-3. \]

Group:

\[ 4x(2x+1)-3(2x+1). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{8x^2-2x-3=(4x-3)(2x+1)}. \]

Why the AC Method Works

Suppose

\[ ax^2+bx+c \]

factors as

\[ (px+q)(rx+s). \]

Expanding gives

\[ prx^2+(ps+qr)x+qs. \]

Therefore,

\[ a=pr, \] \[ b=ps+qr, \]

and

\[ c=qs. \]

The two middle contributions \(ps\) and \(qr\) have product

\[ (ps)(qr)=prqs=ac. \]

That is why the AC method searches for two numbers whose product is \(ac\) and whose sum is \(b\).

Factoring by Inspection

Once the multiplication structure becomes familiar, some trinomials can be factored directly.

For

\[ 2x^2+5x+2, \]

we may recognize

\[ (2x+1)(x+2) \]

because

\[ (2x)(x)=2x^2 \]

and

\[ 1\cdot2=2, \]

while the cross-products produce

\[ 4x+x=5x. \]

Inspection can be faster, but the AC method remains a dependable fallback.

Perfect-Square Trinomials

Recall the special products

\[ (a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2 \]

and

\[ (a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2. \]

Reading these backward gives the factoring identities

\[ \boxed{a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2} \]

and

\[ \boxed{a^2-2ab+b^2=(a-b)^2}. \]

Recognizing a Perfect-Square Trinomial

A trinomial is a perfect square when:

  1. the first term is a square;
  2. the last term is a square;
  3. the middle term is twice the product of the square roots of those terms.

The sign of the middle term determines whether the binomial contains addition or subtraction.

Example: Perfect Square with Addition

Factor

\[ x^2+10x+25. \]

The first term is

\[ x^2=(x)^2. \]

The last term is

\[ 25=5^2. \]

Twice their product is

\[ 2(x)(5)=10x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2+10x+25=(x+5)^2}. \]

Example: Perfect Square with Subtraction

Factor

\[ 4x^2-12x+9. \]

The first term is

\[ (2x)^2. \]

The last term is

\[ 3^2. \]

The middle term is

\[ -2(2x)(3)=-12x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{4x^2-12x+9=(2x-3)^2}. \]

Difference of Squares

Recall the conjugate identity

\[ (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2. \]

Reading it backward gives

\[ \boxed{a^2-b^2=(a+b)(a-b)}. \]

This is one of the most useful factoring patterns in algebra.

Example: Difference of Squares

Factor

\[ x^2-49. \]

Recognize

\[ x^2=(x)^2 \]

and

\[ 49=7^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2-49=(x+7)(x-7)}. \]

Example: Difference of Squares with Coefficients

Factor

\[ 9x^2-25. \]

Write

\[ 9x^2=(3x)^2 \]

and

\[ 25=5^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{9x^2-25=(3x+5)(3x-5)}. \]

Difference of Higher Powers That Are Squares

The square pattern can involve powers larger than \(2\).

Consider

\[ x^6-16. \]

Since

\[ x^6=(x^3)^2 \]

and

\[ 16=4^2, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{x^6-16=(x^3+4)(x^3-4)}. \]

Factor Repeated Differences of Squares Completely

Sometimes factoring once reveals another difference of squares.

Consider

\[ x^4-16. \]

First,

\[ x^4-16=(x^2+4)(x^2-4). \]

Then

\[ x^2-4=(x+2)(x-2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^4-16=(x^2+4)(x+2)(x-2)}. \]

Over the real numbers, \(x^2+4\) does not factor into real linear factors.

Sum of Squares Is Different

The difference-of-squares identity does not apply to

\[ a^2+b^2. \]

For example,

\[ x^2+9 \]

does not factor as

\[ (x+3)(x-3), \]

because that product equals

\[ x^2-9. \]

The sign is essential.

Factoring After Removing a GCF

Special forms often appear only after a common factor is removed.

Factor

\[ 3x^3-27x. \]

First factor the GCF:

\[ 3x(x^2-9). \]

Now factor the difference of squares:

\[ x^2-9=(x+3)(x-3). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{3x^3-27x=3x(x+3)(x-3)}. \]

Factoring a Perfect Square After a GCF

Factor

\[ 2x^3+12x^2+18x. \]

First factor the GCF \(2x\):

\[ 2x(x^2+6x+9). \]

The trinomial is a perfect square:

\[ x^2+6x+9=(x+3)^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{2x^3+12x^2+18x=2x(x+3)^2}. \]

Factoring by Grouping Can Lead to a Special Form

Consider

\[ x^3-2x^2-4x+8. \]

Group:

\[ x^2(x-2)-4(x-2). \]

Factor the common binomial:

\[ (x-2)(x^2-4). \]

Then factor the difference of squares:

\[ x^2-4=(x+2)(x-2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^3-2x^2-4x+8=(x-2)^2(x+2)}. \]

Complete Factoring Is a Sequence of Decisions

Factoring is rarely just one isolated technique.

A polynomial may require several stages:

  1. remove a GCF;
  2. factor by grouping;
  3. factor a trinomial;
  4. recognize a perfect-square form;
  5. factor a difference of squares;
  6. repeat until no further known factorization applies.

A Practical Factoring Decision Tree

When asked to factor a polynomial, use this order.

  1. GCF: Is there a factor common to every term?
  2. Number of terms: Are there two, three, or four terms?
  3. Two terms: Does the expression match a special form such as a difference of squares?
  4. Three terms: Is it a perfect-square trinomial or a factorable quadratic trinomial?
  5. Four terms: Can grouping create a repeated factor?
  6. Repeat: Can any resulting factor be factored further?

Factoring \(ax^2+bx+c\): A Reliable Procedure

  1. Factor out any GCF.
  2. Identify \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\).
  3. Compute \(ac\).
  4. Find two integers whose product is \(ac\) and whose sum is \(b\).
  5. Split the middle term using those two numbers.
  6. Factor by grouping.
  7. Check whether any factor can be factored further.
  8. Expand to verify the result.

Checking by Multiplication

Factoring and multiplication are inverse processes, so expansion provides a direct check.

Suppose

\[ 6x^2+11x+3=(3x+1)(2x+3). \]

Expand the right side:

\[ 6x^2+9x+2x+3. \]

Combine:

\[ 6x^2+11x+3. \]

The original polynomial is recovered.

Checking a Perfect-Square Factorization

Suppose

\[ x^2-14x+49=(x-7)^2. \]

Expand:

\[ (x-7)(x-7) = x^2-7x-7x+49. \]

Therefore,

\[ x^2-14x+49. \]

The factorization is correct.

Checking a Difference of Squares

Suppose

\[ 16x^2-81=(4x+9)(4x-9). \]

The factors are conjugates, so the middle terms cancel:

\[ (4x)^2-9^2 = 16x^2-81. \]

The original expression returns.

Common Error: Forgetting the GCF

Consider

\[ 4x^2+12x+8. \]

A student may notice

\[ x^2+3x+2=(x+1)(x+2), \]

but the original polynomial has a factor of \(4\).

The complete factorization is

\[ \boxed{4(x+1)(x+2)}. \]

Common Error: Finding Numbers with the Right Product but Wrong Sum

To factor

\[ x^2+8x+15, \]

the factor pair \(1\) and \(15\) has the correct product but the wrong sum.

The correct pair is

\[ 3,\ 5 \]

because

\[ 3\cdot5=15 \]

and

\[ 3+5=8. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2+8x+15=(x+3)(x+5)}. \]

Common Error: Ignoring Signs

To factor

\[ x^2-5x-14, \]

the numbers must multiply to \(-14\), so they must have opposite signs.

The pair

\[ -7,\ 2 \]

gives

\[ -7+2=-5. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2-5x-14=(x-7)(x+2)}. \]

Common Error: Using the Monic Rule When \(a\neq1\)

For

\[ 2x^2+7x+3, \]

looking only for two numbers whose product is \(3\) and sum is \(7\) will not reveal the correct factors.

The leading coefficient \(2\) must be included in the structure.

Using \(ac=6\) leads to the correct factorization:

\[ \boxed{(2x+1)(x+3)}. \]

Common Error: Misidentifying a Perfect Square

Consider

\[ x^2+8x+16. \]

The first and last terms are squares:

\[ x^2 \]

and

\[ 4^2. \]

The middle term must equal

\[ 2(x)(4)=8x, \]

which it does.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^2+8x+16=(x+4)^2}. \]

Checking the middle term is essential.

Common Error: Factoring a Sum of Squares as Conjugates

The expression

\[ x^2+25 \]

does not factor as

\[ (x+5)(x-5). \]

That product is

\[ x^2-25. \]

The difference-of-squares identity requires subtraction.

Common Error: Stopping Before the Factorization Is Complete

Consider

\[ x^4-81. \]

Factoring once gives

\[ (x^2+9)(x^2-9). \]

But

\[ x^2-9 \]

is another difference of squares:

\[ (x+3)(x-3). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x^4-81=(x^2+9)(x+3)(x-3)}. \]

Example: Complete Factoring Workflow

Factor completely:

\[ 12x^3-48x. \]

First factor the GCF:

\[ 12x(x^2-4). \]

Recognize the difference of squares:

\[ x^2-4=(x+2)(x-2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{12x^3-48x=12x(x+2)(x-2)}. \]

Example: Complete Trinomial Workflow

Factor completely:

\[ 10x^2+35x+30. \]

First factor the GCF:

\[ 5(2x^2+7x+6). \]

For the trinomial,

\[ ac=2\cdot6=12. \]

The numbers \(3\) and \(4\) multiply to \(12\) and add to \(7\).

Split:

\[ 2x^2+3x+4x+6. \]

Group:

\[ x(2x+3)+2(2x+3). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{10x^2+35x+30=5(x+2)(2x+3)}. \]

Example: Complete Factoring with Grouping and a Special Form

Factor completely:

\[ 2x^3+4x^2-8x-16. \]

First factor the GCF:

\[ 2(x^3+2x^2-4x-8). \]

Group:

\[ 2[x^2(x+2)-4(x+2)]. \]

Factor the repeated binomial:

\[ 2(x+2)(x^2-4). \]

Factor the difference of squares:

\[ x^2-4=(x+2)(x-2). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{2(x+2)^2(x-2)}. \]

What to Remember

Factoring trinomials means reversing binomial multiplication.

For

\[ x^2+bx+c, \]

find numbers \(m\) and \(n\) satisfying

\[ \boxed{m+n=b} \]

and

\[ \boxed{mn=c}. \]

For

\[ ax^2+bx+c, \]

the AC method searches for two numbers whose product is

\[ ac \]

and whose sum is

\[ b. \]

The important special forms are

\[ \boxed{a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2}, \] \[ \boxed{a^2-2ab+b^2=(a-b)^2}, \]

and

\[ \boxed{a^2-b^2=(a+b)(a-b)}. \]

Always remove the GCF first, and always inspect the resulting factors to see whether factoring can continue.
