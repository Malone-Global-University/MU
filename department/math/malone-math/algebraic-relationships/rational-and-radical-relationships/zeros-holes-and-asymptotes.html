Zeros, Holes, and Asymptotes

The graph of a rational function is controlled by the structure of its numerator and denominator. Zeros come from numerator factors that remain, holes come from factors that cancel, and vertical asymptotes come from denominator factors that remain. Degree comparisons and polynomial division then reveal how the graph behaves far from the origin.

The Central Idea

Consider a rational function

\[ f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}. \]

Its graph is strongly influenced by three kinds of algebraic information:

  • zeros of the numerator;
  • zeros of the denominator;
  • factors shared by numerator and denominator.

Factoring reveals which of these zeros create x-intercepts, holes, or vertical asymptotes.

Begin with the Domain

Before interpreting any graph, determine where the rational function is defined.

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}, \]

every real zero of \(Q(x)\) must be excluded.

These excluded values may later appear graphically as holes or vertical asymptotes.

Zeros of a Rational Function

A zero of \(f(x)\) is an input value for which

\[ f(x)=0. \]

A fraction equals zero when its numerator equals zero and its denominator does not.

Therefore, for

\[ f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}, \]

zeros occur where

\[ P(x)=0 \]

while

\[ Q(x)\neq0. \]

Zeros Become x-Intercepts

If

\[ f(a)=0, \]

then the graph contains the point

\[ (a,0). \]

Therefore zeros of a rational function correspond to x-intercepts, provided the function is actually defined there.

Example: Find the Zero

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x-4}{x+2}. \]

The numerator is zero when

\[ x-4=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=4. \]

Since the denominator at \(x=4\) is nonzero,

\[ \boxed{x=4} \]

is a zero, and the graph crosses or touches the x-axis at

\[ \boxed{(4,0)}. \]

Several Zeros

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{(x-2)(x+5)}{x+1}. \]

The numerator is zero at

\[ x=2 \]

and

\[ x=-5. \]

Neither value makes the denominator zero.

Therefore the x-intercepts are

\[ \boxed{(2,0),\qquad(-5,0)}. \]

Multiplicity Still Matters

Rational functions inherit the same basic multiplicity behavior seen in polynomial graphs.

For example,

\[ f(x)=\frac{(x-3)^2}{x+1} \]

has a zero of multiplicity \(2\) at

\[ x=3. \]

Because the multiplicity is even, the graph typically touches the x-axis and turns rather than crossing there.

A Denominator Zero Is Not a Function Zero

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+2}{x-3}. \]

The denominator is zero at

\[ x=3. \]

This does not make \(f(3)=0\).

Instead,

\[ f(3) \]

is undefined.

Common Factors Need Special Attention

Suppose numerator and denominator share a factor:

\[ f(x)=\frac{(x-2)(x+3)}{(x-2)(x+5)}. \]

The factor \(x-2\) appears in both numerator and denominator.

Algebraically, it can be canceled for \(x\neq2\), but the original function remains undefined at \(x=2\).

This produces a hole.

What Is a Hole?

A hole is a missing point in the graph of a function.

In a rational function, a hole commonly occurs when a factor cancels from numerator and denominator.

The canceled factor identifies the excluded x-value.

The simplified expression identifies the y-value that the graph would have had there.

Example: Find a Hole

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{(x-2)(x+3)}{(x-2)(x+5)}. \]

The original restriction is

\[ x\neq2,\qquad x\neq-5. \]

Cancel \(x-2\):

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+3}{x+5}, \qquad x\neq2,\ -5. \]

The canceled factor tells us there is a hole at \(x=2\).

Finding the y-Coordinate of a Hole

Continue with the simplified expression

\[ \frac{x+3}{x+5}. \]

Evaluate it at the excluded value \(x=2\):

\[ \frac{2+3}{2+5} = \frac57. \]

Therefore the graph has a hole at

\[ \boxed{\left(2,\frac57\right)}. \]

Why the Hole Is Not Filled

The simplified formula

\[ \frac{x+3}{x+5} \]

is defined at \(x=2\), but the original function

\[ \frac{(x-2)(x+3)}{(x-2)(x+5)} \]

is not.

The simplified expression tells us the value approached near \(x=2\), not the actual value of the original function there.

Removable Discontinuity

A hole is also called a removable discontinuity.

The discontinuity is called removable because assigning the missing limiting value at the hole would make the simplified graph continuous at that point.

The original rational function, however, still has the hole unless the function is explicitly redefined.

Vertical Asymptotes

A vertical asymptote occurs where a denominator factor remains after common factors are canceled.

Near such an x-value, the denominator approaches zero while the numerator remains nonzero.

The function's magnitude can therefore grow without bound.

Example: Vertical Asymptote

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-4}. \]

The denominator is zero at

\[ x=4. \]

No factor cancels.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=4} \]

is a vertical asymptote.

Holes and Vertical Asymptotes Both Come from Denominator Zeros

The difference is what happens after factoring.

If a denominator factor cancels, its zero becomes a hole.

If a denominator factor remains, its zero becomes a vertical asymptote.

Thus:

\[ \boxed{ \text{canceled denominator factor}\to\text{hole} } \] \[ \boxed{ \text{uncanceled denominator factor}\to\text{vertical asymptote} }. \]

Example: Hole and Vertical Asymptote Together

Consider

\[ f(x)= \frac{(x-1)(x+2)} {(x-1)(x-3)}. \]

Restrictions:

\[ x\neq1,\qquad x\neq3. \]

Cancel \(x-1\):

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+2}{x-3}, \qquad x\neq1,\ 3. \]

Therefore:

  • \(x=1\) gives a hole;
  • \(x=3\) gives a vertical asymptote.

Find the Hole in the Combined Example

Use the simplified formula

\[ \frac{x+2}{x-3}. \]

At \(x=1\),

\[ \frac{1+2}{1-3} = -\frac32. \]

Therefore the hole is

\[ \boxed{\left(1,-\frac32\right)}. \]

The vertical asymptote remains

\[ \boxed{x=3}. \]

Repeated Denominator Factors

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+1}{(x-2)^2}. \]

The denominator factor \(x-2\) remains and has multiplicity \(2\).

Therefore,

\[ x=2 \]

is still a vertical asymptote.

Multiplicity affects the behavior on the two sides of the asymptote.

Odd and Even Multiplicity at Vertical Asymptotes

A denominator factor with odd multiplicity changes sign as \(x\) passes through its zero.

A denominator factor with even multiplicity keeps the same sign on both sides.

This often determines whether the graph approaches opposite infinities or the same infinity on the two sides of a vertical asymptote.

Example: Odd-Multiplicity Vertical Asymptote

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac1{x-2}. \]

As \(x\to2^-\), the denominator is a small negative number, so

\[ f(x)\to-\infty. \]

As \(x\to2^+\), the denominator is a small positive number, so

\[ f(x)\to+\infty. \]

The graph approaches opposite infinities on the two sides.

Example: Even-Multiplicity Vertical Asymptote

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac1{(x-2)^2}. \]

The denominator is positive on both sides of \(x=2\).

Therefore,

\[ f(x)\to+\infty \]

from both sides.

Numerator Sign Also Matters Near an Asymptote

For a function such as

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-3}, \]

the sign near \(x=3\) depends on both numerator and denominator.

Since \(x+1\) is positive near \(3\), the sign behavior is controlled by \(x-3\).

In more complicated examples, factor-by-factor sign analysis is useful.

Horizontal Asymptotes

A horizontal asymptote describes the value a function approaches as \(x\) becomes very large in magnitude.

Rational functions often have horizontal asymptotes determined by the degrees and leading coefficients of the numerator and denominator.

Degree of the Numerator Less Than Degree of the Denominator

Suppose

\[ f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \]

and

\[ \deg P<\deg Q. \]

Then the denominator grows faster in magnitude than the numerator.

Therefore,

\[ f(x)\to0 \]

as \(|x|\to\infty\).

The horizontal asymptote is

\[ \boxed{y=0}. \]

Example: Horizontal Asymptote at Zero

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{3x+1}{x^2+4}. \]

The numerator has degree \(1\), and the denominator has degree \(2\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{y=0} \]

is the horizontal asymptote.

Equal Degrees

If

\[ \deg P=\deg Q, \]

then the highest-degree terms dominate the behavior for large \(|x|\).

Suppose

\[ P(x)\sim ax^n \]

and

\[ Q(x)\sim bx^n. \]

Then

\[ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \to \frac{a}{b}. \]

The horizontal asymptote is

\[ \boxed{y=\frac{a}{b}}. \]

Example: Equal Degrees

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{2x^2+3x-1}{5x^2+4}. \]

Both numerator and denominator have degree \(2\).

The ratio of leading coefficients is

\[ \frac25. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{y=\frac25} \]

is the horizontal asymptote.

Why Leading Terms Control End Behavior

Divide numerator and denominator by the highest common power of \(x\).

For

\[ \frac{2x^2+3x-1}{5x^2+4}, \]

divide by \(x^2\):

\[ \frac{2+\frac3x-\frac1{x^2}} {5+\frac4{x^2}}. \]

As \(|x|\) becomes large,

\[ \frac3x,\quad\frac1{x^2},\quad\frac4{x^2} \]

all approach zero.

The remaining ratio approaches

\[ \frac25. \]

When the Numerator Degree Is Greater

If

\[ \deg P>\deg Q, \]

there is generally no horizontal asymptote.

Instead, polynomial division reveals a polynomial that describes the function's end behavior.

Oblique or Slant Asymptotes

If the numerator degree is exactly one greater than the denominator degree, polynomial division produces a linear quotient.

That line is called an oblique asymptote or slant asymptote.

Example: Find a Slant Asymptote

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x^2+1}{x-1}. \]

Divide:

\[ x^2+1=(x-1)(x+1)+2. \]

Therefore,

\[ f(x)=x+1+\frac{2}{x-1}. \]

As \(|x|\to\infty\),

\[ \frac{2}{x-1}\to0. \]

Thus the slant asymptote is

\[ \boxed{y=x+1}. \]

Polynomial Asymptotes

If the numerator degree exceeds the denominator degree by more than one, polynomial division produces a higher-degree polynomial quotient.

The rational function approaches that polynomial as \(|x|\) becomes large.

Slant asymptotes are simply the degree-one case of this broader idea.

A Horizontal Asymptote Is Not a Barrier

A rational function may cross a horizontal asymptote.

Horizontal asymptotes describe end behavior, not forbidden y-values.

For example,

\[ f(x)=\frac{x}{x+1} \]

has horizontal asymptote

\[ y=1. \]

Other rational functions can cross their horizontal asymptotes at finite x-values.

A Vertical Asymptote Is Different

A vertical asymptote occurs at an excluded x-value where a denominator factor remains.

The graph cannot contain a point with that x-coordinate because the function is undefined there.

Thus vertical and horizontal asymptotes describe different types of behavior.

Finding y-Intercepts

Although this lesson focuses on zeros, holes, and asymptotes, the y-intercept is another useful graph feature.

Set

\[ x=0 \]

if \(0\) belongs to the domain.

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{x-2}{x+3}, \]

we get

\[ f(0)=\frac{-2}{3}. \]

Therefore the y-intercept is

\[ \boxed{\left(0,-\frac23\right)}. \]

A Full Factored Analysis

Consider

\[ f(x)= \frac{(x-4)(x+1)} {(x-2)(x+1)}. \]

From the original denominator,

\[ x\neq2,\qquad x\neq-1. \]

Cancel \(x+1\):

\[ f(x)=\frac{x-4}{x-2}, \qquad x\neq2,\ -1. \]

We can now classify the major features.

Classify the Features

For

\[ f(x)= \frac{(x-4)(x+1)} {(x-2)(x+1)}, \]
  • the remaining numerator zero \(x=4\) gives an x-intercept;
  • the canceled denominator zero \(x=-1\) gives a hole;
  • the remaining denominator zero \(x=2\) gives a vertical asymptote.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{x-intercept: }(4,0)} \] \[ \boxed{\text{vertical asymptote: }x=2}. \]

Find the Hole in the Full Analysis

Use the simplified formula

\[ \frac{x-4}{x-2}. \]

At \(x=-1\),

\[ \frac{-1-4}{-1-2} = \frac{-5}{-3} = \frac53. \]

Therefore the hole is

\[ \boxed{\left(-1,\frac53\right)}. \]

Find the Horizontal Asymptote in the Full Analysis

The simplified function

\[ \frac{x-4}{x-2} \]

has equal numerator and denominator degrees.

The leading coefficients are both \(1\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{y=1} \]

is the horizontal asymptote.

Why Simplification Comes Before Classification

If we looked only at denominator zeros before canceling, we might wrongly call every excluded value a vertical asymptote.

Factoring and cancellation distinguish:

  • removable discontinuities from canceled factors;
  • vertical asymptotes from remaining denominator factors.

This is why graph analysis begins with algebraic simplification while preserving the original domain.

Zeros Can Disappear Through Cancellation

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{(x-3)(x+2)}{x-3}. \]

The numerator appears to be zero at \(x=3\), but the denominator is also zero there.

After cancellation,

\[ f(x)=x+2, \qquad x\neq3. \]

Therefore \(x=3\) is not a zero of the original function.

It is a hole.

A Zero Must Survive Simplification

For a factored rational function, a numerator factor creates an actual zero only if it remains after all common factors are canceled.

This leads to a useful rule:

\[ \boxed{ \text{remaining numerator factor}\to\text{zero} }. \]

A numerator factor that cancels does not create an x-intercept at its zero.

Vertical Asymptotes Can Also Be Removed by Cancellation

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x-5}{(x-5)(x+1)}. \]

The original denominator is zero at

\[ x=5 \]

and

\[ x=-1. \]

Cancel \(x-5\):

\[ f(x)=\frac1{x+1}, \qquad x\neq5,\ -1. \]

Therefore \(x=5\) is a hole, not a vertical asymptote.

Graphical Meaning of Sign Changes at Zeros

If a remaining numerator factor has odd multiplicity, the sign of the function often changes as the graph passes through the zero.

If the multiplicity is even, the sign generally stays the same and the graph touches the axis.

This is the rational-function version of polynomial zero behavior.

Graphical Meaning of Sign Changes at Vertical Asymptotes

If a remaining denominator factor has odd multiplicity, its sign changes across the excluded x-value.

If its multiplicity is even, its sign does not.

Combined with the signs of the other factors, this predicts whether the graph approaches \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\) from each side.

Sign Charts Help Organize Rational Graphs

A sign chart uses zeros, holes, and vertical asymptotes to divide the number line into intervals.

One test point from each interval reveals whether the rational function is positive or negative there.

This information helps sketch the graph between its major structural features.

Example: Sign Analysis

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x-1}{x+2}. \]

Critical x-values are

\[ x=-2 \]

and

\[ x=1. \]

These divide the real line into

\[ (-\infty,-2),\qquad(-2,1),\qquad(1,\infty). \]

Testing gives signs

\[ +,\quad-,\quad+. \]

Can a Graph Cross a Vertical Asymptote?

No point on the graph can have the x-coordinate of a vertical asymptote, because the function is undefined there.

The graph may have branches on both sides, but the vertical line itself remains excluded.

Can a Graph Cross a Horizontal Asymptote?

Yes.

A horizontal asymptote describes only the function's behavior as \(|x|\) becomes very large.

It does not generally prevent the graph from crossing that horizontal line at finite x-values.

Can a Graph Cross a Slant Asymptote?

Yes.

Like a horizontal asymptote, a slant asymptote describes end behavior.

A rational graph may cross its slant asymptote one or more times at finite x-values.

Common Error: Calling Every Denominator Zero a Vertical Asymptote

Consider

\[ \frac{(x-2)(x+1)}{(x-2)(x-5)}. \]

Both \(x=2\) and \(x=5\) make the original denominator zero.

But \(x-2\) cancels.

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{x=2\text{ gives a hole}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{x=5\text{ gives a vertical asymptote}}. \]

Common Error: Treating a Hole as an x-Intercept

Suppose

\[ f(x)=\frac{(x-3)(x+4)}{x-3}. \]

The numerator has a factor \(x-3\), but that factor also appears in the denominator.

Since it cancels, \(x=3\) is excluded.

Therefore the graph does not contain the point \((3,0)\).

Instead, \(x=3\) gives a hole.

Common Error: Forgetting the Original Domain After Simplifying

Consider

\[ \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}. \]

Simplifying gives

\[ x+1. \]

But the original restriction

\[ x\neq1 \]

remains.

The graph is the line \(y=x+1\) with a hole at

\[ \boxed{(1,2)}. \]

Common Error: Using Denominator Degree Alone for End Behavior

Horizontal and slant asymptotes depend on comparing numerator and denominator degrees.

Looking only at the denominator degree is insufficient.

Always compare

\[ \deg P \]

with

\[ \deg Q. \]

Common Error: Assuming Horizontal Asymptotes Cannot Be Crossed

Horizontal asymptotes describe limiting behavior as

\[ x\to\pm\infty. \]

They are not graph barriers.

A rational function may cross a horizontal asymptote at finite x-values.

Common Error: Confusing Undefined with Infinite

At a vertical asymptote \(x=a\), the function is not equal to

\[ \infty \]

or

\[ -\infty. \]

The function is undefined at \(x=a\).

The symbols \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\) describe unbounded behavior as \(x\) approaches \(a\), not function values at \(a\).

A Reliable Procedure for Graph Structure

  1. Factor the numerator and denominator completely.
  2. Record all restrictions from the original denominator.
  3. Cancel common factors while preserving their excluded x-values.
  4. Use canceled denominator factors to locate holes.
  5. Use remaining numerator factors to locate zeros and x-intercepts.
  6. Use remaining denominator factors to locate vertical asymptotes.
  7. Find the y-coordinate of each hole using the simplified formula.
  8. Find the y-intercept when \(x=0\) is allowed.
  9. Compare degrees or divide polynomials to determine end behavior.
  10. Use sign analysis when a more complete sketch is needed.

Example: Full Structural Analysis

Analyze

\[ f(x)= \frac{(x-3)(x+2)} {(x-3)(x-1)}. \]

Original restrictions:

\[ x\neq3,\qquad x\neq1. \]

Cancel \(x-3\):

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+2}{x-1}, \qquad x\neq3,\ 1. \]

Complete the Structural Analysis

The remaining numerator zero is

\[ x=-2, \]

so the x-intercept is

\[ (-2,0). \]

The canceled factor gives a hole at \(x=3\).

The remaining denominator gives a vertical asymptote at

\[ x=1. \]

Equal degrees give the horizontal asymptote

\[ y=1. \]

Find the Hole and y-Intercept

For

\[ \frac{x+2}{x-1}, \]

evaluate at \(x=3\):

\[ \frac{3+2}{3-1} = \frac52. \]

Therefore the hole is

\[ \boxed{\left(3,\frac52\right)}. \]

At \(x=0\),

\[ f(0)=\frac2{-1}=-2. \]

Therefore the y-intercept is

\[ \boxed{(0,-2)}. \]

What to Remember

Factored form reveals the structural features of a rational graph.

The most important correspondences are:

  • remaining numerator zeros give zeros and x-intercepts;
  • canceled denominator factors give holes;
  • remaining denominator factors give vertical asymptotes;
  • hole coordinates come from evaluating the simplified formula at the excluded x-value;
  • horizontal asymptotes come from degree comparison when the numerator degree is not greater than the denominator degree;
  • slant or polynomial asymptotes come from polynomial division when the numerator degree is greater.

A compact structural summary is:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{remaining numerator factor} &\to \text{zero},\\ \text{canceled denominator factor} &\to \text{hole},\\ \text{remaining denominator factor} &\to \text{vertical asymptote}. \end{aligned} } \]

The central habit is: factor first, preserve the original domain, and classify features only after cancellation is understood.
