Radical Equations and Extraneous Solutions

Radical equations contain variables inside roots. Solving them usually requires isolating a radical and then raising both sides to a power that reverses the radicalization. This process can create equations with more solutions than the original problem allows, especially when both sides are squared. For that reason, every proposed solution must be checked in the original radical equation.

The Central Idea

A radical equation contains a variable in a radical expression, such as

\[ \sqrt{x+5}=7. \]

To solve it, reverse the square root by squaring both sides:

\[ x+5=49. \]

Then solve:

\[ x=44. \]

Finally, verify in the original equation:

\[ \sqrt{44+5} = \sqrt{49} = 7. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=44}. \]

Why the Radical Should Be Isolated First

Suppose

\[ \sqrt{x+3}+2=8. \]

Squaring immediately would produce

\[ \left(\sqrt{x+3}+2\right)^2, \]

which creates extra terms and makes the equation more complicated.

Instead, first isolate the radical:

\[ \sqrt{x+3}=6. \]

Then square:

\[ x+3=36. \]

Example: Isolate, Square, Solve, Check

Solve

\[ \sqrt{x+3}+2=8. \]

Isolate:

\[ \sqrt{x+3}=6. \]

Square:

\[ x+3=36. \]

Solve:

\[ x=33. \]

Check:

\[ \sqrt{33+3}+2 = 6+2 = 8. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=33}. \]

Domain Conditions Come First

Over the real numbers, an even-index radical requires a nonnegative radicand.

For

\[ \sqrt{2x-6}, \]

we require

\[ 2x-6\geq0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x\geq3}. \]

Any proposed solution below \(3\) cannot satisfy the original real equation.

Domain Restrictions Are a First Filter

Suppose an algebraic calculation produces

\[ x=1 \]

for an equation containing

\[ \sqrt{x-4}. \]

Since the real domain requires

\[ x\geq4, \]

the proposed value \(x=1\) can be rejected immediately.

Domain analysis does not replace substitution, but it often reveals impossible candidates quickly.

Why Squaring Can Create Extraneous Solutions

Squaring is not one-to-one over the real numbers.

If

\[ a=b, \]

then certainly

\[ a^2=b^2. \]

But the reverse implication is not always true.

For example,

\[ 3^2=(-3)^2. \]

Thus squaring can erase sign information and introduce solutions that were not present in the original equation.

What Is an Extraneous Solution?

An extraneous solution is a value that satisfies an equation produced during the solving process but does not satisfy the original equation.

Radical equations are especially prone to extraneous solutions because even powers can remove sign restrictions.

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \text{every proposed solution must be checked in the original equation} }. \]

Example: An Extraneous Solution

Solve

\[ \sqrt{x+1}=x-1. \]

Because the left side is a principal square root, it is nonnegative. Therefore the right side must also satisfy

\[ x-1\geq0, \]

so

\[ x\geq1. \]

Square Both Sides

Starting from

\[ \sqrt{x+1}=x-1, \]

square:

\[ x+1=(x-1)^2. \]

Expand:

\[ x+1=x^2-2x+1. \]

Move all terms to one side:

\[ x^2-3x=0. \]

Factor:

\[ x(x-3)=0. \]

Candidates:

\[ x=0,\qquad x=3. \]

Check the Candidates

For

\[ x=0, \]

the original equation gives

\[ \sqrt1=-1, \]

which is false.

Therefore \(x=0\) is extraneous.

For

\[ x=3, \]

the original equation gives

\[ \sqrt4=2, \]

and

\[ 3-1=2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=3}. \]

Sign Information Can Predict Extraneous Candidates

In

\[ \sqrt{x+1}=x-1, \]

the left side is always nonnegative.

Therefore any valid solution must satisfy

\[ x-1\geq0. \]

The candidate \(x=0\) violates this condition even before substitution.

This is another reason to inspect the original equation before applying powers mechanically.

A Radical Equation Can Have No Solution

Consider

\[ \sqrt{x+2}=-4. \]

The principal square root is always nonnegative.

Therefore it can never equal \(-4\).

The equation has no real solution:

\[ \boxed{\varnothing}. \]

Do Not Square an Obviously Impossible Equation

If we squared

\[ \sqrt{x+2}=-4, \]

we would obtain

\[ x+2=16 \]

and hence

\[ x=14. \]

But

\[ \sqrt{16}=4\neq-4. \]

The candidate is entirely extraneous.

Sign reasoning can prevent unnecessary work.

Odd-Root Equations Behave Differently

Cubing is one-to-one over the real numbers.

Therefore, when

\[ \sqrt[3]{A}=B, \]

cubing both sides gives

\[ A=B^3 \]

without the same sign-loss problem caused by squaring.

Checking is still a good habit, especially in more complicated equations.

Example: Cube-Root Equation

Solve

\[ \sqrt[3]{2x-1}=3. \]

Cube both sides:

\[ 2x-1=27. \]

Then

\[ 2x=28, \]

so

\[ \boxed{x=14}. \]

Check:

\[ \sqrt[3]{28-1} = \sqrt[3]{27} = 3. \]

Equations with a Coefficient Outside the Radical

Solve

\[ 2\sqrt{x-1}=10. \]

Divide by \(2\):

\[ \sqrt{x-1}=5. \]

Square:

\[ x-1=25. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=26. \]

Check:

\[ 2\sqrt{25}=10. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{x=26}. \]

Equations with Radicals on Both Sides

Consider

\[ \sqrt{x+5}=\sqrt{2x-1}. \]

Both sides are principal square roots.

Square:

\[ x+5=2x-1. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=6. \]

Check:

\[ \sqrt{11}=\sqrt{11}. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{x=6}. \]

Use Domain Intersections

In

\[ \sqrt{x+5}=\sqrt{2x-1}, \]

the first radicand requires

\[ x\geq-5. \]

The second requires

\[ 2x-1\geq0, \]

so

\[ x\geq\frac12. \]

Together, the original equation requires

\[ \boxed{x\geq\frac12}. \]

One Squaring Step May Produce a Linear Equation

Many simple radical equations become linear after one power operation.

For example,

\[ \sqrt{3x+4}=5 \]

becomes

\[ 3x+4=25. \]

Then

\[ 3x=21 \]

and

\[ \boxed{x=7}. \]

One Squaring Step May Produce a Quadratic Equation

Consider

\[ \sqrt{x+6}=x. \]

Since the left side is nonnegative, any valid solution must satisfy

\[ x\geq0. \]

Square:

\[ x+6=x^2. \]

Rearrange:

\[ x^2-x-6=0. \]

Example: Quadratic Result and Extraneous Candidate

Factor:

\[ x^2-x-6 = (x-3)(x+2). \]

Candidates:

\[ x=3,\qquad x=-2. \]

Check \(x=3\):

\[ \sqrt9=3. \]

Valid.

Check \(x=-2\):

\[ \sqrt4=2\neq-2. \]

Extraneous.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=3}. \]

Equations with Two Radical Terms

Some equations contain more than one radical:

\[ \sqrt{x+1}+\sqrt{x-2}=3. \]

Squaring both radicals together immediately creates a product of radicals.

A reliable strategy is to isolate one radical, square, simplify, isolate the remaining radical, and square again.

Example: Two Radicals

Solve

\[ \sqrt{x+1}+\sqrt{x-2}=3. \]

The real domain requires

\[ x\geq2. \]

Isolate one radical:

\[ \sqrt{x+1} = 3-\sqrt{x-2}. \]

First Squaring Step

Square both sides:

\[ x+1 = \left(3-\sqrt{x-2}\right)^2. \]

Expand:

\[ x+1 = 9-6\sqrt{x-2}+x-2. \]

Simplify:

\[ x+1 = x+7-6\sqrt{x-2}. \]

Subtract \(x\):

\[ 1 = 7-6\sqrt{x-2}. \]

Isolate Again and Square Again

Continue:

\[ -6=-6\sqrt{x-2}. \]

Divide by \(-6\):

\[ 1=\sqrt{x-2}. \]

Square:

\[ 1=x-2. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=3. \]

Check the Two-Radical Solution

Substitute \(x=3\):

\[ \sqrt{3+1}+\sqrt{3-2} = \sqrt4+\sqrt1. \] \[ =2+1. \] \[ =3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=3}. \]

Squaring a Binomial Correctly

A major source of errors is forgetting the middle term.

If

\[ (a+b)^2, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ (a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2 }. \]

Therefore,

\[ \left(\sqrt A+\sqrt B\right)^2 = A+2\sqrt{AB}+B. \]

It is not simply \(A+B\).

Example: Why the Middle Term Matters

Consider

\[ \left(\sqrt{x}+1\right)^2. \]

The correct expansion is

\[ x+2\sqrt{x}+1. \]

Writing only

\[ x+1 \]

discards the cross term and changes the equation.

Equations with a Radical on One Side and a Polynomial on the Other

Consider

\[ \sqrt{2x+3}=x. \]

Sign reasoning requires

\[ x\geq0. \]

Square:

\[ 2x+3=x^2. \]

Rearrange:

\[ x^2-2x-3=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (x-3)(x+1)=0. \]

Complete the Polynomial-Side Example

Candidates:

\[ x=3,\qquad x=-1. \]

The sign condition \(x\geq0\) already eliminates \(-1\).

Check \(x=3\):

\[ \sqrt9=3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=3}. \]

Sometimes All Algebraic Candidates Are Extraneous

Solve

\[ \sqrt{x+4}=x-4. \]

The right side must be nonnegative:

\[ x\geq4. \]

Square:

\[ x+4=(x-4)^2. \]

Expand:

\[ x+4=x^2-8x+16. \]

Rearrange:

\[ x^2-9x+12=0. \]

Use the Quadratic Formula When Needed

For

\[ x^2-9x+12=0, \]

the quadratic formula gives

\[ x = \frac{9\pm\sqrt{81-48}}{2}. \] \[ = \frac{9\pm\sqrt{33}}{2}. \]

Only candidates satisfying \(x\geq4\) can possibly work.

The smaller candidate is below \(4\), so it is impossible.

The larger candidate must still be checked in the original equation.

Checking Complicated Exact Candidates

When a candidate contains radicals, verification may require exact substitution or careful numerical checking.

The principle does not change:

\[ \boxed{ \text{substitute into the original equation, not merely the squared equation} }. \]

The original equation is the authority for deciding whether a candidate is valid.

Radical Equations with Fractions

Consider

\[ \sqrt{\frac{x+1}{2}}=3. \]

Square:

\[ \frac{x+1}{2}=9. \]

Multiply by \(2\):

\[ x+1=18. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=17. \]

Check:

\[ \sqrt{\frac{18}{2}} = \sqrt9 = 3. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{x=17}. \]

Radical Equations with Rational Restrictions

Suppose an equation contains

\[ \sqrt{\frac{x-1}{x+2}}. \]

The denominator requires

\[ x\neq-2. \]

The square-root radicand also requires

\[ \frac{x-1}{x+2}\geq0. \]

Radical and rational restrictions must be combined before interpreting possible solutions.

Nested Restrictions Matter

A radical expression may contain a rational expression, or a rational expression may contain radicals.

Each structural layer contributes its own restrictions.

A reliable domain analysis asks:

  • which denominators must be nonzero?
  • which even-index radicands must be nonnegative?
  • which quantities must be positive because they appear in denominators under radicals?

Absolute-Value Equations Are Closely Related

Since

\[ \sqrt{x^2}=|x|, \]

radical equations sometimes reveal absolute-value structure.

For example,

\[ \sqrt{(x-3)^2}=5 \]

becomes

\[ |x-3|=5. \]

Therefore,

\[ x-3=5 \]

or

\[ x-3=-5. \]

Example: Solve Through Absolute Value

Continue:

\[ x-3=5 \]

gives

\[ x=8, \]

while

\[ x-3=-5 \]

gives

\[ x=-2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=8,\ -2}. \]

Graphical Interpretation of a Radical Equation

An equation such as

\[ \sqrt{x+1}=x-1 \]

can be viewed as asking where the graphs

\[ y=\sqrt{x+1} \]

and

\[ y=x-1 \]

intersect.

A valid algebraic solution corresponds to an actual intersection point. An extraneous solution does not.

Why Graphing Can Help but Does Not Replace Algebra

A graph can reveal:

  • whether solutions seem to exist;
  • approximately where intersections occur;
  • whether a proposed candidate is plausible.

But exact algebra is usually needed to determine exact solutions and to distinguish closely spaced or non-obvious values.

Extraneous Solutions Are Not Algebra Mistakes

An extraneous solution can arise even when every algebraic manipulation was performed correctly.

The issue is that some transformations, especially squaring, are not logically reversible without additional conditions.

Therefore checking is part of the solving method, not merely a correction for careless work.

Implication Versus Equivalence

From

\[ \sqrt{A}=B, \]

we may conclude

\[ A=B^2. \]

But from

\[ A=B^2, \]

we cannot automatically conclude

\[ \sqrt A=B, \]

because the principal square root requires

\[ B\geq0. \]

This logical asymmetry is the source of many extraneous solutions.

Common Error: Squaring Before Isolating

For

\[ \sqrt{x+2}+3=7, \]

isolate first:

\[ \sqrt{x+2}=4. \]

Then square.

Squaring the entire original left side creates unnecessary cross terms.

Common Error: Forgetting the Middle Term

It is incorrect to write

\[ (a+b)^2=a^2+b^2. \]

The correct identity is

\[ \boxed{ (a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2 }. \]

This matters greatly when two radicals remain on one side of an equation.

Common Error: Accepting Every Root of the Resulting Polynomial

After squaring, a radical equation may become quadratic.

Solving the quadratic finds candidates, not guaranteed solutions to the original equation.

Every candidate must be checked.

Common Error: Checking in the Squared Equation

A candidate obtained after squaring will often satisfy the squared equation by construction.

That does not prove it satisfies the original radical equation.

Verification must return to the equation given at the start.

Common Error: Ignoring Principal-Root Sign

If

\[ \sqrt{A}=B, \]

then any valid real solution must have

\[ B\geq0. \]

A negative right side is incompatible with a principal even root.

Common Error: Forgetting Radicand Restrictions

For

\[ \sqrt{5-x}, \]

the real domain requires

\[ 5-x\geq0. \]

Therefore,

\[ x\leq5. \]

Any candidate above \(5\) is invalid before substitution.

Common Error: Assuming Odd Roots Need Nonnegative Radicands

Cube roots and other odd roots accept negative real radicands.

For example,

\[ \sqrt[3]{-27}=-3. \]

The nonnegative-radicand restriction belongs to even roots, not all radicals.

A Reliable Procedure for One Square Root

  1. Determine the real-domain restrictions.
  2. Isolate the square-root expression.
  3. Inspect the sign required on the other side.
  4. Square both sides.
  5. Simplify and solve the resulting equation.
  6. Treat the algebraic results as candidates.
  7. Substitute every candidate into the original equation.
  8. Reject all extraneous solutions.

A Reliable Procedure for Two Square Roots

  1. Determine all domain restrictions.
  2. Isolate one radical.
  3. Square both sides carefully.
  4. Simplify and isolate the remaining radical.
  5. Square again.
  6. Solve the resulting algebraic equation.
  7. Check every candidate in the original equation.

A Reliable Procedure for Odd-Root Equations

  1. Isolate the odd-index radical.
  2. Raise both sides to the corresponding odd power.
  3. Solve the resulting equation.
  4. Check the result when useful, especially if other operations were involved.

Odd-power transformations preserve sign information more faithfully than even-power transformations.

Example: Full One-Radical Workflow

Solve

\[ \sqrt{3x-2}=x. \]

Domain and sign conditions require

\[ 3x-2\geq0 \]

and

\[ x\geq0. \]

Square:

\[ 3x-2=x^2. \]

Rearrange:

\[ x^2-3x+2=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (x-1)(x-2)=0. \]

Check the Full Workflow Candidates

Candidates:

\[ x=1,\qquad x=2. \]

Check \(x=1\):

\[ \sqrt{3(1)-2} = \sqrt1 = 1. \]

Valid.

Check \(x=2\):

\[ \sqrt{6-2} = \sqrt4 = 2. \]

Valid.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=1,\ 2}. \]

Example: Full Extraneous-Solution Workflow

Solve

\[ \sqrt{2x+3}=x. \]

Since the left side is nonnegative,

\[ x\geq0. \]

Square:

\[ 2x+3=x^2. \]

Rearrange:

\[ x^2-2x-3=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (x-3)(x+1)=0. \]

Candidates:

\[ x=3,\qquad x=-1. \]

Only \(x=3\) satisfies the sign condition and the original equation:

\[ \boxed{x=3}. \]

What to Remember

Radical equations are solved by reversing radicalization with appropriate powers, but even-power transformations can lose sign information.

The essential habits are:

  • identify real-domain restrictions before solving;
  • isolate a radical before raising both sides to a power;
  • inspect the sign required by a principal even root;
  • square carefully, including middle terms in binomial squares;
  • isolate and square again when more than one radical remains;
  • treat solutions of the transformed equation as candidates;
  • check every candidate in the original equation;
  • reject extraneous solutions;
  • remember that odd-root equations do not have the same sign-loss issue as square-root equations.

The central principle is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{solving produces candidates; substitution into the original equation determines solutions} }. \]

Section Synthesis

Rational and radical relationships are linked by a common theme: algebraic expressions have structure, and that structure determines both what operations are valid and where the expressions are defined.

In the rational half of this section, denominator factors produced domain restrictions, cancellation rules, holes, asymptotes, and reciprocal relationships.

In the radical half, root indices produced domain conditions, principal-value conventions, rational exponents, simplification rules, and the possibility of extraneous solutions.

Across both families, the same mathematical habit remains essential: transform expressions carefully while preserving the conditions under which the original objects are defined.
