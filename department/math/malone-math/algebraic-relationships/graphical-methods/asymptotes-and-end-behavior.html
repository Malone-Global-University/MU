Asymptotes and End Behavior

A graph can reveal important structure even where no point is actually drawn. Near excluded inputs, a function may grow without bound along a vertical line. Far to the left or right, a graph may approach a constant height, a slanted line, a higher-degree polynomial, or simply follow the dominant term of its own formula. Asymptotes and end behavior give us a precise language for describing these long-range and boundary patterns.

The Central Idea

End behavior describes what a graph does as the input moves far to the right or far to the left. Asymptotic behavior describes a graph approaching a simpler line or curve in a limiting way.

These ideas answer questions such as:

  • What happens as \(x\to\infty\)?
  • What happens as \(x\to-\infty\)?
  • What happens as \(x\) approaches an excluded value?
  • Does the graph approach a line or other simpler expression?

Two Different Ways to Go Far

There are two fundamentally different directions in which a graph can display extreme behavior.

  1. Near a finite input: \(x\) approaches a particular number such as \(2\).
  2. At the ends: \(x\) becomes arbitrarily large positive or negative.

Vertical asymptotes usually concern the first situation. Horizontal, slant, and polynomial asymptotes usually concern the second.

What Is an Asymptote?

An asymptote is a line, or in more advanced settings another simple curve, that describes limiting behavior of a graph.

The important word is limiting. An asymptote describes what happens as inputs approach a boundary or move indefinitely far away. It does not simply mean that two graphs look close over a short visible interval.

A Common Misconception: Asymptotes Cannot Be Crossed

A graph is not generally forbidden from crossing an asymptote.

  • A graph may cross a horizontal asymptote.
  • A graph may cross a slant asymptote.
  • A vertical asymptote \(x=a\) is different because it describes unbounded behavior near a fixed input; defining an isolated value at \(x=a\) would not remove that nearby behavior.

The defining feature is approach behavior, not a rule saying “never touch.”

Vertical Asymptotes

A vertical line

\[ \boxed{x=a} \]

is a vertical asymptote when the function becomes unbounded as \(x\) approaches \(a\) from at least one side.

Reading a Vertical Asymptote from a Graph

If the graph rises or falls without bound while moving closer and closer to the vertical line \(x=3\), then

\[ x=3 \]

is a vertical asymptote.

The graph may behave differently on the two sides of the line.

One-Sided Behavior Near a Vertical Asymptote

Near \(x=a\), we often describe the left and right sides separately.

\[ x\to a^- \qquad\text{means approach }a\text{ from the left}, \] \[ x\to a^+ \qquad\text{means approach }a\text{ from the right}. \]

The outputs on the two sides need not head in the same direction.

Example: A Reciprocal Shift

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x-2}. \]

The denominator becomes zero at \(x=2\), and the function becomes unbounded near that input. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=2} \]

is a vertical asymptote.

The Two Sides of \(1/(x-2)\)

When \(x\) approaches \(2\) from the left, \(x-2\) is a very small negative number, so

\[ \frac{1}{x-2}\to-\infty. \]

From the right, \(x-2\) is a very small positive number, so

\[ \frac{1}{x-2}\to+\infty. \]

Domain Restrictions and Vertical Asymptotes

A vertical asymptote often occurs at an excluded input, but an excluded input alone is not enough to prove that a vertical asymptote exists.

We must inspect the behavior near the excluded value.

A Hole Is Not a Vertical Asymptote

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}. \]

Factoring gives

\[ f(x)=\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=x+2, \qquad x\ne2. \]

The graph has a hole at \((2,4)\), but the nearby outputs remain finite. Therefore \(x=2\) is not a vertical asymptote.

Cancellation Must Be Checked First

For rational functions, denominator zeros are candidates for vertical asymptotes only after common factors are simplified.

A denominator factor that cancels usually produces a hole. A denominator factor that remains may produce a vertical asymptote.

Example: A Hole and a Vertical Asymptote Together

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{(x-1)(x+3)}{(x-1)(x-4)}. \]

Cancel the common factor:

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+3}{x-4},\qquad x\ne1,4. \]

The cancelled factor gives a hole at \(x=1\). The remaining denominator factor gives the vertical asymptote

\[ \boxed{x=4}. \]

Several Vertical Asymptotes

A function may have more than one vertical asymptote. For example,

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{(x-2)(x+5)} \]

has denominator zeros at \(x=2\) and \(x=-5\), with no cancellation. Thus its vertical asymptotes are

\[ \boxed{x=2\quad\text{and}\quad x=-5}. \]

Sign Analysis Near a Vertical Asymptote

Knowing the location of a vertical asymptote does not tell us whether the graph rises toward \(+\infty\) or falls toward \(-\infty\) on each side.

Test the sign of the expression on intervals immediately to the left and right of the asymptote. This reveals the direction of the branches.

Repeated Denominator Factors

The multiplicity of a denominator factor can affect the two-sided behavior.

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{(x-1)^2}, \]

the denominator is positive on both sides of \(x=1\), so both branches rise toward \(+\infty\).

Odd-Power Denominator Factors

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{(x-1)^3}, \]

the denominator changes sign across \(x=1\). One side approaches \(-\infty\), while the other approaches \(+\infty\).

Horizontal Asymptotes

A horizontal line

\[ \boxed{y=L} \]

is a horizontal asymptote when the function approaches \(L\) as \(x\) moves indefinitely far to the right, far to the left, or both.

Horizontal Asymptotes Describe End Behavior

A horizontal asymptote is about the ends of a graph, not about every point in the middle.

If

\[ f(x)\to3\quad\text{as}\quad x\to\infty, \]

then \(y=3\) is a right-end horizontal asymptote.

Example: The Reciprocal Function

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x}, \]

the outputs approach \(0\) as \(x\to\infty\) and as \(x\to-\infty\). Therefore,

\[ \boxed{y=0} \]

is a horizontal asymptote.

A Graph May Cross a Horizontal Asymptote

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x}{x^2+1}. \]

Its horizontal asymptote is \(y=0\), yet the graph contains the point \((0,0)\). Crossing the asymptote does not contradict the end behavior.

Different Behavior on the Two Ends

The left and right ends of a graph should always be considered separately.

A function can approach one value as \(x\to\infty\) and a different value as \(x\to-\infty\). In such a case, the graph has different horizontal asymptotic behavior on its two ends.

End Behavior Notation

We commonly describe right-end behavior with

\[ x\to\infty, \]

and left-end behavior with

\[ x\to-\infty. \]

These symbols do not mean that \(x\) ever becomes an actual number called infinity. They describe unbounded movement along the input axis.

Four Basic Polynomial End Patterns

Polynomial end behavior depends primarily on two features of the leading term:

  • whether the degree is even or odd;
  • whether the leading coefficient is positive or negative.

These two choices create four basic end-behavior patterns.

Even Degree, Positive Leading Coefficient

For a polynomial whose leading term looks like

\[ ax^{2n},\qquad a>0, \]

both ends rise:

\[ f(x)\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\quad x\to\pm\infty. \]

Even Degree, Negative Leading Coefficient

For

\[ ax^{2n},\qquad a<0, \]

both ends fall:

\[ f(x)\to-\infty\quad\text{as}\quad x\to\pm\infty. \]

Odd Degree, Positive Leading Coefficient

For

\[ ax^{2n+1},\qquad a>0, \]

the left end falls and the right end rises:

\[ f(x)\to-\infty\text{ as }x\to-\infty, \qquad f(x)\to+\infty\text{ as }x\to\infty. \]

Odd Degree, Negative Leading Coefficient

For

\[ ax^{2n+1},\qquad a<0, \]

the left end rises and the right end falls.

The Leading-Term Principle

For very large \(|x|\), the highest-degree term of a polynomial dominates the lower-degree terms.

Thus

\[ 4x^5-9x^3+2x-100 \]

has the same basic end behavior as \(4x^5\).

Example: Polynomial End Behavior

Consider

\[ f(x)=-3x^4+7x^2-5. \]

The leading term is \(-3x^4\): even degree and negative leading coefficient. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f(x)\to-\infty\text{ as }x\to\pm\infty}. \]

Polynomials Usually Do Not Have Horizontal Asymptotes

A nonconstant polynomial does not level off toward a finite constant as \(|x|\to\infty\). Its magnitude eventually grows without bound.

Its end behavior is instead summarized by its leading term.

Rational Functions and Degree Comparison

For a rational function

\[ f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}, \]

the relative degrees of \(P\) and \(Q\) often determine the end behavior.

Numerator Degree Less Than Denominator Degree

If

\[ \deg P<\deg Q, \]

then the denominator grows faster in magnitude, so

\[ f(x)\to0. \]

Therefore the horizontal asymptote is

\[ \boxed{y=0}. \]

Example: Lower-Degree Numerator

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{3x+1}{x^2+4}, \]

the numerator has degree \(1\) and the denominator has degree \(2\). Thus

\[ \boxed{y=0} \]

is the horizontal asymptote.

Equal Numerator and Denominator Degrees

If \(P\) and \(Q\) have the same degree, the end behavior is controlled by the ratio of their leading coefficients.

If

\[ f(x)=\frac{a_nx^n+\cdots}{b_nx^n+\cdots}, \]

then the horizontal asymptote is

\[ \boxed{y=\frac{a_n}{b_n}}. \]

Example: Equal Degrees

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{5x^2-3}{2x^2+x+7}, \]

the leading-coefficient ratio is \(5/2\). Therefore,

\[ \boxed{y=\frac52} \]

is the horizontal asymptote.

Numerator Degree Greater Than Denominator Degree

If the numerator degree exceeds the denominator degree, the function generally does not have a horizontal asymptote.

Polynomial division reveals the simpler expression that controls the end behavior.

Slant Asymptotes

A nonhorizontal line

\[ \boxed{y=mx+b} \]

can be a slant or oblique asymptote when the graph approaches that line as \(|x|\) becomes large.

When a Slant Asymptote Commonly Appears

For a rational function, a slant asymptote commonly occurs when the degree of the numerator is exactly one greater than the degree of the denominator.

Polynomial division identifies the line.

Example: Finding a Slant Asymptote

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x^2+1}{x-1}. \]

Polynomial division gives

\[ \frac{x^2+1}{x-1}=x+1+\frac{2}{x-1}. \]

As \(|x|\) becomes large,

\[ \frac{2}{x-1}\to0. \]

Therefore the slant asymptote is

\[ \boxed{y=x+1}. \]

Why Polynomial Division Reveals the Asymptote

The quotient gives the large-scale trend, while the remainder term becomes small relative to that trend.

In

\[ f(x)=x+1+\frac{2}{x-1}, \]

the difference between the function and the line \(y=x+1\) approaches zero.

A Graph May Cross a Slant Asymptote

As with horizontal asymptotes, crossing a slant asymptote is allowed.

What matters is that the vertical difference between the graph and the line approaches zero at the relevant end or ends.

Polynomial Asymptotes

If the numerator degree exceeds the denominator degree by more than one, polynomial division can produce a quadratic, cubic, or higher-degree quotient.

The graph may approach that polynomial as \(|x|\) grows. This is a polynomial asymptote, a generalization of horizontal and slant asymptotes.

Example: A Quadratic Asymptote

Suppose division gives

\[ f(x)=x^2-2x+3+\frac{5}{x+4}. \]

Since the remainder term approaches zero as \(|x|\to\infty\), the graph approaches

\[ \boxed{y=x^2-2x+3}. \]

A Unified View of End Asymptotes

Horizontal, slant, and higher-degree polynomial asymptotes all follow the same idea:

\[ f(x)-g(x)\to0 \]

as \(x\) moves toward the relevant end, where \(y=g(x)\) is the asymptotic model.

End Behavior Can Be Described Without an Asymptote

Not every useful end-behavior description involves an asymptote.

For example,

\[ f(x)=x^4-2x \]

satisfies

\[ f(x)\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\quad x\to\pm\infty, \]

even though no horizontal or slant asymptote is present.

Exponential End Behavior

The parent exponential function

\[ f(x)=b^x,\qquad b>1, \]

grows without bound as \(x\to\infty\) and approaches \(0\) as \(x\to-\infty\). Thus

\[ \boxed{y=0} \]

is a horizontal asymptote.

Exponential Decay

For

\[ f(x)=b^x,\qquad 0 the directions reverse: the graph approaches \(0\) as \(x\to\infty\) and grows without bound as \(x\to-\infty\).

Translated Exponential Asymptotes

If

\[ g(x)=A b^{x-h}+k, \]

then the vertical translation shifts the horizontal asymptote from \(y=0\) to

\[ \boxed{y=k}. \]

Logarithmic End Behavior

The parent logarithmic function

\[ f(x)=\log_b x \]

has domain \(x>0\). As \(x\to0^+\), its magnitude becomes unbounded, producing the vertical asymptote

\[ \boxed{x=0}. \]

Translated Logarithmic Asymptotes

For

\[ g(x)=A\log_b(x-h)+k, \]

the input shift moves the vertical asymptote to

\[ \boxed{x=h}. \]

Reciprocal Transformations

A transformed reciprocal function

\[ g(x)=\frac{a}{x-h}+k \]

has the asymptotes

\[ \boxed{x=h} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \boxed{y=k}. \]

These lines form a natural coordinate framework for the two branches.

Transformations Move Asymptotes with the Graph

Asymptotes are structural features. When a parent graph is translated, reflected, stretched, or compressed, its asymptotes transform along with it.

This is one reason the transformation framework from the previous lesson is so useful.

Vertical Reflection and End Behavior

Multiplying a function by \(-1\) reflects its graph across the \(x\)-axis. Every positive-infinity direction becomes negative-infinity behavior and vice versa.

A horizontal asymptote \(y=L\) becomes \(y=-L\).

Horizontal Reflection and End Behavior

Replacing \(x\) with \(-x\) reflects a graph across the \(y\)-axis. The left and right end behaviors exchange places.

A vertical asymptote \(x=a\) is reflected to \(x=-a\).

Asymptotes and Domain

A vertical asymptote commonly occurs at an input excluded from the natural domain of an algebraic formula, and it often splits that domain into separate intervals. More generally, assigning an isolated function value at the asymptotic input would not eliminate the unbounded nearby behavior.

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x-2}, \]

the domain is

\[ (-\infty,2)\cup(2,\infty). \]

Horizontal Asymptotes and Range

A horizontal asymptote does not automatically exclude its \(y\)-value from the range.

Some graphs cross their horizontal asymptotes, so whether that output is in the range must be checked from the actual function or graph.

Reciprocal Range Example

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x}+3, \]

the graph never reaches \(y=3\), so its range is

\[ (-\infty,3)\cup(3,\infty). \]

Here the horizontal asymptote does correspond to a missing output.

Asymptotes and Intercepts Are Different Features

An intercept is a point where the graph meets an axis. An asymptote describes limiting behavior near a line or curve.

A rational graph may have several intercepts and several asymptotes, and they should be found and recorded separately.

Finding \(x\)-Intercepts with Asymptotes Present

For a rational function in simplified form,

\[ f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}, \]

an \(x\)-intercept occurs where \(P(x)=0\), provided the input remains in the domain.

Denominator zeros instead concern holes or vertical asymptotes.

Finding the \(y\)-Intercept

If \(x=0\) belongs to the domain, evaluate

\[ f(0). \]

The point \((0,f(0))\) is the \(y\)-intercept. If \(x=0\) is excluded, the graph has no \(y\)-intercept.

Asymptotes Help Organize a Rational Graph

Before plotting many points, identify the structural features:

  • domain restrictions;
  • holes;
  • vertical asymptotes;
  • horizontal, slant, or polynomial asymptotes;
  • intercepts;
  • signs on the resulting intervals.

These features usually reveal the broad architecture of the graph.

Example: Sketching Structural Features

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-2}. \]

The denominator gives the vertical asymptote \(x=2\). Equal degrees give the horizontal asymptote \(y=1\). The \(x\)-intercept is \((-1,0)\), and the \(y\)-intercept is \((0,-\tfrac12)\).

These four features already constrain the shape strongly before additional points are plotted.

Rewriting Can Expose the Geometry

The same function can be rewritten as

\[ \frac{x+1}{x-2}=1+\frac{3}{x-2}. \]

This form makes the transformed reciprocal structure visible: shift right by \(2\), shift up by \(1\), and scale vertically by \(3\).

Approach from Above or Below

A graph approaching a horizontal or slant asymptote may lie above it, below it, or cross from one side to the other.

Rewriting the function as

\[ f(x)=g(x)+r(x) \]

helps: the sign of the small remainder \(r(x)\) tells which side of the asymptote the graph occupies.

End Behavior from a Graph

When reading a graph, follow the left branch in the direction of decreasing \(x\) and the right branch in the direction of increasing \(x\).

Arrows indicate continuation. Do not treat the edge of the graphing window as an endpoint unless the graph explicitly marks one.

The Graphing Window Can Hide End Behavior

A curve that appears almost horizontal in one window may later turn sharply. Likewise, an actual asymptote may not become visually convincing until much larger input magnitudes are shown.

Use algebraic structure to verify what the visible window suggests.

Scale Can Distort Apparent Approach

Unequal horizontal and vertical scales can make two curves appear closer or farther apart than they really are.

Asymptotic claims should be based on coordinate behavior, not merely on visual angle or apparent spacing.

Near-Parallel Is Not the Same as Asymptotic

Two curves can look parallel over a finite interval without the distance between them approaching zero.

To establish an asymptote, examine the limiting difference or use the algebraic form of the function.

Exact Versus Estimated Asymptotes

A graph may suggest an asymptote such as \(x\approx2\) or \(y\approx3\), but graphical reading is generally approximate.

When a formula is available, factoring, degree comparison, or division can often identify the asymptote exactly.

End Behavior and Function Families

Parent-function families carry recognizable end behavior:

  • polynomials follow leading-term patterns;
  • reciprocal functions approach horizontal and vertical asymptotes;
  • exponentials have one horizontal asymptotic direction;
  • logarithms have a vertical asymptotic boundary;
  • radical functions begin at or are restricted by domain boundaries rather than necessarily having asymptotes.

A Domain Boundary Is Not Automatically an Asymptote

For

\[ f(x)=\sqrt{x}, \]

the domain begins at \(x=0\), but the graph contains the finite endpoint \((0,0)\). The line \(x=0\) is not a vertical asymptote.

Unbounded Does Not Automatically Mean Asymptotic

The graph of \(y=x^2\) is unbounded, but its growth does not occur by approaching a vertical line at a finite input.

Unbounded output and vertical asymptotic behavior are related ideas, but they are not interchangeable.

Vertical Asymptotes Can Separate Graph Branches

A vertical asymptote often partitions a graph into branches that cannot connect through the excluded input.

Each branch should be analyzed on its own domain interval.

End Behavior Can Differ Across Branches

A rational function with several vertical asymptotes may have many local branches, but it still has only two global ends corresponding to \(x\to-\infty\) and \(x\to\infty\).

Do not confuse behavior near an internal vertical asymptote with global end behavior.

Local Infinity Versus End Infinity

Compare

\[ x\to2 \]

with

\[ x\to\infty. \]

In the first, the input stays near a finite number while the output may become unbounded. In the second, the input itself moves without bound.

A Gentle Introduction to Limit Language

Later mathematics formalizes these ideas with limits. For now, statements such as

\[ f(x)\to L\quad\text{as}\quad x\to\infty \]

mean that the outputs can be made arbitrarily close to \(L\) by moving far enough to the right.

Infinite Limit Language

A statement such as

\[ f(x)\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\quad x\to a^+ \]

means that the outputs can be made arbitrarily large positive by approaching \(a\) from the right.

It does not mean that the function takes the value “infinity.”

Asymptotes in Applied Models

Horizontal asymptotes often represent saturation, capacity, equilibrium, or a long-run baseline in applied models.

A model may approach a value indefinitely without reaching it exactly, or it may cross that value while still approaching it in the long run. The meaning depends on the model, not merely on the graph shape.

Vertical Asymptotes in Applied Models

A vertical asymptote can signal a boundary where a formula ceases to make sense, where a modeled quantity becomes arbitrarily large, or where the model itself is no longer physically meaningful.

Always distinguish mathematical behavior from the realistic domain of the application.

Asymptotes Are Descriptions, Not Physical Barriers

In pure mathematics, an asymptote describes limiting geometry. In an application, the corresponding line may represent a theoretical threshold, but it should not automatically be interpreted as an actual physical wall or impossible value.

Comparing Two Functions by End Behavior

End behavior helps compare growth rates. For large positive \(x\), for example, an exponential function can eventually outgrow any fixed-degree polynomial.

This lesson uses that fact descriptively. Later analysis courses make such comparisons more systematic.

Dominant Terms in Rational Functions

The degree rules for rational functions arise because lower-degree terms become relatively insignificant for large \(|x|\).

Dividing numerator and denominator by the highest relevant power of \(x\) makes this behavior visible.

Example: Equal Degrees by Division

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{5x^2-3}{2x^2+x+7}, \]

divide numerator and denominator by \(x^2\):

\[ f(x)=\frac{5-3/x^2}{2+1/x+7/x^2}. \]

As \(|x|\to\infty\), the reciprocal-power terms approach zero, leaving

\[ \frac52. \]

Example: Lower Numerator Degree by Division

For

\[ f(x)=\frac{4x+1}{x^2+9}, \]

divide by \(x^2\):

\[ f(x)=\frac{4/x+1/x^2}{1+9/x^2}. \]

Every term in the numerator approaches zero, so \(f(x)\to0\).

A Horizontal Asymptote Is Not Necessarily Two-Sided

When we say that \(y=L\) is a horizontal asymptote, we should know whether the approach occurs as \(x\to\infty\), as \(x\to-\infty\), or both.

Reporting the direction makes the statement more precise.

A Slant Asymptote May Also Be One-Sided

More complicated functions can approach a line on one end without approaching the same line on the other.

End behavior should therefore be checked separately rather than assumed to be symmetric.

Asymptotic Symmetry Is Not Graph Symmetry

Two ends approaching related asymptotes does not by itself make a function even or odd.

Symmetry requires the specific relationships developed in the earlier Symmetry and Function Families lesson.

Using a Table to Investigate End Behavior

If a formula is difficult to interpret immediately, a table with increasingly large positive and negative inputs can provide evidence.

For a suspected horizontal asymptote \(y=L\), compare the outputs with \(L\) and watch whether the difference appears to shrink.

Tables Near a Vertical Asymptote

To inspect behavior near \(x=a\), choose inputs such as

\[ a-0.1,\quad a-0.01,\quad a-0.001 \]

and separately

\[ a+0.1,\quad a+0.01,\quad a+0.001. \]

The two sides must be investigated independently.

Numerical Evidence Is Not the Same as Proof

A table can strongly suggest asymptotic behavior, but finitely many numerical samples cannot establish what happens for every sufficiently close or distant input.

Algebraic structure provides the stronger justification when available.

Graphing Technology and Asymptotes

Graphing software is useful for exploration, but it can connect points across a discontinuity, clip very large values, or make branches appear to touch an asymptote because of pixel resolution.

Treat the display as evidence to interpret, not as a substitute for the function's domain and algebraic structure.

Common Error: Every Denominator Zero Is a Vertical Asymptote

This fails when a common factor cancels.

Always simplify enough to distinguish removable holes from factors that remain in the denominator.

Common Error: A Graph Can Never Touch an Asymptote

Horizontal and slant asymptotes describe end behavior. A graph may meet or cross them at finite inputs.

Replace the “never touch” rule with the correct limiting definition.

Common Error: The Edge of the Window Is an Endpoint

A graph that disappears at the edge of a plotting window may simply continue beyond what is shown.

Use arrows, domain information, or the formula before drawing conclusions about end behavior.

Common Error: Confusing \(x=a\) with \(y=a\)

Vertical asymptotes have equations of the form

\[ x=a, \]

while horizontal asymptotes have equations of the form

\[ y=L. \]

Common Error: Reporting a Point Instead of an Asymptote

An asymptote is a line or curve, not an ordered pair.

Write \(x=2\), not \((2,0)\), for a vertical asymptote. Write \(y=3\), not \((0,3)\), for a horizontal asymptote.

Common Error: Using Degree Rules Before Simplifying

If numerator and denominator share a factor, cancellation can change the apparent degree structure and create holes.

Factor and simplify first, while preserving excluded values from the original expression.

Common Error: Ignoring the Two Ends

A complete end-behavior description should normally address both

\[ x\to-\infty \qquad\text{and}\qquad x\to\infty. \]

Do not assume the left end mirrors the right end.

Common Error: Calling Any Large Output a Vertical Asymptote

Large output values alone do not establish a vertical asymptote. The outputs must become unbounded as the input approaches a particular finite value.

Common Error: Confusing Growth with Approach

The graph of \(x^3\) grows without bound, but it does not approach a horizontal asymptote.

End growth and asymptotic leveling are different behaviors.

Common Error: Treating Infinity as an Attained Value

Symbols such as \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\) describe unbounded direction. They are not ordinary real-number outputs that a function reaches.

A Reliable Procedure for Vertical Asymptotes of Rational Functions

  1. Factor the numerator and denominator when possible.
  2. Record all excluded inputs from the original denominator.
  3. Cancel common factors algebraically, but keep track of the exclusions they created.
  4. Treat cancelled denominator factors as hole candidates.
  5. Treat remaining denominator zeros as vertical-asymptote candidates.
  6. Check nearby behavior or signs to determine the branch directions.

A Reliable Procedure for Rational End Behavior

  1. Simplify common factors while preserving domain exclusions.
  2. Compare the degrees of numerator and denominator.
  3. If the numerator degree is smaller, test \(y=0\).
  4. If the degrees are equal, use the ratio of leading coefficients.
  5. If the numerator degree is larger, perform polynomial division.
  6. Interpret the quotient as the candidate end asymptote.
  7. Confirm that the remainder term approaches zero.

A Reliable Procedure for Reading End Behavior from a Graph

  1. Check the axis scales and graph window.
  2. Identify whether arrows indicate continuation.
  3. Trace the graph toward the far left.
  4. Describe what happens as \(x\to-\infty\).
  5. Trace the graph toward the far right.
  6. Describe what happens as \(x\to\infty\).
  7. Look for a line or simpler curve the graph approaches.
  8. Use algebra to verify any suspected asymptote when a formula is available.

Example: Full Rational-Graph Analysis

Analyze

\[ f(x)=\frac{2x^2-8}{x^2-x-6}. \]

Factor:

\[ f(x)=\frac{2(x-2)(x+2)}{(x-3)(x+2)}. \]

Cancel \(x+2\):

\[ f(x)=\frac{2(x-2)}{x-3},\qquad x\ne-2,3. \]

Therefore \(x=-2\) is a hole input and

\[ \boxed{x=3} \]

is a vertical asymptote. Equal original/simplified leading degrees give the horizontal asymptote

\[ \boxed{y=2}. \]

Finding the Hole in the Full Example

Evaluate the simplified expression at \(x=-2\):

\[ \frac{2(-2-2)}{-2-3}=\frac{-8}{-5}=\frac85. \]

Thus the removable hole is at

\[ \boxed{\left(-2,\frac85\right)}. \]

Finding the Intercepts in the Full Example

The remaining numerator is zero at \(x=2\), so the \(x\)-intercept is

\[ \boxed{(2,0)}. \]

At \(x=0\),

\[ f(0)=\frac{-8}{-6}=\frac43, \]

so the \(y\)-intercept is

\[ \boxed{\left(0,\frac43\right)}. \]

Rewriting the Full Example Around Its Asymptotes

The simplified form can be rewritten as

\[ \frac{2(x-2)}{x-3}=2+\frac{2}{x-3}. \]

This immediately displays the reciprocal structure centered around the asymptotes \(x=3\) and \(y=2\).

What the Full Example Teaches

One rational graph can simultaneously contain:

  • a removable hole;
  • a vertical asymptote;
  • a horizontal asymptote;
  • an \(x\)-intercept;
  • a \(y\)-intercept;
  • separate branches determined by the domain.

These features should be assembled into one coherent graph rather than treated as isolated calculations.

Practice Answers

Practice 1

The denominator is zero at \(x=-3\), with no cancellation:

\[ \boxed{x=-3}. \]

Practice 2

\[ \frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} =x+3,\qquad x\ne3. \]

The factor cancels, so \(x=3\) produces a hole rather than a vertical asymptote. The hole is at \((3,6)\).

Practice 3

The numerator and denominator have equal degree. The ratio of leading coefficients is \(7/2\):

\[ \boxed{y=\frac72}. \]

Practice 4

The leading term is \(-2x^5\), which has odd degree and a negative leading coefficient:

\[ \boxed{f(x)\to+\infty\text{ as }x\to-\infty}, \] \[ \boxed{f(x)\to-\infty\text{ as }x\to\infty}. \]

Practice 5

Divide:

\[ \frac{x^2+3x+5}{x+1}=x+2+\frac{3}{x+1}. \]

Therefore the slant asymptote is

\[ \boxed{y=x+2}. \]

Practice 6

The denominator is zero at \(x=2\) and \(x=-4\), and neither factor cancels:

\[ \boxed{x=2\quad\text{and}\quad x=-4}. \]

Practice 7

The parent exponential has horizontal asymptote \(y=0\). The vertical shift downward by \(5\) gives

\[ \boxed{y=-5}. \]

Practice 8

The logarithm requires \(x+6>0\), and its boundary is \(x=-6\). Thus

\[ \boxed{x=-6}. \]

Practice 9

Cancel the common factor:

\[ \frac{(x-1)(3x+2)}{(x-1)(x+5)} = \frac{3x+2}{x+5},\qquad x\ne1,-5. \]

The cancelled factor gives a hole at \(x=1\). Its height is

\[ \frac{3(1)+2}{1+5}=\frac56, \]

so the hole is \(\left(1,\frac56\right)\). The remaining denominator gives

\[ \boxed{x=-5} \]

as the vertical asymptote. Equal degrees give

\[ \boxed{y=3} \]

as the horizontal asymptote.

Practice 10

A horizontal asymptote describes end behavior, not a prohibition against intersection. A graph may cross \(y=2\) at one or more finite inputs and still satisfy \(f(x)\to2\) as \(x\to\infty\), \(x\to-\infty\), or both.

What to Remember

  • Vertical asymptotes describe unbounded behavior near finite input values.
  • Horizontal asymptotes describe finite limiting heights at one or both ends.
  • Slant and polynomial asymptotes describe nonhorizontal long-run trends.
  • A hole is not a vertical asymptote.
  • Horizontal and slant asymptotes may be crossed.
  • For rational functions, simplify common factors before classifying denominator zeros.
  • Degree comparison and polynomial division reveal rational end behavior.
  • Polynomial end behavior is controlled by the leading term.
  • Left-end and right-end behavior should be analyzed separately.
  • Infinity describes unbounded direction, not an attained real-number value.

The most useful organizing habit is to ask two separate questions:

  1. What happens near each finite domain boundary?
  2. What happens as the graph continues indefinitely left and right?
