Intercepts, Zeros, and Intersections

Some points on a graph carry more information than ordinary points. Where a graph meets an axis, we obtain an intercept. Where a function reaches an output of zero, we obtain a zero and usually an \(x\)-intercept. Where two graphs meet, we obtain a common solution to both relations. These special points connect graphical structure directly to equations and systems.

The Central Idea

An intersection is a point shared by two graphical objects.

The coordinate axes are themselves graphs:

\[ x\text{-axis}:\quad y=0, \] \[ y\text{-axis}:\quad x=0. \]

Therefore, intercepts are special cases of intersections.

\[ \boxed{\text{intercept}=\text{intersection with a coordinate axis}}. \]

This simple idea unifies intercepts, zeros, and systems of equations.

The Two Coordinate Axes

Every point on the \(x\)-axis has vertical coordinate zero:

\[ (x,0). \]

Every point on the \(y\)-axis has horizontal coordinate zero:

\[ (0,y). \]

These coordinate patterns tell us how to calculate intercepts algebraically.

What Is an \(x\)-Intercept?

An \(x\)-intercept is a point where a graph meets the \(x\)-axis.

Because every point on the \(x\)-axis has \(y=0\), an \(x\)-intercept has the form

\[ \boxed{(a,0)}. \]

A graph may have no \(x\)-intercepts, one \(x\)-intercept, or several.

What Is a \(y\)-Intercept?

A \(y\)-intercept is a point where a graph meets the \(y\)-axis.

Because every point on the \(y\)-axis has \(x=0\), a \(y\)-intercept has the form

\[ \boxed{(0,b)}. \]

A function of \(x\) can have at most one \(y\)-intercept, although a more general relation can have several points on the \(y\)-axis.

Finding an \(x\)-Intercept Algebraically

To find an \(x\)-intercept, set

\[ \boxed{y=0} \]

and solve for \(x\).

This works because the calculation forces the point onto the \(x\)-axis.

Example: Finding an \(x\)-Intercept

Consider

\[ y=2x-6. \]

Set \(y=0\):

\[ 0=2x-6. \]

Solve:

\[ 6=2x, \] \[ x=3. \]

Therefore, the \(x\)-intercept is

\[ \boxed{(3,0)}. \]

Finding a \(y\)-Intercept Algebraically

To find a \(y\)-intercept, set

\[ \boxed{x=0} \]

and solve for \(y\).

This forces the point onto the \(y\)-axis.

Example: Finding a \(y\)-Intercept

For

\[ y=2x-6, \]

set \(x=0\):

\[ y=2(0)-6. \]

Thus,

\[ y=-6. \]

The \(y\)-intercept is

\[ \boxed{(0,-6)}. \]

Intercepts Are Points, Not Just Numbers

If a graph meets the \(x\)-axis at \(x=3\), the intercept is the ordered pair

\[ (3,0), \]

not merely the number \(3\).

Likewise, if a graph meets the \(y\)-axis at \(y=-6\), the intercept is

\[ (0,-6). \]

In informal discussion people sometimes name an intercept by its nonzero coordinate, but the graphical object is a point.

Reading Intercepts from a Graph

When a graph is already available, intercepts can often be read directly.

  • Look where the graph meets the horizontal axis for \(x\)-intercepts.
  • Look where the graph meets the vertical axis for \(y\)-intercepts.
  • Use the axis scale carefully before assigning coordinates.

If the crossing falls between labeled ticks, the graphical value may only be an estimate.

Exact and Estimated Intercepts

Algebra can sometimes produce an exact intercept such as

\[ \left(\sqrt{2},0\right). \]

A graph may show only an approximation:

\[ \left(1.41,0\right)\text{ approximately}. \]

Exact and approximate forms serve different purposes. A graph is often best for visual interpretation, while algebra can preserve exact values.

A Function Zero

A zero of a function is an input that produces an output of zero.

If

\[ f(c)=0, \]

then \(c\) is a zero of \(f\).

Graphically, provided \(c\) is in the domain, the point

\[ \boxed{(c,0)} \]

lies on the graph.

Zeros and \(x\)-Intercepts

For a function \(y=f(x)\), the equation for an \(x\)-intercept is

\[ f(x)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{zeros of }f\longleftrightarrow\text{\(x\)-intercepts of its graph}}. \]

The zero is the input value; the intercept is the corresponding point.

Example: Zero versus Intercept

Let

\[ f(x)=x-5. \]

Solve

\[ f(x)=0: \] \[ x-5=0, \] \[ x=5. \]

The zero is

\[ \boxed{5}, \]

while the \(x\)-intercept is

\[ \boxed{(5,0)}. \]

Roots, Zeros, and Solutions

Several words are closely related but refer to slightly different objects.

Term Typical meaning
Solution of \(f(x)=0\) An input value satisfying the equation
Zero of \(f\) An input \(c\) for which \(f(c)=0\)
Root of \(f(x)=0\) A solution value of the equation
\(x\)-intercept The graph point \((c,0)\)

In many elementary algebra settings, the same numerical value appears in all four descriptions, but the words emphasize different representations.

Example: Several Zeros

Let

\[ f(x)=x^2-9. \]

Find the zeros:

\[ x^2-9=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (x-3)(x+3)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=3\qquad\text{or}\qquad x=-3. \]

The zeros are \(-3\) and \(3\), and the \(x\)-intercepts are

\[ \boxed{(-3,0)\text{ and }(3,0)}. \]

A Graph Can Have No Real \(x\)-Intercepts

Consider

\[ y=x^2+1. \]

An \(x\)-intercept would require

\[ x^2+1=0, \]

so

\[ x^2=-1. \]

No real number has square \(-1\). Therefore, the real graph never meets the \(x\)-axis.

\[ \boxed{\text{no real \(x\)-intercepts}}. \]

Touching the Axis Still Counts

A graph does not need to cross through an axis to have an intercept.

For

\[ y=(x-2)^2, \]

the graph touches the \(x\)-axis at

\[ \boxed{(2,0)} \]

and turns back upward.

The point is still an \(x\)-intercept because it belongs to both the graph and the \(x\)-axis.

Crossing versus Touching

Near some zeros, a graph crosses from one side of the \(x\)-axis to the other. Near other zeros, it touches the axis and remains on the same side.

For example,

\[ y=x \]

crosses at the origin, while

\[ y=x^2 \]

touches the axis there.

Later polynomial analysis will connect this behavior to multiplicity and local sign changes.

Factored Form Reveals Zeros

Factored form can make zeros visible immediately.

Suppose

\[ f(x)=(x-4)(x+1). \]

For \(f(x)=0\), the zero-product principle gives

\[ x-4=0 \]

or

\[ x+1=0. \]

Thus the zeros are

\[ 4\qquad\text{and}\qquad -1, \]

giving intercepts

\[ \boxed{(4,0)\text{ and }(-1,0)}. \]

The \(y\)-Intercept of a Function

For a function \(y=f(x)\), a \(y\)-intercept exists exactly when \(0\) is in the domain.

When it exists, its coordinate is

\[ \boxed{(0,f(0))}. \]

This makes the \(y\)-intercept especially easy to calculate from function notation.

Example: Function Notation and the \(y\)-Intercept

Let

\[ f(x)=3x^2-2x+7. \]

Evaluate

\[ f(0)=3(0)^2-2(0)+7=7. \]

Therefore, the \(y\)-intercept is

\[ \boxed{(0,7)}. \]

A Function May Have No \(y\)-Intercept

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x}. \]

To have a \(y\)-intercept, we would need \(x=0\). But

\[ f(0)=\frac{1}{0} \]

is undefined.

Therefore, the graph has

\[ \boxed{\text{no \(y\)-intercept}}. \]

Domain Restrictions Matter

Algebraic simplification can sometimes hide a point that is excluded from the original relation.

Consider

\[ f(x)=\frac{x(x-2)}{x}. \]

For \(x\ne0\), this simplifies to

\[ f(x)=x-2. \]

But \(x=0\) is still excluded by the original denominator. The graph has a hole at \((0,-2)\), so that point is not a \(y\)-intercept.

An Apparent Crossing Must Actually Belong to the Graph

A plotted curve or simplified formula may suggest a point on an axis, but an intercept must belong to the actual relation.

Holes, excluded endpoints, and domain restrictions can remove points that a simpler-looking expression would otherwise contain.

Always check the original domain when a restriction is present.

Intercepts of an Implicit Relation

Intercepts are not limited to formulas written as \(y=f(x)\).

Consider the circle

\[ x^2+y^2=25. \]

To find \(x\)-intercepts, set \(y=0\):

\[ x^2=25, \] \[ x=\pm5. \]

Thus the \(x\)-intercepts are

\[ \boxed{(-5,0)\text{ and }(5,0)}. \]

Finding the Circle's \(y\)-Intercepts

For the same circle,

\[ x^2+y^2=25, \]

set \(x=0\):

\[ y^2=25. \]

Therefore,

\[ y=\pm5. \]

The \(y\)-intercepts are

\[ \boxed{(0,-5)\text{ and }(0,5)}. \]

This illustrates why a general relation can have more than one \(y\)-intercept.

Intercepts of a Relation Can Be Found by Restriction

Setting \(y=0\) restricts attention to the \(x\)-axis.

Setting \(x=0\) restricts attention to the \(y\)-axis.

In this sense, finding intercepts means solving the original relation under an additional axis condition.

What Is an Intersection of Two Graphs?

An intersection point is a point that belongs to both graphs.

If two equations are

\[ R_1(x,y) \]

and

\[ R_2(x,y), \]

then an intersection \((a,b)\) satisfies both conditions simultaneously.

\[ \boxed{(a,b)\text{ is a common solution}}. \]

Intersections Solve Systems Graphically

Consider the system

\[ \begin{cases} y=x+1,\\ y=-x+5. \end{cases} \]

A solution must lie on both lines. Therefore, the solution is found where the two graphs intersect.

Example: Solving a System by Intersection

For

\[ y=x+1 \]

and

\[ y=-x+5, \]

set the two expressions for \(y\) equal:

\[ x+1=-x+5. \]

Solve:

\[ 2x=4, \] \[ x=2. \]

Then

\[ y=2+1=3. \]

The graphs intersect at

\[ \boxed{(2,3)}. \]

Why Setting Functions Equal Finds Intersections

Suppose two graphs are

\[ y=f(x) \]

and

\[ y=g(x). \]

At an intersection, both graphs have the same input and the same output. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f(x)=g(x)}. \]

Solving this equation finds the input coordinates of their intersections.

Intersection Coordinates Require Both Values

Solving

\[ f(x)=g(x) \]

often gives the \(x\)-coordinate first.

To obtain the full intersection point, substitute that value into either original function to find \(y\).

A graphical intersection is an ordered pair, not merely an \(x\)-value.

One Intersection

Two nonparallel lines intersect exactly once.

For example,

\[ y=2x+1 \]

and

\[ y=-x+7 \]

have different slopes, so their graphs meet at one point.

Algebraically, the corresponding system has one solution.

No Intersection

Consider

\[ y=2x+1 \]

and

\[ y=2x-4. \]

The lines have the same slope but different \(y\)-intercepts. They are distinct parallel lines.

\[ \boxed{\text{no intersection}}. \]

The corresponding system has no solution.

Infinitely Many Intersections

Two equations can describe the same graph.

For example,

\[ y=2x+1 \]

and

\[ 2y=4x+2 \]

are equivalent.

Every point on one line is also on the other, so there are infinitely many shared points.

Graphical System Classification

Graph behavior Number of shared points System conclusion
Graphs meet once One One solution
Graphs do not meet Zero No solution
Graphs coincide Infinitely many Infinitely many solutions

Nonlinear systems can have other finite numbers of intersections, so this three-case table is especially characteristic of pairs of lines.

A Line and a Parabola Can Intersect Twice

Consider

\[ y=x^2 \]

and

\[ y=4. \]

Set them equal:

\[ x^2=4. \]

Thus

\[ x=\pm2. \]

The intersection points are

\[ \boxed{(-2,4)\text{ and }(2,4)}. \]

A Line and a Parabola Can Touch Once

Consider

\[ y=x^2 \]

and

\[ y=0. \]

The equation

\[ x^2=0 \]

has one real solution:

\[ x=0. \]

The line touches the parabola at

\[ \boxed{(0,0)}. \]

A Line and a Parabola Can Miss Each Other

Consider

\[ y=x^2 \]

and

\[ y=-1. \]

An intersection would require

\[ x^2=-1, \]

which has no real solution.

Therefore, the real graphs do not intersect.

Intersections Can Outnumber Two

More complicated curves can intersect many times.

The number of visible intersections depends on the equations, their domains, and the graphing window.

There is no general rule that two graphs must meet at most twice.

Axis Intercepts Are Systems Too

Finding an \(x\)-intercept of

\[ y=f(x) \]

is equivalent to solving the system

\[ \begin{cases} y=f(x),\\ y=0. \end{cases} \]

Likewise, finding a \(y\)-intercept solves

\[ \begin{cases} y=f(x),\\ x=0. \end{cases} \]

This is why intercepts fit naturally into the larger concept of intersections.

Zeros Are Intersections with the Zero Function

The \(x\)-axis can be viewed as the graph of the zero function

\[ g(x)=0. \]

A zero of \(f\) therefore occurs where

\[ f(x)=g(x), \]

or simply

\[ f(x)=0. \]

A zero is thus a special kind of function intersection.

Graphical Estimation of an Intersection

Sometimes an intersection does not fall on convenient grid coordinates.

A graph can then provide an estimate by reading the coordinates of the crossing as carefully as the scale allows.

For example, a graph might suggest

\[ x\approx1.7, \]

even when the exact value is not immediately obvious.

Graphical Estimates Depend on Scale

If adjacent tick marks represent one unit, a coordinate might be estimated to the nearest tenth.

If adjacent tick marks represent one hundred units, that same visual spacing cannot support tenth-unit precision.

Never report more graphical precision than the scale justifies.

Zooming Can Improve an Estimate

A closer graphing window can make an intersection easier to estimate.

However, zooming does not automatically create an exact value. It improves graphical resolution; it does not replace algebraic or numerical verification.

A Graphing Window Can Hide Intersections

If two curves do not appear to meet in the visible window, that does not prove that they never intersect.

An intersection may lie beyond the current horizontal or vertical bounds.

The visible graph is only a window into the complete mathematical object.

Example: An Intersection Outside the Window

Suppose the graphing window shows only

\[ -10\le x\le10. \]

The lines

\[ y=x \]

and

\[ y=-x+100 \]

intersect where

\[ x=-x+100, \]

so

\[ x=50, \qquad y=50. \]

The intersection \((50,50)\) exists even though it is invisible in the stated window.

Graphical Evidence and Algebraic Evidence

A graph is strong evidence about where solutions lie, but algebra can establish exact coordinates when symbolic methods are available.

These representations should support one another:

  • graph first to understand the geometry;
  • solve algebraically when an exact result is needed;
  • return to the graph to verify whether the answer is plausible.

Checking an Intercept by Substitution

Suppose we claim that \((3,0)\) is an \(x\)-intercept of

\[ y=2x-6. \]

Substitute \(x=3\) and \(y=0\):

\[ 0=2(3)-6. \] \[ 0=0. \]

The equation is true, so the point lies on the graph. Since its \(y\)-coordinate is zero, it lies on the \(x\)-axis as well.

Checking an Intersection by Substitution

Suppose two graphs are

\[ y=x+1 \]

and

\[ y=-x+5. \]

Test \((2,3)\):

\[ 3=2+1 \]

and

\[ 3=-2+5. \]

Both are true, so \((2,3)\) belongs to both graphs.

A Candidate Must Satisfy Every Relation

In a system, satisfying only one equation is not enough.

A point is an intersection only if it satisfies every relation whose graph is supposed to meet there.

This is the graphical counterpart of checking a candidate solution in each equation of a system.

Intersections of Inequality Boundaries

In systems of inequalities, boundary lines or curves may intersect at important corner or transition points.

However, the complete solution to an inequality system is usually a region, not merely the boundary intersections.

Boundary intersections help organize the geometry, but shading determines which points satisfy all inequalities.

An Intercept Can Be an Endpoint or Boundary Point

For a restricted graph, an intercept may occur exactly at an endpoint.

Whether that point belongs to the graph depends on whether the endpoint is included in the domain or relation.

Closed endpoints belong to the graph; open endpoints do not.

Context Can Give Intercepts Meaning

In an applied graph, an intercept may represent a meaningful condition.

For example, if \(x\) represents time and \(y\) represents height, then an \(x\)-intercept may represent a time when the modeled height is zero.

A \(y\)-intercept may represent the modeled height at time zero.

The \(y\)-Intercept Often Represents an Initial Value

When the horizontal variable measures elapsed time, the value \(x=0\) usually marks the starting moment.

Therefore, the \(y\)-intercept

\[ (0,f(0)) \]

often represents an initial amount, starting position, opening balance, or baseline condition.

An \(x\)-Intercept Can Mark a Threshold

If the vertical quantity measures profit, height, distance from a reference level, or another signed quantity, an \(x\)-intercept can mark where that quantity becomes zero.

The mathematical meaning is always the same:

\[ f(x)=0. \]

The practical interpretation depends on the variables and units.

Intersections Can Mark Equal Quantities

If two functions represent two competing quantities, an intersection occurs when their outputs are equal.

For example, if

\[ C(x) \]

represents cost and

\[ R(x) \]

represents revenue, then a break-even input satisfies

\[ C(x)=R(x). \]

Graphically, this is an intersection of the two curves.

Context Can Restrict Which Intersections Matter

A mathematical system may have several real intersections, but an application may accept only those within a meaningful domain.

For example, negative time or a negative number of manufactured units may be mathematically valid coordinates but inappropriate for a particular model.

Always interpret intersections within the stated constraints.

An Intercept Outside the Meaningful Domain May Not Be Relevant

Suppose a model is intended only for

\[ x\ge0. \]

If the extended formula has an \(x\)-intercept at \((-4,0)\), that point belongs to the unrestricted algebraic graph but not to the stated model.

Domain conditions are part of interpretation.

Coordinate Units Matter at Intersections

An intersection point such as

\[ (12,450) \]

is incomplete as an applied interpretation unless the axes are understood.

The coordinate might mean 12 months and 450 dollars, 12 meters and 450 newtons, or something entirely different.

Read labels and units before interpreting special points.

Table Values Can Reveal Intercepts

A table may display a zero directly.

\(x\) \(f(x)\)
1 6
2 3
3 0
4 \(-3\)

Since

\[ f(3)=0, \]

the table identifies the zero \(3\) and the graph point \((3,0)\).

A Sign Change Can Suggest a Zero Between Samples

Suppose a continuous function has

\[ f(2)>0 \]

and

\[ f(3)<0. \]

The graph moves from above the \(x\)-axis to below it between those inputs. Under continuity, this indicates at least one zero between \(2\) and \(3\).

Later analysis will formalize this reasoning more fully.

A Sign Change Is Not Required for Every Zero

The function

\[ f(x)=x^2 \]

has the zero \(x=0\), but the function is nonnegative on both sides of the zero.

Therefore, a sign-change search can detect many crossings but can miss zeros where the graph merely touches the axis.

Zeros Can Be Approximated Numerically

When an equation cannot be solved conveniently in exact symbolic form, graphing and numerical methods can estimate a zero.

A useful workflow is:

  1. graph the function;
  2. locate the approximate crossing or touch;
  3. narrow the interval;
  4. use a numerical solver if greater precision is required;
  5. substitute the result to check the residual.

Residuals Help Check Approximate Zeros

If \(x\approx a\) is proposed as a zero of \(f\), evaluate

\[ f(a). \]

For an approximate zero, the result should be close to zero within the required tolerance.

A small residual supports the numerical approximation, though the acceptable size depends on the problem and desired precision.

Intersections Can Also Be Approximated Numerically

To approximate an intersection of

\[ y=f(x) \]

and

\[ y=g(x), \]

solve

\[ f(x)-g(x)=0. \]

This converts the intersection problem into a zero-finding problem.

Intersection Problems Can Become Zero Problems

The equation

\[ f(x)=g(x) \]

is equivalent to

\[ f(x)-g(x)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{intersection of }f\text{ and }g \longleftrightarrow \text{zero of }f-g}. \]

This is another important bridge between graphical and algebraic methods.

Example: Convert an Intersection to a Zero

Find where

\[ f(x)=x^2 \]

and

\[ g(x)=2x+3 \]

intersect.

Set them equal:

\[ x^2=2x+3. \]

Move everything to one side:

\[ x^2-2x-3=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (x-3)(x+1)=0. \]

Thus

\[ x=3\qquad\text{or}\qquad x=-1. \]

Finish the Intersection Coordinates

For \(x=3\),

\[ y=3^2=9. \]

For \(x=-1\),

\[ y=(-1)^2=1. \]

Therefore, the graphs intersect at

\[ \boxed{(-1,1)\text{ and }(3,9)}. \]

Symmetry Can Create Paired Intercepts

Symmetric graphs often have intercepts that occur in pairs.

For example, if a graph is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis and contains

\[ (a,0), \]

then it also contains

\[ (-a,0). \]

Symmetry can therefore help predict or check intercept structure.

Not Every Axis Crossing Is a Function Zero

For a general relation, an \(x\)-intercept is still a point with \(y=0\).

The term zero of a function, however, specifically refers to an input of a function that produces zero.

The graphical geometry can be similar even when the underlying object is not a function of \(x\).

Vertical Lines Have No \(y\)-Intercept Unless \(x=0\)

Consider the vertical line

\[ x=4. \]

Every point on the line has \(x=4\), so it never meets the \(y\)-axis \(x=0\).

Therefore, it has no \(y\)-intercept.

It does meet the \(x\)-axis at

\[ \boxed{(4,0)}. \]

When a Relation Coincides with an Axis

The relation

\[ x=0 \]

is the \(y\)-axis itself.

Instead of meeting the \(y\)-axis at one isolated point, it coincides with the axis everywhere. This is another reminder that an intersection can be an entire shared graph rather than a single point, and that general relations can behave differently from ordinary functions \(y=f(x)\).

A Horizontal Line Usually Has One \(y\)-Intercept

For

\[ y=5, \]

setting \(x=0\) gives the point

\[ \boxed{(0,5)}. \]

The graph has no \(x\)-intercept because \(y\) is always \(5\), never zero.

The Zero Function Is a Special Case

For

\[ f(x)=0, \]

every input in the domain is a zero because

\[ f(x)=0 \]

for all such \(x\).

The graph is the \(x\)-axis itself, so every graph point is an \(x\)-axis point.

Intercept Form of a Line

When both intercepts are nonzero, a line can sometimes be written in intercept form:

\[ \frac{x}{a}+\frac{y}{b}=1. \]

The \(x\)-intercept is

\[ (a,0), \]

and the \(y\)-intercept is

\[ (0,b). \]

This form makes the two axis crossings explicit.

Example: Reading Intercept Form

Consider

\[ \frac{x}{6}+\frac{y}{3}=1. \]

The intercepts can be read directly:

\[ \boxed{(6,0)\text{ and }(0,3)}. \]

Substitution confirms both points satisfy the equation.

Intercepts Can Help Sketch a Line

Two distinct points determine a line.

When a line has convenient \(x\)- and \(y\)-intercepts, plotting those two points provides a fast graphing method.

This is useful, but the purpose of the present lesson is the meaning of those points as solutions, not merely the mechanics of drawing a line.

Intercepts Do Not Describe the Whole Graph

Two graphs can share the same intercepts and still have very different shapes between and beyond those points.

Intercepts are important landmarks, but they are not a complete description of a function or relation.

Later lessons will add domain, range, increase, decrease, extrema, symmetry, discontinuities, asymptotes, and end behavior.

Common Error: Setting the Wrong Variable to Zero

To find an \(x\)-intercept, set

\[ y=0, \]

not \(x=0\).

To find a \(y\)-intercept, set

\[ x=0. \]

A useful memory aid is that the coordinate not named by the axis becomes zero.

Common Error: Confusing a Zero with an Ordered Pair

If \(f(4)=0\), then

\[ 4 \]

is a zero of the function, while

\[ (4,0) \]

is the corresponding \(x\)-intercept.

Keep numerical inputs and graphical points distinct.

Common Error: Assuming Every Function Has an \(x\)-Intercept

A function may never attain the value zero.

For example,

\[ f(x)=x^2+4 \]

is always positive for real \(x\), so it has no real zeros and no real \(x\)-intercepts.

Common Error: Assuming Every Function Has a \(y\)-Intercept

A \(y\)-intercept requires \(x=0\) to belong to the domain.

Functions such as

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{x} \]

exclude \(x=0\), so no \(y\)-intercept exists.

Common Error: Counting a Hole as an Intercept

An open circle or hole marks a point that is not included in the graph.

Even if the hole lies on an axis, it is not an intercept unless the relation includes that point through some other defined value.

Common Error: Assuming No Visible Intersection Means No Solution

Two graphs may intersect outside the current graphing window.

Before concluding that a system has no solution, expand the window or analyze the equations algebraically.

Common Error: Reading the Scale as One Unit per Tick

A point crossing the horizontal axis at the third tick mark is not necessarily at \(x=3\).

If each tick represents \(5\) units, the coordinate is \(x=15\).

Always decode the scale before reading intercepts or intersections.

Common Error: Reporting Excess Precision

If a graph supports only a rough estimate near \(x=2.4\), reporting

\[ x=2.413827 \]

from visual inspection alone is unjustified.

Match the number of reported digits to the precision of the method.

Common Error: Solving \(f(x)=0\) When the Goal Is \(f(x)=g(x)\)

To find where two functions intersect, solve

\[ f(x)=g(x), \]

not necessarily

\[ f(x)=0. \]

The latter finds intersections with the \(x\)-axis, not with an arbitrary second function.

Common Error: Finding Only the \(x\)-Coordinate of an Intersection

Suppose solving

\[ f(x)=g(x) \]

gives

\[ x=2. \]

The graphical intersection is not complete until the corresponding \(y\)-coordinate is found.

Substitute \(x=2\) into either original equation.

Common Error: Ignoring Domain Restrictions in a System

Algebraic manipulation can produce a candidate value that lies outside the domain of one original relation.

Such a value cannot represent a true intersection of the original graphs.

Check each candidate in the original system.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding \(x\)-Intercepts

  1. Start with the original equation or relation.
  2. Set \(y=0\).
  3. Solve the resulting equation for all allowed real \(x\)-values.
  4. Apply any domain restrictions.
  5. Write each intercept as \((x,0)\).
  6. Substitute back when verification is useful.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding \(y\)-Intercepts

  1. Start with the original equation or relation.
  2. Set \(x=0\).
  3. Solve for all allowed \(y\)-values.
  4. Apply any domain restrictions.
  5. Write each intercept as \((0,y)\).
  6. Verify in the original relation if needed.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding Function Zeros

  1. Set \(f(x)=0\).
  2. Solve for every allowable input.
  3. Check for extraneous or excluded values.
  4. Report the input values as zeros.
  5. If asked for graph intercepts, convert each zero \(c\) to \((c,0)\).

A Reliable Procedure for Finding Intersections

  1. Identify the two relations whose shared points are required.
  2. If both are functions of \(x\), set their outputs equal.
  3. Otherwise, use an appropriate system-solving method.
  4. Solve for candidate coordinate values.
  5. Find the remaining coordinate for each candidate.
  6. Check every candidate in the original relations.
  7. Apply domain or contextual restrictions.
  8. Report the surviving ordered pairs.

A Reliable Graphical Procedure

  1. Confirm which curves, axes, or relations are being compared.
  2. Read the axis labels, units, and scale.
  3. Locate visible crossings or points of contact.
  4. Estimate coordinates only to supported precision.
  5. Adjust the window if important behavior may be hidden.
  6. Use algebra or numerical methods when an exact or more precise result is needed.
  7. Verify that each reported point satisfies the intended conditions.

Example: Full Intercept Workflow

Find the intercepts of

\[ 3x+2y=12. \]

For the \(x\)-intercept, set \(y=0\):

\[ 3x=12, \] \[ x=4. \]

Thus,

\[ (4,0). \]

For the \(y\)-intercept, set \(x=0\):

\[ 2y=12, \] \[ y=6. \]

Therefore, the intercepts are

\[ \boxed{(4,0)\text{ and }(0,6)}. \]

Example: Full Zero Workflow

Find the zeros of

\[ f(x)=x^2+x-6. \]

Set the function equal to zero:

\[ x^2+x-6=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (x+3)(x-2)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=-3\qquad\text{or}\qquad x=2. \]

The zeros are

\[ \boxed{-3\text{ and }2}, \]

and the corresponding \(x\)-intercepts are

\[ \boxed{(-3,0)\text{ and }(2,0)}. \]

Example: Full Intersection Workflow

Find the intersections of

\[ y=x^2-1 \]

and

\[ y=x+1. \]

Set the expressions equal:

\[ x^2-1=x+1. \]

Rearrange:

\[ x^2-x-2=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (x-2)(x+1)=0. \]

Thus,

\[ x=2\qquad\text{or}\qquad x=-1. \]

Finish the Full Intersection Workflow

Substitute into

\[ y=x+1. \]

For \(x=2\),

\[ y=3. \]

For \(x=-1\),

\[ y=0. \]

Therefore, the intersection points are

\[ \boxed{(2,3)\text{ and }(-1,0)}. \]

Notice that \((-1,0)\) is simultaneously an intersection of the two curves and an \(x\)-intercept of both at that point.

One Point Can Play Several Roles

A single point may be important for more than one reason.

For example, \((2,0)\) could be:

  • an \(x\)-intercept of one function;
  • a zero of that function represented graphically;
  • an intersection with the \(x\)-axis;
  • an intersection of two different graphs;
  • a solution to a system.

The coordinates stay the same; the interpretation changes with the question.

What to Remember

Intercepts, zeros, and intersections are all ways of identifying shared conditions on graphs.

  • an \(x\)-intercept is a point where \(y=0\);
  • a \(y\)-intercept is a point where \(x=0\);
  • a zero of \(f\) is an input \(c\) for which \(f(c)=0\);
  • a zero \(c\) corresponds to the \(x\)-intercept \((c,0)\);
  • an intersection point satisfies both graphical relations;
  • for \(y=f(x)\) and \(y=g(x)\), intersections satisfy \(f(x)=g(x)\);
  • intersections can be converted into zeros by solving \(f(x)-g(x)=0\);
  • domain restrictions can remove apparent intercepts or intersections;
  • graphical values may be estimates rather than exact coordinates;
  • the graphing window can hide solutions;
  • substitution verifies whether a candidate point really belongs to every required graph.

The central connection is

\[ \boxed{ \text{zeros and intercepts are special cases of graphical intersection} }. \]
