2.5 Functions and Dependency

Describing how quantities depend on one another and studying the structures, representations, and transformations of functional relationships.

Relationships That Assign and Depend

A function describes a relationship in which inputs determine outputs. This simple idea provides one of the most important organizing structures in mathematics. Functions allow us to describe how quantities depend on one another, represent patterns of change, compare mathematical behaviors, and construct models of real systems.

Functions can be represented in many ways. A relationship may appear as a formula, table, graph, algorithm, verbal rule, piecewise definition, implicit equation, or parametric description. Each representation reveals different information, and mathematical fluency requires being able to move among them.

This section develops functions as mathematical objects rather than treating them as formulas alone. The lessons examine inputs and outputs, domains and ranges, function behavior, composition, invertibility, alternative definitions, and the transformations that connect entire families of related functions.

Functions as More Than Formulas

A formula is one way to describe a function, but the function itself is the relationship between its allowable inputs and resulting outputs. The same function may be represented symbolically, numerically, graphically, verbally, or algorithmically without changing the underlying relationship.

This distinction becomes increasingly important as mathematics becomes more sophisticated. Some functions have simple formulas, while others are defined piecewise, recursively, implicitly, parametrically, or through computational procedures. The concept of function is broad enough to include all of these forms.

Learning to recognize the same relationship across different representations makes it possible to choose whichever form is most useful for the problem at hand. A graph may reveal behavior immediately, a table may display measured data, a formula may support symbolic manipulation, and an algorithm may describe a process that is difficult to express in closed form.

Dependency and Behavior

Functions provide a precise language for dependency. An independent variable is often treated as the input or quantity being selected, while a dependent variable records the value determined by the relationship. Parameters may alter the relationship itself, producing an entire family of related functions.

Once a function has been defined, we can ask structural questions about its behavior. Where is it increasing or decreasing? Where does it remain constant? Does it have maximum or minimum values? Where does it cross an axis? Does it return an input unchanged? Does every input produce a distinct output, and does every possible output occur?

These questions move function study beyond simple evaluation. They provide a framework for describing the shape, structure, and behavior of mathematical relationships before more specialized function families are introduced.

Building New Relationships from Existing Ones

Functions can also be treated as objects that can be combined and transformed. Composition links functions sequentially so that one relationship acts on the output of another. Inverse functions reverse suitable relationships. Transformations shift, stretch, compress, or reflect known functions to produce new members of a family.

Parametric, implicit, and piecewise representations extend this flexibility even further. They make it possible to describe relationships that would be awkward, incomplete, or impossible to express using a single explicit formula.

Together, these ideas establish the functional viewpoint that will organize much of the mathematics that follows. Linear, polynomial, rational, exponential, logarithmic, trigonometric, and many other relationships can all be studied as members of function families with recognizable structures and behaviors.
