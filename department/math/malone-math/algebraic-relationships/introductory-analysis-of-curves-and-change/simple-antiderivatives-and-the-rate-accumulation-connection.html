Simple Antiderivatives and the Rate–Accumulation Connection

Differentiation moves from a quantity to its local rate of change. Antidifferentiation moves in the reverse direction: from a rate back toward a family of quantities that could have produced it. Definite integration then uses this reverse relationship to recover accumulated change across an interval. This rate–accumulation connection unifies the main ideas developed throughout introductory analysis of curves and change.

The Central Idea

Suppose

\[ F'(x)=f(x). \]

Then \(f(x)\) is the rate of change of \(F(x)\).

Reversing the derivative asks:

Which function has derivative \(f(x)\)?

Any such function \(F(x)\) is called an antiderivative of \(f(x)\).

\[ \boxed{ F'(x)=f(x) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F\text{ is an antiderivative of }f }. \]

Derivative and Antiderivative

Differentiation and antidifferentiation point in opposite directions:

\[ \boxed{ F(x) \xrightarrow{\text{differentiate}} F'(x) }. \]

If

\[ F'(x)=f(x), \]

then

\[ \boxed{ f(x) \xrightarrow{\text{antidifferentiate}} F(x)+C }. \]

The \(+C\) will become important shortly.

Example: Reverse a Simple Derivative

We know

\[ \frac{d}{dx}(x^2)=2x. \]

Therefore, an antiderivative of

\[ 2x \]

is

\[ x^2. \]

But \(x^2\) is not the only one.

Why Antiderivatives Come in Families

Consider

\[ x^2, \qquad x^2+5, \qquad x^2-12. \]

Their derivatives are all

\[ 2x. \]

This happens because constants differentiate to zero.

Therefore, every function of the form

\[ \boxed{x^2+C} \]

is an antiderivative of \(2x\).

The Constant of Integration

The symbol

\[ C \]

represents an arbitrary constant.

It is called the constant of integration.

If \(F(x)\) is one antiderivative of \(f(x)\), then

\[ \boxed{ F(x)+C } \]

represents the entire family of antiderivatives.

Indefinite Integral Notation

The family of antiderivatives of \(f(x)\) is written

\[ \boxed{ \int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+C }. \]

This is called an indefinite integral.

The integral sign indicates antidifferentiation, and \(dx\) identifies the variable.

Definite and Indefinite Integrals Are Related but Different

An indefinite integral gives a family of antiderivative functions:

\[ \int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+C. \]

A definite integral gives a number representing signed accumulation over an interval:

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

These ideas are connected through the rate–accumulation theorem developed later in this lesson.

Reverse the Power Rule

The derivative power rule says

\[ \frac{d}{dx}(x^{n+1}) = (n+1)x^n. \]

To reverse this, divide by \(n+1\):

\[ \boxed{ \int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C }, \qquad n\ne-1. \]

In this introductory lesson, we focus mainly on nonnegative integer powers.

How to Use the Reverse Power Rule

To antidifferentiate

\[ x^n, \]
  1. increase the exponent by \(1\);
  2. divide by the new exponent;
  3. add \(C\).

Symbolically,

\[ x^n \longrightarrow \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C. \]

Example: Antiderivative of \(x^2\)

Increase the exponent:

\[ 2+1=3. \]

Divide by the new exponent:

\[ \int x^2\,dx = \frac{x^3}{3}+C. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \int x^2\,dx = \frac{x^3}{3}+C }. \]

Check by Differentiating

A reliable way to verify an antiderivative is to differentiate it.

\[ \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{x^3}{3}+C \right) = \frac13(3x^2)+0. \] \[ = x^2. \]

The original integrand is recovered.

Example: Antiderivative of \(x^5\)

\[ \int x^5\,dx = \frac{x^6}{6}+C. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \int x^5\,dx = \frac{x^6}{6}+C }. \]

Constant Multiple Rule for Antiderivatives

Constants can be carried through antidifferentiation:

\[ \boxed{ \int c\,f(x)\,dx = c\int f(x)\,dx }. \]

For example,

\[ \int 6x^2\,dx = 6\left(\frac{x^3}{3}\right)+C. \] \[ = \boxed{2x^3+C}. \]

Sum and Difference Rules

Simple sums and differences may be antidifferentiated term by term:

\[ \boxed{ \int[f(x)+g(x)]\,dx = \int f(x)\,dx + \int g(x)\,dx }. \]

Likewise,

\[ \boxed{ \int[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx = \int f(x)\,dx - \int g(x)\,dx }. \]

Antiderivative of a Constant

Because

\[ \frac{d}{dx}(cx)=c, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{ \int c\,dx = cx+C }. \]

For example,

\[ \int 7\,dx = \boxed{7x+C}. \]

Example: Antiderivative of a Polynomial

Find

\[ \int \left( 3x^2-4x+5 \right) dx. \]

Integrate term by term:

\[ \int3x^2\,dx=x^3, \] \[ \int-4x\,dx=-2x^2, \] \[ \int5\,dx=5x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ x^3-2x^2+5x+C }. \]

Verify the Polynomial Antiderivative

Differentiate:

\[ \frac{d}{dx} \left( x^3-2x^2+5x+C \right) \] \[ = 3x^2-4x+5. \]

The integrand is recovered exactly.

The Rate–Accumulation Connection

Suppose \(F'(x)=f(x)\).

Then \(f(x)\) is the rate of change of \(F(x)\).

Accumulating that rate from \(a\) to \(b\) recovers the net change in \(F\):

\[ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) }. \]

This is a central form of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

What the Theorem Says Conceptually

Differentiation tells us the local rate.

Integration accumulates that rate.

If we begin with a quantity \(F\), differentiate to obtain its rate \(F'\), and then accumulate that rate across an interval, we recover the total change in the original quantity:

\[ \boxed{ F' \xrightarrow{\text{accumulate from }a\text{ to }b} F(b)-F(a) }. \]

Evaluating a Definite Integral

If \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\), then

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a). \]

A compact notation is

\[ \boxed{ \left[F(x)\right]_a^b = F(b)-F(a) }. \]

Evaluate the antiderivative at the upper endpoint and subtract its value at the lower endpoint.

Example: Evaluate \(\int_0^2 x\,dx\)

An antiderivative of \(x\) is

\[ \frac{x^2}{2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \int_0^2 x\,dx = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^2. \] \[ = \frac{2^2}{2} - \frac{0^2}{2}. \] \[ = \boxed{2}. \]

Geometric Check of the Same Integral

The graph

\[ y=x \]

from \(0\) to \(2\) forms a triangle with base \(2\) and height \(2\).

\[ A = \frac12(2)(2) = \boxed{2}. \]

The geometric area agrees with the antiderivative calculation.

Example: Evaluate \(\int_1^3 2x\,dx\)

An antiderivative of \(2x\) is

\[ x^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \int_1^3 2x\,dx = \left[x^2\right]_1^3. \] \[ = 9-1 = \boxed{8}. \]

This matches the earlier rate–accumulation example involving \(F(x)=x^2\).

Example: Definite Integral of a Polynomial

Evaluate

\[ \int_0^2 (3x^2+2) \,dx. \]

An antiderivative is

\[ F(x)=x^3+2x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \left[ x^3+2x \right]_0^2 \] \[ = (8+4)-(0+0) \] \[ = \boxed{12}. \]

Why the \(+C\) Disappears in Definite Integrals

Suppose an antiderivative is

\[ F(x)+C. \]

Then

\[ [F(b)+C]-[F(a)+C] \] \[ = F(b)+C-F(a)-C. \]

The constants cancel:

\[ \boxed{ F(b)-F(a) }. \]

This is why a separate \(+C\) is not included in the final numerical value of a definite integral.

Indefinite Integrals Need \(+C\)

For an indefinite integral,

\[ \int f(x)\,dx, \]

there are no endpoints to subtract.

The full family of antiderivatives must therefore be represented:

\[ \boxed{ F(x)+C }. \]

Definite Integrals Produce Numbers

Once the endpoints are fixed,

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]

represents one accumulated value.

By contrast,

\[ \int f(x)\,dx \]

represents a family of functions.

Keeping these two outputs distinct is essential.

Initial Conditions Select One Antiderivative

Suppose

\[ F'(x)=2x \]

and

\[ F(0)=5. \]

First antidifferentiate:

\[ F(x)=x^2+C. \]

Use the initial condition:

\[ F(0)=0+C=5. \]

Thus,

\[ C=5. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ F(x)=x^2+5 }. \]

Rate plus Initial Value Determines the Quantity

A rate function alone determines a family of possible quantity functions.

An initial value selects one member of that family.

\[ \boxed{ \text{rate} + \text{initial condition} \longrightarrow \text{specific quantity function} }. \]

This idea appears throughout differential equations, motion, growth models, finance, and applied mathematics.

Example: Position from Velocity

Suppose velocity is

\[ v(t)=4t+1 \]

and

\[ s(0)=3. \]

Since

\[ s'(t)=v(t), \]

antidifferentiate:

\[ s(t) = 2t^2+t+C. \]

Use \(s(0)=3\):

\[ C=3. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ s(t)=2t^2+t+3 }. \]

Recovering Displacement from Velocity

With

\[ v(t)=4t+1, \]

the displacement from \(t=1\) to \(t=3\) is

\[ \int_1^3(4t+1)\,dt. \]

An antiderivative is

\[ 2t^2+t. \]

Therefore,

\[ [2t^2+t]_1^3 = (18+3)-(2+1) \] \[ = 21-3 = \boxed{18}. \]

Net Change Theorem

If a quantity \(Q(x)\) has rate of change

\[ Q'(x), \]

then the net change from \(a\) to \(b\) is

\[ \boxed{ Q(b)-Q(a) = \int_a^b Q'(x)\,dx }. \]

This is often called the net change theorem.

Final Value from Initial Value and Rate

Rearranging the net change relationship gives

\[ \boxed{ Q(b) = Q(a) + \int_a^b Q'(x)\,dx }. \]

This formalizes the accumulation pattern:

\[ \boxed{ \text{final} = \text{initial} + \text{accumulated change} }. \]

Example: Tank Volume from Flow Rate

Suppose a tank initially contains

\[ 100\text{ liters} \]

and the net flow rate is

\[ r(t)=2t+3 \]

liters per minute.

Find the amount after \(4\) minutes.

Accumulate the Flow

The net change in volume is

\[ \int_0^4(2t+3)\,dt. \]

An antiderivative is

\[ t^2+3t. \]

Evaluate:

\[ [t^2+3t]_0^4 = 16+12 = 28. \]

Thus, the tank gains

\[ \boxed{28\text{ liters}}. \]

Find the Final Tank Volume

Initial amount:

\[ 100\text{ liters}. \]

Net change:

\[ 28\text{ liters}. \]

Therefore,

\[ 100+28 = \boxed{128\text{ liters}}. \]

Signed Accumulation Still Matters

A definite integral is signed.

If a rate is negative over part of an interval, that part contributes negatively to net change.

Antiderivative evaluation automatically incorporates these signs.

Example: Negative Definite Integral

Evaluate

\[ \int_0^2(-3)\,dx. \]

An antiderivative is

\[ -3x. \]

Therefore,

\[ [-3x]_0^2 = -6-0 = \boxed{-6}. \]

The negative value represents net negative accumulation.

Area versus Signed Integral

If \(f(x)\ge0\) on \([a,b]\), then

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]

equals the ordinary geometric area under the curve.

If \(f\) changes sign, the integral gives signed area rather than total geometric area.

To obtain total geometric area, negative regions must be treated by magnitude.

Connection to Rectangular Approximation

The previous lesson approximated

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]

using sums such as

\[ \sum f(x_i^*)\Delta x. \]

Antiderivatives provide a far more efficient exact method for many simple functions:

\[ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) }. \]

The numerical and algebraic viewpoints compute the same accumulated quantity.

Why Numerical Methods Still Matter

Antiderivatives do not make numerical methods obsolete.

Numerical accumulation remains useful when:

  • only measured data are available;
  • the function has no convenient elementary antiderivative;
  • a quick approximation is sufficient;
  • technology is used to process large data sets.

Exact and numerical approaches complement one another.

Common Error: Forgetting \(+C\)

For an indefinite integral,

\[ \int 2x\,dx, \]

writing only

\[ x^2 \]

gives one antiderivative but not the complete family.

The full answer is

\[ \boxed{x^2+C}. \]

Common Error: Adding \(+C\) to a Definite Integral Result

A definite integral such as

\[ \int_0^2 x\,dx \]

evaluates to one number:

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

The arbitrary constant cancels during endpoint subtraction, so the final answer does not include \(+C\).

Common Error: Reversing the Power Rule Incorrectly

To antidifferentiate

\[ x^4, \]

do not subtract from the exponent.

Increase it:

\[ 4+1=5, \]

then divide by \(5\):

\[ \boxed{ \int x^4\,dx = \frac{x^5}{5}+C }. \]

Common Error: Multiplying by the New Exponent

Antidifferentiation reverses the derivative power rule.

Therefore,

\[ \int x^3\,dx \]

is not

\[ 4x^4. \]

The correct antiderivative is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{x^4}{4}+C }. \]

Common Error: Evaluating Lower Minus Upper

The endpoint rule is

\[ \boxed{ F(b)-F(a) }. \]

It is upper endpoint minus lower endpoint.

Reversing the order changes the sign of the definite integral.

Common Error: Using the Integrand at the Endpoints

To evaluate

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \]

first find an antiderivative \(F\).

Then compute

\[ F(b)-F(a). \]

In general,

\[ f(b)-f(a) \]

is not the definite integral.

Common Error: Confusing Initial Value with the Constant of Integration

The constant \(C\) is unknown until an additional condition is supplied.

If

\[ F(2)=7, \]

substitute \(x=2\) into the antiderivative family and solve for \(C\).

The initial value is information used to determine the constant; it is not automatically equal to \(C\).

Common Error: Confusing Rate with Accumulated Quantity

If

\[ v(t)=5t \]

is a velocity, then \(5t\) has velocity units.

Its integral over time has position units.

The rate and its accumulation are different physical quantities.

A Reliable Procedure for Simple Indefinite Integrals

  1. Rewrite the integrand as a sum of simple terms if useful.
  2. Integrate term by term.
  3. For \(x^n\), increase the exponent by \(1\).
  4. Divide by the new exponent.
  5. Carry constant multipliers through the process.
  6. Integrate constants as \(cx\).
  7. Add one overall constant of integration \(C\).
  8. Differentiate the answer to verify it.

A Reliable Procedure for Simple Definite Integrals

  1. Find an antiderivative \(F(x)\) of the integrand.
  2. Write the evaluation notation \([F(x)]_a^b\).
  3. Compute \(F(b)\).
  4. Compute \(F(a)\).
  5. Subtract: \(F(b)-F(a)\).
  6. Do not add \(+C\) to the final numerical result.
  7. Interpret the sign and units in context.

A Reliable Procedure for Rate–Accumulation Problems

  1. Identify the rate function.
  2. Identify the interval of accumulation.
  3. Integrate the rate over that interval.
  4. Interpret the integral as net change.
  5. If an initial quantity is known, add the net change to it.
  6. Report the final quantity with appropriate units.

Example: Full Indefinite-Integral Workflow

Find

\[ \int (4x^3-6x+2) \,dx. \]

Integrate each term:

\[ \int4x^3\,dx=x^4, \] \[ \int-6x\,dx=-3x^2, \] \[ \int2\,dx=2x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ x^4-3x^2+2x+C }. \]

Example: Full Definite-Integral Workflow

Evaluate

\[ \int_1^2 (4x^3-6x+2) \,dx. \]

Use

\[ F(x)=x^4-3x^2+2x. \]

Then

\[ F(2) = 16-12+4 = 8, \]

and

\[ F(1) = 1-3+2 = 0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \int_1^2 (4x^3-6x+2) \,dx = 8 }. \]

What to Remember

An antiderivative reverses differentiation:

\[ \boxed{ F'(x)=f(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C }. \]

For simple powers,

\[ \boxed{ \int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C }, \qquad n\ne-1. \]

Definite accumulation is found by endpoint evaluation:

\[ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) }. \]
  • indefinite integrals represent families of antiderivatives;
  • the constant of integration \(C\) is required for indefinite integrals;
  • definite integrals represent signed accumulated change and evaluate to numbers;
  • \(+C\) cancels in definite-integral endpoint subtraction;
  • an initial condition selects one antiderivative from the family;
  • integrating a rate over an interval gives net change;
  • final quantity equals initial quantity plus accumulated net change;
  • rectangular approximation and antiderivative evaluation are two routes to the same definite accumulation;
  • differentiation and integration form complementary local-rate and accumulation viewpoints.
