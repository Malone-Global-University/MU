Point-Slope and Two-Point Forms

Slope-intercept form is convenient when the \(y\)-intercept is known, but many problems give different information. Point-slope form builds a line directly from one known point and a slope, while two-point form builds the same relationship directly from two known points.

The Central Idea

Suppose a line has slope

\[ m=3 \]

and passes through

\[ (2,5). \]

Slope tells us that for any other point \((x,y)\) on the line,

\[ 3= \frac{y-5}{x-2}. \]

Multiply by \(x-2\):

\[ y-5=3(x-2). \]

This is point-slope form.

It describes the line directly from the information we were given:

\[ \boxed{ y-y_1=m(x-x_1) }. \]

Point-Slope Form

If a nonvertical line has slope \(m\) and passes through

\[ (x_1,y_1), \]

then its equation can be written

\[ \boxed{ y-y_1=m(x-x_1) }. \]

The quantities

\[ x-x_1 \]

and

\[ y-y_1 \]

measure changes from the known point to a variable point on the same line.

Point-Slope Form Comes Directly from the Definition of Slope

Start with a known point

\[ (x_1,y_1) \]

and any other point

\[ (x,y) \]

on a nonvertical line.

The slope is

\[ m= \frac{y-y_1}{x-x_1}. \]

Multiply both sides by

\[ x-x_1: \] \[ m(x-x_1)=y-y_1. \]

Reordering gives

\[ \boxed{ y-y_1=m(x-x_1) }. \]

Point-slope form is therefore not a separate rule to memorize. It is the slope formula rewritten as an equation.

Why the Known Point Is Built into the Formula

Substitute

\[ x=x_1 \]

into

\[ y-y_1=m(x-x_1). \]

Then

\[ y-y_1=m(0)=0. \]

So

\[ y=y_1. \]

Therefore, the equation automatically contains the point

\[ \boxed{(x_1,y_1)}. \]

Example: Positive Slope

Write an equation for the line with slope

\[ m=4 \]

through

\[ (3,7). \]

Use point-slope form:

\[ y-7=4(x-3). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ y-7=4(x-3) }. \]

This equation already fully describes the line.

Point-Slope Form Does Not Need to Be Simplified

The equation

\[ y-7=4(x-3) \]

is complete.

If slope-intercept form is useful, expand:

\[ y-7=4x-12. \]

Add \(7\):

\[ \boxed{y=4x-5}. \]

Both equations describe the same line.

Example: Negative Slope

Write an equation for the line with slope

\[ m=-2 \]

through

\[ (-1,6). \]

Substitute into point-slope form:

\[ y-6=-2(x-(-1)). \]

Simplify the double negative:

\[ \boxed{ y-6=-2(x+1) }. \]

Watch Double Negatives

If the known point is

\[ (-3,-5), \]

then point-slope form contains

\[ x-(-3)=x+3 \]

and

\[ y-(-5)=y+5. \]

For slope \(2\), the equation is

\[ \boxed{ y+5=2(x+3) }. \]

The signs come from subtracting the coordinates of the known point.

Example: Fractional Slope

Write an equation for the line with slope

\[ m=\frac35 \]

through

\[ (10,-2). \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ y+2= \frac35(x-10) }. \]

There is no need to convert the fraction to a decimal.

Any Point on the Line Can Be Used

Suppose a line has slope

\[ m=2 \]

and contains both

\[ (1,3) \]

and

\[ (4,9). \]

Using \((1,3)\):

\[ y-3=2(x-1). \]

Using \((4,9)\):

\[ y-9=2(x-4). \]

Both equations simplify to

\[ \boxed{y=2x+1}. \]

Point-slope form may be anchored at any known point on the line.

Point-Slope Form as a Change Equation

The equation

\[ y-y_1=m(x-x_1) \]

can be read as

\[ \boxed{ \text{output change} = (\text{rate})(\text{input change}) }. \]

This is the same relationship developed earlier:

\[ \Delta y=m\,\Delta x. \]

Point-slope form simply anchors those changes at a known point.

Predicting a New Output from a Known Point

Suppose a line has slope

\[ m=5 \]

and contains

\[ (4,12). \]

What is \(y\) when

\[ x=10? \]

Use the change:

\[ x-x_1=10-4=6. \]

Then

\[ y-12=5(6)=30. \]

So

\[ \boxed{y=42}. \]

Increment Form

Point-slope form can also be rearranged as

\[ y=y_1+m(x-x_1). \]

This emphasizes prediction:

\[ \boxed{ \text{new output} = \text{known output} + (\text{rate})(\text{input change}) }. \]

It is often useful in applied problems.

Example: Constant-Speed Motion

At time

\[ t=2\text{ hours}, \]

a vehicle is

\[ 150\text{ miles} \]

from a reference point.

It continues at

\[ 60\text{ miles per hour}. \]

Using the known point

\[ (2,150), \]

the model is

\[ d-150=60(t-2). \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ d=150+60(t-2) }. \]

This says: start from the known distance and add \(60\) miles for each additional hour.

Converting the Motion Model

Expand

\[ d=150+60(t-2). \]

Then

\[ d=150+60t-120. \]

So

\[ \boxed{ d=60t+30 }. \]

The slope-intercept form reveals that the model would have distance \(30\) miles at \(t=0\), while the point-slope form emphasizes the directly observed state at \(t=2\).

Why Point-Slope Form Can Be More Natural

Many situations do not begin at input \(0\).

We may know:

  • a measurement at a later time;
  • a price at a particular quantity;
  • a temperature at a particular moment;
  • a location at a known coordinate;
  • a population at a known year.

Point-slope form lets us build a model directly from the information actually given.

Horizontal Lines in Point-Slope Form

A horizontal line has

\[ m=0. \]

Through the point

\[ (x_1,y_1), \]

point-slope form gives

\[ y-y_1=0(x-x_1). \]

So

\[ \boxed{y=y_1}. \]

This correctly describes the horizontal line through the known point.

Vertical Lines Require a Separate Form

A vertical line has undefined slope.

Therefore, no finite number \(m\) can be substituted into

\[ y-y_1=m(x-x_1). \]

The vertical line through

\[ (x_1,y_1) \]

is instead written

\[ \boxed{x=x_1}. \]

From One Point and Slope to Slope-Intercept Form

Suppose

\[ m=3 \]

and the line passes through

\[ (5,8). \]

Point-slope form:

\[ y-8=3(x-5). \]

Expand:

\[ y-8=3x-15. \]

Add \(8\):

\[ \boxed{ y=3x-7 }. \]

Point-slope form and slope-intercept form are equivalent representations of the same nonvertical line.

From an \(x\)-Intercept and Slope

Suppose a line has slope

\[ m=-4 \]

and \(x\)-intercept

\[ (6,0). \]

Use the known point directly:

\[ y-0=-4(x-6). \]

So

\[ \boxed{ y=-4(x-6) }. \]

Expanding gives

\[ \boxed{ y=-4x+24 }. \]

From a \(y\)-Intercept and Slope

If the known point is already the \(y\)-intercept

\[ (0,b), \]

then point-slope form becomes

\[ y-b=m(x-0). \]

So

\[ y-b=mx, \]

and therefore

\[ \boxed{y=mx+b}. \]

Slope-intercept form is thus a special arrangement of point-slope form using the \(y\)-intercept as the anchor point.

Two-Point Information

Sometimes slope is not given directly.

Instead, we know two distinct points:

\[ (x_1,y_1) \]

and

\[ (x_2,y_2). \]

If

\[ x_1\ne x_2, \]

the slope is

\[ m= \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}. \]

Substituting this slope into point-slope form produces two-point form.

Two-Point Form

For two points with different \(x\)-coordinates,

\[ (x_1,y_1) \]

and

\[ (x_2,y_2), \]

the line through them can be written

\[ \boxed{ y-y_1 = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} (x-x_1) }. \]

This form uses only the two known points.

Two-Point Form Is Point-Slope Form with the Slope Filled In

Start with

\[ y-y_1=m(x-x_1). \]

For the two known points,

\[ m= \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}. \]

Substitute:

\[ y-y_1 = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} (x-x_1). \]

So two-point form is not a fundamentally different structure. It simply calculates the needed slope from the given points.

Ratio Form of the Two-Point Relationship

The same relationship may be written

\[ \boxed{ \frac{y-y_1}{x-x_1} = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} }, \]

whenever the displayed denominators are nonzero.

This says that the slope from the known point \((x_1,y_1)\) to a variable point \((x,y)\) equals the slope between the two known points.

Cross-Multiplied Two-Point Form

To avoid a fraction, multiply across:

\[ \boxed{ (x_2-x_1)(y-y_1) = (y_2-y_1)(x-x_1) }. \]

This form is often algebraically convenient and also handles horizontal lines naturally.

If

\[ x_1=x_2, \]

the two points instead determine a vertical line and should be treated separately.

Example: Build a Line from Two Points

Find the equation through

\[ (2,5) \]

and

\[ (6,17). \]

Compute the slope:

\[ m= \frac{17-5}{6-2} = \frac{12}{4} = 3. \]

Use the first point:

\[ y-5=3(x-2). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ y-5=3(x-2) }. \]

In slope-intercept form:

\[ y-5=3x-6, \] \[ \boxed{ y=3x-1 }. \]

Use Either Point

For the same line, use

\[ (6,17) \]

instead:

\[ y-17=3(x-6). \]

Expand:

\[ y-17=3x-18. \]

So

\[ y=3x-1. \]

Both anchors produce the same equation.

Swapping the Two Points Does Not Change the Slope

Using

\[ (2,5) \]

and

\[ (6,17), \]

we found

\[ m= \frac{17-5}{6-2} = 3. \]

Reverse both subtraction orders:

\[ m= \frac{5-17}{2-6} = \frac{-12}{-4} = 3. \]

The slope is unchanged.

Do Not Mix Point Orders

An incorrect calculation would be

\[ \frac{17-5}{2-6} = \frac{12}{-4} = -3. \]

The numerator and denominator no longer describe the same direction of change.

If the \(y\)-difference is

\[ y_2-y_1, \]

the corresponding \(x\)-difference must be

\[ x_2-x_1. \]

Example with a Negative Slope

Find the line through

\[ (-2,8) \]

and

\[ (4,-4). \]

The slope is

\[ m= \frac{-4-8}{4-(-2)} = \frac{-12}{6} = -2. \]

Using \((-2,8)\):

\[ y-8=-2(x+2). \]

So

\[ \boxed{ y-8=-2(x+2) }. \]

Expanding:

\[ y-8=-2x-4, \] \[ \boxed{ y=-2x+4 }. \]

Example with a Fractional Slope

Find the line through

\[ (1,2) \]

and

\[ (7,5). \]

The slope is

\[ m= \frac{5-2}{7-1} = \frac36 = \frac12. \]

Point-slope form gives

\[ \boxed{ y-2= \frac12(x-1) }. \]

Horizontal Two-Point Case

Suppose the points are

\[ (-3,7) \]

and

\[ (5,7). \]

The slope is

\[ m= \frac{7-7}{5-(-3)} = 0. \]

Point-slope form gives

\[ y-7=0(x+3). \]

So

\[ \boxed{y=7}. \]

Vertical Two-Point Case

Suppose the points are

\[ (4,-2) \]

and

\[ (4,9). \]

The slope formula would require

\[ \frac{9-(-2)}{4-4} = \frac{11}{0}, \]

which is undefined.

The points determine the vertical line

\[ \boxed{x=4}. \]

Neither ordinary point-slope form with finite \(m\) nor slope-intercept form applies.

Two Distinct Points Always Determine One Line

In Euclidean coordinate geometry, any two distinct points determine exactly one line.

There are two cases:

  • if their \(x\)-coordinates differ, the line has a finite slope and can use point-slope or two-point form;
  • if their \(x\)-coordinates are equal, the line is vertical and has equation \(x=c\).

Choosing the Easier Point

After finding the slope, either point may be used in point-slope form.

Choose the point that makes the arithmetic cleaner.

For example, if the points are

\[ (0,4) \]

and

\[ (7,18), \]

using \((0,4)\) makes the conversion especially simple:

\[ y-4=2(x-0), \]

so

\[ y=2x+4. \]

Point-Slope Form Preserves Given Information

Suppose a problem states:

“A line has slope \(-3\) and passes through \((8,11)\).”

The equation

\[ \boxed{ y-11=-3(x-8) } \]

visibly preserves both given facts.

This can make point-slope form easier to verify than a fully expanded equation.

Verify the Known Point

For

\[ y-11=-3(x-8), \]

substitute

\[ x=8. \]

Then

\[ y-11=-3(0)=0. \]

So

\[ y=11. \]

The anchor point is verified immediately.

Verify the Slope

From

\[ y-11=-3(x-8), \]

an input change of

\[ \Delta x=2 \]

produces

\[ \Delta y=-3(2)=-6. \]

Starting from

\[ (8,11), \]

another point is therefore

\[ (10,5). \]

Check:

\[ \frac{5-11}{10-8} = \frac{-6}{2} = -3. \]

Finding an Unknown Coordinate from a Slope

Suppose the line through

\[ (2,4) \]

and

\[ (7,y) \]

has slope \(3\).

Use

\[ 3= \frac{y-4}{7-2}. \]

Then

\[ 3= \frac{y-4}{5}. \]

Multiply by \(5\):

\[ 15=y-4. \]

So

\[ \boxed{y=19}. \]

Finding an Unknown Input Coordinate

Suppose the line through

\[ (-1,6) \]

and

\[ (x,-2) \]

has slope \(-2\).

Then

\[ -2= \frac{-2-6}{x-(-1)}. \]

So

\[ -2= \frac{-8}{x+1}. \]

Multiply:

\[ -2(x+1)=-8. \]

Thus,

\[ x+1=4, \]

and

\[ \boxed{x=3}. \]

Two-Point Form and Tables

Suppose a table gives:

\(x\) \(y\)
314
829

The slope is

\[ m= \frac{29-14}{8-3} = \frac{15}{5} = 3. \]

Using the first row:

\[ \boxed{ y-14=3(x-3) }. \]

This converts a pair of table observations directly into an affine equation.

Checking Additional Table Values

If the same table also contains

\[ (12,41), \]

check the equation:

\[ 41-14 \stackrel{?}{=} 3(12-3). \]

The left side is

\[ 27, \]

and the right side is

\[ 27. \]

The point is consistent with the same affine relationship.

Two Points Do Not Prove a Real Process Is Affine

Any two distinct nonvertical data points determine a line.

Therefore, two measurements always permit a two-point equation.

But that does not prove the underlying process follows a constant rate outside those two observations.

Additional data, theory, or a modeling assumption is needed to justify extending the line.

Two-Point Form as an Interpolation Rule

If a relationship is known to be affine between two points, then two-point form can estimate outputs between them.

Suppose

\[ (2,10) \]

and

\[ (8,34) \]

lie on an affine relationship.

The slope is

\[ m= \frac{34-10}{8-2} = 4. \]

At \(x=5\):

\[ y-10=4(5-2). \]

Thus,

\[ y=22. \]

So the interpolated point is

\[ \boxed{(5,22)}. \]

Interpolation will be studied more fully later.

Point-Slope Form and Units

In an applied equation

\[ y-y_1=m(x-x_1), \]

the units must remain consistent.

If \(y\) is measured in dollars and \(x\) in hours, then

\[ m \]

has units

\[ \frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{hour}}. \]

Then

\[ m(x-x_1) \]

has units of dollars, matching

\[ y-y_1. \]

Example: Temperature Change

At \(10\) minutes, a substance has temperature

\[ 72^\circ\text{C}. \]

It cools at a constant rate of

\[ -1.5^\circ\text{C per minute}. \]

Using the point

\[ (10,72), \]

the model is

\[ T-72=-1.5(t-10). \]

So

\[ \boxed{ T=72-1.5(t-10) }. \]

The form directly expresses temperature change relative to the known measurement.

Example: Production Rate

At \(3\) hours, a production line has completed \(140\) units.

It continues producing at \(35\) units per hour.

The point-slope model is

\[ P-140=35(t-3). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ P=140+35(t-3) }. \]

Finding the Implied Initial Value

Expand the production model:

\[ P=140+35t-105. \]

So

\[ \boxed{ P=35t+35 }. \]

The affine extension implies

\[ P(0)=35. \]

Whether that value is physically meaningful depends on the actual history and valid domain of the process.

Point-Slope Form Can Reduce Unnecessary Extrapolation

If the only trustworthy measurement is at \(t=3\), the form

\[ P=140+35(t-3) \]

keeps attention on change from the known state.

The slope-intercept form

\[ P=35t+35 \]

is algebraically equivalent, but its intercept may tempt us to interpret \(t=0\) even when that time lies outside the intended model interval.

From Point-Slope to a General Linear Equation

Point-slope form can be expanded and rearranged.

For example,

\[ y-5=3(x-2) \]

becomes

\[ y-5=3x-6. \]

Move terms:

\[ 3x-y=1. \]

So the same line may be written

\[ \boxed{3x-y=1}. \]

The next lesson will study standard form systematically.

Equivalent Forms Preserve the Same Line

These equations all describe the same relationship:

\[ y-5=3(x-2), \] \[ y=3x-1, \]

and

\[ 3x-y=1. \]

Different forms expose different features:

  • point-slope form exposes a known point and slope;
  • slope-intercept form exposes slope and \(y\)-intercept;
  • standard form organizes both variables on one side.

Point-Slope Form and Function Notation

For a function \(f\), point-slope form may be written

\[ \boxed{ f(x)-f(x_1)=m(x-x_1) }. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ f(x)=f(x_1)+m(x-x_1) }. \]

This emphasizes that the output at \(x\) is obtained by starting from the known output \(f(x_1)\) and adding the change produced by the slope.

Affine Difference Property Revisited

For an affine function

\[ f(x)=mx+b, \]

we have

\[ f(x)-f(x_1) = m(x-x_1). \]

The intercept disappears from the difference.

This is why point-slope form works without knowing \(b\).

Why Point-Slope Form Is Especially Useful

Point-slope form is efficient when a problem gives:

  • a slope and one point;
  • a rate and one observed state;
  • an \(x\)-intercept and slope;
  • two points, after computing their slope;
  • a tangent or local line later in calculus.

It avoids solving for the \(y\)-intercept unless that information is actually needed.

A Gentle Preview of Tangent Lines

Later, calculus often determines the slope of a curve at a particular point.

If a function has value

\[ f(a) \]

at \(x=a\), and the tangent slope there is \(m\), the tangent line can be written

\[ \boxed{ y-f(a)=m(x-a) }. \]

This is exactly point-slope form.

The algebra developed here therefore remains useful far beyond straight-line problems.

A Reliable Point-Slope Workflow

  1. Identify the slope \(m\).
  2. Identify a known point \((x_1,y_1)\).
  3. Write \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\).
  4. Substitute carefully, especially when coordinates are negative.
  5. Leave the equation in point-slope form unless another form is requested or useful.
  6. Verify the known point and slope.

A Reliable Two-Point Workflow

  1. Identify the two distinct points.
  2. Check whether their \(x\)-coordinates are equal.
  3. If they are equal, write the vertical line \(x=c\).
  4. Otherwise compute \(m=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\).
  5. Choose either point as the anchor.
  6. Write point-slope form.
  7. Convert to another form only if needed.
  8. Check the second point.

A Reliable Modeling Workflow

  1. Identify the input and output quantities.
  2. Identify a known observation \((x_1,y_1)\).
  3. Determine the constant rate \(m\).
  4. Write \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\).
  5. Attach units to the slope.
  6. Interpret the equation as change from the known state.
  7. Restrict the domain when the real situation requires it.

Common Errors

Using \(y+y_1\) Instead of \(y-y_1\)

The formula is

\[ y-y_1=m(x-x_1). \]

If a coordinate is negative, subtraction may simplify to addition.

Forgetting Double Negatives

If \(x_1=-4\), then

\[ x-x_1=x-(-4)=x+4. \]

Using the Wrong Slope Between Two Points

Keep the subtraction order consistent in numerator and denominator.

Assuming Point-Slope Form Must Be Expanded

Point-slope form is already a complete equation of the line.

Forcing a Vertical Line into Point-Slope Form

Vertical lines have undefined slope and are written \(x=c\).

Assuming Two Data Points Prove Constant Rate

Two points determine a line, but a real process may still be nonlinear.

Ignoring Units

In applications, the slope must be interpreted as output units per input unit.

Choosing a Point Not on the Line

The anchor point must satisfy the line. A wrong point produces a different equation even if the slope is correct.

Expand the point-slope form:

\[ y+2=-3x+15. \]

Subtract \(2\):

\[ y=-3x+13. \]

So the equations are equivalent.

Key Ideas

  • Point-slope form is \(\displaystyle y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\).
  • Point-slope form is the slope formula rearranged.
  • The known point \((x_1,y_1)\) is built directly into the equation.
  • The equation can be read as output change equals rate times input change.
  • The equivalent prediction form is \(\displaystyle y=y_1+m(x-x_1)\).
  • Any point on the same line may serve as the anchor point.
  • Horizontal lines work with \(m=0\).
  • Vertical lines have undefined slope and must be written separately as \(x=c\).
  • Two-point form substitutes \(\displaystyle m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\) into point-slope form.
  • Two points with different \(x\)-coordinates determine a unique finite slope and one nonvertical line.
  • Two points with the same \(x\)-coordinate determine a vertical line.
  • Reversing both subtraction orders preserves slope; mixing the orders changes its sign incorrectly.
  • Point-slope form can be converted to slope-intercept or standard form when useful.
  • Point-slope form is often preferable when a problem gives a rate and an observed state away from \(x=0\).
  • The same form later becomes important for tangent-line equations in calculus.

Where This Leads

Point-slope and two-point forms show that a line can be built directly from local information: one point plus a rate, or two points that determine that rate. They make the constant-difference structure of affine relationships explicit without requiring the \(y\)-intercept first.

Next, these same lines will be organized in standard form, which treats the variables more symmetrically and handles vertical lines naturally. From there, the course will develop horizontal, vertical, parallel, and perpendicular relationships, efficient graphing methods, and construction of lines from increasingly varied data and geometric conditions.
