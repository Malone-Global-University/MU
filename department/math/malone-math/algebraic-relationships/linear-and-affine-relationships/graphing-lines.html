Graphing Lines

A straight line can be graphed from many kinds of information: a slope and intercept, a point and slope, two points, two intercepts, a table, or a standard-form equation. The most efficient method depends on what the problem gives us. Fluency means recognizing the available structure and choosing a method that minimizes unnecessary work.

The Central Idea

Consider the line

\[ y=2x+1. \]

We can graph it in several equivalent ways.

From slope-intercept form, we see:

\[ m=2, \qquad b=1. \]

So the graph contains

\[ (0,1). \]

Because

\[ 2=\frac21, \]

we can move right \(1\) and up \(2\) to find another point:

\[ (1,3). \]

Another repetition gives

\[ (2,5). \]

These points all lie on one straight line.

The equation, the slope, the intercept, the table of points, and the graph are different representations of the same relationship.

What Does It Mean to Graph a Line?

To graph a line means to represent all coordinate pairs that satisfy its equation.

For

\[ y=2x+1, \]

every point \((x,y)\) on the graph must satisfy

\[ y=2x+1. \]

For example:

\[ (0,1), \qquad (1,3), \qquad (2,5), \qquad (-1,-1). \]

A graph is not merely a picture. It is a geometric representation of the complete solution set.

Two Distinct Points Determine a Line

A straight line is uniquely determined by two distinct points.

Therefore, most graphing methods have the same underlying goal:

\[ \boxed{\text{find two reliable points}} \]

and draw the unique line through them.

Different equation forms simply make certain points or directions easier to obtain.

Method 1: Graph from Slope-Intercept Form

If an equation is written

\[ \boxed{y=mx+b}, \]

then:

  • \(b\) gives the \(y\)-intercept;
  • \(m\) gives rise over run.

This is often the fastest graphing method when the equation is already in slope-intercept form.

Example: Positive Integer Slope

Graph

\[ y=3x-2. \]

The \(y\)-intercept is

\[ \boxed{(0,-2)}. \]

The slope is

\[ 3=\frac31. \]

From \((0,-2)\), move:

\[ \Delta x=1, \qquad \Delta y=3. \]

This gives

\[ (1,1). \]

Repeat:

\[ (2,4). \]

Draw the line through the points.

Example: Negative Slope

Graph

\[ y=-2x+5. \]

Start at the \(y\)-intercept:

\[ (0,5). \]

Write the slope as

\[ -2=\frac{-2}{1}. \]

Move right \(1\) and down \(2\):

\[ (1,3). \]

Another step gives

\[ (2,1). \]

The graph falls from left to right.

Example: Fractional Slope

Graph

\[ y=\frac34x-1. \]

The \(y\)-intercept is

\[ (0,-1). \]

The slope is

\[ \frac34. \]

Move right \(4\) and up \(3\):

\[ (0,-1)\longrightarrow(4,2). \]

Another valid movement is left \(4\) and down \(3\):

\[ (0,-1)\longrightarrow(-4,-4). \]

Negative Fractional Slopes

Suppose

\[ m=-\frac25. \]

Two convenient interpretations are:

\[ \Delta x=5, \qquad \Delta y=-2, \]

or

\[ \Delta x=-5, \qquad \Delta y=2. \]

Both describe the same line direction.

Use Repeated Slope Steps as a Check

If a line has slope

\[ \frac23, \]

then moving right \(3\) and up \(2\) from any point on the line should land on another point on the line.

Repeating the same movement provides several checkpoints and helps reveal graphing mistakes.

Method 2: Graph from Point-Slope Form

If an equation is written

\[ \boxed{ y-y_1=m(x-x_1) }, \]

then:

  • \((x_1,y_1)\) is a known point;
  • \(m\) is the slope.

Plot the known point and use the slope to find another point.

Example: Point-Slope Form

Graph

\[ y-4=2(x-3). \]

The equation reveals the point

\[ \boxed{(3,4)} \]

and slope

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

From \((3,4)\), move right \(1\) and up \(2\):

\[ (4,6). \]

The line through these points is the graph.

Read Negative Coordinates Carefully

Consider

\[ y+3=-\frac12(x-4). \]

Rewrite the left side mentally as

\[ y-(-3). \]

So the known point is

\[ \boxed{(4,-3)}. \]

The slope is

\[ -\frac12. \]

From \((4,-3)\), move right \(2\) and down \(1\).

Point-Slope Form Does Not Need Conversion Before Graphing

For

\[ y-4=2(x-3), \]

we could expand to

\[ y=2x-2. \]

But conversion is unnecessary if the point and slope are already visible.

Graphing efficiently often means using the information in its current form instead of rewriting automatically.

Method 3: Graph from Two Points

If two distinct points are known, plot them and draw the line through them.

For example, if a line contains

\[ (-2,1) \]

and

\[ (4,7), \]

those two points already determine the graph.

No slope calculation is required merely to draw the line.

Why We May Still Calculate the Slope

Although two points are enough to graph the line, calculating slope can provide a useful check.

For

\[ (-2,1) \]

and

\[ (4,7), \]

the slope is

\[ m= \frac{7-1}{4-(-2)} = \frac66 = 1. \]

The graph should therefore rise one unit for each unit moved to the right.

Method 4: Graph from Intercepts

If the \(x\)- and \(y\)-intercepts are easy to find, they provide two natural points.

For a line

\[ Ax+By=C, \]

set

\[ y=0 \]

to find the \(x\)-intercept, and set

\[ x=0 \]

to find the \(y\)-intercept.

Example: Standard Form by Intercepts

Graph

\[ 3x+2y=12. \]

Set \(y=0\):

\[ 3x=12, \] \[ x=4. \]

So one point is

\[ \boxed{(4,0)}. \]

Set \(x=0\):

\[ 2y=12, \] \[ y=6. \]

So another point is

\[ \boxed{(0,6)}. \]

Draw the line through these intercepts.

Intercept Graphing Is Not Always the Best Choice

Consider

\[ 7x+11y=13. \]

The intercepts are

\[ \left(\frac{13}{7},0\right) \]

and

\[ \left(0,\frac{13}{11}\right). \]

These are valid, but they may be inconvenient to plot by hand.

Another representation or a carefully chosen table may be easier.

Method 5: Graph from a Table

We can choose input values, compute corresponding outputs, and plot the resulting points.

For

\[ y=2x-1, \]

choose convenient values:

\(x\) \(y=2x-1\)
\(-1\)\(-3\)
0\(-1\)
11
23

Plotting any two distinct points is sufficient, while additional points provide verification.

Choose Table Values Strategically

Good table values simplify arithmetic.

For

\[ y=\frac12x+3, \]

even \(x\)-values such as

\[ -4,\quad -2,\quad 0,\quad 2,\quad 4 \]

avoid unnecessary fractions.

Graphing should be mathematically accurate, but it need not create avoidable arithmetic work.

Graphing from Standard Form by Solving for \(y\)

Another option is to convert

\[ Ax+By=C \]

to slope-intercept form when

\[ B\ne0. \]

For example,

\[ 2x+4y=8 \]

becomes

\[ 4y=-2x+8, \] \[ y=-\frac12x+2. \]

Now graph using the \(y\)-intercept and slope.

Choose Between Intercepts and Slope

For

\[ 2x+4y=8, \]

intercept graphing gives

\[ (4,0) \]

and

\[ (0,2). \]

Slope-intercept conversion gives

\[ y=-\frac12x+2. \]

Both methods are easy.

The better method is simply the one that is clearer and faster for the problem at hand.

Graphing Horizontal Lines

For

\[ y=4, \]

every point has \(y\)-coordinate \(4\).

Examples include

\[ (-2,4), \qquad (0,4), \qquad (5,4). \]

The graph is a horizontal line through height \(4\).

Graphing Vertical Lines

For

\[ x=-3, \]

every point has \(x\)-coordinate \(-3\).

Examples include

\[ (-3,-4), \qquad (-3,0), \qquad (-3,6). \]

The graph is a vertical line through \(x=-3\).

Do Not Use Slope-Intercept Form for Vertical Lines

A vertical line has undefined slope.

There is no finite number \(m\) that turns

\[ y=mx+b \]

into

\[ x=c. \]

Graph vertical lines directly from their constant \(x\)-coordinate.

Graphing a Line Through the Origin

Consider

\[ y=-\frac32x. \]

The \(y\)-intercept is

\[ (0,0). \]

The slope is

\[ -\frac32. \]

From the origin, move right \(2\) and down \(3\):

\[ (2,-3). \]

Or move left \(2\) and up \(3\):

\[ (-2,3). \]

When Both Intercepts Are the Origin

A nonzero-slope line through the origin has the same point as both its \(x\)- and \(y\)-intercept.

One point does not determine the line.

So intercept graphing alone is not sufficient.

Use the slope or compute another point.

Graphing from a Word Description

Suppose a quantity starts at \(5\) and increases by \(3\) for every unit of input.

The starting value gives the point

\[ (0,5). \]

The rate gives slope

\[ m=3. \]

The equation is

\[ y=3x+5, \]

and the graph can be constructed directly from the verbal information.

Example: Fixed Fee and Unit Rate

A service charges \$10 initially plus \$4 per hour.

Let \(t\) be time and \(C\) be cost.

The model is

\[ C=4t+10. \]

Graphically:

  • the vertical intercept is \(10\);
  • the slope is \(4\) dollars per hour.

The graph starts at the fixed charge and rises at the hourly rate.

Graphing a Decreasing Model

A tank begins with \(120\) liters and loses \(15\) liters per minute.

The model is

\[ V=-15t+120. \]

The graph begins at

\[ (0,120) \]

and falls with slope

\[ -15. \]

The \(t\)-intercept is found from

\[ 0=-15t+120, \]

so

\[ t=8. \]

The meaningful physical segment may therefore run from

\[ (0,120) \]

to

\[ (8,0). \]

The Algebraic Line May Extend Beyond the Model

The equation

\[ V=-15t+120 \]

defines an infinite algebraic line if no domain restriction is stated.

But a physical tank model may only make sense for

\[ 0\le t\le8. \]

Graphing a mathematical relation and graphing a realistic model are not always identical tasks.

Restricted Line Segments

Suppose

\[ y=2x+1, \qquad 0\le x\le5. \]

The formula describes a line, but the domain restriction tells us to graph only the portion between

\[ x=0 \]

and

\[ x=5. \]

The endpoints are

\[ (0,1) \]

and

\[ (5,11). \]

The graph is a line segment rather than the entire infinite line.

Open and Closed Endpoints

If a restriction is

\[ 0then \(x=0\) is excluded and \(x=5\) is included.

Graphically:

  • use an open endpoint at the excluded boundary;
  • use a closed endpoint at the included boundary.

This connects graphing lines with interval notation and domain restrictions developed earlier.

Graphing a Line from One Point and a Direction

Sometimes a problem gives no equation at all.

If we know the point

\[ (2,-1) \]

and a direction vector

\[ (3,4), \]

then the line moves:

\[ \Delta x=3, \qquad \Delta y=4. \]

So another point is

\[ (5,3). \]

The slope is

\[ \frac43. \]

Plot the two points and draw the line.

Direction Vectors and Slope

A direction vector

\[ (a,b) \]

with

\[ a\ne0 \]

corresponds to slope

\[ \boxed{ m=\frac{b}{a} }. \]

Direction vectors provide another bridge between algebraic graphing and later vector geometry.

Graphing a Line from a Normal Vector

Standard form

\[ Ax+By=C \]

has normal vector

\[ (A,B). \]

A direction vector along the line is

\[ (B,-A). \]

So if one point on the line is known, this direction can generate a second point without first solving for slope.

Example Using a Direction Vector from Standard Form

Consider

\[ 2x+3y=12. \]

A direction vector is

\[ (3,-2). \]

One easy point is the \(y\)-intercept

\[ (0,4). \]

Add the direction vector:

\[ (0,4)+(3,-2)=(3,2). \]

Check:

\[ 2(3)+3(2)=12. \]

The points \((0,4)\) and \((3,2)\) determine the graph.

Graphing Parallel Lines

Consider

\[ y=2x+1 \]

and

\[ y=2x-4. \]

Both have slope

\[ 2. \]

Graph each from its own \(y\)-intercept, then use the same slope step.

The lines maintain the same direction and never intersect.

Graphing Perpendicular Lines

Consider

\[ y=2x+1 \]

and

\[ y=-\frac12x+3. \]

The slopes are negative reciprocals:

\[ 2\left(-\frac12\right)=-1. \]

Graphing both reveals their right-angle intersection.

The algebra predicts the geometry before the lines are drawn.

Graphing as a Verification Tool

A graph can help detect algebraic mistakes.

Suppose an equation is believed to have positive slope, but the drawn line falls from left to right.

Something is inconsistent.

Similarly, if the equation predicts \(y\)-intercept

\[ (0,5), \]

but the graph crosses at \((0,-5)\), the graph or algebra must be corrected.

Algebra Should Verify the Graph Too

Graphing by eye is not enough.

If a plotted point is claimed to lie on

\[ y=3x-2, \]

substitute its coordinates into the equation.

For \((4,10)\):

\[ 3(4)-2=10. \]

The point is verified.

Use More Than Two Points When Accuracy Matters

Two points determine a line mathematically.

But when drawing by hand, a mistaken point can produce an incorrect graph.

A third point can serve as a check.

If all three calculated points align, confidence in the graph increases.

Do Not Connect Incorrect Data Automatically

Suppose a table gives

\[ (0,1), \qquad (1,3), \qquad (2,8). \]

The first two points have slope

\[ 2, \]

while the next pair has slope

\[ 5. \]

The points are not collinear.

Drawing one straight line through them would misrepresent the data.

Testing Collinearity by Slope

Three or more points are collinear if they lie on one line.

One way to test this is to compare slopes between pairs.

For

\[ (0,2), \qquad (2,6), \qquad (5,12), \]

we have

\[ \frac{6-2}{2-0}=2, \]

and

\[ \frac{12-6}{5-2}=2. \]

The equal rates show that the points are collinear.

Testing Collinearity by an Equation

From

\[ (0,2) \]

and

\[ (2,6), \]

the line is

\[ y=2x+2. \]

Check the third point:

\[ 2(5)+2=12. \]

So

\[ (5,12) \]

lies on the same line.

Graphing from an Equation with Fractions

Suppose

\[ 3x+4y=7. \]

Solving for \(y\) gives

\[ y=-\frac34x+\frac74. \]

That form is correct, but the intercept is fractional.

We may instead search for convenient integer-coordinate solutions.

Finding Convenient Points Algebraically

For

\[ 3x+4y=7, \]

choose

\[ x=1. \]

Then

\[ 3+4y=7, \] \[ y=1. \]

So

\[ (1,1) \]

is on the line.

Choose

\[ x=5. \]

Then

\[ 15+4y=7, \] \[ 4y=-8, \] \[ y=-2. \]

So another point is

\[ (5,-2). \]

These integer points may be easier to plot accurately.

Graphing Is a Representation Choice

There is rarely only one valid graphing method.

For the same line, we may use:

  • slope and \(y\)-intercept;
  • two intercepts;
  • a known point and slope;
  • two known points;
  • a table;
  • a direction vector;
  • standard-form structure.

Good mathematical judgment means selecting the representation that exposes useful information with the least unnecessary work.

Choosing a Method from Slope-Intercept Form

If the equation is already

\[ y=mx+b, \]

usually use:

\[ \boxed{\text{\(y\)-intercept + slope}}. \]

There is generally no reason to convert to standard form merely to graph it.

Choosing a Method from Point-Slope Form

If the equation is already

\[ y-y_1=m(x-x_1), \]

usually use:

\[ \boxed{\text{known point + slope}}. \]

The form already tells you exactly what to plot first.

Choosing a Method from Standard Form

If the equation is

\[ Ax+By=C, \]

consider:

  • intercepts, if they are simple;
  • solving for \(y\), if slope-intercept form becomes simple;
  • choosing convenient values, if both alternatives produce awkward fractions.

Choosing a Method from a Table

If a table already provides at least two distinct collinear points, plot them directly.

If you also need the equation or rate, calculate the slope afterward.

Do not create extra algebra unless the problem actually requires it.

Choosing a Method from a Word Problem

Identify:

  • the input variable;
  • the output variable;
  • the rate of change;
  • a known or initial value.

Then translate the situation into points and changes before graphing.

Axes Must Be Labeled

In an applied graph, label each axis with:

  • the quantity represented;
  • the unit of measurement.

For a distance-time graph, for example:

  • horizontal axis: time in hours;
  • vertical axis: distance in miles.

This makes the slope interpretable as miles per hour.

Choose an Appropriate Scale

A graph should use a scale that makes important features visible.

If all values lie between \(500\) and \(600\), an axis running from \(-10{,}000\) to \(10{,}000\) would make the data nearly unreadable.

Scale is part of effective mathematical communication.

Keep Tick Spacing Consistent

If equally spaced marks on an axis represent

\[ 0,\quad 5,\quad 10,\quad 15, \]

do not suddenly treat the next equal interval as \(100\).

Consistent numerical spacing is essential for an accurate graph.

Horizontal and Vertical Axis Scales May Differ

The \(x\)-axis and \(y\)-axis do not need to use the same numerical scale.

For example:

  • one horizontal grid unit may represent \(10\) hours;
  • one vertical grid unit may represent \(100\) dollars.

But this can change the visual appearance of steepness.

The numerical slope must still be calculated from actual coordinate values.

Do Not Estimate Slope Only from Visual Angle

A line may look steep or flat depending on the graph's aspect ratio and axis scales.

The slope is determined by

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}, \]

using coordinate values—not by the physical angle of ink on the page.

Extending a Line

Unless a domain restriction is given, a line equation such as

\[ y=2x+1 \]

usually represents an infinite line extending in both directions.

A hand-drawn graph commonly uses arrowheads to indicate continued extension.

Line, Ray, or Segment?

These are different geometric objects.

  • A line extends infinitely in both directions.
  • A ray begins at one endpoint and extends infinitely in one direction.
  • A segment has two endpoints.

Domain restrictions can turn the graph of an affine formula into a ray or segment.

Graphing Inequalities Preview

Later, a line may act as the boundary of a two-variable inequality.

For example,

\[ 2x+3y=12 \]

may be graphed first as a boundary line before determining which side satisfies

\[ 2x+3y\le12. \]

Accurate line graphing therefore supports later work with regions and constraints.

Graphing Systems Preview

When two line equations are graphed on the same coordinate plane, their relationship becomes visible.

  • one intersection suggests one solution;
  • parallel distinct lines suggest no common solution;
  • identical lines suggest infinitely many common solutions.

This provides a geometric interpretation of systems of equations.

A Reliable Slope-Intercept Graphing Workflow

  1. Identify \(b\).
  2. Plot \((0,b)\).
  3. Write \(m\) as rise over run.
  4. Use the slope to locate a second point.
  5. Use a third point as a check when useful.
  6. Draw the line through the points.

A Reliable Point-Slope Graphing Workflow

  1. Identify \((x_1,y_1)\).
  2. Plot the known point.
  3. Identify the slope \(m\).
  4. Use rise over run to locate another point.
  5. Draw and verify the line.

A Reliable Standard-Form Graphing Workflow

  1. Check first for a horizontal or vertical special case.
  2. Try the intercept method.
  3. If intercepts are awkward, consider solving for \(y\).
  4. If that is also awkward, choose convenient coordinate values.
  5. Plot at least two verified points.
  6. Draw the line through them.

A Reliable Graph Verification Workflow

  1. Check the sign of the slope against the graph's direction.
  2. Check the \(y\)-intercept when one exists.
  3. Substitute plotted points into the original equation.
  4. Check horizontal or vertical special cases.
  5. Check domain restrictions and endpoints.
  6. Check axis labels and scale in applications.

Common Errors

Plotting \(b\) on the \(x\)-Axis

In

\[ y=mx+b, \]

\(b\) gives the point

\[ (0,b), \]

which lies on the \(y\)-axis.

Reversing Rise and Run

Slope is

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}, \]

not

\[ \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta y}. \]

Dropping the Sign of the Slope

A negative slope must produce a line that falls as \(x\) increases.

Forgetting That Vertical Lines Have Undefined Slope

Graph \(x=c\) directly as a vertical line.

Using Only One Point

Except for a separately recognized horizontal or vertical condition, one point alone does not determine a unique line.

Using Unequal Axis Spacing

Coordinate distances must reflect the numerical scale consistently.

Connecting Noncollinear Data with One Straight Line

Verify constant slope before claiming several data points lie on one affine relationship.

Extending a Real-World Model Beyond Its Valid Domain

The formula may define a full line while the application permits only a segment or ray.

The slope is

\[ 2=\frac21. \]

From \((0,3)\), move right \(1\) and up \(2\) to reach

\[ \boxed{(1,5)}. \]

2. Negative Fractional Slope

For

\[ y=-\frac23x+4, \]

give two points suitable for graphing.

Check Answer

Start at

\[ (0,4). \]

Move right \(3\) and down \(2\):

\[ \boxed{(3,2)}. \]

So two suitable points are

\[ \boxed{(0,4)\text{ and }(3,2)}. \]

For

\[ y-5=3(x+2), \]

identify the known point and slope.

Check Answer \[ x+2=x-(-2). \]

So the known point is

\[ \boxed{(-2,5)} \]

and the slope is

\[ \boxed{3}. \]

4. Graph by Intercepts

Find two intercept points for

\[ 2x+5y=20. \]
Check Answer

Set \(y=0\):

\[ 2x=20, \] \[ x=10. \]

So

\[ \boxed{(10,0)}. \]

Set \(x=0\):

\[ 5y=20, \] \[ y=4. \]

So

\[ \boxed{(0,4)}. \]

5. Horizontal Line

Describe the graph of

\[ y=-6. \]
Check Answer

It is a horizontal line through

\[ y=-6. \]

Its slope is

\[ \boxed{0}. \]

6. Vertical Line

Describe the graph of

\[ x=5. \]
Check Answer

It is a vertical line through

\[ x=5. \]

Its slope is

\[ \boxed{\text{undefined}}. \]

7. Build a Table

Give three points on

\[ y=-x+2. \]
Check Answer

For example:

\[ x=0\Rightarrow y=2, \] \[ x=1\Rightarrow y=1, \] \[ x=2\Rightarrow y=0. \]

So three points are

\[ \boxed{(0,2),\ (1,1),\ (2,0)}. \]

8. Check Collinearity

Are

\[ (0,1), \qquad (2,5), \qquad (5,11) \]

collinear?

Check Answer \[ \frac{5-1}{2-0}=2, \]

and

\[ \frac{11-5}{5-2}=2. \]

The slopes agree, so the points are

\[ \boxed{\text{collinear}}. \]

9. Choose a Graphing Method

Which method is especially convenient for

\[ 4x+3y=24? \]
Check Answer

Intercept graphing is especially convenient because

\[ x\text{-intercept}=(6,0) \]

and

\[ y\text{-intercept}=(0,8). \]

Both are easy to plot.

10. Restricted Domain

For

\[ y=3x+1, \qquad 1\le x\le4, \]

find the segment endpoints.

Check Answer

At \(x=1\):

\[ y=4. \]

At \(x=4\):

\[ y=13. \]

So the endpoints are

\[ \boxed{(1,4)\text{ and }(4,13)}. \]

11. Convenient Integer Points

Find two integer-coordinate points on

\[ 3x+2y=7. \]
Check Answer

Choose \(x=1\):

\[ 3+2y=7, \] \[ y=2. \]

So

\[ (1,2). \]

Choose \(x=3\):

\[ 9+2y=7, \] \[ y=-1. \]

So another point is

\[ (3,-1). \]

Thus one valid pair is

\[ \boxed{(1,2)\text{ and }(3,-1)}. \]

12. Verify a Point

Does

\[ (4,9) \]

lie on

\[ y=2x+1? \]
Check Answer \[ 2(4)+1=9. \]

Yes.

\[ \boxed{(4,9)\text{ lies on the line}}. \]

Key Ideas

  • A line graph represents every coordinate pair satisfying its equation.
  • Two distinct points determine a unique line.
  • Slope-intercept form is efficiently graphed using the \(y\)-intercept and rise over run.
  • Point-slope form is efficiently graphed using the known point and slope.
  • Two known points can be plotted directly without first finding an equation.
  • Standard form can often be graphed efficiently by intercepts.
  • A table can generate points when another form is inconvenient.
  • Horizontal lines have equation \(y=c\); vertical lines have equation \(x=c\).
  • A line through the origin needs another point or slope because both ordinary intercepts coincide.
  • Different equation forms suggest different graphing strategies.
  • Choosing convenient values can avoid unnecessary fractions.
  • Additional points can verify a hand-drawn graph.
  • Substitution verifies whether a plotted point lies on the line.
  • Domain restrictions can turn an infinite affine line into a segment or ray.
  • Axis labels, units, and scale are part of mathematically accurate graphing.
  • Visual steepness can be distorted by axis scaling, so numerical slope remains the reliable measure.

Where This Leads

Graphing lines unifies the symbolic, numerical, geometric, and applied representations developed throughout this section. A slope, intercept, pair of points, table, equation, or verbal rate can all be translated into the same straight-line structure.

From here, the focus shifts from graphing a line that is already specified to constructing lines from data and conditions. That includes determining equations from observations, constraints, geometric relationships, and model requirements before moving into linear equations, inequalities, interpolation, extrapolation, trends, and systems.
