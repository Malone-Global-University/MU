Common Denominators and Rationalization

Fractions and radicals often become easier to combine or manipulate after they are rewritten into equivalent forms with more useful denominators.

The Central Idea

Two common algebraic rewrites involve denominators:

  • Common denominators let us combine rational expressions that initially have different denominators.
  • Rationalization replaces a denominator containing radicals with an equivalent denominator that does not contain those radicals.

Both techniques are based on a simple principle:

Multiply by a form of \(1\) so the value does not change.

For example,

\[ \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{2}{2} = \frac{2}{6}. \]

The appearance changes, but the value does not.

Equivalent Fractions

A fraction can be rewritten by multiplying its numerator and denominator by the same nonzero quantity.

If \(c\ne0\), then

\[ \frac{a}{b} = \frac{ac}{bc}, \]

provided the original denominator \(b\ne0\).

This works because

\[ \frac{c}{c}=1. \]

So

\[ \frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{c}{c} = \frac{a}{b}. \]

This is the foundation of common denominators and many rationalization techniques.

Why Common Denominators Are Needed

Addition and subtraction of fractions operate on equal-sized parts.

For example,

\[ \frac{1}{4}+\frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{4}. \]

The denominators match, so the numerators can be combined.

But in

\[ \frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}, \]

halves and thirds are different-sized parts. We first rewrite both fractions using a shared denominator.

A common denominator is \(6\):

\[ \frac{1}{2}=\frac{3}{6}, \] \[ \frac{1}{3}=\frac{2}{6}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{3}{6}+\frac{2}{6} = \frac{5}{6}. \]

The Least Common Denominator

Any common multiple of the denominators can work, but the least common denominator usually keeps the arithmetic simplest.

For example, to combine

\[ \frac{3}{8}+\frac{5}{12}, \]

we seek the least common multiple of \(8\) and \(12\).

\[ \operatorname{lcm}(8,12)=24. \]

Rewrite:

\[ \frac{3}{8}=\frac{9}{24}, \] \[ \frac{5}{12}=\frac{10}{24}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{9}{24}+\frac{10}{24} = \frac{19}{24}. \]

Common Denominators with Variables

The same idea applies to algebraic fractions.

Consider

\[ \frac{2}{x}+\frac{3}{y}. \]

A common denominator is

\[ xy. \]

Rewrite each fraction:

\[ \frac{2}{x} = \frac{2y}{xy}, \] \[ \frac{3}{y} = \frac{3x}{xy}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{2}{x}+\frac{3}{y} = \frac{2y+3x}{xy}. \]

The original expressions require

\[ x\ne0 \]

and

\[ y\ne0. \]

Factoring Denominators First

When denominators are algebraic expressions, factoring them often reveals the least common denominator.

Consider

\[ \frac{1}{x^2-1} + \frac{2}{x-1}. \]

Factor:

\[ x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1). \]

So the denominators are

\[ (x-1)(x+1) \]

and

\[ x-1. \]

The least common denominator is

\[ (x-1)(x+1). \]

Rewrite the second fraction:

\[ \frac{2}{x-1} = \frac{2(x+1)}{(x-1)(x+1)}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{1}{x^2-1} + \frac{2}{x-1} = \frac{1+2(x+1)}{(x-1)(x+1)}. \]

Simplify the numerator:

\[ \frac{2x+3}{(x-1)(x+1)}. \]

The original domain excludes

\[ x=\pm1. \]

Different Powers in Denominators

When a factor appears with different exponents, the common denominator must contain the highest required power.

Consider

\[ \frac{1}{x^2}+\frac{3}{x^3}. \]

The least common denominator is

\[ x^3. \]

Rewrite:

\[ \frac{1}{x^2} = \frac{x}{x^3}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{x}{x^3}+\frac{3}{x^3} = \frac{x+3}{x^3}, \]

with

\[ x\ne0. \]

Several Denominator Factors

Consider

\[ \frac{1}{x(x+1)} + \frac{2}{x^2}. \]

The denominator factors are

\[ x(x+1) \]

and

\[ x^2. \]

The least common denominator must contain

\[ x^2(x+1). \]

Rewrite:

\[ \frac{1}{x(x+1)} = \frac{x}{x^2(x+1)}, \]

and

\[ \frac{2}{x^2} = \frac{2(x+1)}{x^2(x+1)}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{x+2(x+1)}{x^2(x+1)} = \frac{3x+2}{x^2(x+1)}. \]

The restrictions are

\[ x\ne0,-1. \]

Subtracting Rational Expressions

Subtraction works the same way, but signs require care.

Consider

\[ \frac{3}{x}-\frac{2}{x+1}. \]

The common denominator is

\[ x(x+1). \]

Rewrite:

\[ \frac{3(x+1)}{x(x+1)} - \frac{2x}{x(x+1)}. \]

Combine:

\[ \frac{3(x+1)-2x}{x(x+1)}. \]

Simplify:

\[ \frac{x+3}{x(x+1)}. \]

The subtraction sign applies to the entire second numerator.

Clearing Denominators in Equations

Common denominators can also help solve equations containing fractions.

Consider

\[ \frac{x}{3}+\frac{1}{2}=2. \]

The least common denominator is \(6\).

Multiply every term in the equation by \(6\):

\[ 6\left(\frac{x}{3}\right) + 6\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = 6(2). \]

This gives

\[ 2x+3=12. \]

Then

\[ 2x=9 \]

and

\[ x=\frac{9}{2}. \]

Multiplying the entire equation by a common denominator often removes fractions in one step.

Variable Denominators Require Restrictions

Suppose we have

\[ \frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{x-2}=1. \]

The original equation requires

\[ x\ne0 \]

and

\[ x\ne2. \]

A common denominator is

\[ x(x-2). \]

Multiplying the equation by that denominator is legitimate only for values already allowed by the original equation.

The excluded values remain excluded even if they disappear from a later simplified equation.

What Rationalization Means

Rationalization rewrites a fraction so that a radical is removed from the denominator.

For example,

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \]

can be rewritten by multiplying numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt{2}\):

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}. \]

Because

\[ \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}}=1, \]

the value of the expression has not changed.

Why Rationalize?

Historically, rationalized denominators were especially convenient for hand calculation, tables, and comparison.

Modern calculators do not require rationalization, but the technique remains useful because it:

  • produces standard exact forms;
  • can simplify later algebra;
  • reveals conjugate structure;
  • is important in limits and symbolic manipulation;
  • provides practice with equivalent forms.

Rationalization is therefore best understood as a strategic rewrite, not as a rule that one form is inherently more correct.

Rationalizing a Single Square Root

Consider

\[ \frac{5}{\sqrt{3}}. \]

Multiply by

\[ \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{5}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{5\sqrt{3}}{3}. \]

The denominator is now rational.

Rationalizing a Coefficient Times a Root

Consider

\[ \frac{7}{2\sqrt{5}}. \]

Multiply numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt{5}\):

\[ \frac{7}{2\sqrt{5}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{7\sqrt{5}}{10}. \]

The numerical coefficient \(2\) stays in the denominator while the radical is removed.

Rationalizing Higher Roots

For higher-index radicals, choose a factor that completes a perfect power.

Consider

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}}. \]

Because

\[ \sqrt[3]{2}\cdot\sqrt[3]{4} = \sqrt[3]{8} = 2, \]

multiply by

\[ \frac{\sqrt[3]{4}}{\sqrt[3]{4}}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}} = \frac{\sqrt[3]{4}}{2}. \]

The same principle works for other roots: supply whatever factor is needed to complete the power in the denominator.

Binomial Radical Denominators

A denominator such as

\[ \sqrt{5}+2 \]

cannot be rationalized by multiplying by \(\sqrt{5}\) alone.

Instead, we use the conjugate.

The conjugate of

\[ a+b \]

is

\[ a-b. \]

The product is a difference of squares:

\[ (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2. \]

This can eliminate square roots from a binomial denominator.

Rationalizing with a Conjugate

Consider

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}+2}. \]

Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate

\[ \sqrt{5}-2. \]

Then

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}+2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{5}-2}{\sqrt{5}-2} = \frac{\sqrt{5}-2}{(\sqrt{5}+2)(\sqrt{5}-2)}. \]

The denominator becomes

\[ 5-4=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}+2} = \sqrt{5}-2. \]

Another Conjugate Example

Rationalize

\[ \frac{3}{4-\sqrt{7}}. \]

The conjugate is

\[ 4+\sqrt{7}. \]

Multiply:

\[ \frac{3}{4-\sqrt{7}} \cdot \frac{4+\sqrt{7}}{4+\sqrt{7}}. \]

The numerator becomes

\[ 12+3\sqrt{7}. \]

The denominator becomes

\[ 16-7=9. \]

So

\[ \frac{3}{4-\sqrt{7}} = \frac{12+3\sqrt{7}}{9}. \]

Simplify:

\[ \frac{4+\sqrt{7}}{3}. \]

Why Conjugates Work

The usefulness of conjugates comes from the identity

\[ (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2. \]

If one of \(a\) or \(b\) contains a square root, squaring it removes that root.

For example,

\[ (\sqrt{x}+3)(\sqrt{x}-3) = x-9. \]

The middle radical terms cancel.

This same pattern will appear later in factoring, limits, complex numbers, and other areas of mathematics.

Rationalizing a Numerator

Sometimes the useful transformation is to remove a radical from the numerator rather than the denominator.

Consider

\[ \frac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt{x}}{h}. \]

Multiplying by the conjugate of the numerator gives

\[ \frac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt{x}}{h} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}}. \]

The numerator becomes

\[ (x+h)-x=h. \]

So, where the expressions are defined and \(h\ne0\),

\[ \frac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt{x}}{h} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}}. \]

This kind of rationalization becomes especially important in calculus.

Rationalization Is an Equivalence Transformation

When we rationalize, we are not approximating the expression.

For example,

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \]

exactly.

The two expressions are equal wherever both are defined.

Rationalization changes representation, not value.

Preserve the Original Domain

Suppose

\[ \frac{\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x}} \]

is simplified to

\[ 1. \]

The original expression requires

\[ x>0 \]

because the denominator \(\sqrt{x}\) must be nonzero.

The simplified expression \(1\) is defined for every real number, but that does not expand the domain of the original expression.

So the proper equivalence is

\[ \frac{\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x}}=1 \qquad\text{for }x>0. \]

Common Denominator Versus Common Factor

These ideas are related but distinct.

A common denominator is used when adding or subtracting rational expressions.

A common factor is used when factoring or canceling products.

For example,

\[ \frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{y} \]

needs a common denominator.

But

\[ \frac{x(x+1)}{x} \]

contains a common factor \(x\) in numerator and denominator.

The first problem requires rewriting the fractions. The second permits cancellation, provided \(x\ne0\).

Do Not Add Denominators

A common error is

\[ \frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b} = \frac{2}{a+b}. \]

This is generally false.

The correct common denominator is \(ab\):

\[ \frac{1}{a} = \frac{b}{ab}, \] \[ \frac{1}{b} = \frac{a}{ab}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b} = \frac{a+b}{ab}, \]

provided \(a\ne0\) and \(b\ne0\).

Do Not Cancel Across Addition

Cancellation applies to factors, not to terms separated by addition or subtraction.

For example,

\[ \frac{x+3}{x} \]

cannot be simplified by canceling the \(x\).

The numerator \(x+3\) is a sum, not a product containing \(x\) as a factor.

By contrast,

\[ \frac{x(x+3)}{x} \]

does contain a factor \(x\), so

\[ \frac{x(x+3)}{x}=x+3 \qquad\text{for }x\ne0. \]

A Strategic View

Common denominators and rationalization are useful because they convert an expression into a form that supports the next operation.

A common denominator prepares fractions for addition or subtraction.

Rationalization may prepare an expression for simplification, comparison, differentiation, limits, or another symbolic transformation.

The goal is not to make every expression look the same. The goal is to choose an equivalent form that makes the next step easier or reveals useful structure.

Worked Example: Add Rational Expressions

Simplify

\[ \frac{2}{x}+\frac{1}{x+3}. \]

The common denominator is

\[ x(x+3). \]

Rewrite:

\[ \frac{2(x+3)}{x(x+3)} + \frac{x}{x(x+3)}. \]

Combine:

\[ \frac{2x+6+x}{x(x+3)}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{2}{x}+\frac{1}{x+3} = \frac{3x+6}{x(x+3)}, \]

with

\[ x\ne0,-3. \]

Worked Example: Factor Before Finding the LCD

Simplify

\[ \frac{1}{x^2-4}+\frac{3}{x+2}. \]

Factor:

\[ x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2). \]

The least common denominator is

\[ (x-2)(x+2). \]

Rewrite the second fraction:

\[ \frac{3}{x+2} = \frac{3(x-2)}{(x+2)(x-2)}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{1+3(x-2)}{(x-2)(x+2)}. \]

Simplify the numerator:

\[ \frac{3x-5}{(x-2)(x+2)}. \]

The restrictions remain

\[ x\ne\pm2. \]

Worked Example: Rationalize a Radical Denominator

Rationalize

\[ \frac{4}{\sqrt{6}}. \]

Multiply by

\[ \frac{\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{6}}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{4\sqrt{6}}{6}. \]

Simplify:

\[ \frac{2\sqrt{6}}{3}. \]

So

\[ \frac{4}{\sqrt{6}} = \frac{2\sqrt{6}}{3}. \]

Worked Example: Rationalize with a Conjugate

Rationalize

\[ \frac{2}{3+\sqrt{5}}. \]

Multiply by the conjugate:

\[ \frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{3-\sqrt{5}}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{2(3-\sqrt{5})}{9-5}. \]

So

\[ \frac{2}{3+\sqrt{5}} = \frac{6-2\sqrt{5}}{4}. \]

Simplify:

\[ \frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}. \]

Common Errors

Changing the Value While Creating a Denominator

Incorrect:

\[ \frac{1}{3}=\frac{2}{6} \]

is true, but only because both numerator and denominator were multiplied by \(2\). Multiplying only the denominator would change the value.

Ignoring Factoring

For

\[ \frac{1}{x^2-9}+\frac{1}{x-3}, \]

the expression \(x^2-9\) should first be factored as

\[ (x-3)(x+3). \]

Using the Same Radical Instead of a Conjugate

For a denominator such as

\[ \sqrt{3}+1, \]

multiplying by \(\sqrt{3}\) does not eliminate the radical from the denominator.

The useful factor is the conjugate

\[ \sqrt{3}-1. \]

Dropping Domain Restrictions

Even if a factor disappears after simplification, values that made the original denominator zero remain excluded.

Practice

1. Find a Common Denominator

Simplify:

\[ \frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{6}. \]
Check Answer

The least common denominator is \(12\):

\[ \frac{3}{12}+\frac{2}{12} = \frac{5}{12}. \]

2. Algebraic Denominators

Simplify:

\[ \frac{2}{x}+\frac{5}{y}. \]
Check Answer \[ \frac{2y}{xy}+\frac{5x}{xy} = \frac{2y+5x}{xy}, \]

with \(x\ne0\) and \(y\ne0\).

3. Factor First

Simplify:

\[ \frac{1}{x^2-1}+\frac{1}{x+1}. \]
Check Answer

Since

\[ x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1), \]

we have

\[ \frac{1}{(x-1)(x+1)} + \frac{x-1}{(x+1)(x-1)} = \frac{x}{(x-1)(x+1)}, \]

with \(x\ne\pm1\).

4. Rationalize a Single Root

Rationalize:

\[ \frac{3}{\sqrt{7}}. \]
Check Answer \[ \frac{3}{\sqrt{7}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{7}}{\sqrt{7}} = \frac{3\sqrt{7}}{7}. \]

5. Rationalize with a Conjugate

Rationalize:

\[ \frac{1}{2+\sqrt{3}}. \]
Check Answer \[ \frac{1}{2+\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{2-\sqrt{3}}{2-\sqrt{3}} = \frac{2-\sqrt{3}}{4-3} = 2-\sqrt{3}. \]

6. Identify the Restriction

What values are excluded from

\[ \frac{1}{x(x-4)}? \]
Check Answer \[ x\ne0 \]

and

\[ x\ne4. \]

Key Ideas

  • Equivalent fractions are created by multiplying numerator and denominator by the same nonzero quantity.
  • Common denominators allow rational expressions to be added or subtracted.
  • Factoring denominators often reveals the least common denominator.
  • The common denominator must contain every required factor to the highest necessary power.
  • Clearing denominators can simplify equations containing fractions.
  • Rationalization removes radicals from denominators by multiplying by a carefully chosen form of \(1\).
  • Single radicals are often rationalized with another radical factor.
  • Binomial radical denominators are usually rationalized with conjugates.
  • Conjugates work because their product forms a difference of squares.
  • Cancellation applies to common factors, not to terms separated by addition or subtraction.
  • Domain restrictions from original denominators must be preserved through every rewrite.
