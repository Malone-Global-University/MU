Rearranging Formulas

A formula does not have one permanently designated output. By applying equivalent transformations, we can isolate whichever variable is useful for the problem at hand.

The Central Idea

A formula is an equation that describes a relationship among quantities.

For example, the distance formula

\[ d=rt \]

relates distance \(d\), rate \(r\), and time \(t\).

The formula is often introduced with \(d\) isolated, but the relationship does not belong exclusively to \(d\). We can rearrange the same equation to solve for rate:

\[ r=\frac{d}{t}, \qquad t\ne0, \]

or for time:

\[ t=\frac{d}{r}, \qquad r\ne0. \]

Each form describes the same underlying relationship, subject to the conditions required by the transformations.

A Formula Is Still an Equation

The same properties of equality used to solve equations also apply to formulas.

If

\[ A=lw, \]

then dividing both sides by \(l\), provided \(l\ne0\), gives

\[ \frac{A}{l}=\frac{lw}{l}, \]

so

\[ w=\frac{A}{l}. \]

Nothing fundamentally new happened. We applied the division property of equality to isolate \(w\).

Rearranging formulas is therefore equation solving with several symbolic quantities instead of one variable and several numbers.

Choose the Target Variable

Before manipulating a formula, decide which variable you want to isolate.

Suppose

\[ P=2l+2w. \]

If the target is \(l\), then all other symbols are treated as known or temporarily fixed quantities.

Subtract \(2w\) from both sides:

\[ P-2w=2l. \]

Divide by \(2\):

\[ l=\frac{P-2w}{2}. \]

The same original formula could instead be rearranged for \(w\):

\[ w=\frac{P-2l}{2}. \]

Undo Operations in Reverse Order

A useful strategy is to identify which operations are being applied to the target variable and undo them in reverse order.

Consider

\[ y=3x+5. \]

To isolate \(x\), notice that \(x\) is first multiplied by \(3\), then \(5\) is added.

Undo the addition first:

\[ y-5=3x. \]

Then undo the multiplication:

\[ x=\frac{y-5}{3}. \]

This reverse-operation viewpoint is often faster than thinking in terms of “moving” symbols from one side to the other.

Example: Solve for a Variable in a Product

Given

\[ F=ma, \]

solve for \(a\).

Divide both sides by \(m\), provided \(m\ne0\):

\[ \frac{F}{m}=\frac{ma}{m}. \]

Therefore,

\[ a=\frac{F}{m}. \]

To solve instead for \(m\), divide by \(a\), provided \(a\ne0\):

\[ m=\frac{F}{a}. \]

Example: Solve for a Variable in a Sum

Given

\[ C=A+B, \]

solve for \(B\).

Subtract \(A\) from both sides:

\[ C-A=B. \]

By symmetry of equality, we may write

\[ B=C-A. \]

This simple pattern appears constantly in formulas involving totals, balances, changes, and component quantities.

Example: Solve for a Variable in a Fraction

Consider

\[ v=\frac{d}{t}. \]

Suppose we want to solve for \(d\).

Multiply both sides by \(t\):

\[ vt=d. \]

Thus,

\[ d=vt. \]

If instead we want \(t\), start with

\[ v=\frac{d}{t}. \]

Multiply by \(t\):

\[ vt=d. \]

Then divide by \(v\), provided \(v\ne0\):

\[ t=\frac{d}{v}. \]

Clearing a Denominator

When the target variable appears in a denominator, it is often useful to clear the denominator first.

Suppose

\[ R=\frac{V}{I}. \]

To isolate \(I\), multiply both sides by \(I\):

\[ RI=V. \]

Then divide by \(R\), provided \(R\ne0\):

\[ I=\frac{V}{R}. \]

Multiplying before dividing usually makes the structure easier to manage.

Formulas with Several Terms

Consider

\[ y=mx+b. \]

Solve for \(x\).

Subtract \(b\):

\[ y-b=mx. \]

Divide by \(m\), provided \(m\ne0\):

\[ x=\frac{y-b}{m}. \]

This rearrangement is useful when a linear relationship is known and we want to recover the input from the output.

Keep the Target Variable Together

Sometimes the target variable appears in more than one term.

Consider

\[ A=xh+xw. \]

Trying to isolate \(x\) term by term is awkward. Instead, factor \(x\):

\[ A=x(h+w). \]

Now divide by \(h+w\), provided

\[ h+w\ne0. \]

Then

\[ x=\frac{A}{h+w}. \]

Factoring can therefore be an essential part of rearranging formulas.

Target Variable on Both Sides

A more involved formula may contain the target variable on both sides.

Consider

\[ ax+b=cx+d. \]

Solve for \(x\).

Subtract \(cx\) from both sides:

\[ ax-cx+b=d. \]

Subtract \(b\):

\[ ax-cx=d-b. \]

Factor \(x\):

\[ x(a-c)=d-b. \]

Divide by \(a-c\), provided \(a-c\ne0\):

\[ x=\frac{d-b}{a-c}. \]

The key move was to collect all terms containing \(x\) and then factor the common \(x\).

Parentheses May Need to Be Removed—or Preserved

Suppose

\[ y=a(x-h)+k. \]

To solve for \(x\), there is no need to expand the parentheses first.

Subtract \(k\):

\[ y-k=a(x-h). \]

Divide by \(a\), provided \(a\ne0\):

\[ \frac{y-k}{a}=x-h. \]

Add \(h\):

\[ x=h+\frac{y-k}{a}. \]

Preserving useful grouping can make the rearrangement shorter and clearer.

Sometimes Expansion Helps

In other formulas, expansion exposes the terms that must be collected.

Consider

\[ P=a(x+b)+cx. \]

Solve for \(x\).

Expand:

\[ P=ax+ab+cx. \]

Subtract \(ab\):

\[ P-ab=ax+cx. \]

Factor \(x\):

\[ P-ab=x(a+c). \]

Divide by \(a+c\), provided \(a+c\ne0\):

\[ x=\frac{P-ab}{a+c}. \]

Whether to expand or preserve grouping depends on which form makes the target variable easier to isolate.

Solving Through a Square

Consider the area formula for a circle:

\[ A=\pi r^2. \]

Solve for \(r\).

Divide by \(\pi\):

\[ \frac{A}{\pi}=r^2. \]

For a geometric radius, \(r\ge0\), so taking the principal square root gives

\[ r=\sqrt{\frac{A}{\pi}}. \]

The context matters. Algebraically, solving

\[ r^2=\frac{A}{\pi} \]

without the geometric restriction would produce

\[ r=\pm\sqrt{\frac{A}{\pi}}. \]

But a radius is conventionally nonnegative.

Solving Through a Cube

Suppose

\[ V=s^3 \]

for the volume of a cube with side length \(s\).

Taking the real cube root gives

\[ s=\sqrt[3]{V}. \]

Unlike an even power, the real cube function is one-to-one, so the real cube root reverses cubing without introducing a \(\pm\) choice.

Solving Through a General Power

Suppose

\[ y=ax^n. \]

First divide by \(a\), provided \(a\ne0\):

\[ \frac{y}{a}=x^n. \]

The next step depends on \(n\) and the number system.

If \(n\) is odd and we work over the real numbers, then

\[ x=\sqrt[n]{\frac{y}{a}}. \]

If \(n\) is even, solving for real \(x\) generally requires

\[ x=\pm\sqrt[n]{\frac{y}{a}}, \]

provided the radicand is nonnegative.

This is one reason rearranging formulas involving powers requires more care than rearranging linear formulas.

Variable Inside a Root

Consider

\[ y=\sqrt{x+4}. \]

To isolate \(x\), square both sides:

\[ y^2=x+4. \]

Then subtract \(4\):

\[ x=y^2-4. \]

However, the original formula also requires

\[ y\ge0 \]

when \(\sqrt{\phantom{x}}\) denotes the principal real square root.

The rearranged expression must be interpreted together with that restriction if it is meant to describe the same relationship.

Rearranging a Reciprocal Relationship

Consider

\[ f=\frac{1}{T}. \]

To isolate \(T\), multiply both sides by \(T\):

\[ fT=1. \]

Then divide by \(f\), provided \(f\ne0\):

\[ T=\frac{1}{f}. \]

The reciprocal relationship is symmetric in form:

\[ f=\frac{1}{T} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad T=\frac{1}{f}, \]

provided the quantities are nonzero where required.

Rearranging a Proportion

Suppose

\[ \frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{d}, \]

with \(b\ne0\) and \(d\ne0\).

To solve for \(a\), multiply both sides by \(b\):

\[ a=\frac{bc}{d}. \]

Equivalently, we could first cross-multiply:

\[ ad=bc, \]

then divide by \(d\):

\[ a=\frac{bc}{d}. \]

Cross-multiplication is simply a compact consequence of multiplying both sides by the nonzero denominators.

Nested Fractions

Consider

\[ y=\frac{a}{b+x}. \]

Solve for \(x\).

Multiply both sides by \(b+x\):

\[ y(b+x)=a. \]

Assuming \(y\ne0\), divide by \(y\):

\[ b+x=\frac{a}{y}. \]

Subtract \(b\):

\[ x=\frac{a}{y}-b. \]

The original formula also requires

\[ b+x\ne0. \]

Restrictions generated by denominators should be tracked throughout the rearrangement.

A Temperature Conversion Example

The Fahrenheit-Celsius conversion formula is

\[ F=\frac{9}{5}C+32. \]

Solve for \(C\).

Subtract \(32\):

\[ F-32=\frac{9}{5}C. \]

Multiply by \(\frac{5}{9}\):

\[ C=\frac{5}{9}(F-32). \]

The new form describes the same temperature relationship but uses Fahrenheit as the input and Celsius as the isolated quantity.

A Geometry Example

The area of a trapezoid is

\[ A=\frac{1}{2}(b_1+b_2)h. \]

Solve for \(h\).

Multiply both sides by \(2\):

\[ 2A=(b_1+b_2)h. \]

Divide by \(b_1+b_2\), provided

\[ b_1+b_2\ne0. \]

Then

\[ h=\frac{2A}{b_1+b_2}. \]

For ordinary geometric lengths, the denominator is positive, so the restriction is automatically satisfied.

A Physics Example

Kinetic energy is given by

\[ K=\frac{1}{2}mv^2. \]

Solve for \(v\).

Multiply by \(2\):

\[ 2K=mv^2. \]

Divide by \(m\), provided \(m\ne0\):

\[ \frac{2K}{m}=v^2. \]

Algebraically,

\[ v=\pm\sqrt{\frac{2K}{m}}. \]

If \(v\) represents speed rather than signed velocity, the nonnegative branch is used:

\[ v=\sqrt{\frac{2K}{m}}. \]

The meaning of the variable determines which algebraic solutions are physically relevant.

Rearrangement and Dimensional Checking

Units can provide a useful check after rearranging a formula.

From

\[ d=rt, \]

we obtain

\[ r=\frac{d}{t}. \]

If distance is measured in kilometers and time in hours, then the units of \(r\) become

\[ \frac{\text{kilometers}}{\text{hours}}, \]

which is exactly what we expect for a rate.

A rearranged formula with inconsistent units is a strong signal that an algebraic mistake may have occurred.

Equivalent Form Does Not Mean Identical Domain Automatically

Algebraic manipulation can introduce restrictions that are easy to overlook.

Suppose

\[ A=xy. \]

Solving for \(x\) gives

\[ x=\frac{A}{y}, \]

but only when

\[ y\ne0. \]

The original formula itself can still make sense when \(y=0\): it then implies \(A=0\).

So the isolated form is not a universally valid replacement unless its domain condition is stated.

When Isolation Is Impossible or Conditional

Not every formula can be rearranged into a single simple expression for every variable.

For example,

\[ y=x^2 \]

does not produce one real-valued inverse expression for \(x\) unless we restrict the domain or accept two branches:

\[ x=\pm\sqrt{y}. \]

Likewise, equations in which the target variable appears in several nonlinear ways may require factoring, special functions, numerical methods, or may not have an elementary closed-form solution at all.

Rearranging a formula is therefore not just symbol moving. It depends on the mathematical structure of the relationship.

A Reliable Rearrangement Strategy

  1. Choose the target variable.
  2. Identify the operations acting on it.
  3. Undo outer operations in a useful order.
  4. Collect all target-variable terms if the variable appears more than once.
  5. Factor the target variable when possible.
  6. Divide or apply an inverse operation to isolate it.
  7. Record domain restrictions introduced by division, roots, logarithms, or other operations.
  8. Check the result by substitution or by reversing the algebra.

Worked Example: Target Variable Appears Twice

Solve

\[ P=qx+rx+s \]

for \(x\).

Subtract \(s\):

\[ P-s=qx+rx. \]

Factor \(x\):

\[ P-s=x(q+r). \]

Divide by \(q+r\), provided

\[ q+r\ne0. \]

Then

\[ x=\frac{P-s}{q+r}. \]

Worked Example: Fractional Formula

Solve

\[ Q=\frac{a+b}{c} \]

for \(b\), assuming \(c\ne0\).

Multiply by \(c\):

\[ Qc=a+b. \]

Subtract \(a\):

\[ b=Qc-a. \]

The fraction disappears first, making the remaining rearrangement straightforward.

Worked Example: Square and Shift

Solve

\[ y=a(x-h)^2+k \]

for \(x\), assuming \(a\ne0\).

Subtract \(k\):

\[ y-k=a(x-h)^2. \]

Divide by \(a\):

\[ \frac{y-k}{a}=(x-h)^2. \]

Take square roots:

\[ x-h=\pm\sqrt{\frac{y-k}{a}}. \]

Add \(h\):

\[ x=h\pm\sqrt{\frac{y-k}{a}}. \]

For real solutions, the quantity under the square root must be nonnegative.

Checking a Rearrangement

Suppose we rearrange

\[ y=mx+b \]

to

\[ x=\frac{y-b}{m}. \]

We can check by substituting the rearranged expression back into the original formula:

\[ m\left(\frac{y-b}{m}\right)+b. \]

Provided \(m\ne0\), this simplifies to

\[ y-b+b=y. \]

The original relationship is recovered, so the rearrangement checks out.

Common Errors

Changing Only One Part of a Sum

From

\[ y=3x+6, \]

dividing by \(3\) does not give

\[ \frac{y}{3}=x+6. \]

If the entire right side is divided by \(3\), then

\[ \frac{y}{3}=x+2. \]

Alternatively, subtract \(6\) first:

\[ x=\frac{y-6}{3}. \]

Forgetting a Nonzero Restriction

From

\[ A=bh, \]

the form

\[ h=\frac{A}{b} \]

requires

\[ b\ne0. \]

Forgetting Both Square-Root Branches

If

\[ x^2=y, \]

then in ordinary algebra

\[ x=\pm\sqrt{y}, \]

not merely \(x=\sqrt{y}\), unless context or a domain restriction selects the nonnegative branch.

Expanding When It Makes the Problem Harder

From

\[ y=a(x-h)+k, \]

expansion is possible, but preserving the grouped form makes isolating \(x\) faster.

Practice

1. Solve for Width

Given

\[ A=lw, \]

solve for \(w\).

Check Answer \[ w=\frac{A}{l}, \qquad l\ne0. \]

2. Solve for Time

Given

\[ d=rt, \]

solve for \(t\).

Check Answer \[ t=\frac{d}{r}, \qquad r\ne0. \]

3. Solve a Linear Formula

Given

\[ y=5x-7, \]

solve for \(x\).

Check Answer \[ y+7=5x, \]

so

\[ x=\frac{y+7}{5}. \]

4. Target Variable Appears Twice

Given

\[ A=xy+xz, \]

solve for \(x\).

Check Answer

Factor \(x\):

\[ A=x(y+z). \]

Then

\[ x=\frac{A}{y+z}, \qquad y+z\ne0. \]

5. Solve Through a Square

Given

\[ A=\pi r^2, \]

solve for the nonnegative geometric radius \(r\).

Check Answer \[ r=\sqrt{\frac{A}{\pi}}. \]

6. Rearrange a Fraction

Given

\[ y=\frac{a}{x}, \]

solve for \(x\).

Check Answer \[ xy=a, \]

so

\[ x=\frac{a}{y}, \qquad y\ne0. \]

Key Ideas

  • A formula is an equation, so the properties of equality apply to it.
  • Rearranging a formula means isolating a chosen target variable while preserving the underlying relationship.
  • Undo operations in a useful reverse order.
  • Preserve helpful grouping when possible; expand only when it exposes useful structure.
  • If the target variable appears in several terms, collect those terms and factor the variable.
  • Clearing denominators often simplifies formulas containing fractions.
  • Powers and roots may create multiple branches or domain restrictions.
  • Division by a symbolic quantity requires a nonzero condition.
  • Context can restrict which algebraic solutions are meaningful.
  • Substitution, reverse algebra, and dimensional analysis can help verify a rearranged formula.
