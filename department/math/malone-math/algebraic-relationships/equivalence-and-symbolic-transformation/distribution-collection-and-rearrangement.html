Distribution, Collection, and Rearrangement

Algebra becomes more flexible when we can open grouped expressions, collect common structure, and reorganize terms without changing the value they represent.

The Central Idea

Many algebraic expressions can be written in several equivalent forms.

For example,

\[ 3(x+4) \]

can be rewritten as

\[ 3x+12. \]

Conversely,

\[ 3x+12 \]

can be collected into

\[ 3(x+4). \]

We may also rearrange terms:

\[ 2x+5+3x=2x+3x+5. \]

These transformations do not change the underlying mathematical value. They change only the form in which that value is represented.

This lesson develops three closely related symbolic tools:

  • Distribution moves multiplication across addition or subtraction.
  • Collection identifies and groups common structure.
  • Rearrangement changes the order or grouping of terms when the governing laws permit it.

The Distributive Property

The distributive property connects multiplication with addition.

For numbers or expressions \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\),

\[ a(b+c)=ab+ac. \]

Likewise,

\[ a(b-c)=ab-ac. \]

The factor outside the parentheses multiplies every term inside the parentheses.

For example,

\[ 4(x+3)=4x+12. \]

And

\[ 5(2x-7)=10x-35. \]

Distribution produces an equivalent expression with the grouping removed.

Why Distribution Works

The distributive property is not merely a rule for manipulating symbols. It describes how multiplication interacts with addition.

For example,

\[ 3(4+2) \]

can be evaluated directly:

\[ 3(6)=18. \]

Or we can distribute:

\[ 3(4)+3(2)=12+6=18. \]

Both routes produce the same result.

The same principle continues to work when the quantities are represented by variables.

Distribution Across Several Terms

Distribution is not limited to two terms.

For example,

\[ 2(x+y+z) \]

becomes

\[ 2x+2y+2z. \]

Similarly,

\[ -3(a+2b-c) \]

becomes

\[ -3a-6b+3c. \]

The outside factor multiplies every term inside the grouping.

Distributing a Negative Sign

A negative sign in front of parentheses can be treated as multiplication by \(-1\).

For example,

\[ -(x+5) \]

means

\[ -1(x+5). \]

Distributing \(-1\) gives

\[ -x-5. \]

Likewise,

\[ -(x-4)=-x+4. \]

This is why removing parentheses preceded by subtraction changes the sign of every term inside.

Subtraction of a Group

Consider

\[ 7-(x+3). \]

This can be written as

\[ 7+[-1(x+3)]. \]

Distributing gives

\[ 7-x-3. \]

Then combining constants gives

\[ 4-x. \]

A common error is to change only the first sign inside the parentheses. The negative factor applies to the entire group.

Distribution with Variables

The outside factor may itself contain variables.

For example,

\[ x(x+5) \]

becomes

\[ x^2+5x. \]

Similarly,

\[ 2x(3x-4) \]

becomes

\[ 6x^2-8x. \]

Each multiplication follows the ordinary rules for products of coefficients and powers.

Distribution with Fractions

Fractional factors distribute in the same way.

For example,

\[ \frac{1}{2}(6x+8) \]

becomes

\[ 3x+4. \]

Likewise,

\[ \frac{2}{3}(9x-6) \]

becomes

\[ 6x-4. \]

Distribution can therefore be useful for simplifying numerical coefficients as well as removing grouping symbols.

Collection: Distribution in Reverse

Distribution can be read in either direction.

If

\[ a(b+c)=ab+ac, \]

then we may also recognize

\[ ab+ac=a(b+c). \]

This reverse viewpoint is called collection here because we collect a common factor from several terms.

For example,

\[ 3x+12 \]

contains a common factor of \(3\), so we may write

\[ 3x+12=3(x+4). \]

The expression has changed form, but not value.

Collecting a Common Variable Factor

Common factors can include variables.

For example,

\[ x^2+5x \]

contains a common factor of \(x\):

\[ x^2+5x=x(x+5). \]

Similarly,

\[ 6x^2-9x \]

has common factor \(3x\):

\[ 6x^2-9x=3x(2x-3). \]

This kind of collection prepares the way for more systematic factoring in the next lesson.

Collecting Like Structure

Sometimes the repeated part is an entire expression rather than a single variable.

Consider

\[ 3(x+2)+5(x+2). \]

The repeated factor is

\[ x+2. \]

So we may collect it:

\[ 3(x+2)+5(x+2)=(3+5)(x+2). \]

Therefore,

\[ 3(x+2)+5(x+2)=8(x+2). \]

This is the same logic used when combining like terms, but the common algebraic unit is now a larger expression.

Rearrangement

Rearrangement means changing the order or grouping of terms without changing their values.

For addition, the commutative property allows us to change order:

\[ a+b=b+a. \]

The associative property allows us to change grouping:

\[ (a+b)+c=a+(b+c). \]

Therefore, an expression such as

\[ 4x+7+3x \]

may be rearranged as

\[ 4x+3x+7. \]

This makes the like terms adjacent and easier to combine.

Rearranging Products

Multiplication of real numbers is also commutative and associative.

Therefore,

\[ 3xy\cdot 2x \]

may be rearranged as

\[ 3\cdot2\cdot x\cdot x\cdot y. \]

Then

\[ 3xy\cdot2x=6x^2y. \]

Rearranging factors often makes repeated factors easier to recognize.

Subtraction Requires Care

Subtraction is not commutative.

In general,

\[ a-b\ne b-a. \]

For example,

\[ 7-3=4, \]

but

\[ 3-7=-4. \]

To rearrange expressions containing subtraction safely, it is often useful to treat subtraction as addition of an opposite:

\[ a-b=a+(-b). \]

Then the negative sign travels with the term.

For example,

\[ 5x-3x+7 \]

may be viewed as

\[ 5x+(-3x)+7. \]

The term \(-3x\) can then be rearranged as a complete signed term.

Division Requires Care

Division is also not commutative.

In general,

\[ \frac{a}{b}\ne\frac{b}{a}. \]

Nor can terms in a quotient be rearranged as freely as terms in a sum.

For example,

\[ \frac{x+2}{x} \]

cannot be changed into

\[ \frac{x}{x+2} \]

without changing its value.

Rearrangement must always follow the laws of the operation involved.

Distribution and Combining Like Terms

Distribution often creates like terms that can then be combined.

Consider

\[ 3(x+4)+2x. \]

Distribute:

\[ 3x+12+2x. \]

Then combine like terms:

\[ 5x+12. \]

So

\[ 3(x+4)+2x=5x+12. \]

This sequence—distribute, rearrange if useful, then combine—is one of the most common patterns in elementary algebra.

Distribution on Both Sides of an Equation

Distribution can simplify equations as well as isolated expressions.

Consider

\[ 2(x+3)=14. \]

Distributing gives

\[ 2x+6=14. \]

Then the properties of equality can be used to solve:

\[ 2x=8, \] \[ x=4. \]

The distribution step rewrites one side into an equivalent form. The equation itself remains equivalent because that side still represents the same quantity.

Choosing Whether to Distribute

Distribution is available, but it is not always the best first move.

Consider

\[ 5(x-2)=35. \]

We could distribute:

\[ 5x-10=35. \]

But it may be faster to divide both sides by \(5\):

\[ x-2=7, \]

then add \(2\):

\[ x=9. \]

Equivalent forms give us choices. Good algebra is not merely performing every available operation; it is choosing a useful transformation.

Choosing Whether to Collect

Expanded form is not always the most useful form either.

Consider

\[ 6x+18. \]

The collected form

\[ 6(x+3) \]

makes the common factor visible.

Depending on the problem, one form may make substitution, cancellation, solving, graphing, or further factoring easier than the other.

Rearrangement as Preparation

Sometimes nothing needs to be calculated yet. We simply reorganize an expression so that its structure becomes easier to see.

Consider

\[ 3x+5y+7x-2y. \]

Rearrange by grouping like terms:

\[ 3x+7x+5y-2y. \]

Then combine:

\[ 10x+3y. \]

The rearrangement itself did not alter the value. It prepared the expression for the next useful step.

Multiple Layers of Grouping

Distribution can occur through more than one layer.

Consider

\[ 2[3(x+1)-4]. \]

We may first simplify inside:

\[ 3(x+1)=3x+3, \]

so

\[ 2[3x+3-4]=2(3x-1). \]

Then distribute again:

\[ 6x-2. \]

Thus,

\[ 2[3(x+1)-4]=6x-2. \]

Working from the inner structure outward often helps prevent errors.

Negative Factors and Collection

Sometimes collecting a negative factor produces a more useful form.

For example,

\[ -x+4 \]

can be written as

\[ -(x-4). \]

Check by distributing:

\[ -(x-4)=-x+4. \]

Likewise,

\[ -3x-6=-3(x+2). \]

Choosing a negative common factor can make the expression inside the parentheses cleaner or reveal a useful pattern.

Equivalent Forms Can Emphasize Different Structure

Consider the expression

\[ 2x+2y. \]

Expanded form:

\[ 2x+2y. \]

Collected form:

\[ 2(x+y). \]

The expanded form emphasizes two separate terms. The collected form emphasizes a common scale factor of \(2\).

Neither is inherently more correct. They reveal different structure.

A Structural View of Simplification

It is tempting to think that simplifying always means making an expression shorter.

But algebraic simplification is better understood as choosing a form that is easier to use.

For example,

\[ 4(x+1)+3(x+1) \]

can be distributed into

\[ 4x+4+3x+3, \]

then combined into

\[ 7x+7. \]

Or the common grouped factor can be collected immediately:

\[ [4+3](x+1)=7(x+1). \]

Both answers are equivalent:

\[ 7x+7=7(x+1). \]

Which form is preferable depends on what we intend to do next.

Worked Example: Distribute, Rearrange, Combine

Simplify

\[ 4(2x-3)+5x+7. \]

First distribute:

\[ 8x-12+5x+7. \]

Rearrange mentally into like-term groups:

\[ 8x+5x-12+7. \]

Combine:

\[ 13x-5. \]

Therefore,

\[ 4(2x-3)+5x+7=13x-5. \]

Worked Example: A Negative Group

Simplify

\[ 6x-(2x+9). \]

Treat the subtraction as multiplication by \(-1\):

\[ 6x-2x-9. \]

Combine the like terms:

\[ 4x-9. \]

Therefore,

\[ 6x-(2x+9)=4x-9. \]

Worked Example: Collecting a Common Factor

Rewrite

\[ 12x+18 \]

by collecting the greatest common numerical factor.

Both terms are divisible by \(6\):

\[ 12x=6(2x) \]

and

\[ 18=6(3). \]

Therefore,

\[ 12x+18=6(2x+3). \]

Distribution confirms the equivalence:

\[ 6(2x+3)=12x+18. \]

Worked Example: Repeated Group

Simplify

\[ 2(x-5)+7(x-5). \]

Instead of distributing immediately, recognize the common factor \(x-5\):

\[ [2+7](x-5). \]

So

\[ 2(x-5)+7(x-5)=9(x-5). \]

We could also distribute and combine:

\[ 2x-10+7x-35=9x-45. \]

Both forms are equivalent:

\[ 9(x-5)=9x-45. \]

Common Errors

Distributing to Only One Term

Incorrect:

\[ 3(x+4)=3x+4. \]

Correct:

\[ 3(x+4)=3x+12. \]

The factor must multiply every term inside the parentheses.

Forgetting a Sign Change

Incorrect:

\[ -(x-5)=-x-5. \]

Correct:

\[ -(x-5)=-x+5. \]

The factor \(-1\) multiplies both \(x\) and \(-5\).

Reordering Subtraction as Though It Were Addition

Incorrect:

\[ 8-3=3-8. \]

When rearranging a sum that contains subtraction, treat the negative sign as part of the following term.

Collecting a Factor That Is Not Common

From

\[ 6x+9, \]

we may collect \(3\):

\[ 3(2x+3). \]

But we cannot collect \(x\) from both terms because the constant \(9\) does not contain a factor of \(x\).

Key Ideas

  • Distribution rewrites a product involving a sum or difference as a sum or difference of products.
  • The outside factor must multiply every term inside the grouping.
  • A negative sign before parentheses acts as multiplication by \(-1\).
  • Collection reverses distribution by extracting a common factor.
  • Repeated algebraic groups can be collected just like repeated variables.
  • Rearrangement uses commutative and associative laws where those laws apply.
  • Subtraction and division cannot be reordered freely.
  • Rearranging terms often exposes like terms or common factors.
  • Equivalent forms can emphasize different mathematical structure.
  • The best form depends on what you intend to do next.

Where This Leads

Distribution, collection, and rearrangement give us the basic machinery for moving between equivalent symbolic forms.
