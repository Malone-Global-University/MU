Rates and Unit Rates

A rate compares two quantities with different units. A unit rate tells how much of one quantity corresponds to exactly one unit of the other, making comparisons and predictions much easier.

The Central Idea

Suppose a car travels

\[ 180\text{ miles} \]

in

\[ 3\text{ hours}. \]

The rate is

\[ \frac{180\text{ miles}}{3\text{ hours}}. \]

Divide:

\[ \frac{180}{3}=60. \]

So the car travels at

\[ 60\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}}. \]

This is a unit rate because the denominator is one hour.

The unit rate answers the practical question:

How much distance is traveled for each one hour?

Ratio Versus Rate

A ratio compares two quantities by division.

For example, if a class has 12 students wearing blue and 8 wearing red, then the ratio of blue to red is

\[ \frac{12}{8}. \]

A rate is a ratio in which the compared quantities have different units.

Examples include:

\[ \frac{60\text{ miles}}{1\text{ hour}}, \] \[ \frac{\$15}{3\text{ pounds}}, \] \[ \frac{120\text{ words}}{2\text{ minutes}}. \]

The units are part of the mathematical meaning.

Rates Are Quotients

A rate can usually be written as a quotient:

\[ \text{rate} = \frac{\text{quantity 1}}{\text{quantity 2}}. \]

For example, speed is

\[ \text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}. \]

Unit price is

\[ \text{unit price} = \frac{\text{cost}}{\text{quantity}}. \]

Productivity might be measured as

\[ \frac{\text{units produced}}{\text{hour}}. \]

The numerator tells what is being measured, and the denominator tells what it is measured per.

The Word “Per” Signals Division

Phrases such as

  • miles per hour;
  • dollars per pound;
  • pages per minute;
  • people per square mile;

usually indicate a rate.

Mathematically, “per” corresponds to division.

Thus,

\[ 60\text{ miles per hour} \]

means

\[ \frac{60\text{ miles}}{1\text{ hour}}. \]

What Is a Unit Rate?

A unit rate is a rate with denominator \(1\).

For example,

\[ \frac{150\text{ miles}}{3\text{ hours}} \]

has unit rate

\[ \frac{50\text{ miles}}{1\text{ hour}}. \]

We usually write this as

\[ 50\text{ miles per hour}. \]

Unit rates are especially useful because they describe what happens for one standard unit.

Finding a Unit Rate

Suppose 8 notebooks cost \$20.

The cost per notebook is

\[ \frac{\$20}{8\text{ notebooks}}. \]

Divide:

\[ 20\div8=2.5. \]

So the unit price is

\[ \$2.50\text{ per notebook}. \]

Units Must Travel with the Numbers

Consider

\[ \frac{180\text{ miles}}{3\text{ hours}}. \]

We simplify both the numerical part and the units:

\[ \frac{180}{3} \frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hours}} = 60\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}}. \]

Writing only

\[ 60 \]

throws away important information.

Is \(60\) miles? Hours? Dollars? Miles per hour?

The unit completes the answer.

Rates Can Be Read in Two Directions

Suppose 5 gallons of paint cover 1,500 square feet.

One useful rate is

\[ \frac{1500\text{ ft}^2}{5\text{ gal}} = 300\frac{\text{ft}^2}{\text{gal}}. \]

This tells us square feet covered per gallon.

The reciprocal rate is

\[ \frac{5\text{ gal}}{1500\text{ ft}^2} = \frac{1}{300} \frac{\text{gal}}{\text{ft}^2}. \]

Both are valid, but they answer different questions.

Choose the Orientation That Matches the Question

If the question asks:

How many miles are traveled per hour?

use

\[ \frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hours}}. \]

If it asks:

How many hours are required per mile?

use

\[ \frac{\text{hours}}{\text{mile}}. \]

The reciprocal rate may be mathematically related, but it has a different interpretation.

Unit Rates Make Comparisons Easier

Suppose Store A sells 6 bottles for \$15 and Store B sells 8 bottles for \$18.

Store A:

\[ \frac{\$15}{6} = \$2.50\text{ per bottle}. \]

Store B:

\[ \frac{\$18}{8} = \$2.25\text{ per bottle}. \]

Store B has the lower unit price.

Unit rates put both offers on the same comparison basis.

Comparing Without Unit Rates

We could also compare the original rates by cross multiplication:

\[ \frac{15}{6} \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{18}{8}. \]

Compare

\[ 15\cdot8=120 \]

with

\[ 18\cdot6=108. \]

Since

\[ 120>108, \]

we know

\[ \frac{15}{6}>\frac{18}{8}. \]

But unit prices are often easier to interpret in practical contexts.

Speed as a Rate

Average speed is commonly defined by

\[ v=\frac{d}{t}, \]

where

  • \(v\) is average speed;
  • \(d\) is distance traveled;
  • \(t\) is elapsed time.

If a cyclist travels 42 miles in 3 hours, then

\[ v=\frac{42}{3}=14. \]

So the average speed is

\[ 14\text{ miles per hour}. \]

Average Rate Is Not Always Instantaneous Rate

If a car travels 120 miles in 2 hours, its average speed is

\[ 60\text{ mph}. \]

This does not mean the speedometer showed exactly \(60\) mph every moment.

The rate describes total change divided by total time:

\[ \text{average rate} = \frac{\text{total change}}{\text{total interval}}. \]

Later mathematics will distinguish average rates from instantaneous rates.

Unit Price

Suppose 3 pounds of apples cost \$7.50.

The unit price is

\[ \frac{\$7.50}{3\text{ lb}} = \$2.50\text{ per lb}. \]

This rate tells how much one pound costs.

Wages as a Rate

If someone earns \$144 for 8 hours of work, the hourly wage is

\[ \frac{\$144}{8\text{ hr}} = \$18\text{ per hr}. \]

The unit rate answers:

How much money is earned for one hour of work?

Productivity as a Rate

Suppose a machine produces 480 parts in 6 hours.

The production rate is

\[ \frac{480\text{ parts}}{6\text{ hr}} = 80\text{ parts per hr}. \]

This rate can be used to estimate production under constant conditions.

Density as a Rate

Density compares mass to volume:

\[ \rho=\frac{m}{V}. \]

If a material has mass

\[ 540\text{ g} \]

and volume

\[ 200\text{ cm}^3, \]

then

\[ \rho = \frac{540}{200} = 2.7 \frac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^3}. \]

The unit rate tells how much mass corresponds to one cubic centimeter.

Population Density

Suppose a city has 120,000 residents in 40 square miles.

The population density is

\[ \frac{120000\text{ people}}{40\text{ mi}^2} = 3000\frac{\text{people}}{\text{mi}^2}. \]

This rate describes people per unit area.

Fuel Efficiency

If a car travels 360 miles using 12 gallons of fuel, then

\[ \frac{360\text{ mi}}{12\text{ gal}} = 30\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{gal}}. \]

The familiar phrase “30 miles per gallon” is a unit rate.

The Reciprocal Fuel Rate

Instead of miles per gallon, we could measure gallons per mile:

\[ \frac{12\text{ gal}}{360\text{ mi}} = \frac{1}{30}\frac{\text{gal}}{\text{mi}}. \]

This form is less familiar in the United States but can be useful when calculating fuel consumed over a known distance.

Rate as a Constant Multiplier

If a constant unit rate is

\[ 60\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{hr}}, \]

then after \(t\) hours the distance is

\[ d=60t. \]

The unit rate becomes the constant multiplier connecting time and distance.

This is the beginning of a proportional relationship.

From Unit Rate to Proportional Equation

Suppose apples cost \$2.50 per pound.

If \(p\) is pounds purchased and \(C\) is cost, then

\[ C=2.50p. \]

The unit rate

\[ 2.50\frac{\$}{\text{lb}} \]

becomes the constant of proportionality.

Unit Rate and Slope

For a proportional relationship

\[ y=kx, \]

the constant

\[ k=\frac{y}{x} \]

is a unit rate when the variables represent quantities with units.

Graphically, the same constant is also the slope of the line through the origin.

This connects rate reasoning to algebra and coordinate geometry.

Tables and Unit Rates

Consider the table:

Hours Miles
1 55
2 110
3 165

For every row,

\[ \frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hours}}=55. \]

The constant unit rate is

\[ 55\text{ miles per hour}. \]

Recognizing a Constant Rate

Suppose a table gives pairs \((x,y)\).

If

\[ \frac{y}{x} \]

has the same value for every nonzero \(x\), then the relationship is proportional.

That repeated quotient is the constant of proportionality and often represents a unit rate.

Not Every Rate Is Constant

A rate can change.

For example, a runner may complete:

  • the first mile in 8 minutes;
  • the second in 9 minutes;
  • the third in 10 minutes.

The rate is not constant over the entire run.

We can still calculate an average rate, but we should not assume the same unit rate applies to every interval.

Average Unit Rate

Suppose a runner travels 3 miles in 27 minutes.

The average pace is

\[ \frac{27\text{ min}}{3\text{ mi}} = 9\frac{\text{min}}{\text{mi}}. \]

The average speed is the reciprocal form:

\[ \frac{3\text{ mi}}{27\text{ min}} = \frac{1}{9}\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{min}}. \]

These two rates describe the same trip from different perspectives.

Rate Versus Pace

Speed is often measured as

\[ \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}. \]

Pace is often measured as

\[ \frac{\text{time}}{\text{distance}}. \]

For running,

\[ 6\text{ miles per hour} \]

corresponds to

\[ 10\text{ minutes per mile}. \]

One is the reciprocal of the other after converting units consistently.

Unit Conversions Inside Rates

Suppose a car travels

\[ 90\frac{\text{km}}{\text{hr}}. \]

To convert the rate to kilometers per minute, use

\[ 1\text{ hr}=60\text{ min}. \]

Then

\[ 90\frac{\text{km}}{\text{hr}} \cdot \frac{1\text{ hr}}{60\text{ min}} = 1.5\frac{\text{km}}{\text{min}}. \]

The hour units cancel.

Dimensional Analysis

Unit conversion can be treated algebraically.

For example, convert

\[ 5\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \]

to meters per minute:

\[ 5\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \frac{60\text{ s}}{1\text{ min}} = 300\frac{\text{m}}{\text{min}}. \]

The seconds cancel, leaving the desired unit.

This method is called dimensional analysis or the factor-label method.

Cancel Units Just Like Factors

In

\[ 5\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \frac{60\text{ s}}{\text{min}}, \]

the unit \(\text{s}\) appears once in the numerator and once in the denominator.

So it cancels, leaving

\[ 300\frac{\text{m}}{\text{min}}. \]

Units behave like multiplicative labels and can help us organize conversions correctly.

Compound Rates

Some rates contain more than two unit factors.

For example, fuel consumption might be expressed as

\[ \frac{\text{gallons}}{\text{mile}}, \]

while population growth might be expressed as

\[ \frac{\text{people}}{\text{year}}. \]

A more complex physical rate might be

\[ \frac{\text{kilowatt-hours}}{\text{square meter}\cdot\text{day}}. \]

The same quotient logic still applies.

Rates Can Be Nested

Consider acceleration:

\[ \frac{\text{meters per second}}{\text{second}}. \]

This can be written as

\[ \frac{\text{m}/\text{s}}{\text{s}} = \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}. \]

Acceleration is therefore a rate of change of a rate.

This is an early example of how rate ideas build toward more advanced mathematics.

Interpreting the Numerator and Denominator

For

\[ 80\frac{\text{pages}}{\text{hour}}, \]

the numerator tells us the amount of reading:

\[ 80\text{ pages}. \]

The denominator tells us the reference unit:

\[ 1\text{ hour}. \]

Always ask:

What quantity is changing, and with respect to what?

Unit Rates from Fractions

Suppose

\[ \frac{3}{4}\text{ pound} \]

costs \$2.40.

The unit price is

\[ \frac{\$2.40}{\frac34\text{ lb}}. \]

Divide by \(\frac34\):

\[ 2.40\div\frac34 = 2.40\cdot\frac43 = 3.20. \]

So the unit price is

\[ \$3.20\text{ per lb}. \]

Unit Rates from Decimal Quantities

Suppose 2.5 kilograms cost \$11.25.

The cost per kilogram is

\[ \frac{11.25}{2.5}=4.5. \]

So the unit rate is

\[ \$4.50\text{ per kg}. \]

Scaling a Unit Rate

If a machine produces

\[ 80\text{ parts per hour}, \]

then in \(5\) hours, under a constant-rate assumption, it produces

\[ 80\cdot5=400 \]

parts.

Unit rates can be scaled by multiplication.

Scaling Back to One

If 5 hours correspond to 400 parts, then dividing both quantities by \(5\) gives

\[ 1\text{ hour} \]

and

\[ 80\text{ parts}. \]

Finding the unit rate is often just a matter of scaling one quantity down to \(1\) and scaling the other by the same factor.

Rate Tables

Suppose the unit rate is

\[ 12\text{ dollars per hour}. \]
Hours Dollars
1 12
2 24
5 60

Every row obeys

\[ \text{dollars}=12(\text{hours}). \]

Unit Rate as a Conversion Factor

A unit rate can sometimes be used as a conversion factor between related quantities.

If

\[ 1\text{ hour}\longleftrightarrow60\text{ miles}, \]

then

\[ 60\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}} \]

converts time into distance under a constant-speed assumption.

Likewise, the reciprocal

\[ \frac{1\text{ hour}}{60\text{ miles}} \]

converts distance into time.

Comparing Different Units Requires Conversion

Suppose one runner moves at

\[ 6\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}} \]

and another at

\[ 9\frac{\text{kilometers}}{\text{hour}}. \]

These cannot be compared directly until the distance units match.

Convert one rate to the other's units, then compare.

Do Not Compare Mismatched Denominators Carelessly

Suppose one worker completes

\[ 12\text{ tasks in 30 minutes} \]

and another completes

\[ 18\text{ tasks in 1 hour}. \]

Because the time units differ, compare unit rates in a common unit.

First worker:

\[ \frac{12}{30} = 0.4\text{ task per minute}. \]

Second worker:

\[ \frac{18}{60} = 0.3\text{ task per minute}. \]

The first worker has the higher rate.

Rate Problems Often Contain Assumptions

If we use a rate to predict future quantities, we often assume the rate stays constant.

For example, from

\[ 60\text{ miles per hour} \]

we may predict

\[ 180\text{ miles in 3 hours}. \]

This prediction assumes the same average rate continues.

In real settings, rates may change, so the assumption should be understood rather than hidden.

Worked Example: Unit Price

A 12-pack costs \$8.76. Find the cost per item.

\[ \frac{\$8.76}{12} = \$0.73. \]

So the unit price is

\[ \boxed{\$0.73\text{ per item}}. \]

Worked Example: Speed

A train travels 315 miles in 4.5 hours. Find its average speed.

\[ v=\frac{315}{4.5}. \]

Thus,

\[ v=70. \]

The average speed is

\[ \boxed{70\text{ mph}}. \]

Worked Example: Productivity

A team completes 96 inspections in 8 hours.

The unit rate is

\[ \frac{96}{8} = 12. \]

So the team averages

\[ \boxed{12\text{ inspections per hour}}. \]

Worked Example: Compare Two Offers

Offer A: 5 pounds for \$14.

Offer B: 8 pounds for \$21.60.

Offer A:

\[ \frac{14}{5}=2.80. \]

Offer B:

\[ \frac{21.60}{8}=2.70. \]

So Offer B has the lower unit price:

\[ \boxed{\$2.70\text{ per pound}}. \]

Worked Example: Convert a Rate

Convert

\[ 72\frac{\text{km}}{\text{hr}} \]

to kilometers per minute.

\[ 72\frac{\text{km}}{\text{hr}} \cdot \frac{1\text{ hr}}{60\text{ min}} = 1.2\frac{\text{km}}{\text{min}}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{1.2\text{ km/min}}. \]

A Reliable Rate Workflow

  1. Identify the two quantities being compared.
  2. Decide which quantity belongs in the numerator and which in the denominator.
  3. Write the units explicitly.
  4. Divide to form the rate.
  5. If needed, scale the denominator to \(1\) to obtain a unit rate.
  6. Convert units before comparing rates with different measurement systems.
  7. Interpret the final rate in words.
  8. Check whether a constant-rate assumption is justified.

Common Errors

Dropping the Units

A rate without units is often incomplete or ambiguous.

Reversing the Rate

Miles per hour and hours per mile are reciprocals, but they answer different questions.

Comparing Rates with Different Units Directly

Convert to a common unit first.

Assuming Every Rate Is Constant

A unit rate computed from totals may be an average rather than a rate that holds at every moment.

Dividing the Wrong Way

If the question asks for dollars per pound, compute

\[ \frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{pounds}}, \]

not the reciprocal.

Using a Unit Rate Without Interpreting It

Always state what one unit of the denominator corresponds to.

Practice

1. Find a Unit Rate

A car travels 240 miles in 4 hours. Find the average speed.

Check Answer \[ \frac{240\text{ mi}}{4\text{ hr}} = 60\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{hr}}. \]

Answer:

\[ \boxed{60\text{ mph}}. \]

2. Unit Price

Six cans cost \$9. Find the cost per can.

Check Answer \[ \frac{\$9}{6} = \$1.50. \]

Answer:

\[ \boxed{\$1.50\text{ per can}}. \]

3. Productivity

A printer produces 750 pages in 5 minutes. Find the pages per minute.

Check Answer \[ \frac{750}{5} = 150. \]

Answer:

\[ \boxed{150\text{ pages per minute}}. \]

4. Compare Unit Prices

Which is cheaper per ounce?

Package A: 10 oz for \$4.50.

Package B: 16 oz for \$6.72.

Check Answer

Package A:

\[ \frac{4.50}{10} = 0.45. \]

Package B:

\[ \frac{6.72}{16} = 0.42. \]

Package B is cheaper at

\[ \boxed{\$0.42\text{ per ounce}}. \]

5. Convert a Rate

Convert

\[ 120\frac{\text{km}}{\text{hr}} \]

to kilometers per minute.

Check Answer \[ 120\frac{\text{km}}{\text{hr}} \cdot \frac{1\text{ hr}}{60\text{ min}} = 2\frac{\text{km}}{\text{min}}. \]

Answer:

\[ \boxed{2\text{ km/min}}. \]

6. Reciprocal Rate

A runner moves at 8 miles per hour. What reciprocal rate describes hours per mile?

Check Answer \[ \frac{1\text{ hr}}{8\text{ mi}}. \]

So the rate is

\[ \boxed{\frac18\text{ hour per mile}}. \]

This is equivalent to

\[ 7.5\text{ minutes per mile}. \]

7. Constant-Rate Equation

A machine produces 45 units per hour. Write an equation for the number \(N\) of units produced in \(t\) hours.

Check Answer \[ \boxed{N=45t}. \]

Key Ideas

  • A rate is a ratio comparing quantities with different units.
  • A unit rate expresses how much of one quantity corresponds to one unit of another.
  • The word “per” usually indicates division.
  • Units are part of the mathematical meaning and should be carried through calculations.
  • Reciprocal rates are related but answer different questions.
  • Unit rates make comparisons easier by putting quantities on a common basis.
  • Speed, unit price, wages, productivity, density, fuel efficiency, and population density are all rates.
  • A constant unit rate becomes a constant of proportionality in equations such as \(y=kx\).
  • Unit conversions can be organized with dimensional analysis.
  • Average rates summarize total change over an interval and need not equal an instantaneous rate.
  • Not every observed rate is constant, so predictions may depend on assumptions.
  • Rates connect arithmetic, algebra, measurement, modeling, and later ideas of slope and change.

Where This Leads

Rates and unit rates provide the numerical foundation for proportions and proportional relationships.

From here, the same ideas will extend into ratios, equivalent rates, constants of proportionality, direct variation, inverse variation, scale, similarity, work-rate problems, motion, mixture models, and eventually the broader mathematics of change.
