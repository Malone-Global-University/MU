Logarithm Laws and Changing Bases

Logarithm laws translate multiplication, division, and powers inside a logarithm into addition, subtraction, and multiplication outside it. These laws are the inverse-side counterparts of the exponent laws. They let us rewrite complicated logarithmic expressions, solve exponential equations, and evaluate logarithms in bases that a calculator does not provide directly.

The Central Idea

Logarithms return exponents.

Because exponents turn multiplication of same-base powers into addition of exponents, logarithms turn multiplication into addition:

\[ \boxed{\log_b(MN)=\log_b(M)+\log_b(N)}. \]

Similarly, division becomes subtraction and powers become multiplication.

These are structural consequences of exponent laws, not unrelated formulas invented for logarithms.

Conditions on Logarithm Laws

Throughout the real-number logarithm laws, the base must satisfy

\[ \boxed{b>0,\qquad b\ne1}. \]

Each logarithmic argument must also be positive.

Thus expressions such as

\[ \log_b(M) \]

require

\[ \boxed{M>0}. \]

The Product Law

For positive \(M\) and \(N\),

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(MN) = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N) }. \]

A logarithm of a product becomes a sum of logarithms.

Why the Product Law Works

Let

\[ x=\log_b(M) \]

and

\[ y=\log_b(N). \]

Then

\[ b^x=M \]

and

\[ b^y=N. \]

Multiply:

\[ MN=b^xb^y. \]

The exponent product law gives

\[ MN=b^{x+y}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \log_b(MN)=x+y. \]

Substituting the original logarithms,

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(MN)=\log_b(M)+\log_b(N) }. \]

Example: Expanding a Product

Expand

\[ \log_3(9x), \]

assuming \(x>0\).

Use the product law:

\[ \log_3(9x) = \log_3(9)+\log_3(x). \]

Since

\[ \log_3(9)=2, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{2+\log_3(x)}. \]

Example: Condensing a Sum

Condense

\[ \log_5(x)+\log_5(y), \]

where \(x>0\) and \(y>0\).

Reverse the product law:

\[ \boxed{\log_5(xy)}. \]

The Quotient Law

For positive \(M\) and \(N\),

\[ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b(M)-\log_b(N) }. \]

A logarithm of a quotient becomes a difference of logarithms.

Why the Quotient Law Works

Let

\[ x=\log_b(M) \]

and

\[ y=\log_b(N). \]

Then

\[ M=b^x,\qquad N=b^y. \]

Divide:

\[ \frac{M}{N} = \frac{b^x}{b^y}. \]

The exponent quotient law gives

\[ \frac{M}{N}=b^{x-y}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b(M)-\log_b(N) }. \]

Example: Expanding a Quotient

Expand

\[ \log_2\left(\frac{x}{8}\right), \]

with \(x>0\).

Use the quotient law:

\[ \log_2(x)-\log_2(8). \]

Since

\[ \log_2(8)=3, \]

the result is

\[ \boxed{\log_2(x)-3}. \]

Example: Condensing a Difference

Condense

\[ \log_7(x)-\log_7(y), \]

where \(x>0\) and \(y>0\).

Reverse the quotient law:

\[ \boxed{\log_7\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)}. \]

The Power Law

For a positive logarithmic argument,

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(M^p) = p\log_b(M) }. \]

An exponent inside a logarithm becomes a multiplier outside.

Why the Power Law Works

Let

\[ x=\log_b(M). \]

Then

\[ M=b^x. \]

Raise both sides to power \(p\):

\[ M^p=(b^x)^p. \]

The power-of-a-power law gives

\[ M^p=b^{px}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \log_b(M^p)=px. \]

Since \(x=\log_b(M)\),

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(M^p)=p\log_b(M) }. \]

Example: Expanding a Power

Expand

\[ \log_4(x^5), \]

under conditions making the original logarithm real and the stated expansion valid.

The power law gives

\[ \boxed{5\log_4(x)} \]

when \(x>0\).

Example: Condensing a Coefficient

Condense

\[ 3\log_2(x), \]

with \(x>0\).

Move the coefficient into the logarithm as an exponent:

\[ \boxed{\log_2(x^3)}. \]

A Compact Logarithm-Law Reference

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(MN) = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N) } \] \[ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b(M)-\log_b(N) } \] \[ \boxed{ \log_b(M^p) = p\log_b(M) } \]

These laws assume valid logarithmic bases and positive real logarithmic arguments wherever the individual logarithms appear.

Multiplication Becomes Addition

The product law is worth interpreting conceptually:

\[ \log_b(MN) = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N). \]

Multiplication inside the logarithm becomes addition outside.

This is the inverse-side reflection of

\[ b^xb^y=b^{x+y}. \]

Division Becomes Subtraction

The quotient law says

\[ \log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b(M)-\log_b(N). \]

Division inside the logarithm becomes subtraction outside.

This mirrors

\[ \frac{b^x}{b^y}=b^{x-y}. \]

Powers Become Multipliers

The power law says

\[ \log_b(M^p) = p\log_b(M). \]

An exponent inside becomes an ordinary multiplier outside.

This mirrors

\[ (b^x)^p=b^{px}. \]

Expanding Logarithms

To expand a logarithmic expression means to use logarithm laws to separate products, quotients, and powers into simpler logarithmic pieces.

For example,

\[ \log_b\left(\frac{x^3y}{z^2}\right) \]

expands into

\[ \boxed{ 3\log_b(x)+\log_b(y)-2\log_b(z) }, \]

assuming \(x>0\), \(y>0\), and \(z>0\).

Condensing Logarithms

To condense means to reverse the logarithm laws and combine several logarithms into one.

For example,

\[ 2\log_b(x)+\log_b(y)-\log_b(z) \]

becomes

\[ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{x^2y}{z}\right) }. \]

Example: Full Expansion

Expand

\[ \log_3\left(\frac{9x^4\sqrt{y}}{z^2}\right), \]

assuming \(x>0\), \(y>0\), and \(z>0\).

Start with the quotient law:

\[ \log_3(9x^4\sqrt y)-\log_3(z^2). \]

Expand the numerator product:

\[ \log_3(9)+\log_3(x^4)+\log_3(y^{1/2}) - \log_3(z^2). \]

Apply the power law:

\[ 2+4\log_3(x)+\frac12\log_3(y)-2\log_3(z). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ 2+4\log_3(x)+\frac12\log_3(y)-2\log_3(z) }. \]

Example: Full Condensation

Condense

\[ 2\log_5(x)+3\log_5(y)-\frac12\log_5(z), \]

assuming positive variables.

Move coefficients into exponents:

\[ \log_5(x^2)+\log_5(y^3)-\log_5(z^{1/2}). \]

Combine the sum:

\[ \log_5(x^2y^3)-\log_5(z^{1/2}). \]

Combine the difference:

\[ \boxed{ \log_5\left(\frac{x^2y^3}{\sqrt z}\right) }. \]

Known Logarithmic Values Can Simplify Expressions

Consider

\[ \log_2(32x). \]

Expand:

\[ \log_2(32)+\log_2(x). \]

Since

\[ \log_2(32)=5, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{5+\log_2(x)}. \]

Roots Can Be Handled with the Power Law

Since

\[ \sqrt[n]{x}=x^{1/n}, \]

logarithms of roots can be rewritten as

\[ \log_b(\sqrt[n]{x}) = \log_b(x^{1/n}). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(\sqrt[n]{x}) = \frac1n\log_b(x) }, \]

under the appropriate real-domain conditions.

Example: Logarithm of a Square Root

Simplify

\[ \log_7(\sqrt{x}), \]

with \(x>0\).

Rewrite the radical:

\[ \sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}. \]

Apply the power law:

\[ \boxed{ \frac12\log_7(x) }. \]

Reciprocals Produce Negative Logarithms

For \(x>0\),

\[ \log_b\left(\frac1x\right) = \log_b(1)-\log_b(x). \]

Since

\[ \log_b(1)=0, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac1x\right) = -\log_b(x) }. \]

Logarithms of Powers of the Base

The inverse identity gives

\[ \log_b(b^x)=x. \]

The power law reaches the same result:

\[ \log_b(b^x) = x\log_b(b). \]

Since

\[ \log_b(b)=1, \]

we again obtain

\[ \boxed{x}. \]

The Product Law Does Not Apply to Addition

There is no logarithm law that says

\[ \log_b(M+N) = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N). \]

In general,

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(M+N) \ne \log_b(M)+\log_b(N) }. \]

Numerical Check of the Addition Error

Consider base \(10\):

\[ \log(10+100) = \log(110). \]

But

\[ \log(10)+\log(100) = 1+2 = 3. \]

Since

\[ \log(110)\ne3, \]

logarithms do not distribute across addition.

The Quotient Law Does Not Apply to Subtraction

Similarly,

\[ \log_b(M-N) \]

cannot generally be rewritten as

\[ \log_b(M)-\log_b(N). \]

The quotient law applies to

\[ \log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right), \]

not to a difference \(M-N\).

Domain Restrictions Can Be Hidden by Expansion

Consider

\[ \log_b(x^2). \]

The original logarithm is defined whenever

\[ x^2>0, \]

which means

\[ x\ne0. \]

Writing

\[ 2\log_b(x) \]

would require \(x>0\), so it is not equivalent over the entire real domain of the original expression.

The Absolute-Value Refinement

For real \(x\ne0\),

\[ x^2=|x|^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \log_b(x^2) = \log_b(|x|^2) = 2\log_b|x|. \]

Thus the domain-preserving identity is

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(x^2)=2\log_b|x| }, \qquad x\ne0. \]

Why Textbooks Often Assume Positive Variables

Many introductory logarithm exercises state that all variables are positive.

Under that assumption,

\[ |x|=x, \]

and expansions such as

\[ \log_b(x^2)=2\log_b(x) \]

can be used without repeatedly introducing absolute values.

The assumption simplifies notation, but it should not be forgotten.

Changing Bases

A logarithm may use any valid base, but calculators usually provide direct keys for only a small number of bases.

The change-of-base formula rewrites a logarithm in one base using logarithms in another base:

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\log_c(x)}{\log_c(b)} }. \]

Conditions for Change of Base

In

\[ \log_b(x) = \frac{\log_c(x)}{\log_c(b)}, \]

the original base must satisfy

\[ b>0,\qquad b\ne1, \]

the new base must satisfy

\[ c>0,\qquad c\ne1, \]

and the argument must satisfy

\[ x>0. \]

Why the Change-of-Base Formula Works

Let

\[ y=\log_b(x). \]

Then

\[ b^y=x. \]

Take logarithm base \(c\) of both sides:

\[ \log_c(b^y)=\log_c(x). \]

Apply the power law:

\[ y\log_c(b)=\log_c(x). \]

Divide:

\[ y= \frac{\log_c(x)}{\log_c(b)}. \]

Since \(y=\log_b(x)\),

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\log_c(x)}{\log_c(b)} }. \]

Changing to Common Logarithms

Choosing base \(10\) gives

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\log(x)}{\log(b)} }. \]

Here \(\log\) denotes the common logarithm, base \(10\).

Changing to Natural Logarithms

Choosing base \(e\) gives

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(b)} }. \]

Here \(\ln\) denotes the natural logarithm, base \(e\).

Either Common or Natural Logarithms Work

Both

\[ \frac{\log(x)}{\log(b)} \]

and

\[ \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(b)} \]

equal

\[ \log_b(x). \]

The numerator and denominator must simply use the same new base.

Example: Evaluate \(\log_2(10)\)

A calculator may not have a dedicated base-\(2\) logarithm key.

Use change of base:

\[ \log_2(10) = \frac{\ln(10)}{\ln(2)}. \]

Numerically,

\[ \boxed{\log_2(10)\approx3.3219}. \]

Example: Evaluate \(\log_7(20)\)

Use natural logarithms:

\[ \log_7(20) = \frac{\ln(20)}{\ln(7)}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\log_7(20)\approx1.5395}. \]

Example: Change Base Using Common Logs

Evaluate

\[ \log_4(30). \]

Using common logarithms,

\[ \log_4(30) = \frac{\log(30)}{\log(4)}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\log_4(30)\approx2.4534}. \]

Change of Base Explains Reciprocal Logarithms

Consider

\[ \log_b(c). \]

Change to base \(c\):

\[ \log_b(c) = \frac{\log_c(c)}{\log_c(b)}. \]

Since

\[ \log_c(c)=1, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(c)=\frac{1}{\log_c(b)} }. \]

A Useful Reciprocal Identity

The previous result can be written

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(c)\log_c(b)=1 }. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(c)=\frac1{\log_c(b)} }. \]

Changing Bases Does Not Change the Logarithmic Value

The expression

\[ \log_b(x) \]

represents one specific exponent.

Change of base does not alter that exponent. It merely computes it using a different logarithmic measuring system.

Why Change of Base Is Useful

Change of base is useful because it lets us:

  • evaluate logarithms in arbitrary bases with a calculator;
  • compare logarithmic expressions written with different bases;
  • solve exponential equations numerically;
  • rewrite formulas using the natural logarithm;
  • derive relationships between different exponential scales.

Solving \(b^x=y\) with Change of Base

If

\[ b^x=y, \]

then

\[ x=\log_b(y). \]

Change base:

\[ \boxed{ x= \frac{\ln(y)}{\ln(b)} }. \]

This is one of the most important practical uses of logarithms.

Example: Solve an Exponential Equation

Solve

\[ 3^x=20. \]

Write

\[ x=\log_3(20). \]

Change base:

\[ x= \frac{\ln20}{\ln3}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x\approx2.7268}. \]

Logarithm Laws Can Expose Unknown Exponents

Consider

\[ A=Pb^t. \]

Divide by \(P\):

\[ \frac{A}{P}=b^t. \]

Take logarithms:

\[ \ln\left(\frac{A}{P}\right) = \ln(b^t). \]

Apply the power law:

\[ \ln\left(\frac{A}{P}\right) = t\ln b. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ t= \frac{\ln(A/P)}{\ln b} }. \]

Logarithm Laws and Continuous Exponentials

Suppose

\[ A=A_0e^{kt}. \]

Divide:

\[ \frac{A}{A_0}=e^{kt}. \]

Apply natural logarithms:

\[ \ln\left(\frac{A}{A_0}\right)=kt. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ t= \frac{1}{k} \ln\left(\frac{A}{A_0}\right) }. \]

Common Error: Splitting a Sum

It is incorrect to write

\[ \log_b(x+y) = \log_b(x)+\log_b(y). \]

The product law applies only when the argument is a product:

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(xy)=\log_b(x)+\log_b(y) }. \]

Common Error: Splitting a Difference

It is also incorrect to write

\[ \log_b(x-y) = \log_b(x)-\log_b(y). \]

The quotient law requires division:

\[ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_b(x)-\log_b(y) }. \]

Common Error: Moving a Coefficient Inside Incorrectly

If

\[ 3\log_b(x), \]

is condensed, the \(3\) becomes an exponent:

\[ \boxed{ 3\log_b(x)=\log_b(x^3) }. \]

It does not become

\[ \log_b(3x). \]

Common Error: Dividing Logarithms in Change of Base Backward

The correct formula is

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(b)} }. \]

The logarithm of the argument goes in the numerator, and the logarithm of the original base goes in the denominator.

Common Error: Mixing Logarithm Types in One Ratio

Change of base requires the same logarithmic base in numerator and denominator.

Thus

\[ \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(b)} \]

is valid, and

\[ \frac{\log(x)}{\log(b)} \]

is valid.

A mixed expression such as

\[ \frac{\ln(x)}{\log(b)} \]

is not the standard change-of-base formula.

Common Error: Forgetting Positive Arguments

The expression

\[ \log_b(x)+\log_b(y) \]

requires

\[ x>0,\qquad y>0. \]

Condensing it to

\[ \log_b(xy) \]

does not erase those original restrictions.

Common Error: Expanding \(\log_b(x^2)\) without Checking Domain

The original expression

\[ \log_b(x^2) \]

allows any real

\[ x\ne0. \]

The expression

\[ 2\log_b(x) \]

allows only \(x>0\).

The domain-preserving real identity is

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(x^2)=2\log_b|x| }. \]

A Reliable Procedure for Expanding Logarithms

  1. Record any stated domain assumptions.
  2. Use the quotient law to separate numerator and denominator.
  3. Use the product law to separate factors.
  4. Rewrite radicals as rational exponents when useful.
  5. Use the power law to move exponents in front.
  6. Evaluate any exact logarithms that are immediately recognizable.
  7. Check that the expanded form preserves the intended domain.

A Reliable Procedure for Condensing Logarithms

  1. Verify that the logarithms use the same base.
  2. Move coefficients into logarithms as exponents.
  3. Combine sums using the product law.
  4. Combine differences using the quotient law.
  5. Write the final result as one logarithm.
  6. Preserve restrictions from the original logarithmic arguments.

A Reliable Procedure for Changing Bases

  1. Identify the original base \(b\).
  2. Identify the argument \(x\).
  3. Choose a convenient new base, usually \(10\) or \(e\).
  4. Put the logarithm of the argument in the numerator.
  5. Put the logarithm of the original base in the denominator.
  6. Use the same new base in numerator and denominator.
  7. Evaluate and round only at the end if a decimal approximation is needed.

The pattern is

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\ln x}{\ln b} = \frac{\log x}{\log b} }. \]

Example: Full Expansion Workflow

Expand

\[ \log_2\left(\frac{32x^3y^2}{\sqrt z}\right), \]

assuming \(x>0\), \(y>0\), and \(z>0\).

Separate the quotient:

\[ \log_2(32x^3y^2)-\log_2(z^{1/2}). \]

Separate the numerator factors:

\[ \log_2(32)+\log_2(x^3)+\log_2(y^2) - \log_2(z^{1/2}). \]

Apply the power law:

\[ 5+3\log_2(x)+2\log_2(y)-\frac12\log_2(z). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ 5+3\log_2(x)+2\log_2(y)-\frac12\log_2(z) }. \]

Example: Full Condensing Workflow

Condense

\[ 4\ln x+2\ln y-\ln z, \]

assuming positive variables.

Move coefficients into exponents:

\[ \ln(x^4)+\ln(y^2)-\ln z. \]

Combine the sum:

\[ \ln(x^4y^2)-\ln z. \]

Combine the quotient:

\[ \boxed{ \ln\left(\frac{x^4y^2}{z}\right) }. \]

Example: Full Change-of-Base Workflow

Evaluate

\[ \log_6(17). \]

Choose natural logarithms:

\[ \log_6(17) = \frac{\ln17}{\ln6}. \]

Numerically,

\[ \boxed{ \log_6(17)\approx1.5812 }. \]

What to Remember

The three central logarithm laws are

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(MN)=\log_b(M)+\log_b(N) } \] \[ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b(M)-\log_b(N) } \] \[ \boxed{ \log_b(M^p)=p\log_b(M) }. \]

Change of base is

\[ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\ln x}{\ln b} = \frac{\log x}{\log b} }. \]
  • Products inside logarithms become sums outside.
  • Quotients inside logarithms become differences outside.
  • Powers inside logarithms become multipliers outside.
  • There is no corresponding rule for splitting sums or differences inside logarithms.
  • Logarithmic arguments must remain positive in the real numbers.
  • Expansion and condensation must preserve the domain of the original expression.
  • Change of base lets any valid logarithm be evaluated using a convenient base such as \(10\) or \(e\).

The central habit is: look first at the operation inside the logarithm—product, quotient, or power—and apply only the law that matches that structure.
