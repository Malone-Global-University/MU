Logarithms as Inverses

Exponential functions answer questions such as, “What value results when a base is raised to this exponent?” Logarithms reverse that question. A logarithm asks, “What exponent must be placed on this base to produce that value?” Because logarithms undo exponentiation, exponential and logarithmic relationships are two forms of the same underlying structure.

The Central Idea

Suppose

\[ b^x=y, \]

where

\[ b>0,\qquad b\ne1,\qquad y>0. \]

The same relationship can be written logarithmically as

\[ \boxed{\log_b(y)=x}. \]

These equations say exactly the same thing.

A Logarithm Answers an Exponent Question

Consider

\[ 2^3=8. \]

The logarithmic version is

\[ \boxed{\log_2(8)=3}. \]

The logarithm asks:

To what exponent must \(2\) be raised to produce \(8\)?

The answer is \(3\).

Exponential Form and Logarithmic Form

The conversion pattern is

\[ \boxed{b^x=y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\log_b(y)=x}. \]

The base stays the same.

The exponent becomes the logarithm's output.

The exponential result becomes the logarithm's input.

Reading Logarithmic Notation

The expression

\[ \log_b(y) \]

is read

“log base \(b\) of \(y\)”.

It asks for the exponent \(x\) satisfying

\[ b^x=y. \]

The Three Roles in a Logarithm

In

\[ \log_b(y)=x, \]
  • \(b\) is the base;
  • \(y\) is the argument;
  • \(x\) is the logarithmic value, which is the exponent.

The logarithm returns an exponent.

Example: Convert to Logarithmic Form

Rewrite

\[ 5^3=125 \]

in logarithmic form.

The base is \(5\), the result is \(125\), and the exponent is \(3\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\log_5(125)=3}. \]

Example: Convert to Exponential Form

Rewrite

\[ \log_4(64)=3 \]

in exponential form.

The base is \(4\), the logarithmic value \(3\) becomes the exponent, and the argument \(64\) becomes the result:

\[ \boxed{4^3=64}. \]

Why Logarithms Are Inverses

An exponential function sends an exponent to a value:

\[ x\longmapsto b^x. \]

The logarithm reverses that mapping:

\[ b^x\longmapsto x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\log_b(b^x)=x}. \]

The Other Inverse Identity

If logarithms undo exponentiation, exponentiation must also undo logarithms.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{b^{\log_b(x)}=x}, \]

for \(x>0\).

The Two Fundamental Inverse Identities

\[ \boxed{\log_b(b^x)=x} \]

and

\[ \boxed{b^{\log_b(x)}=x}, \]

where

\[ b>0,\qquad b\ne1,\qquad x>0 \]

where required.

Example: Using an Inverse Identity

Simplify

\[ \log_3(3^7). \]

The logarithm base \(3\) undoes exponentiation by base \(3\):

\[ \boxed{\log_3(3^7)=7}. \]

Example: Exponentiating a Logarithm

Simplify

\[ 5^{\log_5(12)}. \]

Exponentiation by base \(5\) reverses the base-\(5\) logarithm:

\[ \boxed{5^{\log_5(12)}=12}. \]

Evaluating Logarithms by Recognition

To evaluate

\[ \log_2(32), \]

ask:

What exponent on \(2\) produces \(32\)?

Since

\[ 2^5=32, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{\log_2(32)=5}. \]

Logarithms Can Be Zero

Consider

\[ \log_b(1). \]

We ask:

What exponent on \(b\) gives \(1\)?

Since

\[ b^0=1, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{\log_b(1)=0}. \]

The Logarithm of the Base

Consider

\[ \log_b(b). \]

Since

\[ b^1=b, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{\log_b(b)=1}. \]

Logarithms Can Be Negative

Consider

\[ \log_2\left(\frac18\right). \]

Since

\[ 2^{-3}=\frac18, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{\log_2\left(\frac18\right)=-3}. \]

A negative logarithmic value means the required exponent is negative.

Logarithms Can Be Fractional

Consider

\[ \log_9(3). \]

Since

\[ 9^{1/2}=3, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{\log_9(3)=\frac12}. \]

Example: A Rational Logarithmic Value

Evaluate

\[ \log_8(4). \]

We need \(x\) such that

\[ 8^x=4. \]

Rewrite both quantities using base \(2\):

\[ (2^3)^x=2^2. \]

Thus

\[ 3x=2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\log_8(4)=\frac23}. \]

Why the Logarithmic Base Must Be Positive

A standard real logarithm is defined as the inverse of

\[ b^x. \]

To obtain a continuous real-valued exponential function defined for every real \(x\), we require

\[ \boxed{b>0}. \]

Why the Base Cannot Be \(1\)

If

\[ b=1, \]

then

\[ 1^x=1 \]

for every \(x\).

The function would not be one-to-one and therefore would not have an inverse function.

Thus a logarithmic base must satisfy

\[ \boxed{b>0,\qquad b\ne1}. \]

Why the Logarithm's Argument Must Be Positive

For a valid logarithmic base \(b\),

\[ b^x>0 \]

for every real \(x\).

Therefore, the exponential function never produces zero or a negative number.

Since a logarithm reverses an exponential function, its real-valued input must satisfy

\[ \boxed{x>0}. \]

Why \(\log_b(0)\) Is Undefined

Suppose

\[ \log_b(0)=x. \]

This would mean

\[ b^x=0. \]

But for \(b>0\),

\[ b^x>0 \]

for every real \(x\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\log_b(0)\text{ is undefined in the real numbers}}. \]

Why Logarithms of Negative Numbers Are Not Real

Suppose

\[ \log_b(-5)=x. \]

Then we would need

\[ b^x=-5. \]

But a positive base raised to a real exponent remains positive.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\log_b(-5)\text{ is not a real number}}. \]

Domain and Range of an Exponential Function

For

\[ f(x)=b^x, \]

the domain is

\[ \boxed{(-\infty,\infty)} \]

and the range is

\[ \boxed{(0,\infty)}. \]

Domain and Range of a Logarithmic Function

For

\[ g(x)=\log_b(x), \]

the domain is

\[ \boxed{(0,\infty)} \]

and the range is

\[ \boxed{(-\infty,\infty)}. \]

The domain and range swap because the two functions are inverses.

Inverse Functions Swap Inputs and Outputs

If

\[ f(3)=8, \]

then the inverse satisfies

\[ f^{-1}(8)=3. \]

For

\[ f(x)=2^x, \]

the inverse is

\[ f^{-1}(x)=\log_2(x). \]

Thus

\[ 2^3=8 \]

corresponds to

\[ \log_2(8)=3. \]

Points on Inverse Graphs Swap Coordinates

If

\[ (a,b) \]

lies on a function, then

\[ (b,a) \]

lies on its inverse.

Since

\[ (3,8) \]

lies on

\[ y=2^x, \]

the point

\[ (8,3) \]

lies on

\[ y=\log_2(x). \]

Graphs of Inverse Functions

A function and its inverse are reflections across the line

\[ \boxed{y=x}. \]

Therefore, the graphs of

\[ y=b^x \]

and

\[ y=\log_b(x) \]

are mirror images across \(y=x\).

The Exponential Horizontal Asymptote Becomes a Logarithmic Vertical Asymptote

The basic exponential graph

\[ y=b^x \]

has horizontal asymptote

\[ y=0. \]

Reflecting across \(y=x\) turns this into the logarithmic vertical asymptote

\[ \boxed{x=0}. \]

The Point \((0,1)\) Becomes \((1,0)\)

Every basic exponential function satisfies

\[ b^0=1. \]

Therefore, every graph of

\[ y=b^x \]

passes through

\[ (0,1). \]

Its inverse logarithmic graph passes through the reflected point

\[ \boxed{(1,0)}. \]

Increasing Logarithmic Functions

If

\[ b>1, \]

then the exponential function

\[ y=b^x \]

is increasing.

Its inverse

\[ y=\log_b(x) \]

is also increasing.

Decreasing Logarithmic Functions

If

\[ 0 then

\[ y=b^x \]

is decreasing.

Therefore,

\[ y=\log_b(x) \]

is also decreasing.

Example: Evaluate by Converting Forms

Evaluate

\[ \log_7(49). \]

Write the equivalent exponential equation:

\[ 7^x=49. \]

Since

\[ 7^2=49, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{\log_7(49)=2}. \]

Example: Evaluate a Negative Logarithmic Value

Evaluate

\[ \log_5\left(\frac1{125}\right). \]

Since

\[ \frac1{125}=5^{-3}, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{\log_5\left(\frac1{125}\right)=-3}. \]

Example: Evaluate a Fractional Logarithmic Value

Evaluate

\[ \log_{16}(2). \]

We seek \(x\) such that

\[ 16^x=2. \]

Since

\[ 16=2^4, \]

we have

\[ 2^{4x}=2^1. \]

Therefore,

\[ 4x=1 \]

and

\[ \boxed{\log_{16}(2)=\frac14}. \]

Solving a Simple Exponential Equation by Recognition

Solve

\[ 3^x=81. \]

Since

\[ 81=3^4, \]

the equal bases imply

\[ \boxed{x=4}. \]

Solving the Same Equation Logarithmically

Starting with

\[ 3^x=81, \]

rewrite in logarithmic form:

\[ \boxed{x=\log_3(81)}. \]

Since \(\log_3(81)=4\),

\[ \boxed{x=4}. \]

Solving a Simple Logarithmic Equation

Solve

\[ \log_2(x)=5. \]

Rewrite in exponential form:

\[ 2^5=x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=32}. \]

Example: Solve with a Negative Logarithmic Output

Solve

\[ \log_4(x)=-2. \]

Rewrite:

\[ 4^{-2}=x. \]

Since

\[ 4^{-2}=\frac1{16}, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{x=\frac1{16}}. \]

Logarithms Let Us Name Unknown Exponents

Consider

\[ 2^x=10. \]

The value \(10\) is not an obvious integer power of \(2\).

Logarithmic notation gives an exact name to the unknown exponent:

\[ \boxed{x=\log_2(10)}. \]

A calculator can later approximate this value.

Exact Values and Approximate Values

The expression

\[ \log_2(10) \]

is an exact value.

A decimal such as

\[ 3.3219\ldots \]

is an approximation.

Exact logarithmic notation is useful even when no simple rational exponent exists.

Logarithms and Doubling Time

Suppose a growth model has factor \(b>1\).

Doubling time \(D\) satisfies

\[ b^D=2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{D=\log_b(2)}. \]

This is the direct logarithmic meaning of doubling time.

Logarithms and Half-Life

Suppose a decay model has factor

\[ 0 Half-life \(H\) satisfies

\[ b^H=\frac12. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{H=\log_b\left(\frac12\right)}. \]

Why Logarithms Matter in Exponential Models

Exponential models naturally place time or another unknown in the exponent.

For example,

\[ 500(1.08)^t=1000 \]

cannot be solved by ordinary subtraction, multiplication, division, or radicals alone.

A logarithm is the inverse operation that allows the exponent to be recovered.

Inverse Functions and Composition

If two functions are inverses, composing them returns the original input.

Let

\[ f(x)=b^x \]

and

\[ g(x)=\log_b(x). \]

Then

\[ g(f(x)) = \log_b(b^x) = x. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{g\circ f=\operatorname{id}}. \]

The Reverse Composition

Likewise,

\[ f(g(x)) = b^{\log_b(x)} = x, \]

for \(x>0\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{f\circ g=\operatorname{id}} \]

on the logarithm's domain.

Logarithmic Functions Are One-to-One

Because a logarithmic function is an inverse of a one-to-one exponential function, it is also one-to-one.

Therefore, if

\[ \log_b(x)=\log_b(y), \]

then

\[ \boxed{x=y}, \]

provided \(x>0\) and \(y>0\).

Exponential Functions Are One-to-One

Similarly, for a valid exponential base,

\[ b^x=b^y \]

implies

\[ \boxed{x=y}. \]

This property allows equations with matching exponential bases to be solved by equating exponents.

Common Error: Reversing the Wrong Parts

From

\[ 2^5=32, \]

the correct logarithmic form is

\[ \boxed{\log_2(32)=5}. \]

The base remains \(2\).

The exponent \(5\) becomes the logarithmic output, and \(32\) becomes the argument.

Common Error: Thinking \(\log_b(x)\) Means \(b/x\)

Logarithmic notation does not represent division.

The expression

\[ \log_b(x) \]

asks for the exponent that makes

\[ b^{(\text{that exponent})}=x. \]

It is an inverse exponent operation, not a fraction.

Common Error: Treating the Base as the Output

In

\[ \log_3(81)=4, \]

the logarithm's output is \(4\), not \(3\).

The number \(3\) is the base.

The result \(4\) is the exponent required to produce \(81\).

Common Error: Taking the Logarithm of Zero

The expression

\[ \log_b(0) \]

is undefined in the real numbers.

No real exponent on a positive base produces zero.

Common Error: Taking a Real Logarithm of a Negative Number

The expression

\[ \log_b(-7) \]

has no real value for a standard real logarithmic base.

The argument of a real logarithm must be positive.

Common Error: Confusing \(\log_b(x)\) with \(b^x\)

These are inverse operations.

For example,

\[ 2^3=8 \]

while

\[ \log_2(8)=3. \]

One sends exponent \(3\) to value \(8\). The other sends value \(8\) back to exponent \(3\).

Common Error: Confusing an Inverse Function with a Reciprocal

If

\[ f(x)=2^x, \]

then its inverse function is

\[ f^{-1}(x)=\log_2(x). \]

This is not the same as the reciprocal

\[ \frac1{f(x)}=2^{-x}. \]

Inverse functions undo each other's input-output mapping. Reciprocals undo multiplication.

A Reliable Procedure for Converting Exponential to Logarithmic Form

  1. Identify the exponential base.
  2. Keep that same number as the logarithmic base.
  3. Move the exponential result into the logarithm's argument.
  4. Move the exponent to the other side as the logarithmic value.
  5. Check by converting back to exponential form.

The pattern is

\[ \boxed{b^x=y\Longleftrightarrow\log_b(y)=x}. \]

A Reliable Procedure for Evaluating a Logarithm Exactly

  1. Write \(\log_b(y)=x\).
  2. Convert to exponential form \(b^x=y\).
  3. Express \(y\) as a power of \(b\) when possible.
  4. Identify the exponent.
  5. Check the domain condition \(y>0\).

A Reliable Procedure for Solving a Basic Logarithmic Equation

  1. Identify the logarithmic base.
  2. Convert the equation to exponential form.
  3. Evaluate the resulting power.
  4. Solve for the unknown.
  5. Check that any logarithmic argument in the original equation is positive.

Example: Full Conversion Workflow

Rewrite

\[ 7^{-2}=\frac1{49} \]

in logarithmic form.

Base:

\[ 7. \]

Exponential result:

\[ \frac1{49}. \]

Exponent:

\[ -2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\log_7\left(\frac1{49}\right)=-2}. \]

\[ \log_{25}(5)=x. \]

Rewrite \(25\) as \(5^2\):

\[ (5^2)^x=5^1. \]

Therefore,

\[ 2x=1 \]

and

\[ \boxed{x=\frac12}. \]

What to Remember

Exponential and logarithmic forms are equivalent:

\[ \boxed{b^x=y\Longleftrightarrow\log_b(y)=x}. \]
  • A logarithm returns an exponent.
  • The logarithmic base is the same as the exponential base.
  • A valid real logarithmic base satisfies \(b>0\) and \(b\ne1\).
  • The argument of a real logarithm must be positive.
  • \(\log_b(1)=0\).
  • \(\log_b(b)=1\).
  • \(\log_b(b^x)=x\).
  • \(b^{\log_b(x)}=x\) for \(x>0\).
  • Exponential and logarithmic functions are inverses and reflect across \(y=x\).
  • The exponential range becomes the logarithmic domain.

The most important question to ask when reading a logarithm is: what exponent on this base produces the argument?
