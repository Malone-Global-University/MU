Choosing Variables and Defining Parameters

Good notation is part of the solution: it identifies what can change, what is held fixed, and how every symbol connects to the situation.

A Symbol Should Carry a Job Description

Suppose a repair company charges a fixed visit fee and an hourly labor rate. We could write

\[ y=a+bx, \]

but the formula says little until its symbols are defined. A more useful presentation is:

Let \(h\) be the number of labor hours, \(F\) the fixed visit fee in dollars, \(r\) the labor rate in dollars per hour, and \(C\) the total cost in dollars.

\[ C=F+rh, \qquad h\ge0. \]

The second version exposes the model's structure. The labor time and total cost may vary from job to job. The fee and rate can be held fixed for one pricing plan but changed to describe another plan.

Choosing notation is therefore a design decision, not clerical work performed after the mathematics is finished.

\[ \boxed{\text{every symbol needs a meaning, a role, and a permitted range}} \]

Roles That Symbols Can Play

The same letter can play different roles in different problems. The role comes from the model and its definitions, not from the shape of the symbol.

RoleMeaningExample
VariableA quantity permitted to take different values\(t\) in \(d=rt\)
UnknownA value to be determined from available conditions\(x\) in \(3x+4=19\)
Independent variableA selected or controlled inputElapsed time \(t\)
Dependent variableAn output whose value depends on other quantitiesDistance \(d(t)\)
ParameterA quantity held fixed within one case but adjustable across a family of casesRate \(r\) in \(d=rt\)
ConstantA fixed value within the stated setting\(32\) in \(F=\frac95C+32\)
CoefficientA factor multiplying a term\(m\) in \(mx\)
IndexA label identifying a position or member\(n\) in \(a_n\)

These categories overlap in useful ways. A parameter can also be a coefficient. An unknown is often represented by a variable. An index is a variable when it ranges over permitted positions. Each word identifies a different aspect of the symbol's job.

A Reliable Selection Method

  1. Inventory the quantities. List what is known, unknown, measured, selected, fixed, or allowed to change.
  2. Identify the target. Determine what the problem asks you to calculate, compare, optimize, or explain.
  3. Choose the essential varying quantities. Introduce variables only where they improve the model.
  4. Separate inputs from outputs. Decide which quantities determine the others.
  5. Identify adjustable fixed quantities. Treat these as parameters when the model should describe a family of cases.
  6. Assign unambiguous symbols. Prefer familiar or mnemonic letters when they do not conflict.
  7. Define units, domains, and reference points. Explain exactly what each value measures.
  8. Write the relationship. Connect the symbols only after their meanings are stable.
  9. Audit the notation. Confirm that every symbol is defined and used consistently.

The objective is not to maximize the number of letters. It is to build the smallest clear symbolic system that preserves the relationships needed for the task.

Writing Complete Variable Definitions

A strong definition answers several questions at once:

  • What quantity does the symbol represent?
  • What units are used?
  • From what reference point is it measured?
  • What values are permitted?
Incomplete DefinitionImproved Definition
Let \(t\) be time.Let \(t\) be the elapsed time in hours after the experiment begins, with \(0\le t\le8\).
Let \(x\) be tickets.Let \(x\) be the number of adult tickets sold; \(x\) is a nonnegative integer.
Let \(d\) be distance.Let \(d\) be the vehicle's distance from the warehouse, measured in miles along the planned route.
Let \(p\) be price.Let \(p\) be the listed price per item in dollars before tax.

Definitions should be written before the main derivation when a reader could reasonably interpret a symbol in more than one way.

Choosing Clear Symbol Names

Familiar conventions can reduce the reader's workload:

Common SymbolFrequent UseCaution
\(x,y,z\)Generic variables or coordinatesThe letters have no universal meaning
\(t\)TimeState the unit and starting time
\(d\)DistanceMay also mean diameter or a differential
\(r\)Rate or radiusDefine which meaning applies
\(n\)Count, stage, or indexState whether zero or one is the first value
\(C\)Cost, circumference, or capacityA capital letter is distinct from lowercase \(c\)
\(A\)Area, amount, or a pointContext remains necessary

A mnemonic letter is helpful only when it remains unambiguous. Do not use \(m\) simultaneously for meters, minutes, mass, and slope in one derivation.

In ordinary algebra, adjacent letters imply multiplication:

\[ cost=c\cdot o\cdot s\cdot t. \]

Therefore, a word such as “cost” should not be used as an informal multi-letter algebraic variable unless the notation system explicitly treats it as a named identifier. In textbook algebra, use \(C\), a subscripted symbol such as \(C_{\text{total}}\), or a clearly defined function such as \(C(t)\).

Independent and Dependent Variables

Consider a tank that begins with \(50\) liters and fills at \(6\) liters per minute:

\[ V=50+6t. \]
  • \(t\) is the independent variable because elapsed time is the input used to locate a state of the model.
  • \(V\) is the dependent variable because the tank volume is determined from \(t\).

Function notation can make this dependence explicit:

\[ V(t)=50+6t. \]

The distinction is structural, not absolute. If the same relationship is rearranged to determine time from a measured volume,

\[ t=\frac{V-50}{6}, \]

then \(V\) can serve as the input for that task. Choose roles according to the question being answered.

Parameters and Families of Relationships

A parameter allows one symbolic structure to represent many related cases.

The linear family

\[ y=mx+b \]

contains many lines. For a particular line, \(m\) and \(b\) are held fixed while \(x\) and \(y\) vary. Changing \(m\) or \(b\) selects a different member of the family.

Parameter ChoiceResulting RelationshipEffect
\(m=2,\ b=1\)\(y=2x+1\)Rate of change \(2\), initial value \(1\)
\(m=2,\ b=5\)\(y=2x+5\)Same rate, different initial value
\(m=-3,\ b=1\)\(y=-3x+1\)Different rate, same initial value

A service-cost family can be written

\[ C(h)=F+rh, \]

where \(h\) is the input, \(C(h)\) is the output, and \(F\) and \(r\) are parameters. One company might use \(F=40\) and \(r=65\); another could use different values without changing the model's basic structure.

When helpful, all inputs and parameters can be displayed:

\[ C(h;F,r)=F+rh. \]

The semicolon visually separates the primary input \(h\) from the adjustable parameters \(F\) and \(r\). This notation is optional, but the distinction it records is important.

Roles Depend on Context

No symbol is inherently a variable, parameter, or constant. Its role depends on what is allowed to change during the current analysis.

Consider the circle equation

\[ x^2+y^2=r^2. \]
  • For one circle centered at the origin, \(r\) is fixed while \(x\) and \(y\) vary over points on the circle.
  • For a family of circles, \(r\) is a parameter that can be changed to select a circle.
  • If a particular point \((x,y)\) is known and the radius must be found, \(r\) is an unknown.

The symbol \(r\) did not change. The question changed, so its mathematical role changed.

Likewise, in

\[ y=2x+5, \]

\(2\) is a fixed coefficient and \(5\) is a fixed constant term; both are constants within this particular equation. In the family \(y=mx+b\), their positions are occupied by adjustable parameters.

Choosing the Right Number of Variables

Introduce enough variables to express the relevant freedom, but do not create independent symbols for quantities already determined by others.

Use One Variable When One Choice Determines the Rest

A rectangle's length is five meters greater than its width. If

\[ w=\text{width in meters}, \]

then its length is already determined:

\[ l=w+5. \]

For many tasks, we can use \(w\) as the only independent variable rather than treating \(l\) and \(w\) as unrelated choices.

Use Several Variables When Several Quantities Can Vary Independently

If a theater can sell adult tickets and student tickets in many combinations, let

\[ a=\text{number of adult tickets}, \] \[ s=\text{number of student tickets}. \]

If the prices are \(p_a\) and \(p_s\), total revenue is

\[ R=p_a a+p_s s. \]

Without another condition, neither ticket count is determined by the other.

Use a Constraint to Reduce the Model

If exactly \(N\) tickets are sold, then

\[ a+s=N. \]

We may write \(s=N-a\) and express revenue using one independent count:

\[ R=p_a a+p_s(N-a). \]

Subscripts and Indexed Quantities

Subscripts let one base symbol represent a collection of related quantities.

NotationPossible Meaning
\(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\)Values in positions \(1\) through \(n\)
\(p_a,p_s\)Adult-ticket price and student-ticket price
\(T_{\text{initial}},T_{\text{final}}\)Initial and final temperatures
\(r_1,r_2\)Two different rates
\(C_{\text{fixed}},C_{\text{variable}}\)Fixed and variable cost components

A subscript is ordinarily a label, not multiplication. Thus \(x_2\) usually means “\(x\) sub two,” while \(2x\) means two times \(x\).

Subscripts are especially useful when several quantities have the same kind of unit or play parallel roles. They preserve the relationship among the symbols without forcing unrelated letters.

Units, Domains, and Reference Points

A variable definition is incomplete when its numerical value can be interpreted in incompatible ways.

Units

For

\[ C=F+rh, \]

the units must be compatible:

\[ [C]=\text{dollars}, \qquad [F]=\text{dollars}, \qquad [r]=\frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{hour}}, \qquad [h]=\text{hours}. \]

Then

\[ [rh]=\left(\frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{hour}}\right)(\text{hours}) =\text{dollars}. \]

Domains

  • A count of people is usually a nonnegative integer.
  • Elapsed time is often a nonnegative real number.
  • A probability lies between \(0\) and \(1\), inclusive.
  • A denominator must not equal zero.
  • A physical length is ordinarily positive, though a directed coordinate may be negative.

Reference Points

“Time \(t\)” may mean clock time, time since launch, time after payment, or time remaining. Define the reference point:

Let \(t\) be the number of seconds elapsed since the object was released.

Constraints and Assumptions

Variable definitions describe possible values. Constraints connect those values or limit the model.

Suppose \(a\) and \(s\) count adult and student tickets for a \(240\)-seat event. Reasonable conditions include

\[ a,s\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}, \] \[ a+s\le240. \]

If every seat is sold, the inequality becomes

\[ a+s=240. \]

Other assumptions may specify that:

  • a rate remains constant,
  • prices do not change during the modeled period,
  • measurements use a stated precision,
  • objects are treated as identical,
  • friction, delay, or loss is ignored, or
  • the model applies only within an observed range.

An assumption should be stated when removing it would materially change the relationship.

Worked Example: A Pricing Model

A streaming service charges a monthly subscription fee plus a fee for each premium rental. Construct a reusable model.

Inventory the Quantities

  • monthly subscription fee,
  • price per premium rental,
  • number of premium rentals,
  • total monthly charge.

Define the Variables and Parameters

  • Let \(n\) be the number of premium rentals during the month, where \(n\) is a nonnegative integer.
  • Let \(C(n)\) be the total monthly charge in dollars.
  • Let \(F\) be the fixed monthly subscription fee in dollars.
  • Let \(p\) be the price per premium rental in dollars per rental.

Write the Model

\[ \boxed{C(n)=F+pn}. \]

Interpret the Roles

For one pricing plan, \(F\) and \(p\) are fixed parameters while \(n\) and \(C(n)\) vary. Changing \(F\) or \(p\) creates a different pricing plan within the same model family.

Check the Units

\[ \text{dollars} =\text{dollars} +\left(\frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{rental}}\right)(\text{rentals}). \]

Worked Example: Reducing a Geometry Model

A rectangle has perimeter \(50\) meters. Its length is \(5\) meters greater than its width. Choose variables and construct an equation.

Choose One Independent Dimension

Let \(w\) be the width in meters. Because a physical width must be positive,

\[ w>0. \]

Express the Dependent Dimension

The length is five meters greater:

\[ l=w+5. \]

Use the Perimeter Condition

\[ 2l+2w=50. \]

Substitute \(l=w+5\):

\[ \boxed{2(w+5)+2w=50}. \]

We could have introduced \(l\) and \(w\) as two variables and retained two equations. Substitution reduces the model to one unknown because the stated relationship already determines \(l\) from \(w\).

Changing the Parameterization

The same relationship can be described with different choices of variables and parameters. A useful change of notation preserves meaning while making a new task easier.

Suppose distance is modeled in hours by

\[ d=rt, \]

where \(r\) is measured in miles per hour and \(t\) in hours. If time is instead recorded in minutes, let \(m=60t\). Then

\[ t=\frac{m}{60} \]

and

\[ d=r\left(\frac{m}{60}\right) =\frac{r}{60}m. \]

The numerical rate parameter changes from miles per hour to miles per minute, but the physical relationship remains the same.

Reparameterization is valuable when it:

  • matches the available data,
  • uses more natural units,
  • reduces the number of independent variables,
  • makes a constraint automatic, or
  • reveals a useful structure.

Notation Audit

Before solving or presenting a model, audit its notation.

Audit QuestionPurpose
Is every symbol defined?Prevents unexplained notation
Does each symbol keep one meaning?Prevents silent role changes
Are capitalization and subscripts consistent?Distinguishes related quantities
Are units stated and compatible?Checks the operations in the model
Are domains and restrictions recorded?Excludes meaningless values
Are parameters distinguished from primary variables?Clarifies what changes within and across cases
Can dependent quantities be expressed from the chosen inputs?Tests whether the model is usable
Is any variable unnecessary?Keeps the model efficient

Finally, translate the model back into natural language. If the explanation requires changing a definition halfway through, the notation should be revised before further work continues.

Common Modeling Errors

Using a Symbol Before Defining It

A familiar letter does not guarantee a familiar meaning. Define it.

Giving One Symbol Two Meanings

Do not let \(r\) represent both radius and rate in the same model.

Treating a Unit as the Variable

If \(m\) represents a number of minutes, state that meaning. Do not rely on \(m\) also being a standard abbreviation for meters.

Confusing a Parameter with a Universal Constant

A parameter is adjustable across members of a model family. It need not be fixed everywhere.

Introducing Too Many Independent Variables

If \(l=w+5\), length is already determined once width is chosen.

Introducing Too Few Variables

Two ticket categories may vary independently unless a total or another constraint connects them.

Using a Multi-Letter Word as Ordinary Algebraic Notation

Unformatted adjacent letters are usually multiplied. Use a single letter, function name, or subscripted symbol.

Omitting the Domain

A formula may accept values that the situation does not. Counts, measurements, and indices require appropriate domains.

Changing Units Without Changing the Parameter

A rate of \(60\) miles per hour is not numerically \(60\) miles per minute. Update both the number and the unit.

Practice

  1. Write a complete definition for a variable representing the elapsed time of an eight-hour work shift.
  2. In \(y=mx+b\), identify the usual variables and parameters when the equation describes a family of lines.
  3. A concert sells adult and student tickets, with exactly \(240\) tickets sold. Choose one independent count and express the other count from it.
  4. A car-rental company charges a fixed fee \(F\) plus \(r\) dollars per mile. Define all symbols and write a total-cost model.
  5. A rectangle has perimeter \(80\) centimeters, and its length is twice its width. Choose one variable and construct an equation.
  6. Classify the roles of \(x\), \(y\), and \(r\) in \(x^2+y^2=r^2\) when the equation describes one circle of fixed radius.
  7. Write an appropriate domain for the number \(n\) of machines operating in a factory that owns \(12\) machines.
  8. Explain why “Let \(x\) be cars” is incomplete, and write an improved definition for a production model.
  9. A population model is \(P(t)=P_0(1+r)^t\). Identify the input, output, and parameters, and give their likely meanings.
  10. A speed is \(72\) kilometers per hour. Reparameterize the relationship \(d=72t\) so time \(m\) is measured in minutes and distance remains in kilometers.
Selected Answers
  1. Let \(t\) be the number of hours elapsed since the work shift began, with \(0\le t\le8\).
  2. \(x\) and \(y\) are the primary variables; \(m\) and \(b\) are parameters selecting a particular line.
  3. Let \(a\) be the number of adult tickets sold. Then the number of student tickets is \(s=240-a\), with \(a\in\{0,1,\ldots,240\}\).
  4. Let \(d\ge0\) be distance driven in miles, \(C(d)\) total cost in dollars, \(F\) the fixed fee in dollars, and \(r\) the rate in dollars per mile. Then \(C(d)=F+rd\).
  5. Let \(w>0\) be the width in centimeters. The length is \(2w\), so \(2w+2(2w)=80\).
  6. \(x\) and \(y\) vary over coordinates on the circle; \(r\) is fixed for that circle and can be treated as a parameter of the larger family.
  7. \(n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,12\}\).
  8. The original definition does not say whether \(x\) counts, labels, or measures cars. Improved: Let \(x\) be the number of cars manufactured during one eight-hour shift; \(x\) is a nonnegative integer.
  9. \(t\) is elapsed time, \(P(t)\) is population at time \(t\), \(P_0\) is initial population, and \(r\) is the growth rate per time period. \(P_0\) and \(r\) are parameters.
  10. Because \(t=m/60\), \(d=72(m/60)=1.2m\), where \(m\) is minutes and the new rate is \(1.2\) kilometers per minute.

Summary

  • A symbol's role comes from the model and the question, not from the letter itself.
  • Variables represent permitted variation; unknowns are values to determine.
  • Independent variables serve as inputs, while dependent variables are determined from other quantities.
  • Parameters are held fixed within one case and adjusted across a family of related cases.
  • A coefficient describes a symbol's position as a multiplying factor and can also be a parameter or constant.
  • Complete definitions include meaning, units, domain, and reference point when relevant.
  • Use enough variables to preserve the model's freedom, but express quantities already determined by others.
  • Subscripts distinguish parallel or indexed quantities without implying multiplication.
  • Constraints and assumptions belong with the symbolic model.
  • A notation audit catches ambiguity before it becomes an algebraic error.
