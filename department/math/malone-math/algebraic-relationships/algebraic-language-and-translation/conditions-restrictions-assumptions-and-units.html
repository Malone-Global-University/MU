Conditions, Restrictions, Assumptions, and Units

A mathematical relationship is complete only when we know where it applies, which values are permitted, what has been assumed, and what every quantity measures.

A Formula Is Not the Whole Model

Suppose a tank begins with \(120\) liters of water and drains at \(6\) liters per minute. Its volume can be represented by

\[ V=120-6t. \]

The formula is algebraically valid for every real value of \(t\), but the physical model is not. Time begins at \(t=0\), and the tank becomes empty after \(20\) minutes. The meaningful model is

\[ V=120-6t, \qquad 0\le t\le20, \]

where \(t\) is measured in minutes and \(V\) in liters, assuming the drainage rate remains constant.

The condition, units, and assumption are not decorative notes. They determine what the symbols mean and where the result can be trusted.

\[ \boxed{\text{relationship}+\text{conditions}+\text{units}+\text{assumptions} =\text{usable model}} \]

Four Parts of a Complete Model

PartPurposeExample
ConditionStates a requirement under which a claim or action applies\(x\ge18\) for an age requirement
RestrictionExcludes values that are undefined, impossible, or outside the intended setting\(x\ne3\) when \(x-3\) is a denominator
AssumptionDeclares what the model treats as true, fixed, or negligibleThe travel speed remains constant
UnitIdentifies what a numerical value measures\(r=45\) miles per hour

These categories can overlap. A condition can restrict a domain. A unit can reveal an unstated assumption about the measurement system. The distinctions remain useful because they tell us why each piece of information belongs in the model.

A Reliable Modeling Method

  1. Define the quantities. State what each symbol represents.
  2. Record explicit conditions. Preserve words such as “at least,” “only if,” “between,” and “no more than.”
  3. Derive mathematical restrictions. Inspect denominators, radicals, logarithms, and other operations that may exclude inputs.
  4. Add contextual constraints. Decide whether quantities can be negative, fractional, unlimited, or outside a given interval.
  5. State material assumptions. Identify simplifications that affect whether the model remains accurate.
  6. Attach units. Give every measured quantity and parameter an appropriate unit.
  7. Check dimensional consistency. Confirm that quantities being added, compared, or equated are compatible.
  8. Mark approximation and precision. Distinguish exact definitions from measured or rounded values.
  9. Verify the result in the original model. Algebraic manipulation does not automatically preserve contextual validity.

Conditions

A condition states what must be true for a statement, rule, choice, or conclusion to apply.

Natural-Language ConditionSymbolic Form
A person must be at least eighteen years old\(a\ge18\)
The load may not exceed \(900\) kilograms\(L\le900\)
The temperature must remain between \(2^\circ\text{C}\) and \(8^\circ\text{C}\), inclusive\(2\le T\le8\)
The number must be positive\(x>0\)
The count must be even\(n=2k\) for some integer \(k\)
The two quantities must differ\(x\ne y\)

Boundary words matter:

  • at least includes the lower boundary,
  • greater than excludes the lower boundary,
  • at most includes the upper boundary, and
  • less than excludes the upper boundary.

A condition may be necessary for a result without being sufficient by itself. For example, \(x\ne3\) is necessary for \(\frac{1}{x-3}\) to be defined, but it does not specify the expression's value.

Restrictions

A restriction removes values that cannot be used in the stated mathematical or practical setting.

Stated Restrictions

A problem may provide a restriction directly:

Use only whole numbers from \(1\) through \(12\).

\[ n\in\{1,2,\ldots,12\}. \]

Contextual Restrictions

If \(n\) counts employees, then

\[ n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}. \]

If \(t\) is elapsed time during a ten-minute test, then

\[ 0\le t\le10. \]

Operational Restrictions

The symbolic operations themselves may exclude values. For example,

\[ \frac{x+1}{x-4} \]

requires

\[ x\ne4. \]

A complete solution keeps the original restriction visible even when later simplification makes it less obvious.

Restrictions Derived from Operations

Different operations impose different requirements. The following table assumes real-number arithmetic unless another number system is stated.

FormRequired ConditionReason
\(\frac{A}{B}\)\(B\ne0\)Division by zero is undefined
\(\sqrt{A}\)\(A\ge0\)An even root of a negative real number is not real
\(\sqrt[3]{A}\)No sign restriction on real \(A\)Odd roots of negative real numbers are real
\(\log_b A\)\(A>0,\ b>0,\ b\ne1\)A real logarithm needs a positive argument and valid base
\(A^0\)\(A\ne0\)\(0^0\) is not assigned the ordinary exponent-law value \(1\)

Restrictions can combine. For

\[ f(x)=\frac{\sqrt{x-2}}{x-5}, \]

the square root requires

\[ x-2\ge0, \]

while the denominator requires

\[ x-5\ne0. \]

Therefore, the real domain is

\[ \boxed{x\ge2,\qquad x\ne5} \]

or, in interval notation,

\[ [2,5)\cup(5,\infty). \]

Assumptions

An assumption is something the model accepts as true for the purpose of analysis. Assumptions make complicated situations manageable, but they also define the model's limits.

ModelPossible AssumptionWhat Could Break It
\(d=rt\)The rate \(r\) remains constantStops, acceleration, traffic, or route changes
\(C=F+rh\)The hourly rate does not changeOvertime, minimum billing, taxes, or discounts
\(A=lw\)The object is a rectangle and dimensions are measured consistentlyCurved or irregular boundaries
Population growth ruleGrowth conditions remain similarResource limits, migration, policy, or environmental change
Measured trendObserved data are representativeSampling bias or a change outside the observed period

Not every obvious fact needs to be repeated. State assumptions that materially affect interpretation, calculation, or transfer of the result.

A good assumption is:

  • clear enough to evaluate,
  • appropriate for the model's purpose,
  • limited to the intended context, and
  • revisable when better information becomes available.

Units and Dimensions

A unit tells us how a quantity is measured. A dimension identifies the general kind of quantity, such as length, time, mass, area, or volume.

In the relationship

\[ d=rt, \]

suppose

\[ [r]=\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}} \qquad\text{and}\qquad [t]=\text{hours}. \]

Then

\[ [d] =\left(\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}}\right)(\text{hours}) =\text{miles}. \]

The units confirm that multiplication produces a distance.

Add Only Compatible Quantities

In

\[ C=F+rh, \]

both \(F\) and \(rh\) must be measured in the same cost unit before they can be added.

An expression such as

\[ 5\text{ meters}+3\text{ seconds} \]

does not produce a meaningful ordinary sum because length and time are different dimensions.

Equations Require Dimensional Agreement

If one side of an equation has units of area and the other has units of length, the equation cannot represent equality between those physical quantities. Dimensional consistency does not prove a formula is correct, but dimensional inconsistency proves that something is wrong.

Unit Conversion

A conversion factor is a ratio equal to \(1\). Multiplying by it changes the unit label without changing the measured quantity.

Convert \(72\) kilometers per hour to kilometers per minute:

\[ 72\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \left(\frac{1\text{ h}}{60\text{ min}}\right) =1.2\frac{\text{km}}{\text{min}}. \]

The hour units cancel, leaving kilometers per minute.

For squared and cubed units, the entire conversion factor must be raised to the corresponding power. Since

\[ 1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}, \]

we have

\[ 1\text{ m}^2 =(100\text{ cm})^2 =10{,}000\text{ cm}^2, \]

not \(100\text{ cm}^2\).

Convert quantities before adding or comparing them:

\[ 2\text{ m}+35\text{ cm} =200\text{ cm}+35\text{ cm} =235\text{ cm}. \]

Exact, Approximate, and Measured Values

The symbol connecting quantities should reflect the available precision.

TypeExampleInterpretation
Exact definition\(1\text{ ft}=12\text{ in}\)The conversion is defined exactly
Exact mathematical constant\(\pi\)The symbol denotes an exact value
Rounded approximation\(\pi\approx3.14\)The decimal represents a nearby value
Measured value\(l=8.2\text{ cm}\)The reported value is limited by measurement precision
Estimated model parameter\(r\approx0.037\)The parameter was inferred or approximated

Using an equal sign for a rounded value can overstate precision:

\[ \pi\ne3.14, \qquad \pi\approx3.14. \]

Calculated results should not imply more certainty than the measurements, estimates, and assumptions supporting them.

Domains and Contextual Constraints

The algebraic domain and the contextual domain are not always the same.

QuantityPossible Mathematical DomainPossible Contextual Domain
Number of employees \(n\)Real numbers\(n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,N\}\)
Elapsed time \(t\)\(t\in\mathbb{R}\)\(0\le t\le T\)
Probability \(p\)\(p\in\mathbb{R}\)\(0\le p\le1\)
Percentage rate \(r\)\(r\in\mathbb{R}\)Depends on whether decreases beyond \(100\%\) or large increases are meaningful
Rectangle width \(w\)\(w\in\mathbb{R}\)\(w>0\), often with an upper bound

A formula may remain algebraically defined beyond the range where it is a useful model. Extrapolating outside the intended domain requires additional justification.

Presenting a Model Clearly

A compact model can be presented in four parts.

1. Define the Quantities

Let \(t\) be elapsed time in hours and \(D(t)\) be distance traveled in miles.

2. State the Relationship

\[ D(t)=55t. \]

3. Give the Domain and Conditions

\[ 0\le t\le4. \]

4. State Material Assumptions

Assume the vehicle travels at a constant \(55\) miles per hour without stops during the four-hour interval.

The same information may also be written in a “where” clause:

\[ D=55t, \qquad 0\le t\le4, \]

where \(D\) is distance in miles and \(t\) is elapsed time in hours, assuming a constant speed.

Worked Example: A Rental Model

A truck-rental company charges \(\$45\) plus \(\$0.80\) per mile. A customer may drive at most \(300\) miles, and fuel costs are not included.

Define the Quantities

  • Let \(m\) be the number of miles driven.
  • Let \(C(m)\) be the rental charge in dollars before fuel costs.

Write the Relationship

\[ C(m)=45+0.80m. \]

State the Domain

\[ 0\le m\le300. \]

If the company records mileage only in whole miles, use integer values. If it records tenths of a mile, a corresponding decimal domain is appropriate.

State the Assumptions

  • The fixed and per-mile charges do not change during the rental.
  • No taxes, damage fees, late fees, or discounts are included.
  • Fuel is paid separately and is not represented by \(C(m)\).

Check the Units

\[ \text{dollars} =\text{dollars} +\left(\frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{mile}}\right)(\text{miles}). \]

Worked Example: An Algebraic Domain

Determine the real-number restrictions for

\[ g(x)=\sqrt{\frac{x+2}{x-1}}. \]

The radicand must be nonnegative, and the denominator must not be zero:

\[ \frac{x+2}{x-1}\ge0, \qquad x\ne1. \]

The critical values are \(x=-2\), where the numerator is zero, and \(x=1\), where the denominator is zero. Test the intervals they create:

IntervalSign of RadicandPermitted?
\((-\infty,-2)\)PositiveYes
\(x=-2\)ZeroYes
\((-2,1)\)NegativeNo
\(x=1\)UndefinedNo
\((1,\infty)\)PositiveYes

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{Dom}(g)=(-\infty,-2]\cup(1,\infty)}. \]

Notice that the square-root condition and denominator restriction had to be considered together.

Validity Check

After obtaining a result, check it against every layer of the original model.

CheckQuestion
SymbolicDoes the result satisfy the original equation or inequality?
DomainIs the value permitted by every operational restriction?
ContextCan the quantity reasonably take that value?
UnitsDoes the result have the required unit and dimension?
BoundaryAre inclusive and exclusive endpoints handled correctly?
AssumptionDoes the case remain within the model's stated assumptions?
PrecisionIs the reported accuracy supported by the input data?

Algebra can produce a number that is invalid in context. A solution of \(-3\) may satisfy an intermediate equation but fail if it represents a length, count, or elapsed time. The final check returns the answer to the problem that gave it meaning.

Common Modeling Errors

Writing Only the Formula

A relationship without definitions, conditions, or units may be impossible to interpret correctly.

Forgetting a Zero Denominator

A factor that cancels during simplification may still identify a value excluded from the original expression.

Applying an Even-Root Restriction to an Odd Root

Real square roots require nonnegative radicands; real cube roots accept negative radicands.

Confusing an Assumption with a Fact

“The rate is constant” may be a useful modeling assumption, but it is not automatically true in every situation.

Mixing Incompatible Units

Convert quantities to compatible units before adding, comparing, or substituting them.

Converting Linear Units but Not Squared or Cubed Units

Area and volume conversions require squaring or cubing the entire linear conversion factor.

Ignoring Integer Requirements

A count of objects usually cannot be fractional even when an algebraic formula accepts real inputs.

Using Equality for an Approximation

Use \(\approx\) when a decimal is rounded or a value is estimated.

Accepting Every Algebraic Candidate

Substitution, squaring, clearing denominators, and other transformations can create or conceal invalid values. Check candidates in the original relationship.

Practice

  1. State the real-number restriction for \(\frac{7}{x+4}\).
  2. State the real-number domain of \(\sqrt{3x-6}\).
  3. State the real-number domain of \(\frac{\sqrt{x+1}}{x-2}\).
  4. A theater has \(450\) seats. Define a variable for tickets sold and state an appropriate domain.
  5. A machine produces \(24\) parts per hour for \(h\) hours. Write a production model with units and state one material assumption.
  6. Convert \(90\) kilometers per hour to kilometers per minute.
  7. Convert \(2.5\) square meters to square centimeters.
  8. Explain what is wrong with \(3\text{ meters}+4\text{ seconds}=7\).
  9. A pool contains \(18{,}000\) liters and drains at \(600\) liters per minute. Write the model and its physically meaningful time domain.
  10. A measurement is reported as \(12.4\) centimeters to the nearest tenth. Should it ordinarily be treated as exact? Explain.
Selected Answers
  1. \(x\ne-4\).
  2. \(3x-6\ge0\), so \(x\ge2\).
  3. \(x\ge-1\) and \(x\ne2\), or \([-1,2)\cup(2,\infty)\).
  4. Let \(n\) be the number of tickets sold. Then \(n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,450\}\).
  5. \(P=24h\), where \(P\) is parts and \(h\) is hours. Assume the production rate remains constant and uninterrupted.
  6. \(90/60=1.5\) kilometers per minute.
  7. \(2.5(100)^2=25{,}000\) square centimeters.
  8. Length and time are different dimensions and cannot be combined into an ordinary sum.
  9. \(V=18{,}000-600t\), where \(V\) is liters, \(t\) is minutes, and \(0\le t\le30\), assuming a constant drainage rate.
  10. No. It is a measured value with limited precision; the actual length is represented only to the stated measurement resolution.

Summary

  • Conditions state when a claim, rule, or action applies.
  • Restrictions exclude undefined, impossible, or contextually invalid values.
  • Operational restrictions can arise from denominators, radicals, logarithms, and other structures.
  • Assumptions simplify reality and define the limits of a model's reliability.
  • Units identify what values measure and provide a dimensional check on operations and equations.
  • Unit conversions multiply by ratios equal to \(1\); squared and cubed units require powered conversion factors.
  • Exact, approximate, and measured values should be labeled according to their actual precision.
  • The algebraic domain may be broader than the domain permitted by the situation.
  • Every final result should be checked against the original relationship, domain, context, units, assumptions, and precision.
\[ \boxed{\text{define the limits before trusting the result}} \]
