Terms, Factors, Bases, and Exponents

Reading an algebraic expression means seeing how its parts are assembled, not merely recognizing the symbols it contains.

The Internal Anatomy of an Expression

Consider the expression

\[ 4x^3-7x+9. \]

At the outermost level, it is a sum of three signed terms:

\[ 4x^3+(-7x)+9. \]

Within the first term, \(4\) and \(x^3\) are factors. Within the power \(x^3\), \(x\) is the base and \(3\) is the exponent.

These relationships form a useful hierarchy:

\[ \boxed{\text{expression}\supset\text{terms}\supset\text{factors}\supset\text{bases and exponents}} \]

The same written symbol can occupy different levels depending on grouping. Learning to see those levels makes simplifying, factoring, solving, and interpreting formulas much easier.

Terms

A term is a part of an expression separated from other parts by addition at the outermost level.

Because subtraction can be rewritten as addition of an opposite,

\[ 6x^2-3x+8 \]

may be understood as

\[ 6x^2+(-3x)+8. \]

Its terms are:

\[ 6x^2,\qquad -3x,\qquad 8. \]

Only addition or subtraction at the current outer level separates terms. The expression

\[ 5(x+2) \]

is one product at the outermost level. The sum \(x+2\) is grouped inside a factor, so \(5x\) and \(10\) do not become visible terms until distribution is performed:

\[ 5(x+2)=5x+10. \]

Signs and Term Boundaries

The sign immediately preceding a term belongs to that term when the expression is read as a sum.

In

\[ x^2-4x-7, \]

the three signed terms are

\[ x^2,\qquad -4x,\qquad -7. \]

This viewpoint prevents a common mistake: naming \(4x\) as the second term while forgetting its negative sign.

Signs can perform different jobs:

NotationRole of the Sign
\(a-b\)Binary subtraction: subtract \(b\) from \(a\)
\(-b\)Unary negation: take the opposite of \(b\)
\(a+(-b)\)Add the negative term \(-b\)

The notation may be compact, but the structural role of the sign should remain clear.

Factors

A factor is an object being multiplied within a product.

In the term

\[ -6x^2y, \]

the factors may be displayed as

\[ (-1)\cdot6\cdot x^2\cdot y. \]

We usually combine the numerical factors and write the numerical coefficient \(-6\).

A factor can be a numeral, variable, power, expression, function value, or other mathematical object:

\[ 3x, \qquad 2(x+5), \qquad (x-1)(x+1), \qquad 4\sin(\theta). \]

Grouping can turn an entire sum into one factor. In

\[ 7(a+b), \]

the factors are \(7\) and \(a+b\).

Coefficients and Variable Factors

A coefficient is a factor multiplying another object. In a conventional algebraic term, the numerical coefficient is written first.

TermNumerical CoefficientVariable Part
\(8x^3\)\(8\)\(x^3\)
\(-5ab^2\)\(-5\)\(ab^2\)
\(x\)\(1\)\(x\)
\(-y^4\)\(-1\)\(y^4\)
\(\frac23m^2n\)\(\frac23\)\(m^2n\)

When one variable is treated as the main variable, other symbolic factors may serve as coefficients. If we view

\[ ay^2+by+c \]

as an expression in \(y\), then \(a\) and \(b\) are coefficients of powers of \(y\).

Bases and Exponents

In a power

\[ b^n, \]

\(b\) is the base and \(n\) is the exponent.

For a positive whole-number exponent, exponentiation represents repeated multiplication:

\[ b^4=b\cdot b\cdot b\cdot b. \]

The exponent counts how many copies of the base appear as factors. It is not generally a number that multiplies the base:

\[ b^4\ne4b. \]

Examples:

PowerBaseExponentExpanded Product
\(5^3\)\(5\)\(3\)\(5\cdot5\cdot5\)
\(x^4\)\(x\)\(4\)\(x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x\)
\((a+b)^2\)\(a+b\)\(2\)\((a+b)(a+b)\)
\((-3)^2\)\(-3\)\(2\)\((-3)(-3)\)

The Scope of an Exponent

An exponent applies to its complete base. Grouping determines what that base is.

Compare

\[ -3^2 \]

with

\[ (-3)^2. \]

In \(-3^2\), the exponent applies only to \(3\), and the negative sign remains outside:

\[ -3^2=-(3^2)=-9. \]

In \((-3)^2\), the parentheses make \(-3\) the complete base:

\[ (-3)^2=(-3)(-3)=9. \]

The same principle applies to variables:

\[ -x^2=-(x^2), \]

while

\[ (-x)^2=x^2. \]

Now compare

\[ 3x^2 \]

and

\[ (3x)^2. \]

Only \(x\) is squared in the first expression:

\[ 3x^2=3\cdot x^2. \]

The entire product is squared in the second:

\[ (3x)^2=9x^2. \]

Zero, One, Negative, and Rational Exponents

Exponent One

\[ a^1=a. \]

The exponent \(1\) is usually omitted.

Exponent Zero

For \(a\ne0\),

\[ a^0=1. \]

The condition matters because \(0^0\) is not assigned this value by the ordinary zero-exponent law.

Negative Exponents

For \(a\ne0\),

\[ a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}. \]

A negative exponent indicates a reciprocal power; it does not make the value automatically negative.

Rational Exponents

Under appropriate real-number conditions,

\[ a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a} \]

and

\[ a^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}. \]

These notations connect exponentiation and radicalization. Domain restrictions become important when even roots or zero denominators are involved.

Compound Bases and Factors

A complete expression can serve as a base:

\[ (x+4)^3. \]

The base is \(x+4\), so

\[ (x+4)^3=(x+4)(x+4)(x+4). \]

A fraction can also be a base:

\[ \left(\frac ab\right)^2=\frac{a^2}{b^2}, \qquad b\ne0. \]

A power can itself be a factor:

\[ 5(x-2)^4. \]

At the outer level, the factors are \(5\) and \((x-2)^4\). Within the power, the base is \(x-2\) and the exponent is \(4\).

This layered reading is essential:

\[ \boxed{\text{identify the outer operation first, then examine each inner object}} \]

Like Terms and Unlike Terms

Like terms have the same variable factors raised to the same exponents. Their numerical coefficients may differ.

TermsLike?Reason
\(3x\) and \(-8x\)YesBoth have variable part \(x\)
\(5x^2\) and \(\frac12x^2\)YesBoth have variable part \(x^2\)
\(2xy\) and \(-7xy\)YesBoth have variable part \(xy\)
\(x\) and \(x^2\)NoThe exponents differ
\(x^2y\) and \(xy^2\)NoThe exponent structures differ
\(4a\) and \(4b\)NoThe variable factors differ

Like terms can be combined by adding their coefficients:

\[ 3x^2+5x^2=8x^2. \]

Unlike terms cannot be combined by addition without changing the expression's meaning:

\[ x+x^2\ne2x^3. \]

Degree as Exponent Structure

For a monomial in one variable, the degree is the exponent of that variable.

TermDegree
\(7x^5\)\(5\)
\(-3x\)\(1\)
\(9\)\(0\)

For a monomial containing several variables, the total degree is the sum of the exponents:

\[ 4x^2y^3 \]

has total degree

\[ 2+3=5. \]

The degree of a nonzero polynomial is the greatest degree among its terms after like terms have been combined.

This is an early preview of how exponent structure helps classify polynomial relationships.

Worked Example: Reading a Polynomial

Analyze

\[ -4x^3+6x^2-2x+9. \]

Identify the Terms

\[ -4x^3,\qquad 6x^2,\qquad -2x,\qquad 9. \]

Identify Coefficients and Variable Parts

TermCoefficientVariable Part
\(-4x^3\)\(-4\)\(x^3\)
\(6x^2\)\(6\)\(x^2\)
\(-2x\)\(-2\)\(x\)
\(9\)\(9\)None

Identify Bases and Exponents

In \(x^3\), the base is \(x\) and the exponent is \(3\). In \(x^2\), the base is \(x\) and the exponent is \(2\). The term \(-2x\) contains the understood power \(x^1\).

Identify Degree

The highest exponent is \(3\), so the polynomial has degree \(3\). Its leading coefficient is \(-4\).

Worked Example: A Growth Formula

An investment of \(P\) dollars grows by a factor of \(1+r\) each period. After \(n\) periods, its value is modeled by

\[ A=P(1+r)^n. \]

Identify the Outer Factors

The expression is a product of

\[ P \]

and

\[ (1+r)^n. \]

Identify the Power

The base is the complete growth factor \(1+r\), and the exponent is the number of periods \(n\).

Interpret Repeated Multiplication

When \(n=3\),

\[ A=P(1+r)^3=P(1+r)(1+r)(1+r). \]

Each period contributes another factor of \(1+r\). The exponent records repeated proportional growth rather than repeated addition.

Common Structural Errors

Splitting Terms Inside Parentheses

In \(4(x+3)\), the grouped sum is one factor at the outer level.

Dropping a Term's Sign

The second term of \(x^2-5x+2\) is \(-5x\), not merely \(5x\).

Calling Added Parts Factors

In \(x+7\), \(x\) and \(7\) are terms. They are not factors of the complete expression unless the expression is first factored into a product.

Multiplying the Base by the Exponent

\[ x^3\ne3x. \]

Ignoring Exponent Scope

\[ -2^2=-4, \qquad (-2)^2=4. \]

Combining Unlike Terms

\[ 3x+4x^2 \]

cannot be simplified to \(7x^3\).

Assuming a Negative Exponent Makes a Negative Value

\[ 2^{-3}=\frac{1}{8}, \]

which is positive.

Practice

  1. List the signed terms of \(5x^2-3x-8\).
  2. Identify the factors in \(-7a^2b\).
  3. State the numerical coefficient and variable part of \(\frac34x^3y\).
  4. Identify the base and exponent in \((m+n)^5\).
  5. Evaluate \(-4^2\) and \((-4)^2\), explaining the difference.
  6. Rewrite \(x^{-4}\) without a negative exponent, stating the necessary restriction.
  7. Rewrite \(a^{3/2}\) using radical notation under appropriate real-number conditions.
  8. Determine which pairs are like terms: \(3x^2y\), \(-5xy^2\), \(8x^2y\), and \(x^2\).
  9. Find the total degree of \(-6a^3b^2\).
  10. Describe the outer terms and inner factors of \(2(x-1)^3+5\).
Selected Answers
  1. \(5x^2\), \(-3x\), and \(-8\).
  2. For example, \(-1\), \(7\), \(a^2\), and \(b\); the combined numerical coefficient is \(-7\).
  3. Coefficient: \(\frac34\); variable part: \(x^3y\).
  4. Base: \(m+n\); exponent: \(5\).
  5. \(-4^2=-16\), while \((-4)^2=16\); parentheses determine whether the negative sign belongs to the base.
  6. \(\frac{1}{x^4}\), with \(x\ne0\).
  7. \(\sqrt{a^3}\), equivalently \((\sqrt a)^3\) when the real-number conditions are satisfied.
  8. \(3x^2y\) and \(8x^2y\) are like terms.
  9. \(3+2=5\).
  10. The outer terms are \(2(x-1)^3\) and \(5\); the first term has factors \(2\) and \((x-1)^3\).

Summary

  • Terms are separated by addition or subtraction at the current outer level.
  • A term's sign belongs with the term when an expression is read as a sum.
  • Factors are objects multiplied within a product.
  • A coefficient is a factor multiplying another mathematical object.
  • In a power, the exponent acts on its complete base.
  • Grouping determines whether a sign, coefficient, product, sum, or fraction belongs to the base.
  • Zero, negative, and rational exponents extend exponent notation under stated conditions.
  • Like terms share the same variable factors with the same exponents.
  • Degree records exponent structure and helps classify monomials and polynomials.
  • Reading from the outer operation inward reveals how an expression is assembled.
