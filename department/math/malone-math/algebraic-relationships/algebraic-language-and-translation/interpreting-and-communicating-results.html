Interpreting and Communicating Results

A calculation is not finished when a number appears. The result must be connected back to the original question, expressed with the right units and precision, and communicated clearly enough that another person can understand what was found and what it means.

An Answer Is Not Yet a Conclusion

Suppose a calculation gives:

\[ x=7.4. \]

That alone may be mathematically correct but practically incomplete.

What is \(x\)?

If \(x\) is the width of a room, the useful conclusion might be:

\[ \boxed{\text{The room is approximately }7.4\text{ m wide.}} \]

If \(x\) is the number of buses required, then \(7.4\) cannot be the final physical answer at all. The context may require:

\[ \boxed{8\text{ buses}}. \]

The calculation produces a mathematical result. Interpretation turns that result into an answer to the actual problem.

\[ \boxed{ \text{calculated result} \to \text{interpretation} \to \text{communicated conclusion} } \]

Result versus Answer

A result is what the mathematical process produces. An answer is that result interpreted in the context of the question.

Example

A rate calculation gives:

\[ \frac{180}{3}=60. \]

The result is \(60\).

The answer is:

\[ \boxed{60\text{ miles per hour}}. \]

Without the quantity name and unit, the result is incomplete.

Return to the Original Question

The final step should answer the question that was actually asked.

If the problem asks:

“How much money remains?”

then reporting the amount spent does not finish the problem, even if that intermediate calculation was correct.

A useful habit is to reread the final question before writing the conclusion.

Name the Quantity

Do not leave a variable detached from its meaning.

Instead of:

\[ x=12, \]

write:

\[ \boxed{\text{The ladder reaches }12\text{ ft up the wall.}} \]

The interpretation tells the reader what the number represents.

Report Units

Units should appear whenever the quantity has units.

For example:

\[ A=35 \]

is incomplete if the problem asks for the area of a rectangle measured in centimeters.

The meaningful answer is:

\[ \boxed{A=35\text{ cm}^2}. \]

Units are part of the conclusion, not a decorative label added afterward.

Use Appropriate Precision

A calculator may display many digits, but the problem may not justify them.

Suppose a measured radius is known only to the nearest tenth:

\[ r=4.2\text{ cm}. \]

If a calculation produces:

\[ A=55.4176944093\text{ cm}^2, \]

reporting every displayed digit suggests false precision.

A more appropriate conclusion might be:

\[ \boxed{A\approx55.4\text{ cm}^2}. \]

Distinguish Exact and Approximate Results

Use equality for exact relationships and approximation symbols when values have been rounded.

Exact:

\[ \sqrt2 \]

Approximate:

\[ \sqrt2\approx1.414. \]

It is misleading to write:

\[ \sqrt2=1.414 \]

because the decimal has been rounded.

Interpret Discrete Results

Counts often require contextual interpretation.

Example

A van holds \(8\) passengers. A group has \(34\) people.

\[ \frac{34}{8}=4.25. \]

The mathematical quotient is \(4.25\), but the number of vans must be a whole number.

Four vans carry only \(32\) people, so:

\[ \boxed{5\text{ vans are required}}. \]

This is not ordinary rounding to the nearest integer. The context determines the direction.

Interpret Sign and Direction

A negative result often carries meaning.

For example, if displacement is:

\[ -12\text{ m}, \]

the negative sign may indicate movement in the chosen negative direction.

If a financial change is:

\[ -\$250, \]

it may represent a loss or decrease.

Do not automatically remove a negative sign because the magnitude alone looks more familiar.

Respect Domain and Context

A mathematically valid candidate can still be inappropriate for the problem context.

If a quadratic model produces:

\[ t=-2, \qquad t=5, \]

and \(t\) represents elapsed time after an experiment begins, then usually:

\[ \boxed{t=5} \]

is the relevant answer.

The negative solution may still have algebraic meaning, but not the requested contextual meaning.

From Mathematical Result to Final Conclusion

State Important Assumptions

A conclusion may depend on assumptions that should be made explicit.

Example

Using:

\[ d=rt \]

to predict travel distance assumes a constant rate \(r\) over the time interval.

A careful conclusion might say:

“At a constant speed of \(60\text{ mph}\), the vehicle travels \(180\text{ miles}\) in \(3\text{ hours}\).”

The phrase “at a constant speed” communicates the model assumption.

Communicate Limitations

Applied results often come with uncertainty or limited scope.

A useful conclusion may need to say:

  • the result is approximate,
  • the data were measured,
  • the model applies only over a certain range,
  • the prediction assumes current conditions continue,
  • the estimate has a stated tolerance or error bound.

Communicating a limitation does not weaken good mathematics. It identifies the boundary of what the mathematics justifies.

Write for the Audience

The same mathematical result may be communicated differently to different readers.

Technical Audience

\[ r=2.37\pm0.04\text{ cm}. \]

General Audience

“The radius is about \(2.4\text{ cm}\), with only a few hundredths of a centimeter of uncertainty.”

The mathematical content should remain accurate even when the wording changes.

Choose a Useful Final Representation

The best final form depends on what the reader needs.

  • Use an exact expression when symbolic structure matters.
  • Use a decimal when practical magnitude matters.
  • Use a percentage when relative comparison matters.
  • Use a graph when trend or shape matters.
  • Use a table when several cases need comparison.

Example

The exact probability:

\[ \frac38 \]

may be more intuitive to some readers as:

\[ 0.375 \]

or:

\[ 37.5\%. \]

All three represent the same value, but they serve different communication purposes.

Comparative and Relative Statements

Sometimes the result matters because of how it compares with another value.

Suppose a quantity increases from \(80\) to \(100\).

Absolute increase:

\[ 100-80=20. \]

Relative increase:

\[ \frac{20}{80}=0.25. \]

Percentage increase:

\[ \boxed{25\%}. \]

Saying “the quantity increased by \(20\)” and “the quantity increased by \(25\%\)” communicates different but related information.

Communicate Verification Status

When useful, say how the result was checked.

For example:

“The solution is \(x=4\), and substitution into the original equation confirms the result.”

Or:

“Both the algebraic solution and the graph locate the intersection at \((2,3)\).”

This is especially useful in technical work, reports, or high-stakes calculations where the reliability of the answer matters.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Distance

A vehicle travels \(55\text{ mph}\) for \(3\text{ h}\).

\[ d=rt=(55)(3)=165. \]

Weak ending:

\[ 165. \]

Better conclusion:

\[ \boxed{\text{The vehicle travels }165\text{ miles.}} \]

Example 2: Approximate Geometry

A circle has radius \(4\text{ cm}\).

\[ A=\pi(4)^2=16\pi\text{ cm}^2. \]

Exact:

\[ \boxed{16\pi\text{ cm}^2}. \]

Approximate:

\[ \boxed{50.3\text{ cm}^2} \]

to the nearest tenth.

Example 3: Discrete Interpretation

A classroom holds \(24\) students. How many classrooms are needed for \(101\) students?

\[ \frac{101}{24}\approx4.21. \]

Since partial classrooms do not satisfy the capacity requirement:

\[ \boxed{5\text{ classrooms are required}}. \]

Example 4: Contextual Root

A height model produces:

\[ t=-1.2, \qquad t=4.8. \]

If \(t\) is elapsed time after launch, the relevant conclusion is:

\[ \boxed{\text{The object reaches the stated height at }t=4.8\text{ s after launch.}} \]

Example 5: Percentage Interpretation

A price rises from \$50 to \$60.

\[ \frac{60-50}{50} = 0.20. \]

Conclusion:

\[ \boxed{\text{The price increased by }20\%.} \]

Example 6: Model Assumption

A worker completes \(18\) units per hour for \(5\) hours.

\[ 18(5)=90. \]

A careful conclusion is:

\[ \boxed{\text{At a constant rate of }18\text{ units/hour, the worker completes }90\text{ units in }5\text{ hours.}} \]

Common Errors

1. Reporting Only a Bare Number

A result needs meaning and units when the problem has context.

2. Answering the Wrong Question

An intermediate result may be correct but not be the requested quantity.

3. Using False Precision

Calculator digits do not create measurement accuracy.

4. Writing Approximate Values as Exact Equalities

Use \(\approx\) when rounding has occurred.

5. Rounding Discrete Quantities Mechanically

Context determines whether a count should round up, down, or remain exact.

6. Removing a Negative Sign without Interpretation

The sign may encode direction, change, or orientation.

7. Hiding Important Assumptions

A model-based conclusion should identify assumptions that materially affect the result.

8. Communicating More Detail Than the Audience Needs

Precision and rigor should remain, but unnecessary detail can hide the central conclusion.

Practice

Units

A calculation gives \(v=45\), where \(v\) is speed measured in kilometers per hour. Write the complete answer.

Check Answer \[ \boxed{v=45\text{ km/h}}. \]

Approximation

Which symbol is appropriate if:

\[ \pi \]

is reported as \(3.14\)?

Check Answer \[ \boxed{\pi\approx3.14}. \]

Discrete Result

A calculation shows that \(3.2\) buses would be needed to carry all passengers. What should the final answer usually be?

Check Answer \[ \boxed{4\text{ buses}} \]

if every passenger must be transported.

Context

A quadratic time model gives \(t=-3\) and \(t=6\). If the question asks for time after the experiment begins, which result is usually relevant?

Check Answer \[ \boxed{t=6}. \]

Assumption

Why might the phrase “assuming a constant rate” matter in a final conclusion?

Check Answer

Because the computed result depends on that model assumption. If the rate changes, the conclusion may no longer apply.

The Deeper Idea

Mathematics begins by translating a situation into mathematical objects. Interpretation reverses that movement.

\[ \text{real or abstract situation} \to \text{mathematical model} \to \text{solution} \to \text{meaningful conclusion}. \]

So communication is not separate from problem solving. It completes the cycle.

\[ \boxed{ \text{good mathematics} = \text{correct reasoning} + \text{correct interpretation} + \text{clear communication} } \]

The strongest final answer tells the reader:

  • what was found,
  • what the value means,
  • what units it uses,
  • whether it is exact or approximate,
  • what assumptions or restrictions matter,
  • why the result is trustworthy.

Summary

  • A mathematical result is not automatically a complete answer.
  • Return to the original question before writing the final conclusion.
  • Name the quantity being reported.
  • Include units whenever the quantity has units.
  • Use precision justified by the data and the problem.
  • Distinguish exact values from rounded approximations.
  • Interpret discrete results according to the physical or logical context.
  • Preserve meaningful signs and directions.
  • Reject or reinterpret values that fall outside the relevant domain or context.
  • State assumptions that materially affect the conclusion.
  • Communicate uncertainty, model limits, or tolerances when relevant.
  • Adapt wording and representation to the audience without changing the mathematics.
  • Use absolute, relative, or percentage statements according to what the comparison requires.
  • When useful, report how the result was verified.

The final line of a solution should reconnect the mathematics to the problem that started it. A strong conclusion does more than announce a number: it tells the reader what was found, what it means, how precisely it is known, and under what conditions it should be trusted.
