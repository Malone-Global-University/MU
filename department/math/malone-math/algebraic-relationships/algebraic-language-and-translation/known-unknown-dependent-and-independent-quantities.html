Known, Unknown, Dependent, and Independent Quantities

Before solving a problem, separate what information is available from how the quantities are related.

Two Different Questions

Suppose a taxi charges \(\$4\) to begin a trip and \(\$3\) per mile. The total cost is modeled by

\[ C=4+3m. \]

If the trip is \(6\) miles long and we want the cost, then:

  • \(m=6\) is known,
  • \(C\) is unknown,
  • \(m\) is the independent quantity in the model, and
  • \(C\) is the dependent quantity because its value is determined from \(m\).

Now suppose the total cost is known to be \(\$22\), and we want the number of miles. Then \(C=22\) is known and \(m\) is unknown—but the model can still describe \(C\) as dependent on \(m\).

This reveals two separate classification axes:

\[ \boxed{\text{known or unknown concerns available information}} \] \[ \boxed{\text{independent or dependent concerns the structure of variation}} \]

Two Independent Classification Axes

AxisQuestionPossible Roles
Information statusDo we currently have the value or relationship?Known or unknown
Dependency structureWhich quantity is selected or controlled, and which is determined in response?Independent or dependent

Because these axes answer different questions, they can combine in several ways.

CombinationExample
Known independent quantityA given time \(t=4\) hours used to calculate distance
Unknown independent quantityThe number of hours required to reach a known distance
Known dependent quantityA measured total cost used to determine how many units were purchased
Unknown dependent quantityThe total cost predicted from a selected number of units

There is no contradiction in calling a dependent quantity known or an independent quantity unknown. The labels describe different features.

Known Quantities

A known quantity is information available for use in the current problem.

A known quantity may be:

  • a given numerical value,
  • a measured value,
  • a stated constant or rate,
  • a previously calculated result,
  • a symbolic quantity whose value need not be numerical,
  • a condition, formula, or relationship known to hold.

In the problem

\[ A=lw, \qquad A=48, \qquad l=8, \]

the area \(A\), length \(l\), and formula \(A=lw\) are known information.

Known does not always mean exact. A measured length such as

\[ l\approx8.2\text{ cm} \]

is known only to the stated precision. Its uncertainty should be preserved when interpreting the result.

Unknown Quantities

An unknown quantity is information sought, missing, or not yet determined in the current problem.

An unknown may be:

  • a single numerical value,
  • one of several possible values,
  • an interval or solution set,
  • a parameter in a model,
  • a function or rule,
  • a geometric object, probability, rate, or other mathematical quantity.

In

\[ 3x+5=20, \]

\(x\) is an unknown number.

In

\[ x^2=9, \]

the unknown is not one value but a solution set:

\[ \{-3,3\}. \]

In a data problem, the unknown may be an entire relationship such as the best-fitting line

\[ y=mx+b, \]

where the parameters \(m\) and \(b\) must be estimated.

Independent Quantities

An independent quantity is a quantity selected, controlled, or used as an input within a chosen representation of a relationship.

In

\[ C=12+5n, \]

the number of items \(n\) can be treated as independent. We select a value of \(n\), and the model supplies the corresponding cost.

Independent quantities are often represented:

  • on the horizontal axis of a graph,
  • in the first column of an input-output table,
  • as the input in function notation,
  • as the quantity deliberately varied in an experiment.

These are conventions, not universal laws. A graph can place variables on different axes, and a relationship may be reorganized to use a different input.

Dependent Quantities

A dependent quantity is a quantity whose value is determined or constrained by other quantities in the chosen relationship.

If

\[ A=\pi r^2, \]

then the area \(A\) depends on the selected radius \(r\).

If the radius doubles, the area becomes

\[ A=\pi(2r)^2=4\pi r^2, \]

so the dependent quantity changes according to the structure of the relationship.

A dependent quantity is often represented:

  • on the vertical axis of a graph,
  • in the output column of a table,
  • on the left side of a function rule,
  • as a response or measured outcome.

Again, these placements are conventions. The mathematical dependency must be determined from the relationship and context.

Inputs and Outputs

For a function, the independent and dependent roles are often described as input and output.

Consider

\[ f(x)=2x^2-3. \]

The variable \(x\) is the input. The value \(f(x)\) is the output determined by that input.

Different inputs can sometimes produce the same output. Dependency does not require a one-to-one relationship.

Dependence Is Not Automatically Causation

In algebra, saying that \(y\) depends on \(x\) means that the chosen model or rule determines \(y\) from \(x\). It does not automatically prove that changing \(x\) physically causes \(y\) to change.

For example, a dataset may show a relationship between ice-cream sales and electricity use during the summer. Both may increase with temperature. Writing one as a function of the other can describe an association, but it does not establish direct causation.

Causal conclusions require additional evidence, assumptions, and experimental or scientific reasoning.

\[ \boxed{\text{mathematical dependence describes a rule; causal dependence describes how the world works}} \]

Multiple Variables and Multiple Unknowns

A relationship may involve several independent quantities.

For a rectangular prism,

\[ V=lwh. \]

The volume \(V\) depends on three dimensions: \(l\), \(w\), and \(h\). If all three dimensions are selected as inputs, then \(V\) is the dependent output.

A problem may also contain several unknowns. One equation such as

\[ x+y=10 \]

does not usually determine one unique ordered pair. It describes infinitely many real pairs:

\[ y=10-x. \]

Another independent condition may be needed to determine a unique pair:

\[ x+y=10, \] \[ x-y=2. \]

Together, these equations determine

\[ x=6, \qquad y=4. \]

Before solving, compare the number of unknown quantities with the number and independence of the available conditions.

Parameters, Controls, and Fixed Conditions

A parameter is usually held fixed while the independent variables vary within one selected relationship.

In

\[ y=mx+b, \]

\(m\) and \(b\) can be treated as parameters, while \(x\) is the independent variable and \(y\) the dependent variable.

If the problem asks us to find the line passing through two known points, then \(m\) and \(b\) become unknown parameters. They are unknown in the information sense even though they are parameters in the model structure.

For a particular solution such as

\[ y=3x-2, \]

the parameter values are now known:

\[ m=3, \qquad b=-2. \]

This is another example of the two classification axes working together.

Some relations do not naturally divide into one globally independent and one globally dependent variable. The circle

\[ x^2+y^2=25 \]

does not define a single real value of \(y\) for every allowed \(x\). Solving for \(y\) gives two branches:

\[ y=\pm\sqrt{25-x^2}. \]

The chosen representation affects how dependency is expressed.

Worked Example: Distance, Rate, and Time

A vehicle travels at a constant rate of \(55\) miles per hour for \(3.5\) hours. Find the distance traveled.

Identify the Relationship

\[ d=rt. \]

Organize the Quantities

QuantityInformation StatusModel Role
\(r=55\text{ mi/h}\)KnownFixed rate parameter
\(t=3.5\text{ h}\)KnownIndependent input
\(d\)UnknownDependent output

Calculate

\[ d=(55\text{ mi/h})(3.5\text{ h})=192.5\text{ mi}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{d=192.5\text{ miles}}. \]

Change the Question

If the distance \(192.5\) miles and rate \(55\) miles per hour were given instead, time would become the unknown:

\[ t=\frac dr=\frac{192.5}{55}=3.5\text{ hours}. \]

The information roles changed, but the underlying relationship did not.

Worked Example: Building a Revenue Model

A vendor earns \(\$18\) for each unit sold. Let \(q\) be the number of units and \(R\) the revenue.

Construct the Relationship

\[ R=18q. \]

Identify the Structural Roles

  • \(q\) is the independent quantity.
  • \(R\) is the dependent quantity.
  • \(18\) is a known constant rate in dollars per unit.

First Question: Predict Revenue

If \(q=25\) is known, then \(R\) is unknown:

\[ R=18(25)=450. \]

Second Question: Recover Sales

If \(R=450\) is known, then \(q\) is unknown:

\[ 450=18q, \] \[ q=25. \]

The dependent quantity can be known, and the independent quantity can be unknown. The problem's information status does not erase the model's dependency structure.

Common Classification Errors

Assuming Every Unknown Is Independent

In a prediction problem, the unknown is often the dependent output.

Assuming Every Known Quantity Is Independent

A measured dependent quantity can be known and used to recover an unknown input.

Treating Dependency as Permanent

A formula can often be rearranged to express a different quantity in terms of the others.

Assuming the Horizontal Axis Is Always Independent

This is a common convention, not a mathematical necessity. Read the labels and context.

Confusing Association with Causation

A model describing \(y\) as a function of \(x\) does not by itself prove that \(x\) causes \(y\).

Ignoring Additional Unknowns

One equation may not uniquely determine several unknown quantities. Count the available conditions.

Forgetting Units and Domains

A numerical result may fail as an answer if it has the wrong units or violates contextual restrictions.

Practice

  1. In \(C=10+4n\), identify a reasonable independent and dependent quantity.
  2. If \(n=8\) is given and \(C\) is requested, classify both quantities on the two axes.
  3. If \(C=42\) is given and \(n\) is requested, explain which roles change and which remain.
  4. In \(A=\pi r^2\), classify \(A\), \(r\), and \(\pi\) when predicting area from radius.
  5. Explain why an independent quantity can still be unknown.
  6. In \(V=lwh\), list the possible independent quantities when volume is calculated from dimensions.
  7. Determine whether \(x+y=12\) uniquely determines both \(x\) and \(y\) over the real numbers.
  8. In \(y=mx+b\), describe the usual roles of \(x\), \(y\), \(m\), and \(b\).
  9. Explain why a statistical relationship between two quantities does not automatically establish causation.
  10. Create a practical formula with one dependent quantity, two independent quantities, one fixed parameter, and appropriate units.
Selected Answers
  1. \(n\) is a reasonable independent quantity and \(C\) the dependent quantity.
  2. \(n\) is known and independent; \(C\) is unknown and dependent.
  3. \(C\) becomes known and \(n\) unknown; the model can still treat \(n\) as independent and \(C\) as dependent.
  4. \(r\) is the independent input, \(A\) the dependent output, and \(\pi\) a known constant.
  5. Independent describes its structural role as an input; unknown describes whether its value is currently available.
  6. \(l\), \(w\), and \(h\).
  7. No. It describes infinitely many real ordered pairs unless another independent condition is supplied.
  8. Usually \(x\) is independent, \(y\) dependent, and \(m\) and \(b\) are fixed parameters for one selected line.
  9. A relationship can result from shared causes, selection effects, coincidence, or other structures; causal conclusions need additional evidence.

Summary

  • Known and unknown describe the information available in the current problem.
  • Independent and dependent describe how quantities vary within a chosen representation.
  • The two classification axes are separate and can combine in four ways.
  • An unknown may be a value, set, parameter, function, rule, or other mathematical object.
  • Independent quantities commonly act as inputs; dependent quantities commonly act as outputs.
  • A dependent quantity can be known, and an independent quantity can be unknown.
  • Dependency in a model does not automatically establish real-world causation.
  • Several unknowns may require several independent conditions.
  • Parameters can be structurally fixed while remaining unknown in a particular problem.
  • Roles must be interpreted from the relationship, representation, domain, units, and question being asked.
