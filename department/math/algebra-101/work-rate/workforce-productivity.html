Workforce and Productivity Problems

Analyze how workforce size, individual productivity, and time interact when several equally productive workers or machines contribute to the same job.

Overview

Workforce and productivity problems extend the Work Principle by explicitly considering the number of workers, machines, or other productive units assigned to a task. Instead of describing each worker separately, these problems often assume that every worker contributes at the same constant rate.

If \(N\) equally productive workers each work at rate \(R\) for time \(T\), then the total work completed is

\[ \boxed{W=NRT}. \]

This model explains why increasing the number of equally productive workers can reduce the time needed to complete a fixed amount of work.

Extending the Work Principle

For one worker, the Work Principle is

\[ W=RT. \]

If two identical workers operate at the same rate \(R\), their combined rate is

\[ R+R=2R. \]

If \(N\) identical workers operate together, their combined rate is

\[ R_{\text{combined}}=NR. \]

Substituting this into the Work Principle gives

\[ W=(NR)T. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{W=NRT}. \]

Worker-Hours

When workers have equal productivity, the product of workforce size and time is often useful.

For example, if \(6\) workers each work for \(8\) hours, the total labor used is

\[ 6(8)=48 \]

worker-hours.

A worker-hour represents one worker working for one hour. Similar units include worker-days, machine-hours, and crew-hours.

If productivity per worker remains constant, the same job requires the same total amount of worker-time:

\[ \boxed{N_1T_1=N_2T_2}. \]

This relationship is a special case of the Work Principle.

Workforce Size and Time Are Inversely Related

For a fixed job and constant individual productivity, increasing the number of workers decreases the required time.

Suppose the total work and individual rate are constant:

\[ W=NRT. \]

Solving for time gives

\[ T=\frac{W}{NR}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{T\propto\frac{1}{N}}. \]

This is an inverse relationship. If the workforce doubles, the completion time is cut in half, assuming productivity remains unchanged.

If the workforce triples, the completion time becomes one-third as long.

Example 1: Increasing the Workforce

Six workers can complete a job in \(10\) days. Assuming every worker has the same productivity, how long would \(15\) workers require?

The original amount of labor is

\[ 6(10)=60 \]

worker-days.

Let \(t\) represent the number of days required by \(15\) workers.

Then

\[ 15t=60. \]

Divide by \(15\):

\[ t=4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{4\text{ days}}. \]

The workforce increased by a factor of \(2.5\), so the completion time decreased by the same factor.

Deriving the Same Result from the Work Principle

The worker-hours method is convenient, but it comes directly from

\[ W=NRT. \]

For the first workforce,

\[ W=6R(10). \]

For the second workforce,

\[ W=15R(t). \]

Because the job and individual rate \(R\) are unchanged,

\[ 6R(10)=15R(t). \]

Cancel \(R\):

\[ 60=15t. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{t=4}. \]

The shortcut \(N_1T_1=N_2T_2\) is therefore not a separate rule. It follows directly from the Work Principle under the assumption of equal productivity.

Example 2: Fewer Workers

Twelve workers can complete a project in \(15\) days. After planning changes, only \(9\) workers are available. Assuming equal productivity, how long will the project take?

Use

\[ N_1T_1=N_2T_2. \]

Substitute:

\[ 12(15)=9t. \]

Thus,

\[ 180=9t. \]

Divide by \(9\):

\[ t=20. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{20\text{ days}}. \]

Because the workforce decreased, the completion time increased.

Finding the Required Workforce

The same model can be used when the time is fixed and the required number of workers is unknown.

Suppose \(8\) workers can complete a job in \(18\) days. How many workers would be required to finish the same job in \(12\) days?

Use

\[ 8(18)=N(12). \]

Simplify:

\[ 144=12N. \]

Therefore,

\[ N=12. \]

The project requires

\[ \boxed{12\text{ workers}}. \]

Finding Productivity per Worker

Some problems provide the size of the workforce and the total completion time and ask for the rate of one worker.

Suppose \(5\) identical machines complete one production run in \(4\) hours.

Since one complete run is

\[ W=1, \]

use

\[ W=NRT. \]

Substitute:

\[ 1=5R(4). \]

Therefore,

\[ 1=20R. \]

So

\[ \boxed{R=\frac{1}{20}}. \]

Each machine individually completes one-twentieth of the production run per hour.

One machine working alone would therefore require

\[ \boxed{20\text{ hours}}. \]

Example 3: Machine Productivity

Eight identical machines can manufacture an order in \(6\) hours. How long would \(3\) identical machines require to manufacture the same order?

The original machine-hours are

\[ 8(6)=48. \]

Let \(t\) represent the new time:

\[ 3t=48. \]

Therefore,

\[ t=16. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{16\text{ hours}}. \]

When the Workforce Changes During the Job

A more advanced workforce problem may involve a workforce that changes before the job is complete. In that case, the job must be divided into stages.

For example:

  • \(10\) workers operate for \(3\) days;
  • then \(5\) additional workers join;
  • the \(15\)-worker crew finishes the remaining work.

The work completed during the first stage is

\[ W_1=10R(3). \]

The work completed during the second stage is

\[ W_2=15Rt. \]

For one full job,

\[ \boxed{10R(3)+15Rt=1}. \]

This combines workforce productivity with staggered work.

Example 4: Additional Workers Join

Ten workers can complete a job in \(12\) days. They work for \(3\) days, after which \(5\) additional equally productive workers join the project. How many more days will the enlarged crew need to finish the job?

The total labor required is

\[ 10(12)=120 \]

worker-days.

During the first \(3\) days, the original crew completes

\[ 10(3)=30 \]

worker-days of work.

The remaining labor is

\[ 120-30=90 \]

worker-days.

After \(5\) additional workers join, the workforce is

\[ 10+5=15. \]

Let \(t\) represent the additional time:

\[ 15t=90. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{t=6\text{ days}}. \]

When Workers Leave Before Completion

The same reasoning applies when the workforce decreases.

Suppose \(16\) workers begin a job, but after several days, \(4\) workers leave. The first stage is calculated using \(16\) workers, while the second stage uses only \(12\).

The total work equation has the form

\[ 16RT_1+12RT_2=1. \]

This structure allows workforce changes to be incorporated without abandoning the Work Principle.

Equal Productivity Is an Assumption

The shortcut

\[ N_1T_1=N_2T_2 \]

works only when each worker or machine contributes at the same constant rate.

Real workers may differ in skill, experience, efficiency, or equipment. Adding more workers may also create coordination delays, space limitations, or other inefficiencies.

If productivity differs, the individual rates must be modeled separately.

For example, if three workers have rates \(R_1\), \(R_2\), and \(R_3\), the combined rate is

\[ R_{\text{combined}} = R_1+R_2+R_3, \]

not simply \(3R\).

Relative Productivity

Sometimes workers have different but related productivity levels.

Suppose Worker B is twice as productive as Worker A.

If Worker A's rate is

\[ r, \]

then Worker B's rate is

\[ 2r. \]

If there are \(4\) workers like A and \(3\) workers like B, the combined rate is

\[ 4r+3(2r). \]

Thus,

\[ R_{\text{combined}} = 4r+6r = 10r. \]

This weighted-rate approach allows the workforce model to accommodate groups with different productivity levels.

Example 5: Two Productivity Levels

A senior technician works twice as fast as a junior technician. Four junior technicians and two senior technicians can complete a job in \(6\) hours. How long would one junior technician require to complete the job alone?

Let the rate of one junior technician be

\[ r. \]

Each senior technician has rate

\[ 2r. \]

The combined rate is

\[ 4r+2(2r). \]

Simplify:

\[ R_{\text{combined}}=8r. \]

Since the group completes one job in \(6\) hours,

\[ 8r=\frac{1}{6}. \]

Therefore,

\[ r=\frac{1}{48}. \]

One junior technician would require

\[ \boxed{48\text{ hours}} \]

to complete the job alone.

Productivity Expressed as a Percentage

A problem may describe one worker as being a percentage more or less productive than another.

If Worker A has rate \(r\) and Worker B is \(25\%\) more productive, then

\[ R_B=1.25r. \]

If Worker C is \(20\%\) less productive than Worker A, then

\[ R_C=0.80r. \]

These rates can then be added normally:

\[ R_{\text{combined}} = r+1.25r+0.80r. \]

This extends workforce problems into proportional reasoning and percentage relationships.

Method for Solving Workforce and Productivity Problems

  1. Identify the complete job. Represent one full job by \(W=1\) when appropriate.
  2. Identify the workforce size. Determine how many workers, machines, or units are active.
  3. Determine whether productivity is equal. The shortcut \(N_1T_1=N_2T_2\) requires equal individual rates.
  4. Use \(W=NRT\) when individual productivity is known or represented algebraically.
  5. Use worker-hours or machine-hours when the job and individual productivity remain constant.
  6. Divide the problem into stages if the workforce changes.
  7. Add the work completed during all stages.
  8. Solve for the unknown workforce, rate, or time.
  9. Check the direction of the result. More equally productive workers should reduce completion time; fewer should increase it.

Common Errors

  • Treating workforce size and time as directly proportional. For a fixed job, more equally productive workers require less time.
  • Using \(N_1T_1=N_2T_2\) when workers have different productivity levels. Unequal rates must be modeled separately.
  • Ignoring changes in workforce size. A worker who joins or leaves creates a new stage with a different combined rate.
  • Confusing total labor with elapsed time. Forty worker-hours could represent one worker for \(40\) hours, five workers for \(8\) hours, or another equivalent combination.
  • Assuming real-world productivity always scales perfectly. Algebra problems often assume constant productivity, but the assumption should be recognized explicitly.

Practice Problems

  1. Eight workers can complete a project in \(15\) days. How long would \(12\) equally productive workers require?
  2. Nine machines can complete a production run in \(8\) hours. How long would \(6\) identical machines require?
  3. Ten workers can complete a job in \(18\) days. How many workers are required to finish the same job in \(12\) days?
  4. Six identical machines complete a job in \(5\) hours. What is the work rate of one machine?
  5. Twelve workers can complete a project in \(20\) days. They work for \(5\) days, after which \(3\) workers leave. How many additional days will the remaining workers need?
  6. Eight workers can complete a project in \(24\) days. After \(6\) days, \(4\) additional workers join. How much longer will the project take?
  7. A senior worker is twice as productive as a junior worker. Three senior workers and four junior workers complete a job in \(10\) hours. How long would one junior worker take alone?
  8. Worker B is \(50\%\) more productive than Worker A. Two workers like A and two workers like B complete a job in \(12\) hours. How long would one Worker A take alone?

Practice Answers

  1. \[ 8(15)=12t. \] \[ 120=12t. \] \[ \boxed{t=10\text{ days}}. \]
  2. \[ 9(8)=6t. \] \[ 72=6t. \] \[ \boxed{t=12\text{ hours}}. \]
  3. \[ 10(18)=12N. \] \[ 180=12N. \] \[ \boxed{N=15\text{ workers}}. \]
  4. Use \[ 1=6R(5). \] \[ 1=30R. \] \[ \boxed{R=\frac{1}{30}\text{ job per hour}}. \]
  5. The total required labor is \[ 12(20)=240 \] worker-days. In the first \(5\) days, \[ 12(5)=60 \] worker-days are completed. Remaining labor: \[ 240-60=180. \] After \(3\) workers leave, \[ 12-3=9 \] workers remain. Thus, \[ 9t=180. \] \[ \boxed{t=20\text{ additional days}}. \]
  6. The total labor required is \[ 8(24)=192 \] worker-days. During the first \(6\) days, \[ 8(6)=48 \] worker-days are completed. Remaining: \[ 192-48=144. \] The new workforce is \[ 8+4=12. \] Thus, \[ 12t=144. \] \[ \boxed{t=12\text{ additional days}}. \]
  7. Let one junior worker's rate be \(r\). A senior worker's rate is \[ 2r. \] The group rate is \[ 3(2r)+4r=10r. \] Since they complete the job in \(10\) hours, \[ 10r=\frac{1}{10}. \] \[ r=\frac{1}{100}. \] Therefore one junior worker requires \[ \boxed{100\text{ hours}}. \]
  8. Let Worker A's rate be \(r\). Worker B's rate is \[ 1.5r. \] The group rate is \[ 2r+2(1.5r)=5r. \] Since the group completes the job in \(12\) hours, \[ 5r=\frac{1}{12}. \] \[ r=\frac{1}{60}. \] Therefore one Worker A requires \[ \boxed{60\text{ hours}}. \]

Summary

Workforce and productivity problems extend the Work Principle by including the number of productive units:

\[ \boxed{W=NRT}. \]

When the job and individual productivity remain constant,

\[ \boxed{N_1T_1=N_2T_2}. \]

This relationship shows that workforce size and completion time are inversely related:

\[ \boxed{T\propto\frac{1}{N}}. \]

The shortcut works only when the workers or machines have equal constant productivity. When rates differ, each productivity level must be represented separately and the rates added.

Workforce problems therefore connect the Work Principle with combined rates, inverse variation, proportional reasoning, and staged work.
