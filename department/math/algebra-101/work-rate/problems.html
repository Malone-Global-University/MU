Work-Rate Problems

Apply the Work Principle to the major classes of algebraic work-rate problems.

Problem Types

Work-rate problems can appear in many different forms, but most can be organized into a small number of recurring mathematical structures. The setting may involve workers, machines, pipes, pumps, crews, production systems, or other processes, yet each problem ultimately depends on the relationship between work, rate, and time.

The fundamental relationship remains

\[ W = RT. \]

When one complete job is represented by \(1\), a worker or process that completes the job in \(t\) units of time has rate

\[ R = \frac{1}{t}. \]

The following lessons examine the major cases separately before combining them in mixed problems.

Work-Rate Problem Categories

  1. 1. Single-Rate Problems

    Solve problems involving one worker, machine, or process. Determine the amount of work completed, the rate of work, or the time required when the other quantities are known.

    The central relationship is

    \[ W = RT. \]

  2. 2. Combined-Rate Problems

    Analyze situations in which two or more workers or processes contribute toward the same job at the same time. Their rates are added to determine the total rate of work.

    \[ R_{\text{combined}} = R_1 + R_2 + \cdots \]

  3. 3. Opposing-Rate Problems

    Solve situations in which one process contributes toward the desired result while another works against it, such as a pipe filling a tank while a drain empties it.

    \[ R_{\text{net}} = R_{\text{positive}} - R_{\text{negative}} \]

  4. 4. Staggered Work Problems

    Study problems in which workers or processes begin at different times. One may work alone before another joins, causing the total work to be divided across different time intervals.

    A typical structure is

    \[ R_1T_1 + R_2T_2 + \cdots = 1. \]

  5. 5. Interrupted and Remaining-Work Problems

    Analyze situations in which a worker starts a job and then stops, leaves, or is replaced. Determine how much work has already been completed and how much remains.

    \[ W_{\text{completed}} + W_{\text{remaining}} = 1 \]

  6. 6. Unknown and Related-Rate Problems

    Solve problems in which one or more completion times are unknown or related algebraically. These problems often produce rational equations that must be simplified and solved.

    For example,

    \[ \frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{x+3}=\frac{1}{4}. \]

  7. 7. Workforce and Productivity Problems

    Examine how the number of workers, machines, or production units affects completion time when individual productivity remains constant.

    A useful model is

    \[ W = NRT, \]

    where \(N\) represents the number of equally productive workers or machines.

  8. 8. Mixed Work-Rate Problems

    Combine several work-rate concepts in the same problem. These problems may involve multiple workers, staggered start times, opposing processes, changing workforce size, or unknown rates.

    The primary challenge is determining which rates and time intervals belong in the model before solving the resulting equation.

How to Approach a Work-Rate Problem

Although the details differ among problem types, a consistent strategy can be used in most cases.

  1. Define the complete job. Treat the entire task as \(W=1\).
  2. Identify each completion time. Determine how long each worker or process would require to complete the entire job alone.
  3. Convert completion times into rates. If a process requires \(t\) units of time, its rate is \(\frac{1}{t}\).
  4. Determine how long each rate operates. Not every worker or process necessarily operates for the same amount of time.
  5. Assign positive and negative rates. Rates that contribute toward the goal are positive; rates that oppose it are negative.
  6. Write the work equation. Add the work contributed by each process and set the total equal to the required amount of work.
  7. Solve the resulting equation. This may require simplifying rational expressions, finding a common denominator, or solving a quadratic equation.
  8. Check the result. Confirm that the answer is mathematically valid and reasonable within the context of the problem.

The General Work-Rate Model

Many work-rate problems can be represented by adding the work completed by several individual processes:

\[ W_1 + W_2 + W_3 + \cdots = W_{\text{total}}. \]

Since each amount of work can be written as rate multiplied by time,

\[ R_1T_1 + R_2T_2 + R_3T_3 + \cdots = W_{\text{total}}. \]

For one complete job,

\[ \boxed{R_1T_1 + R_2T_2 + R_3T_3 + \cdots = 1}. \]

This general equation is broad enough to describe combined rates, staggered work, interrupted work, and many mixed situations. Opposing processes can be represented by negative rates.

The Main Skill: Choosing the Correct Model

The most advanced part of solving work-rate problems is often not the algebra itself. It is deciding how the situation should be represented mathematically.

A student should first ask:

  • What counts as one complete job?
  • What is the rate of each worker or process?
  • Which rates contribute toward the goal?
  • Which rates oppose the goal?
  • How long does each rate operate?
  • Does the workforce or rate change during the problem?
  • Is the unknown a rate, a completion time, an amount of work, or a number of workers?

Once these questions are answered, the algebraic equation usually follows directly from the Work Principle.

Connection to Rational Equations

Work-rate problems frequently produce rational expressions because completion times appear in denominators. For example, if one worker requires \(x\) hours and another requires \(x+3\) hours, their rates are

\[ \frac{1}{x} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{1}{x+3}. \]

If they complete the job together in \(4\) hours, the model becomes

\[ \frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{x+3}=\frac{1}{4}. \]

The work-rate model therefore provides a practical application of rational expressions, common denominators, rational equations, and quadratic equations.
