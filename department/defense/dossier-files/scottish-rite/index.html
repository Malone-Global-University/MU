Scottish Rite

Overview

Scottish Rite Freemasonry is a family of Masonic appendant organizations that administer degrees numbered from the fourth through the thirty-third. A man ordinarily enters the Rite only after becoming a Master Mason in a recognized Craft lodge.

Scottish Rite is not one worldwide corporation or one command controlling all Freemasonry. It is divided among sovereign or separately governed Supreme Councils, each claiming authority over defined territories and recognizing selected counterpart bodies.

In the United States, four principal mutually recognized Scottish Rite systems must be distinguished:

  1. Mainstream Southern Jurisdiction.
  2. Mainstream Northern Masonic Jurisdiction.
  3. Prince Hall Affiliation Southern Jurisdiction.
  4. Prince Hall Affiliation Northern Jurisdiction.

Other organizations use Scottish Rite names, degree numbers, double-headed eagle emblems, Supreme Council titles, and claims of ancient succession. Similar branding does not establish common authority, recognition, or institutional responsibility.

Scottish Rite Is an Umbrella Identity

The words Scottish Rite identify a ritual family and a class of Masonic institutions. They do not identify one legal entity.

A researcher must determine:

  • Which Supreme Council claims jurisdiction.
  • Whether the body is mainstream, Prince Hall Affiliated, Prince Hall Origin, foreign, independent, or disputed.
  • The member's Valley, Orient, Consistory, or other subordinate body.
  • The degree, office, and period of service involved.
  • Which other Masonic authorities recognize the body.

Failure to identify the exact jurisdiction can falsely merge White-led, Black-led, foreign, independent, and unrecognized organizations.

Why It Is Called Scottish

The Scottish Rite did not originate as one complete system in Scotland. Official Masonic histories generally trace its modern development through France and the French-speaking Atlantic world.

Eighteenth-century high-degree systems used descriptions such as Ecossais, meaning Scottish, to suggest antiquity, prestige, chivalry, or a particular ritual tradition.

The Rite's name is therefore historical and symbolic. It does not establish government by Scotland or exclusive descent from Scottish institutions.

Early High-Degree Masonry

Craft Freemasonry centered on the Entered Apprentice, Fellow Craft, and Master Mason degrees. During the eighteenth century, additional degrees developed in France, Britain, continental Europe, the Caribbean, and North America.

These ceremonies expanded themes involving:

  • King Solomon's Temple.
  • Knighthood and chivalry.
  • Justice and government.
  • Religious tolerance.
  • Philosophy and moral duty.
  • Loss and recovery of knowledge.
  • Biblical, historical, and legendary figures.

Rite of Perfection

A major predecessor was the twenty-five-degree Order or Rite of the Royal Secret, commonly called the Rite of Perfection.

Étienne Morin received authority associated with higher-degree Masonry during the eighteenth century. Henry Andrew Francken transmitted rituals and warrants in the Caribbean and North America.

The modern thirty-three-degree Scottish Rite developed from this earlier system rather than appearing fully formed at one ancient moment.

The 1801 Charleston Supreme Council

On May 31, 1801, a Supreme Council was formally opened in Charleston, South Carolina. It became the first lasting Supreme Council governing a thirty-three-degree Scottish Rite system.

The modern Southern Jurisdiction identifies this body as the Mother Supreme Council of the World.

The phrase is an institutional claim of historical primacy. It does not make the Southern Jurisdiction a government with automatic command over every Scottish Rite Supreme Council in the world.

Northern Masonic Jurisdiction Formation

Competing high-degree bodies operated in New York during the early nineteenth century. The Charleston Supreme Council sent Emanuel De La Motta to examine their claims.

On August 5, 1813, the Supreme Council now called the Northern Masonic Jurisdiction was formally established in New York City.

The Southern and Northern Supreme Councils later developed separate rituals, governing practices, territorial structures, charities, and membership programs.

Two Mainstream United States Jurisdictions

The mainstream American Scottish Rite is divided between:

  • Southern Jurisdiction — Founded in 1801 and governing thirty-five states, the District of Columbia, and territories assigned to it.
  • Northern Masonic Jurisdiction — Founded in 1813 and governing fifteen northeastern and midwestern states.

The division predates the American Civil War and was not created simply as a Union-versus-Confederacy arrangement.

The Four Recognized United States Supreme Councils

The major recognized American structure now includes:

  1. The Supreme Council, Southern Jurisdiction.
  2. The Supreme Council, Northern Masonic Jurisdiction.
  3. The United Supreme Council, Prince Hall Affiliation, Southern Jurisdiction.
  4. The United Supreme Council, Prince Hall Affiliation, Northern Jurisdiction.

Each maintains its own leadership, membership, subordinate bodies, finances, property, history, ritual administration, and jurisdiction.

2022 Statement of Unity

In 2022, leaders of the two mainstream and two Prince Hall Supreme Councils signed a Statement of Unity.

The agreement expressed mutual recognition, cooperation, and a commitment to work for the benefit of the Craft.

The Statement did not merge the four organizations. Recognition and cooperation should not be confused with shared ownership, unified membership, or one supreme national command.

Mainstream Southern Jurisdiction

The Supreme Council, 33°, Southern Jurisdiction, U.S.A. is headquartered at the House of the Temple in Washington, D.C.

It governs mainstream Scottish Rite bodies in thirty-five states and other assigned territories. States are generally called Orients, and local Scottish Rite communities are called Valleys.

As of July 27, 2026, its official materials identify James D. Cole, 33° as Sovereign Grand Commander.

Mainstream Northern Masonic Jurisdiction

The Supreme Council, 33°, Northern Masonic Jurisdiction is headquartered in Lexington, Massachusetts.

It governs Valleys in Connecticut, Delaware, Illinois, Indiana, Maine, Massachusetts, Michigan, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont, and Wisconsin.

As of July 27, 2026, its official materials identify Walter F. Wheeler, 33° as Sovereign Grand Commander.

Prince Hall Southern Jurisdiction

The United Supreme Council, 33°, Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite of Freemasonry, Prince Hall Affiliation, Southern Jurisdiction, U.S.A., Inc. is a separate Black Masonic Supreme Council.

It identifies Memphis, Tennessee, as its institutional headquarters and reports activity through twenty-seven Orients, including domestic and overseas locations.

As of July 27, 2026, its official materials identify Dr. Corey D. Hawkins Sr., 33° as Sovereign Grand Commander.

Prince Hall Northern Jurisdiction

The United Supreme Council, Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite of Freemasonry, Prince Hall Affiliation, Northern Jurisdiction, U.S.A., Inc. is the parallel Prince Hall northern body.

It traces its present institutional consolidation to April 9, 1881, when competing or predecessor Black Supreme Councils were united.

As of July 27, 2026, its official materials identify Dr. Melvin J. Bazemore, 33° as Sovereign Grand Commander.

Why Prince Hall Scottish Rite Developed

Mainstream American Freemasonry and its appendant bodies were historically controlled by White institutions that excluded Black Masons or refused to recognize their lodges.

Black Masons created separate Scottish Rite Supreme Councils, Consistories, and related bodies to obtain higher-degree instruction, governance, fellowship, recognition, and institutional autonomy.

Prince Hall Scottish Rite should therefore not be described as a racial department of the mainstream Supreme Councils.

Recognition Does Not Create Subordination

Mutual recognition permits institutional relations such as visitation, communication, shared events, and acknowledgment of regular Masonic standing.

It does not automatically authorize one Supreme Council to:

  • Appoint the other's leaders.
  • Control the other's property or finances.
  • Discipline the other's members.
  • Rewrite the other's constitution.
  • Command the other's Valleys or Consistories.

Independent and Competing Supreme Councils

Numerous bodies use names such as:

  • Supreme Council of the Thirty-third Degree.
  • Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite.
  • United Supreme Council.
  • Ancient Accepted Scottish Rite Masons.
  • National Supreme Council.
  • Scottish Rite of Louisiana.

Some possess independent histories or competing lineage claims. Others are considered irregular, clandestine, or bogus by the four recognized American Supreme Councils.

Legal incorporation is not the same as Masonic recognition.

Cerneauism

Cerneauism refers to Scottish Rite bodies claiming authority through Joseph Cerneau or related competing high-degree lineages.

Mainstream Southern and Northern jurisdictions have historically rejected many Cerneau Supreme Council claims as irregular.

The label is an internal Masonic legitimacy judgment. A Cerneau body may still exist legally as an association or corporation.

Membership

Current mainstream and Prince Hall Scottish Rite systems generally require a candidate to be a Master Mason in good standing under a Craft grand lodge recognized by the relevant Supreme Council.

A Master Mason does not become a Scottish Rite Mason automatically. He must petition and receive the degrees or required ceremonial communication.

A relative, employee, friend, donor, or attendee at a public event is not thereby a member.

Degrees Four Through Thirty-Two

The Scottish Rite administers twenty-nine degrees between the fourth and thirty-second degrees.

These degrees are intended to expand Masonic moral, philosophical, civic, religious, historical, and symbolic instruction.

Degree titles and dramatizations differ between jurisdictions. A degree in the Northern Masonic Jurisdiction may have a different title, script, setting, or emphasis from the corresponding Southern Jurisdiction degree.

Degree Bodies

A traditional Scottish Rite structure groups degrees into subordinate bodies:

  • Lodge of Perfection
  • Council of Princes of Jerusalem
  • Chapter of Rose Croix
  • Council of Kadosh
  • Consistory

Not every jurisdiction uses all five bodies in the same way.

Lodge of Perfection

The Lodge of Perfection ordinarily administers degrees beginning with the fourth and extending through the fourteenth.

Themes include duty, justice, secrecy, leadership, rebuilding, judgment, integrity, and the completion of Temple-centered narratives.

Council of Princes of Jerusalem

The Council of Princes of Jerusalem commonly administers the fifteenth and sixteenth degrees.

Its ceremonies concern exile, political negotiation, restoration, rebuilding, civic duty, and the return to Jerusalem.

Chapter of Rose Croix

The Chapter of Rose Croix commonly administers degrees centered upon spiritual, philosophical, religious, and ethical renewal.

In the Southern Jurisdiction it traditionally covers the fifteenth through eighteenth degrees. In the Northern Masonic Jurisdiction, the subordinate body commonly centers on the seventeenth and eighteenth degrees.

Variation illustrates why degree structure should not be generalized across all Supreme Councils.

Council of Kadosh

In the Southern Jurisdiction, the Council of Kadosh commonly administers the nineteenth through thirtieth degrees.

Degree themes may include leadership, justice, religious conflict, political authority, knighthood, resistance to tyranny, duty, and philosophical examination.

Consistory

The Consistory administers the final degrees leading to the thirty-second degree.

In the Southern Jurisdiction, the Consistory traditionally centers on the thirty-first and thirty-second degrees. In the Northern Masonic Jurisdiction, it administers degrees nineteen through thirty-two.

Prince Hall local Scottish Rite bodies are also commonly identified as Consistories, but their governing lineage must be verified separately.

The Valley

A Valley is the principal local Scottish Rite geographic organization.

A Valley may contain several subordinate degree bodies, share officers and staff, own a cathedral or temple, administer dues, hold reunions, conduct charity, and manage membership.

Conduct by one Valley should not automatically be attributed to every Valley or the full Supreme Council.

The Orient

An Orient commonly corresponds to a state, territory, country, or other jurisdictional area.

Its chief Scottish Rite authority may be a Sovereign Grand Inspector General, a Deputy, or another officer designated by the relevant Supreme Council.

The word Orient is fraternal geography. It does not create governmental sovereignty.

The Supreme Council

A Supreme Council is the governing body of its Scottish Rite jurisdiction.

Its powers may include:

  • Chartering and supervising subordinate bodies.
  • Maintaining ritual and degree standards.
  • Electing officers and active members.
  • Conferring honors and the thirty-third degree.
  • Recognizing or rejecting other Supreme Councils.
  • Managing property, archives, publications, and charitable programs.
  • Disciplining members and subordinate bodies.

Sovereign Grand Commander

The Sovereign Grand Commander is the senior executive or presiding officer of a Supreme Council.

The title applies within the relevant jurisdiction. It does not make its holder commander of all Freemasonry, every thirty-third-degree Mason, or other Supreme Councils.

Sovereign Grand Inspectors General

Sovereign Grand Inspectors General, commonly abbreviated S.G.I.G., are thirty-third-degree members serving as active Supreme Council members or jurisdictional leaders according to the governing system.

Their powers derive from constitution, office, and appointment—not merely possession of the degree.

The Thirty-Third Degree

The thirty-third degree is conferred by a Supreme Council, generally to recognize distinguished Masonic, civic, or humanitarian service or to qualify members for Supreme Council governance.

It is not ordinarily obtained merely by completing a routine numbered sequence or paying an additional fee.

A thirty-third-degree Mason is not automatically:

  • The superior of every thirty-second-degree Mason.
  • An officer of a Craft grand lodge.
  • A commander of York Rite or Shrine bodies.
  • A government official.
  • A member of one universal inner council.

Honorary and Active Thirty-Third-Degree Members

Jurisdictions distinguish active governing members from honorary recipients.

Titles may include:

  • Sovereign Grand Inspector General.
  • Inspector General Honorary.
  • Active Member.
  • Deputy of the Supreme Council.

The precise authority of a thirty-third-degree Mason must be determined from his office and jurisdiction.

Intermediate Honors

Some jurisdictions confer honors below the thirty-third degree.

The Southern Jurisdiction and Prince Hall systems may use Knight Commander of the Court of Honour, commonly abbreviated K.C.C.H.

The Northern Masonic Jurisdiction uses distinctions including Meritorious Service Awards.

Honor does not automatically establish executive authority.

Caps and Regalia

Scottish Rite caps, aprons, collars, jewels, rings, cords, and sashes may indicate degree, honor, office, jurisdiction, or service.

In the Southern Jurisdiction, cap colors distinguish categories including thirty-second-degree members, K.C.C.H. recipients, honorary thirty-third-degree members, active S.G.I.G.s, deputies, Grand Cross recipients, and long-term members.

Similar colors in another jurisdiction may not carry identical meaning.

Double-Headed Eagle

The double-headed eagle is one of the Scottish Rite's most recognizable emblems.

It appears in different forms, often with crowns, swords, triangles, mottos, degree numbers, and jurisdictional markings.

The symbol has older imperial, religious, and heraldic uses. Its presence does not by itself prove membership in the Scottish Rite, political allegiance to an empire, or participation in the Illuminati.

Albert Pike

Albert Pike was the dominant nineteenth-century leader of the mainstream Southern Jurisdiction. He served as Sovereign Grand Commander from 1859 until his death in 1891.

Pike revised, organized, and interpreted Scottish Rite rituals and produced influential writings that shaped the Southern Jurisdiction and other Supreme Councils.

He did not found the Scottish Rite. The Charleston Supreme Council existed decades before he became its leader.

Morals and Dogma

Pike's Morals and Dogma of the Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite of Freemasonry was published in 1871.

The book contains essays associated with the Scottish Rite degrees and draws from philosophy, comparative religion, mythology, politics, ethics, and nineteenth-century scholarship.

It is not a constitution governing all Freemasonry, and every statement in it is not binding doctrine for every Supreme Council or member.

It remains important because of Pike's influence and because conspiracy writers frequently quote it without its institutional or historical context.

Albert Pike and the Confederacy

Pike served the Confederate States during the American Civil War and held the rank of brigadier general.

His Confederate service is a documented part of the racial and political history surrounding the Southern Jurisdiction.

The Albert Pike memorial in Washington was funded through Scottish Rite efforts and honored him primarily as a Masonic leader. Its public presence became controversial because he was the only Confederate general represented by an outdoor statue in the nation's capital.

Race, Pike, and Institutional Memory

Pike lived and led during slavery, Civil War, Reconstruction, and racial exclusion in American Masonry.

Scottish Rite institutions that celebrate his intellectual and ritual work should not erase his Confederate service or the exclusionary society in which the mainstream system developed.

Conversely, Pike's individual conduct should not be attributed automatically to every current mainstream or Prince Hall Scottish Rite member.

Ku Klux Klan Claims

Internet and conspiracy sources frequently identify Pike as a founder, national leader, or chief judicial officer of the Ku Klux Klan.

Pike's Confederate service is documented. Claims assigning him a specific national Klan office remain disputed and are not established merely by repetition, monument controversy, or his Masonic rank.

The responsible classification is:

  • Documented: Confederate military service and leadership of the Southern Jurisdiction.
  • Documented: Significant influence over Scottish Rite ritual and literature.
  • Disputed: Specific high-level Ku Klux Klan office claims.
  • Not established: That the Scottish Rite as a whole was or is the Ku Klux Klan.

House of the Temple

The House of the Temple in Washington, D.C., is the headquarters of the mainstream Southern Jurisdiction.

It contains executive offices, ceremonial spaces, archives, a library, museum collections, memorials, and the remains of Albert Pike.

The building is a major institutional symbol and public research resource. It is not the headquarters of the Northern Masonic Jurisdiction or either Prince Hall Supreme Council.

Scottish Rite Masonic Museum and Library

The mainstream Northern Masonic Jurisdiction operates the Scottish Rite Masonic Museum and Library in Lexington, Massachusetts.

It preserves records, objects, art, books, manuscripts, and archives relating to Freemasonry and American fraternal history.

Museum stewardship and Supreme Council government are related but should be distinguished when assigning legal, employment, archival, or financial responsibility.

The mainstream Southern Jurisdiction supports:

  • Scholarships.

Programs may operate through national foundations, state foundations, local Valleys, clinics, universities, or partner institutions.

RiteCare Childhood Language Programs

RiteCare programs support children with speech, language, communication, and learning needs.

The network is decentralized. Services may be delivered by universities, hospitals, clinics, nonprofit centers, or local Scottish Rite foundations.

Outcomes and financial responsibility should be evaluated by identifying the actual clinic, Valley, foundation, and service provider.

Northern Masonic Jurisdiction Philanthropy

The mainstream Northern Masonic Jurisdiction supports programs including:

  • The Grand Almoner's Fund.
  • Scholarships and educational assistance.

  • Masonic research and preservation.
  • Local Valley community programs.

Children's Dyslexia Centers

The Northern Masonic Jurisdiction's Children's Dyslexia Centers provide tutoring and training connected to dyslexia intervention.

The centers form one of the jurisdiction's principal public philanthropic identities.

A center, host institution, local Valley, and Supreme Council may possess different operational and legal responsibilities.

Prince Hall Charitable Activity

Prince Hall Scottish Rite Supreme Councils, Orients, Consistories, Assemblies, foundations, and related bodies support scholarships, education, community aid, research, mentoring, health initiatives, and relief.

Programs should be attributed to the exact Prince Hall jurisdiction and legal entity rather than to “the Scottish Rite” generally.

Women's Auxiliaries

Prince Hall Scottish Rite systems include affiliated women's organizations commonly called Assemblies and associated with the Order of the Golden Circle.

Mainstream Scottish Rite bodies may also cooperate with women's and family organizations, but there is no single worldwide women's Scottish Rite auxiliary.

Women's bodies possess their own officers, membership, finances, and institutional responsibilities.

Relationship to York Rite

Scottish Rite and York Rite are separate appendant Masonic systems.

Many Masons belong to both. Shared membership can create close institutional and personal relationships but does not merge their governing bodies.

Neither Rite automatically outranks or commands the other.

Relationship to the Shrine

Scottish Rite membership historically served as one path to Shrine eligibility. Modern Shrine systems generally require Master Mason status without requiring completion of the Scottish Rite.

A Scottish Rite Mason is not automatically a Shriner, and a Shriner is not necessarily a Scottish Rite Mason.

Shriners International and the Prince Hall Shriners remain separate from all four principal Scottish Rite Supreme Councils.

Relationship to Craft Grand Lodges

Scottish Rite membership depends upon a Mason's standing in a Craft lodge, but the Supreme Council is not ordinarily the Craft grand lodge.

Suspension or expulsion from Craft Masonry may affect Scottish Rite eligibility. The precise effect is controlled by relevant constitutions and recognition agreements.

The Supreme Council does not automatically control local Blue Lodges merely because some lodge officers hold Scottish Rite degrees.

Religion and Spiritual Themes

Scottish Rite degrees draw from Jewish, Christian, Islamic, classical, philosophical, chivalric, and comparative-religion sources.

Mainstream American Scottish Rite membership generally requires belief in a Supreme Being but does not publicly require adherence to one denomination.

Degree content and religious qualifications vary across jurisdictions.

Religious Criticism

Religious critics object to Scottish Rite practices because of:

  • Private oaths and ritual.
  • Comparative-religion language.
  • Use of sacred texts and religious symbols.
  • References to hidden or recovered knowledge.
  • Masonic obligations crossing denominational lines.
  • Pike's philosophical interpretations.

Scottish Rite bodies generally describe themselves as moral and fraternal, not as churches or substitute religions.

A theological finding of incompatibility should be distinguished from proof of criminal occult activity or political conspiracy.

Private Ritual and Public Institution

Scottish Rite bodies maintain private ceremonies, signs, passwords, membership deliberations, disciplinary matters, and internal communications.

Their existence, headquarters, governing names, charities, degree titles, museums, publications, officers, and many historical records are public.

Scottish Rite is therefore a family of public institutions with selective membership and confidential ritual, not an invisible organization whose existence is unknown.

Illuminati Claims

Scottish Rite is frequently portrayed as the governing center of the Illuminati because of:

  • The thirty-third degree.
  • The title Supreme Council.
  • Private ceremonies.
  • Double-headed eagle symbolism.
  • Pike's writings.
  • Members holding public or professional influence.

These facts establish hierarchy, secrecy, symbolism, institutional continuity, and networking. They do not independently establish one global hidden command controlling governments, banks, religions, media, or every Masonic body.

The Thirty-Third Degree and Conspiracy Inflation

Popular accounts often describe thirty-third-degree Masons as the secret rulers of lower-degree Masons and public institutions.

The degree can be institutionally important, especially where a recipient is also an active Supreme Council officer. It still does not create universal authority outside the relevant constitution.

Analysis must distinguish:

  • Honorary degree.
  • Active Supreme Council membership.
  • State or Orient leadership.
  • Local Valley office.
  • Authority held outside Masonry.

Networking and Leadership Pipelines

Scottish Rite bodies create durable networks among professionals, clergy, military personnel, judges, law-enforcement officers, educators, business owners, nonprofit leaders, and public officials.

The system offers:

  • Officer development.
  • Mentorship and recognition.
  • Professional and social introductions.
  • Access to charitable and civic projects.
  • Travel and inter-jurisdictional relationships.
  • Publishing, research, and educational platforms.

Networking is not itself proof of misconduct. It becomes relevant when undisclosed loyalty affects public, fiduciary, employment, legal, or security duties.

Gatekeeping Risks

Scottish Rite networks may become gatekeeping mechanisms where members control:

  • Employment and professional advancement.
  • Government contracts, appointments, or licensing.
  • Masonic recognition and access.
  • Charitable funding and institutional partnerships.
  • Property, event venues, or donor relationships.
  • Media, educational, religious, or civic platforms.
  • Discipline, prosecution, investigation, or litigation.

A gatekeeping allegation requires evidence of the decision, relationship, benefit, excluded party, authority, and organizational knowledge.

Potential Conflicts of Interest

Potential conflicts may arise where Scottish Rite relationships intersect with:

  • Courts and law enforcement.
  • Military and veterans' institutions.
  • Public procurement and regulation.
  • Health, language, and education charities.
  • Nonprofit boards and foundations.
  • Real estate and Masonic property.
  • University, museum, and archival governance.
  • Employment, promotion, contracting, or discipline.

Shared membership is relevant evidence of relationship, but it does not prove improper action without a material connection to the decision.

Institutional Responsibility Test

  1. Identify the exact system. Determine the Supreme Council, jurisdiction, Valley, Orient, Consistory, Assembly, charity, or independent body involved.
  2. Verify recognition and lineage. Distinguish mainstream, Prince Hall Affiliated, Prince Hall Origin, Cerneau, foreign, and other claims.
  3. Verify membership and office. Establish the person's degree, dates, active or honorary status, and actual authority.
  4. Separate degree from command. A degree number alone does not prove governing power.
  5. Identify the legal entity. Separate Supreme Council, foundation, Valley, clinic, museum, and property corporations.
  6. Trace resources. Determine whose funds, staff, property, records, donor lists, or platform supported the act.
  7. Locate authorization. Seek minutes, directives, votes, contracts, constitutions, official statements, and credible testimony.
  8. Test knowledge and response. Determine whether leaders approved, knew, concealed, ignored, investigated, or corrected the conduct.
  9. Assign responsibility proportionately. Attribute conduct only to persons and institutions supported by evidence.

Proximity to the Malone Network

No principal Scottish Rite Supreme Council is governed by or institutionally aligned with the Malone Network.

Proximity may nevertheless become significant because Scottish Rite members operate in the same civic, political, educational, religious, military, legal, charitable, business, and professional environments as Malone personnel.

  • Remote — No identified contact, overlap, or access.
  • Adjacent — Shared community, profession, issue, donor, or institutional territory.
  • Connected — Identified relationships with Malone personnel, partners, or audiences.
  • Embedded — Members hold access inside Malone programs, communications, facilities, or leadership circles.
  • Interlocked — Significant shared personnel, funding, property, or operational dependence.

Embedded or interlocked status requires specific evidence.

Hostility Assessment

External governance, secrecy, racial history, Masonic loyalty, or lack of Malone alignment do not independently prove hostility.

Hostility would require documented conduct such as:

  • Targeted infiltration of Malone institutions.
  • Coordinated obstruction of Malone funding, partnerships, employment, or access.
  • False representation of authority over Malone.
  • Retaliation against Malone personnel or supporters.
  • Capture or redirection of Malone programs.
  • Organized reputational, legal, political, financial, cyber, or physical attack.

The current default classification is external and untrusted, not proven hostile.

Controlled Opposition Assessment

Scottish Rite Supreme Councils do not ordinarily present themselves as Malone organizations or Black liberation movements.

The category controlled opposition is therefore not generally applicable to the whole Rite.

A Scottish Rite-linked public figure, civic organization, or reform movement could still be evaluated for controlled-opposition behavior where evidence shows that it claims resistance while being financed, directed, constrained, or repeatedly used by the power structure it claims to oppose.

Institutional conservatism, compromise, incompetence, donor influence, or ideological disagreement do not alone prove control.

Confusion and Fragmentation Assessment

Public confusion is substantial because:

  • Several Supreme Councils use nearly identical names.
  • Mainstream and Prince Hall bodies share degree numbers and symbols.
  • Honorary thirty-third-degree status is confused with executive authority.
  • Valleys, Orients, Consistories, and Supreme Councils are merged in public discussion.
  • Scottish Rite is confused with Craft Masonry, York Rite, Shrine, and the Illuminati.
  • Independent and unrecognized bodies imitate recognized branding.

The Rite is institutionally divided and jurisdictional. It is not necessarily confused internally, although its naming structure makes external confusion predictable.

Evidence Classification

  • Documented fact: Supported by charters, constitutions, proceedings, court records, official publications, financial reports, archives, or corroborated historical sources.
  • Official claim: A statement attributed to a named Supreme Council, Valley, charity, officer, or related body.
  • Documented association: Verified membership, degree, office, attendance, correspondence, donation, or institutional relationship.
  • Testimony: A witness account evaluated for access, specificity, credibility, bias, and corroboration.
  • Circumstantial evidence: Facts supporting an inference without directly proving command, knowledge, or intent.
  • Reasonable inference: An analytical conclusion clearly separated from established fact.
  • Speculation: A possibility lacking adequate supporting evidence.
  • Brand conflation: Treating separate Supreme Councils, Valleys, Prince Hall systems, charities, or degree holders as one actor.

  • Supreme Council — Governing body of one jurisdiction.
  • Sovereign Grand Commander — Senior Supreme Council officer.
  • S.G.I.G. — Sovereign Grand Inspector General.
  • Valley — Local Scottish Rite geographic organization.
  • Orient — State or territorial jurisdiction.
  • Lodge of Perfection — Early Scottish Rite degree body.

  • Prince Hall Scottish Rite — Separate Black Masonic Scottish Rite systems.
  • Cerneauism — Competing Scottish Rite lineage associated with Joseph Cerneau.
