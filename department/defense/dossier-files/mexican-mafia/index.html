Mexican Mafia

Overview

The Mexican Mafia, commonly called La Eme or EME, is a California-origin prison gang whose members and associates exercise influence inside correctional institutions and over portions of the Southern California street-gang environment.

It began in the late 1950s within the California correctional system. California government sources trace its origin to Deuel Vocational Institution in Tracy.

The organization is not the government of Mexico, a Mexican drug cartel, the Sicilian Mafia, or a representative body for Mexican Americans.

Its influence exceeds its formal membership because individual members may use associates, incarcerated intermediaries, neighborhood shot callers, Sureño gangs, relatives, businesses, and criminal partners to transmit orders, collect money, distribute drugs, and enforce compliance.

Core Conclusion

The Mexican Mafia is best classified as: a durable California prison-based criminal organization whose comparatively limited formal membership can exercise disproportionate influence through decentralized member authority, Sureño gang networks, extortion, drug commerce, violence, and prison-to-street communication.

It should not be treated as one ordinary street gang or one fully centralized corporation. Members may cooperate under shared rules and identity while controlling separate territories, crews, street gangs, revenue streams, and associates.

For Malone purposes, the organization is external, untrusted, and operationally adverse wherever verified access, coercion, recruitment, criminal commerce, intimidation, or violence can affect Malone personnel, facilities, programs, or communities.

Name and Language

“La Eme” refers to the Spanish pronunciation of the letter M.

The name “Mexican Mafia” is a criminal brand. It does not establish any lawful relationship with Mexico, Mexican public institutions, Mexican nationals, or Mexican American communities.

Not the Government of Mexico

The Mexican Mafia is a United States prison-gang organization.

No authority from the Mexican state is created by its name, ethnic identity, or criminal relationships in Mexico.

Not a Mexican Drug Cartel

The Mexican Mafia is not interchangeable with the Sinaloa Cartel, Arellano Félix Organization, Jalisco New Generation Cartel, Gulf Cartel, or other Mexico-based trafficking organizations.

Members or associates may transact with cartel-linked suppliers. A business relationship does not establish merger, ownership, or universal command.

Not the Sicilian or Italian Mafia

The Mexican Mafia adopted a “mafia” identity but is not a family within Italian-American La Cosa Nostra.

Similar criminal terminology does not establish shared lineage or governance.

California Mexican Mafia Versus Texas Mexican Mafia

The Texas Mexican Mafia, also known as Mexikanemi or Emi, developed separately in the Texas prison system during the early 1980s.

It should not be treated as a Texas chapter automatically controlled by the California Mexican Mafia.

Other Similar Names

Arizona, New Mexico, federal, and local prison or street organizations may use names that include “Mexican Mafia,” “Mafia,” “Eme,” or the letter M.

Identity must be established through organizational lineage, location, verified relationships, and conduct rather than name similarity alone.

Historical Background

The Mexican Mafia arose in a correctional environment where incarcerated gang members sought protection, influence, status, control of illicit markets, and collective power.

Its early development joined selected Southern California gang members under a prison-based identity that could supersede neighborhood rivalries when shared interests required cooperation.

Origins at Deuel Vocational Institution

California Department of Justice reports identify Deuel Vocational Institution in Tracy as the organization’s late-1950s origin.

Later transfers of members to other institutions helped spread the organization rather than containing it within one facility.

Early Prison Markets

Historical California reporting describes early control over prison gambling, contraband, narcotics, and coercive activities.

These markets created both revenue and leverage over incarcerated people.

Expansion Through Transfers

Correctional transfers can disperse gang members across institutions.

Dispersal may expand contacts, recruit new participants, establish influence in additional facilities, and link prison authority to outside communities.

Prison-to-Street Development

The organization developed the capacity to influence street gangs through incarcerated members, released members, associates, relatives, visitors, contraband communications, and recognized neighborhood intermediaries.

Specific command must be proven in each case.

Organizational Structure

Official sources frequently describe the Mexican Mafia as loosely structured but governed by strong internal expectations.

It does not necessarily operate through one public headquarters, one annual election, or one universal geographic commander.

Individual members may hold substantial authority over particular facilities, neighborhoods, gangs, or revenue streams.

Member Autonomy

A formal member may exercise authority independently within an established sphere while cooperating with other members on shared objectives.

Autonomy does not eliminate collective responsibility when multiple members authorize, finance, enforce, or knowingly benefit from the same enterprise.

Collective Identity

Shared identity can create mutual recognition, protection, enforcement, and obligations across institutions and neighborhoods.

It can also allow a small number of members to magnify their authority through fear of organization-wide retaliation.

No Universal Public Chain of Command

Public records do not establish one continuously functioning national president who personally directs every Mexican Mafia member, associate, Sureño gang, or California prison.

Leadership analysis should remain member-specific, territory-specific, facility-specific, and case-specific.

Formal Members

Formal membership is generally treated as selective and durable.

Official estimates have varied substantially over time. Estimates must be dated and should not be presented as permanent counts.

Associates

Associates may collect money, move drugs, carry messages, obtain weapons, provide transportation, maintain accounts, arrange meetings, or enforce orders without holding formal membership.

Association must not be confused with full organizational status.

Sureño Street Gangs

Sureños are a broad collection of Southern California-rooted street-gang identities associated with the number thirteen and deference to Mexican Mafia authority in many custodial and street contexts.

Sureños are not one single street gang, and not every Sureño is a Mexican Mafia member.

Sureño Set Autonomy

Sureño gangs may retain their own neighborhood names, leaders, rivalries, territories, and criminal interests.

They may fight one another on the street while recognizing common prison alignments or complying with orders attributed to Mexican Mafia authority.

Shot Callers and Intermediaries

Court records often use terms such as shot caller for a person who directs local gang activity or serves as an intermediary.

The title does not automatically establish formal Mexican Mafia membership.

Neighborhood Control

Individual members or associates may claim authority over one or more street gangs, neighborhoods, jail modules, facilities, or illicit markets.

Claimed control must be tested against communications, payments, witness testimony, enforcement actions, and corroborated conduct.

Correctional Leadership

Incarcerated members may retain influence through visitors, contraband telephones, written messages, coded communications, released associates, and trusted intermediaries.

This dossier does not reproduce operational communication methods.

Street Leadership

Released members and trusted associates may coordinate revenue collection, drug distribution, discipline, meetings, and relationships with street gangs.

Street leadership remains distinct from authority inside a particular prison or jail.

Membership and Recruitment

Formal recruitment has historically drawn from experienced Southern California Hispanic gang members encountered in correctional settings.

Ethnicity, incarceration, neighborhood residence, or Sureño identity alone does not prove Mexican Mafia membership.

Selection and Reputation

Recruitment may depend upon reputation, demonstrated loyalty, violence, criminal competence, prison standing, personal relationships, and perceived usefulness.

Public claims about selection should be treated cautiously unless supported by authenticated records or adjudicated evidence.

Youth Exposure

Youth may encounter Mexican Mafia influence indirectly through neighborhood gangs, incarcerated relatives, drug markets, intimidation, music, social media, or local reputation.

Most exposed youth are not formal members.

Women and the Organization

Women may serve as associates, relatives, financial facilitators, couriers, visitors, business operators, intermediaries, witnesses, victims, or participants in criminal enterprises.

Gender does not establish innocence, membership, or authority. Responsibility depends upon verified conduct and knowledge.

Family Relationships

Relatives may be used to transmit messages, hold property, move money, provide transportation, or support incarcerated members.

Family relationship alone does not establish criminal participation.

Former Members and Disengaged Associates

Current status must be assessed rather than assumed from historic affiliation.

Disengagement may create continuing safety risks because former participants can possess sensitive information or face retaliation.

Funding

Documented revenue sources include drug trafficking, extortion, illegal gambling, money laundering, robbery, and other criminal activity.

Revenue may support incarcerated members, weapons, drugs, legal expenses, outside operations, associates, and personal enrichment.

Extortion and Taxation

Federal and California cases document demands for payments from drug dealers, street gangs, businesses, and criminal actors operating within claimed areas.

Criminally imposed “taxes” are extortion, not lawful taxation.

Drug Trafficking

Drug trafficking remains a central documented revenue source.

Cases involve methamphetamine, heroin, cocaine, fentanyl, marijuana, and other controlled substances.

Fentanyl and Synthetic Opioids

Recent indictments include fentanyl trafficking allegations.

Synthetic opioids increase public lethality and may expose users, families, employees, students, and first responders to severe harm.

Methamphetamine

Methamphetamine distribution appears repeatedly in Mexican Mafia and affiliated-gang prosecutions.

Supply may involve domestic networks, cross-border sources, or relationships with independent traffickers.

Contraband in Prisons and Jails

Criminal investigations document smuggling of drugs, telephones, weapons, and other prohibited items into correctional institutions.

Contraband can preserve outside command, facilitate violence, and sustain illicit markets.

Illegal Gambling

Illegal gambling has been documented both historically and in recent prosecutions.

Gambling enterprises may generate revenue, debt, leverage, money-laundering opportunities, and control over locations.

Money Laundering

Money laundering conceals criminal proceeds through accounts, businesses, transfers, purchases, or third parties.

Legitimate-looking activity must not be presumed criminal without evidence tracing funds and knowledge.

Legitimate Businesses

Criminal organizations may use lawful businesses for meetings, employment, reputation, laundering, storage, or access.

A business located in a gang-affected neighborhood is not affiliated merely because members patronize it.

Murder

Convictions and indictments document murders attributed to Mexican Mafia members, associates, and controlled enterprises.

Responsibility must identify the defendant, victim, purpose, authorization, procedural status, and organizational level.

Attempted Murder and Assault

Violence may be used against rivals, noncompliant gang members, debtors, witnesses, suspected informants, incarcerated people, and internal opponents.

An indictment remains an allegation until adjudicated.

Kidnapping

Recent federal allegations include kidnapping connected to extortion, enforcement, and control of criminal activity.

Kidnapping allegations should be tracked through plea, trial, dismissal, conviction, sentence, and appeal.

Witness Intimidation

Witness retaliation and intimidation are severe institutional risks.

Fear can prevent victims, residents, employees, and participants from reporting threats or cooperating with lawful investigations.

Internal Discipline

Government cases describe violence or threats used to punish disobedience, unpaid debts, unauthorized activity, disrespect, cooperation with authorities, or failure to carry out orders.

This dossier does not reproduce internal disciplinary rules.

Violence as Governance

The organization can function as an unauthorized coercive authority by deciding who may sell drugs, collect money, operate gambling, associate, or remain in a location.

Such control is criminal domination rather than legitimate government.

Community Harm

Harm falls heavily upon Latino and other working-class communities where affiliated gangs operate.

  • Homicide and physical injury.
  • Drug addiction and overdose.
  • Business extortion.
  • Youth recruitment.
  • Family trauma.
  • Witness fear.
  • Neighborhood displacement.
  • Increased policing and incarceration.

Threat to Latino Communities

Mexican Mafia members do not represent Mexican Americans, immigrants, Latinos, or residents of neighborhoods they claim to control.

Ethnic identity must not be used to shield criminal authority or justify racial profiling.

Threat to Youth

Youth may be recruited, coerced, exploited as couriers, exposed to violence, or pressured through family and neighborhood relationships.

Prevention should provide credible safety, education, employment, recreation, and reporting alternatives.

Threat to Families

Families may face demands for money, pressure to move messages, searches, retaliation, loss through incarceration, and fear for relatives attempting to disengage.

Threat to Businesses

Businesses may face extortion, forced association, unauthorized use of premises, laundering attempts, employee coercion, or reputational harm.

Controls should focus on conduct and evidence, not ethnicity or location.

Relationship to Sureños

The Mexican Mafia’s influence over Sureño gangs is one of its principal force-multiplying mechanisms.

The relationship can include tribute, dispute control, prison alignment, orders, protection claims, drug commerce, and violent enforcement.

Relationship to MS-13

Federal cases describe MS-13 in California as operating within a broader Southern California gang environment where Mexican Mafia authority may be recognized.

MS-13 remains a separate organization with its own cliques and transnational leadership relationships.

Relationship to 18th Street

18th Street is a separate multinational street-gang network.

Particular 18th Street factions may recognize Sureño or Mexican Mafia authority in relevant California or custodial contexts.

Relationship to Other Sureño Gangs

Puente-13, Wilmas, Lennox-13, El Monte Flores, Pomona gangs, and many other named gangs have appeared in cases involving Mexican Mafia relationships.

Each gang remains separately identifiable and should receive its own responsibility assessment.

Relationship to Nuestra Familia

Nuestra Familia developed as a rival California prison gang associated with northern California Hispanic gang structures.

Mexican Mafia–Nuestra Familia conflict contributed to the broader Sureño–Norteño division.

Relationship to Norteños

Norteños are a broad family of northern California-rooted street gangs often associated with Nuestra Familia.

Norteño identity is not equivalent to formal Nuestra Familia membership.

Relationship to the Aryan Brotherhood

Prison gangs may cooperate, remain neutral, or conflict based upon facility, race-based alignment, commerce, and immediate strategic interests.

No permanent universal alliance should be inferred without current evidence.

Relationship to Black Prison and Street Gangs

Relationships with Black gangs may include rivalry, transactional cooperation, prison conflict, drug commerce, or local nonaggression.

Race-based assumptions are inadequate for case attribution.

Relationship to Mexican Cartels

Members and associates may obtain drugs from cartel-linked suppliers or facilitate distribution networks.

Supplier relationships do not establish that one cartel owns the Mexican Mafia or that every member works for the same cartel.

Relationship to La Familia Michoacana

A 2013 federal case alleged efforts to build an alliance involving La Familia Michoacana and Mexican Mafia-linked actors.

A specific alleged alliance should not be generalized into permanent control over all members or all later cartel relationships.

Relationship to Government

The organization is adversarial to lawful correctional, judicial, and public-safety authority where those institutions disrupt its markets, communications, or coercive control.

No government sponsorship of the Mexican Mafia as an institution is established in this dossier.

Law-Enforcement Response

Federal, state, county, and municipal agencies have used racketeering, narcotics, firearms, money-laundering, violent-crime, and conspiracy laws against members and associates.

Task-force participation does not erase each agency’s separate authority and responsibility.

Correctional Response

Correctional authorities use searches, intelligence, housing decisions, contraband interdiction, disciplinary proceedings, and criminal referrals.

Security classifications must remain reviewable because incorrect gang identification can produce severe consequences.

Prosecution Under RICO

Federal racketeering law allows prosecutors to charge patterns of criminal conduct connected to an enterprise.

A RICO indictment is not itself a conviction. Enterprise allegations and predicate offenses must be adjudicated.

Political Activity

The Mexican Mafia has no established conventional political party, public electoral platform, or lawful representative wing.

Individual efforts to corrupt officials, manipulate institutions, or use political contacts should be assessed as specific conduct rather than a universal political program.

Ideology

The organization’s operative commitments center on loyalty, power, criminal revenue, prison solidarity, reputation, and enforcement.

It should not be classified as a legitimate ethnic-rights, civil-rights, revolutionary, or community-governance organization.

Ethnic Identity and Criminal Authority

Shared Mexican or Mexican American identity can be used as a source of solidarity and recruitment.

Ethnic identity does not confer authority over communities and does not transfer organizational responsibility to an ethnic population.

Prison Protection Narrative

Prison gangs may claim that membership or alignment provides protection.

Protection becomes coercive when an organization creates or exploits the threat, imposes payments, requires violence, or punishes refusal.

Public Misconception: One National Mafia

The California Mexican Mafia is influential but should not be described as the universal headquarters of every Hispanic gang or every organization using the words “Mexican Mafia.”

Public Misconception: Every Sureño Is a Member

Sureño affiliation can indicate alignment within a broader gang system.

It does not establish formal Mexican Mafia membership.

Public Misconception: Every Hispanic Inmate Is Affiliated

Hispanic identity, incarceration, language, neighborhood, or association does not prove gang status.

Classification requires corroborated evidence.

Public Misconception: Mexico Controls the Organization

The organization originated in California.

Cross-border criminal relationships do not convert it into an agency of the Mexican government.

Public Misconception: Cartels and La Eme Are the Same

Cartels are separate trafficking organizations with their own leadership, territory, and priorities.

Transactions, alliances, or shared suppliers do not erase that distinction.

Symbols and Indicators

Public records associate the organization with references to the letter M, the number thirteen, and other gang imagery.

Symbols are contextual indicators, not conclusive proof of membership, authority, or criminal conduct.

Tattoos

Tattoos may reflect current membership, past affiliation, coercion, neighborhood identity, imitation, or prison survival.

A tattoo should be evaluated with current conduct, admissions, communications, witnesses, and corroborating evidence.

Social Media

Social media may be used for reputation, threats, recruitment, memorials, disputes, intelligence collection, or imitation.

Online content may also be false, performative, copied, hacked, or outdated.

Music and Popular Culture

References in music, film, documentaries, books, and online media can spread awareness and mythology.

Artistic reference or neighborhood association does not establish formal membership.

False Identification

False positives may result from ethnicity, neighborhood residence, family relationships, incarceration history, tattoos, clothing, social media, or uncorroborated informant claims.

False intelligence harms innocent people and weakens legitimate security.

Gang Validation

Correctional validation may rely upon admissions, associations, tattoos, communications, conduct, confidential sources, photographs, and records.

Validation is an administrative or investigative classification, not a substitute for proof of a specific crime.

Confidential Informants

Informants may possess valuable access and also incentives to exaggerate, retaliate, reduce charges, obtain payment, or shift responsibility.

Informant evidence should be tested for basis of knowledge, consistency, corroboration, motive, and prior reliability.

Cooperating Witnesses

Cooperating witnesses may explain structure, communications, payments, authority, and criminal events.

Their testimony should be evaluated alongside records, physical evidence, surveillance, financial evidence, and cross-examination.

Institutional Responsibility Test

  1. Identify the exact organization. Separate California EME, Texas Mexican Mafia, cartels, Sureños, and local gangs.
  2. Identify the individual. Record legal name, aliases, location, dates, and alleged role.
  3. Determine status. Separate member, associate, Sureño affiliate, relative, coerced participant, former member, and imitator.
  4. Identify the local structure. Determine prison, jail, street gang, crew, neighborhood, or business involved.
  5. Identify the conduct. State the order, payment, threat, shipment, assault, murder, communication, or financial act.
  6. Locate authorization. Determine whether conduct was individual, member-directed, jointly authorized, or merely attributed by rumor.
  7. Trace resources. Identify money, drugs, weapons, vehicles, accounts, property, facilities, and communications.
  8. Test knowledge and coercion. Determine what each participant knew and whether participation was voluntary.
  9. Preserve procedural status. Do not convert allegations into convictions.
  10. Assign responsibility proportionately. Do not attribute one person’s conduct to all Sureños, Latinos, inmates, or communities.

Evidence Classification

  • Adjudicated fact: Established through plea, conviction, final judgment, or court finding.
  • Documented fact: Supported by authenticated records, communications, financial evidence, or corroborated sources.
  • Official allegation: Charged or alleged by a named authority but not adjudicated.
  • Government classification: Agency designation or validation subject to legal and factual review.
  • Organizational admission: Authenticated statement by a member or authorized actor.
  • Independent reporting: Reputable reporting evaluated for source access and corroboration.
  • Witness testimony: Account tested for credibility, incentive, and consistency.
  • Historical claim: Interpretation based on archives or retrospective accounts.
  • Rumor: Unverified prison, street, media, or online claim.
  • Speculation: Inference lacking sufficient evidence for classification.

Internal Divisions

Disputes may arise over territory, authority, payments, discipline, personal grievances, drug markets, and recognition of orders.

Succession and Leadership Change

Arrests, deaths, incapacitation, transfers, protective custody, and cooperation can alter local authority.

Historic leadership information should not be treated as current without recent corroboration.

Controlled Opposition Assessment

The Mexican Mafia’s conduct can weaken Latino communities, increase incarceration, and create justification for aggressive policing.

Those consequences do not prove that an outside government, political party, cartel, or racial adversary created or centrally controls the organization.

Controlled opposition is therefore not established.

Proximity to the Malone Network

No direct institutional relationship between the Mexican Mafia and the Malone Network is established in this dossier.

Potential proximity may arise through:

  • Programs serving gang-affected neighborhoods.
  • Correctional education and reentry.
  • Youth programs.
  • Employment and contracting.
  • Family relationships.
  • Facilities located in claimed territory.
  • Participants under extortion or retaliation pressure.
  • Attempts to use Malone programs for recruitment, money movement, or meetings.

Conceptual Proximity to Malone

Both prison gangs and legitimate institutions respond to needs involving protection, belonging, order, identity, and organization.

The similarity ends where authority depends upon coercion, criminal commerce, retaliation, secrecy, and unaccountable violence.

Alignment Assessment

The Mexican Mafia is unaligned and adverse to Malone.

Active membership, criminal obligation, undisclosed gang command, or facilitation is incompatible with sensitive Malone roles.

Former members, relatives, victims, and people under coercion require individualized assessment.

Hostility Assessment

No organization-wide operation directed specifically against Malone is established.

Hostility becomes direct where verified actors threaten Malone personnel, extort participants, recruit through programs, misuse facilities, conceal criminal activity, or retaliate against reporting.

  • Demands for payment or permission to operate.
  • Use of Malone facilities for meetings, drugs, weapons, or money movement.
  • Undisclosed active gang authority in a sensitive role.
  • Pressure on correctional, reentry, or youth participants.
  • Attempts to identify witnesses or confidential reporters.

Protective Posture for Malone

  • Use conduct-based, role-appropriate vetting.
  • Do not classify by race, surname, neighborhood, incarceration, or tattoos alone.
  • Protect participant addresses, schedules, family information, and correctional records.
  • Separate active obligation from past affiliation and rehabilitation.
  • Establish visitor, facility, vehicle, financial, and technology controls.
  • Provide confidential reporting and anti-retaliation procedures.
  • Escalate credible threats to qualified legal and public-safety professionals.
  • Preserve evidence and procedural status.
  • Prohibit vigilantism, collective punishment, and unsupported public accusation.
  • Support lawful disengagement and victim protection.

Correctional Program Controls

  • Minimize unnecessary disclosure of participant identities.
  • Control communications and visitor access consistent with law.
  • Separate educational service from intelligence collection unless legally authorized.
  • Document coercion and retaliation concerns.
  • Coordinate with facility authorities without surrendering independent institutional judgment.

Youth Program Controls

  • Train staff to recognize coercion without racial profiling.
  • Protect minors’ schedules and family data.
  • Prohibit recruitment, gang display used for intimidation, and criminal commerce.
  • Create safe reporting pathways.
  • Refer immediate threats to appropriate professionals.

Financial Controls

  • Verify vendors and beneficial ownership where risk warrants.
  • Require documented payment purposes.
  • Restrict cash handling.
  • Separate approval, custody, and reconciliation functions.
  • Investigate unexplained pressure, kickbacks, or payments.

Facility Controls

  • Maintain visitor and access rules.
  • Protect cameras, keys, credentials, and schedules.
  • Prohibit weapons, narcotics, unauthorized gambling, and intimidation.
  • Document threats and suspicious use of space.
  • Do not negotiate unlawful tribute or territorial permission.

Information Security

Gang-related threats can exploit addresses, family information, correctional status, witness identities, schedules, and personal communications.

Access should be limited to legitimate institutional need.

Witness and Reporter Protection

Reporting systems should minimize unnecessary exposure of identity and preserve records of threats.

Malone should not promise protection it lacks the legal or operational capacity to provide.

Disengagement and Rehabilitation

People can leave criminal activity and become constructive institutional participants.

Assessment should consider current obligations, time since activity, accountability, truthfulness, restitution, retaliation risk, relationships, and the sensitivity of the proposed role.

Victims and Coerced Participants

Some people associated with gang activity are victims, debtors, threatened relatives, coerced couriers, exploited partners, or people acting under immediate pressure.

Coercion affects responsibility but does not eliminate the need for safety controls.

Community Protection Without Gang Rule

Communities require protection from both organized crime and abusive or discriminatory government practices.

The answer is neither gang domination nor indiscriminate criminalization. Durable safety requires accountable institutions, consent, due process, professional capacity, and measurable outcomes.

Security Without Ethnic Profiling

Mexican Mafia history does not justify treating Mexican Americans, immigrants, Spanish speakers, formerly incarcerated people, or residents of affected neighborhoods as gang members.

Overbroad suspicion produces inaccurate intelligence and damages cooperation.

Monitoring Priorities

  • Prison-to-street communications affecting participants.
  • Sureño recruitment in youth and reentry programs.
  • Extortion demands involving facilities, contractors, or businesses.
  • Threats against witnesses, staff, students, and families.
  • Use of legitimate businesses or nonprofits for criminal purposes.
  • False claims of Malone affiliation or recognition.
  • Current status of former members and disengaging associates.
  • Gang-validation errors and false identification.
  • Evidence of direct targeting, infiltration, or institutional capture.

Responsible Analysis

The Mexican Mafia should not be romanticized as a protector of Hispanic inmates or neighborhoods.

It should not be used as a pretext to stigmatize Mexican Americans, incarcerated people, immigrants, or entire communities.

The governing rule is: identify the exact organization, member, associate, street gang, facility, order, payment, and procedural status; separate criminal authority from ethnic identity; and assign responsibility only at the level supported by evidence.

Preliminary Malone Classification

The Mexican Mafia is not aligned with Malone.

Its preliminary classification is: California-origin prison-based criminal organization; historically rooted in late-1950s correctional gang consolidation; loosely structured but governed by durable shared identity and coercive rules; operationally extended through individual members, associates, and Sureño gang networks; external, untrusted, and adverse; documented involvement in murder, assault, kidnapping, extortion, narcotics, illegal gambling, money laundering, contraband, and witness intimidation; distinct from the Texas Mexican Mafia, Mexican cartels, Mexican people, and the government of Mexico; not proven controlled opposition; no direct Malone operation presently established; severe threat wherever verified access, recruitment, extortion, retaliation, or criminal command exists.
