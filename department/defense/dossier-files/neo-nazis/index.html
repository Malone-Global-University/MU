Neo-Nazis

Overview

Neo-Nazism is a post-1945 ideological family that preserves, adapts, or revives central features of Adolf Hitler's National Socialism.

Its recurring features include racial hierarchy, antisemitism, authoritarian rule, hostility to pluralist democracy, conspiracy narratives, and admiration for Nazi symbols, leaders, institutions, or methods.

“Neo-Nazis” does not identify one corporation, one international president, one treasury, or one universal chain of command. It identifies a fragmented environment containing formal organizations, small cells, propaganda networks, skinhead formations, prison-linked groups, accelerationist communities, unaffiliated adherents, and lone offenders.

Core Conclusion

Neo-Nazism is best classified as: a fragmented transnational extremist ideology and movement whose adherents seek racial domination or exclusion and whose violent factions have used threats, hate crimes, terrorism-related plots, intimidation, sabotage, murder, and online recruitment.

Not every person who possesses a prohibited-looking symbol, studies Nazi history, or makes an offensive statement belongs to a neo-Nazi organization. Membership, intent, present activity, and organizational authorization require evidence.

For Malone purposes, neo-Nazi organizations and active adherents are ideologically hostile and operationally adverse wherever they target Black people, Jewish people, religious institutions, immigrants, LGBTQ+ people, democratic institutions, journalists, public officials, or Malone personnel.

Definition

A neo-Nazi is a person or organization that consciously revives, defends, adapts, or materially identifies with Nazi ideology after the defeat of Nazi Germany in 1945.

The classification ordinarily requires more than general racism. Relevant indicators include explicit National Socialist doctrine, Hitler veneration, Holocaust denial or justification, Nazi racial theory, Nazi organizational models, or sustained use of Nazi symbols as political identity.

What Neo-Nazism Is Not

Neo-Nazism is not synonymous with every conservative, nationalist, populist, Republican, rural, military, Christian, German American, or White person.

It is also not synonymous with every white supremacist organization. Some Klan, prison-gang, separatist, or white-nationalist groups possess distinct histories, theology, structures, and symbols even when substantial ideological overlap exists.

Historical Nazi Foundation

The original Nazi Party was the German National Socialist German Workers' Party led by Adolf Hitler.

It established a one-party dictatorship, dismantled democratic institutions, pursued territorial war, imposed racial law, and carried out mass persecution and genocide, including the murder of six million Jews during the Holocaust.

Neo-Nazism inherits or reworks parts of this historical system. It should not be analyzed as an abstract aesthetic detached from the governing record of Nazi Germany.

Postwar Development

Nazi Germany ceased to exist in 1945, but adherents, propagandists, veterans, political activists, and later generations preserved or reconstructed Nazi beliefs.

Postwar movements varied. Some attempted electoral participation. Others formed propaganda organizations, paramilitary circles, racist skinhead networks, religious movements, prison alliances, or clandestine terrorist cells.

United States Precursors

Before the United States entered World War II, the German American Bund promoted pro-Nazi politics and modeled public activity on Nazi organizations.

The Bund was a prewar movement rather than a postwar neo-Nazi organization. It is historically related but should not be confused with later American neo-Nazi groups.

American Nazi Party

George Lincoln Rockwell founded the American Nazi Party during the postwar period and became one of the most visible American advocates of explicit National Socialism.

The organization promoted antisemitism, white supremacy, racial separation, Nazi imagery, and authoritarian politics.

Rockwell's organization and successor formations did not become the universal headquarters of all American neo-Nazis.

Fragmentation After Rockwell

Rockwell's 1967 death did not end American neo-Nazism.

Leadership disputes, ideological differences, law-enforcement pressure, personality conflict, regional autonomy, and changing technology produced numerous successor groups and independent organizations.

This fragmentation remains a defining feature.

Transnational Character

Modern neo-Nazi propaganda and networks cross national borders through websites, encrypted communications, digital publishing, travel, fundraising, music, and personal relationships.

A network may contain participants in several countries without possessing a conventional headquarters or enforceable international command.

No Universal Neo-Nazi Government

No evidence establishes one leader, council, treasury, constitution, or command system governing all neo-Nazis.

Separate groups may compete, accuse one another of weakness, reject rival racial or religious theories, or use the same symbols without operational cooperation.

Formal Organizations

Some neo-Nazi organizations adopt conventional forms such as:

  • Named national or regional leadership.
  • Membership applications.
  • Dues or donations.
  • Public websites and publications.
  • Demonstrations and conferences.
  • Local chapters or units.

Formal structure does not establish that every member knows of or approves every act committed by another member.

Small Cells

Violent neo-Nazi activity frequently occurs through small groups rather than large membership organizations.

Small cells may be built around friendship, online contact, military or prison experience, shared propaganda, weapons interests, or a specific plot.

Capability can therefore exceed apparent membership size.

Online Networks

Online neo-Nazi networks may operate through channels, forums, private groups, image boards, gaming spaces, audio programs, file repositories, or encrypted messaging services.

Some are propaganda communities. Others facilitate threats, recruitment, target selection, criminal instruction, or transnational coordination.

Lone Actors

A lone offender may act without receiving a direct order from a recognized organization.

Ideological inspiration, copied manifestos, online encouragement, and admiration for prior attackers can still influence conduct.

Responsibility remains individual unless coordination, material support, or authorization is proven.

Leaderless Resistance

Some white-supremacist and neo-Nazi propagandists advocate autonomous action without a conventional command hierarchy.

This model reduces the need for formal membership and can complicate attribution. It does not eliminate responsibility for solicitation, conspiracy, assistance, or criminal encouragement when evidence establishes those acts.

Accelerationism

Neo-Nazi accelerationism seeks to intensify disorder, conflict, or institutional collapse in the expectation that a racial authoritarian order can emerge.

Accelerationist adherents may glorify mass attackers, encourage violence, attack social trust, or target infrastructure.

Not every neo-Nazi organization is accelerationist, and not every accelerationist is part of one organization.

Terrorgram Collective

The Terrorgram Collective developed as a transnational online propaganda and coordination network promoting white-supremacist accelerationism and violence.

In January 2025, the United States designated the Terrorgram Collective and three leaders as Specially Designated Global Terrorists.

The designation concerns the named network and designated persons. It does not convert every Telegram user or every neo-Nazi into a designated terrorist.

Atomwaffen Division

Atomwaffen Division emerged as a violent American neo-Nazi organization associated with accelerationist ideology.

Federal prosecutions documented members and leaders conspiring to threaten and intimidate journalists, advocates, religious institutions, and minority communities.

The name, successor branding, former membership, and independent imitation require separate verification.

Brandon Russell and Infrastructure Conspiracy

In February 2025, a federal jury convicted Atomwaffen founder Brandon Russell of conspiring to damage an energy facility in support of neo-Nazi accelerationist beliefs.

The conviction establishes responsibility for the charged conspiracy. It does not establish that every former Atomwaffen participant joined that plan.

The Base

The Base is a multi-state and transnational white-supremacist organization that has used neo-Nazi symbols, encrypted communications, survivalist activity, and accelerationist concepts.

Federal cases established that members coordinated vandalism targeting Jewish and Black institutions and communities.

The Base should not be confused with every person using the word “base” or with unrelated political slogans.

The Base Synagogue Case

A federal jury convicted a Base member for conspiring to interfere with civil rights and defacing a Michigan synagogue with neo-Nazi symbols.

The defendant was sentenced in June 2024.

The conviction provides adjudicated evidence for the named defendant and conspiracy.

Feuerkrieg Division

Feuerkrieg Division was described by federal prosecutors as an international racially or ethnically motivated violent extremist group with neo-Nazi ideology.

Its members encouraged attacks against minorities, Jewish people, LGBTQ+ people, journalists, government, and infrastructure.

Feuerkrieg Journalist Threat

In April 2024, a Feuerkrieg Division leader received a federal prison sentence for conspiring to threaten a Brooklyn journalist.

The case demonstrates how online propaganda can become targeted witness or media intimidation.

Maniac Murder Cult

Maniac Murder Cult is a transnational violent extremist network identified by federal prosecutors as adhering to neo-Nazi ideology.

Its propaganda promoted violence against Jewish people, racial minorities, schools, and other persons it classified as undesirable.

2026 Maniac Murder Cult Sentence

In May 2026, a federal court sentenced a Maniac Murder Cult leader to fifteen years in prison for soliciting hate crimes and distributing instructions connected to planned mass violence.

The adjudicated sentence concerns the named leader. Claims about other members or inspired offenders require separate evidence.

CVLT Child-Exploitation Network

Federal proceedings identified CVLT as an online criminal enterprise that combined neo-Nazism, nihilism, sadism, and child sexual exploitation.

Members coerced minors into producing abuse material and images of self-harm.

This network illustrates that extremist environments can merge ideological violence with predatory criminal activity unrelated to conventional political organizing.

2026 CVLT Guilty Plea

In March 2026, a defendant pleaded guilty to leading a child-exploitation enterprise connected to CVLT.

Other defendants and proceedings must be tracked according to plea, trial, sentencing, and extradition status.

National Socialist Movement

The National Socialist Movement has operated as an American neo-Nazi organization using formal leadership, public demonstrations, publications, and Nazi-derived symbolism.

Leadership, membership, legal ownership, and activity levels have changed over time. Historical identification does not prove present authority.

National Alliance

The National Alliance became an influential American neo-Nazi organization through publications, propaganda, and recruitment.

The name remains associated with white-supremacist ideology and historical criminal cases, including a convicted attempted bombing connected to an affiliate.

Current activity and local claims require verification.

Aryan Nations

Aryan Nations combined neo-Nazi racial doctrine with Christian Identity theology and operated through compounds, publications, gatherings, and affiliated individuals.

Its history, successors, local claims, and criminal associates should be distinguished rather than treated as one uninterrupted organization.

Blood and Honour

Blood and Honour identifies a transnational white-power music and skinhead network with neo-Nazi associations.

Federal cases involving a Tampa formation documented murders connected to members of a local Blood and Honour group.

Local units, national structures, music distribution, and individual acts require separate attribution.

Hammerskins

Hammerskins identify a white-supremacist skinhead network with regional and international formations.

Federal cases have documented hate-crime conduct by members or support groups.

A support organization, social relationship, or attendance at an event does not by itself establish full membership or authorization.

Racist Skinhead Networks

Racist skinheads are a specific subculture within the wider extremist environment.

Skinhead appearance originated in a broader, racially mixed youth subculture. A shaved head, boots, music preference, or working-class style does not prove neo-Nazi belief.

Classification requires ideological and behavioral evidence.

Prison Gangs

Some white-supremacist prison gangs use Nazi symbols and racial ideology.

Their primary operations may center on protection, drugs, extortion, violence, and prison commerce rather than a complete National Socialist political program.

Aryan Brotherhood, Aryan Circle, New Aryan Empire, and similar organizations should be profiled as distinct entities.

Ku Klux Klan Distinction

The Ku Klux Klan predates German Nazism and developed from American racial terror, Protestant nativism, and resistance to Black citizenship.

Some Klan factions later adopted Nazi ideas or cooperated with neo-Nazis. Historical overlap does not erase their separate organizational lineages.

White Nationalist Distinction

White nationalism broadly seeks political power, territory, or state identity organized around White ethnicity.

Neo-Nazism is a narrower category requiring meaningful identification with National Socialism or Nazi-derived doctrine.

Some white nationalists reject the neo-Nazi label while maintaining substantial ideological overlap.

Alt-Right Distinction

The “alt-right” was a broader online and political label containing white nationalists, provocateurs, antisemitic activists, misogynists, and other participants.

Some participants were neo-Nazis. Others were not.

The label should not substitute for evidence of an individual's specific beliefs, organization, or conduct.

Christian Identity Overlap

Christian Identity is a racist religious ideology that may claim White people are the true Israelites and depict Jews and non-White people through hostile racial theology.

Some neo-Nazi organizations adopted Christian Identity. Other neo-Nazis are secular, pagan, atheist, or hostile to Christianity.

Mainstream Christianity is not Christian Identity.

Ideology

Neo-Nazi ideology varies by faction but commonly combines:

  • White racial supremacy or separatism.
  • Antisemitism.
  • Authoritarian political order.
  • Hostility to liberal democracy and equal citizenship.
  • Conspiracy theories about demographic or political control.
  • Admiration for Nazi Germany or its leaders.

Antisemitism

Antisemitism is central to most neo-Nazi systems.

Jewish people may be falsely portrayed as a racial enemy controlling finance, media, government, immigration, war, civil rights, or social change.

These conspiracy claims are not evidence-based and have historically supported exclusion, intimidation, and mass violence.

White Racial Hierarchy

Neo-Nazi doctrine assigns moral, political, or biological superiority to a constructed White or “Aryan” race.

Different groups disagree about who qualifies as White, which ethnic groups are acceptable, and whether separation, expulsion, domination, or extermination is desired.

These internal differences do not negate the common rejection of equal human dignity.

Authoritarianism

Neo-Nazi political models commonly subordinate individual rights, independent courts, free elections, pluralism, and opposition parties to racial state power and leader authority.

Public claims of order or national revival should be evaluated against the historical record of Nazi dictatorship.

Anti-Democratic Orientation

Some neo-Nazis participate in elections or public demonstrations while seeking to replace constitutional democracy with racial authoritarian government.

Tactical use of democratic rights does not establish commitment to democratic equality.

Holocaust Denial and Distortion

Holocaust denial rejects or radically minimizes the documented Nazi genocide of European Jews.

Distortion may acknowledge deaths while denying systematic intent, responsibility, scale, or the operation of extermination centers.

Denial functions as propaganda rehabilitation of Nazism rather than legitimate historical correction.

Conspiracy Narratives

Neo-Nazi propaganda often attributes complex social problems to secret racial, religious, financial, or governmental control.

Repetition, selective evidence, fabricated documents, and circular reasoning substitute for verifiable causal analysis.

Demographic Panic

Neo-Nazi recruitment commonly frames immigration, interracial marriage, declining birth rates, integration, or cultural change as an existential campaign against White people.

This converts ordinary demographic and political disputes into a claimed emergency that can be used to justify dehumanization or violence.

Anti-Black Hostility

Neo-Nazi ideology commonly denies Black equality and depicts Black citizenship, political power, education, family life, or cultural influence as a racial threat.

Historical and modern cases document violence, threats, vandalism, and intimidation directed at Black people and institutions.

Anti-Immigrant Hostility

Neo-Nazi groups may portray immigrants as invaders, racial contaminants, economic enemies, or instruments of conspiracy.

Immigration-policy disagreement alone is not neo-Nazism. Classification requires the broader ideological and evidentiary context.

Anti-LGBTQ Hostility

Many neo-Nazi organizations treat LGBTQ+ people as evidence of social degeneration or as enemies of racial reproduction.

Federal cases document violent factions encouraging attacks against LGBTQ+ communities.

Misogyny and Gender Roles

Neo-Nazi doctrine frequently assigns women a subordinate reproductive and domestic role within the racial community.

Some modern networks also combine misogyny, sexual violence, nihilism, and exploitation.

Women may nevertheless serve as members, organizers, propagandists, recruiters, financiers, or offenders.

Eugenics

Neo-Nazi thought often supports biological ranking, coerced reproduction, sterilization, exclusion, or removal of people classified as genetically undesirable.

Historical Nazi eugenics moved from discrimination and forced sterilization to systematic killing.

Internal Divisions

Neo-Nazi factions divide over:

  • Electoral politics versus revolutionary violence.
  • Christian, pagan, secular, or esoteric religion.
  • Centralized parties versus autonomous cells.
  • Public activism versus clandestine operations.
  • Definitions of White identity.
  • Foreign alliances and geopolitical conflicts.
  • Leadership, money, personality, and security disputes.

Leadership

Leadership exists at the organizational, chapter, cell, channel, or project level.

A self-declared commander may control only a small group or online audience. Titles should be verified through records, communications, finances, testimony, and demonstrated authority.

Membership

Membership standards vary.

Some groups require applications, dues, oaths, attendance, or probationary status. Others operate through informal recognition, private chats, social relationships, or participation in specific acts.

Ideological sympathy is not always organizational membership.

Members and Associates

A dossier should distinguish:

  • Founder or leader.
  • Formal member.
  • Prospective member.
  • Associate or facilitator.
  • Propagandist.
  • Donor or customer.
  • Former member.
  • Undercover officer or informant.
  • Uninvolved relative or acquaintance.

Recruitment

Recruitment may offer identity, status, belonging, certainty, grievance explanation, masculinity, protection, adventure, or perceived historical purpose.

Recruiters may exploit alienation, family conflict, unemployment, humiliation, incarceration, victimization, political anger, or social isolation.

Youth Recruitment

Young people may encounter neo-Nazi content through humor, gaming, music, short-form video, private chats, school conflict, or older peers.

Recruitment can begin with coded jokes or broad grievance content before advancing to explicit racial doctrine.

Online Radicalization

Online radicalization is rarely a simple one-way process.

A person may actively seek extremist material, receive algorithmic exposure, join peer communities, imitate admired figures, or move between ideological and criminal networks.

Platform use alone does not prove radicalization.

Memes and Irony

Neo-Nazi content may be presented as humor, irony, trolling, or shock performance.

Some users remain performative. Others use humor to normalize doctrine, identify receptive recruits, evade moderation, or deny sincere intent.

Context and repeated conduct are essential.

Music and Subculture

White-power music has served as propaganda, recruitment, fundraising, event culture, and transnational networking.

Possession of music is an indicator requiring context, not automatic proof of membership or violence.

Public Demonstrations

Neo-Nazi organizations may use marches, banners, masks, leafleting, rallies, or public meetings to generate publicity and demonstrate presence.

Attendance should be distinguished from leadership, membership, criminal conduct, counterprotest, media work, or observation.

Propaganda

Propaganda may include websites, books, podcasts, flyers, stickers, videos, manifestos, music, merchandise, livestreams, and private digital files.

The objective may be recruitment, fundraising, intimidation, historical revision, target dehumanization, or encouragement of action.

Symbols

Neo-Nazi symbols may include direct Nazi imagery, modified runes, coded numbers, historical military insignia, and newly created organizational logos.

This dossier does not reproduce a comprehensive symbol catalogue.

Symbols must be interpreted according to combination, context, frequency, accompanying language, and verified conduct.

Swastika

The swastika is an ancient symbol used in many cultures and religions.

The Nazi Party appropriated a rotated form as its central emblem, making it a dominant modern symbol of Nazism, antisemitism, and racial terror in Western political contexts.

Religious and historical uses must not be automatically classified as neo-Nazi.

SS Runes and Black Sun

SS-style runes and the Black Sun are used in some neo-Nazi and white-supremacist environments.

They may also appear in historical study, media, tattoos, fantasy culture, or copied online imagery.

No isolated symbol should substitute for a complete assessment.

Coded Numbers

Numbers such as 14 and 88 may be used as white-supremacist or neo-Nazi codes in particular combinations.

The same numbers have ordinary meanings in addresses, dates, sports, ages, and personal identifiers.

Context controls.

Color and Clothing

Black clothing, military-style apparel, boots, masks, camouflage, or shaved heads are not proof of neo-Nazi identity.

Classification based on appearance alone creates false intelligence and may wrongly target nonmembers.

Political Activity

Neo-Nazi political activity can include public advocacy, demonstrations, candidate support, election participation, propaganda campaigns, disruption, or attempts to influence broader movements.

Political activity becomes criminal when it includes prohibited threats, violence, fraud, conspiracy, intimidation, or other offenses.

Electoral Participation

Some neo-Nazis seek ballot access or endorse candidates.

Others reject elections as illegitimate or strategically useless.

A candidate receiving unsolicited support does not establish alliance, authorization, knowledge, or ideological agreement.

Relationship to Major Political Parties

Neo-Nazism is not an official component of the Democratic Party or Republican Party.

Individual extremists may attempt to enter, pressure, exploit, or attach themselves to mainstream coalitions.

Responsibility requires evidence of acceptance, coordination, appointment, funding, policy adoption, or institutional protection.

Relationship to Government

Neo-Nazi groups generally oppose constitutional government when it protects equal citizenship or pluralism.

They may simultaneously seek government employment, military training, police access, public office, benefits, or legal protection.

Use of government institutions does not establish loyalty to constitutional principles.

Military and Law-Enforcement Access

Military or law-enforcement experience can provide status, networks, training, equipment familiarity, or institutional access.

Service history does not prove extremism.

Verified extremist conduct by personnel in sensitive roles warrants role-specific review, due process, and immediate protection of systems and people.

Activities

Neo-Nazi activity ranges from lawful but hostile expression to serious crime.

  • Publishing and propaganda.
  • Meetings and demonstrations.
  • Recruitment and fundraising.
  • Harassment and intimidation.
  • Vandalism and hate crimes.
  • Weapons offenses.
  • Criminal conspiracy and terrorism-related plots.

Violence

Documented neo-Nazi violence includes murder, assault, arson, bomb plots, shootings, threats, stalking, intimidation, sabotage conspiracies, and attacks on religious or minority institutions.

Violence may be organizationally ordered, jointly planned, individually initiated, or ideologically inspired.

Hate Crimes

A hate crime is a criminal act in which prohibited bias motivation satisfies applicable federal or state law.

Hateful belief or speech is not automatically a hate crime.

The underlying offense, statutory elements, jurisdiction, and procedural status must be identified.

Domestic Terrorism

Federal law defines domestic terrorism by characteristics of conduct, intent, and location.

The United States does not maintain a general public designation system for domestic terrorist organizations equivalent to the Foreign Terrorist Organization process.

Federal defendants are ordinarily charged with the underlying crimes rather than a standalone offense named “domestic terrorism.”

Critical-Infrastructure Threat

Accelerationist neo-Nazi actors have plotted attacks against energy facilities and other infrastructure.

These plots seek wider disruption beyond the immediate physical target.

Protective analysis should focus on credible intent, access, surveillance, procurement, communications, and operational preparation without publicizing exploitable vulnerabilities.

Threats Against Journalists

Neo-Nazi groups have targeted journalists and researchers who expose their identities, ideology, or operations.

Documented conduct includes doxxing, mailed threats, cyberstalking, public intimidation, and conspiracies designed to silence reporting.

Threats Against Religious Institutions

Synagogues are primary targets because antisemitism is central to neo-Nazism.

Mosques, Black churches, Christian congregations, and other religious institutions may also be targeted according to a faction's racial, religious, or political doctrine.

Threat to Black Communities

Neo-Nazi ideology directly rejects Black equality and can motivate violence, intimidation, propaganda, infiltration, and attacks on Black institutions.

Malone programs serving Black communities may possess elevated symbolic value to hostile actors even without prior direct contact.

Threat to Jewish Communities

Jewish people and institutions are central targets of neo-Nazi conspiracy and violence.

Threat assessment should consider religious calendars, public events, online targeting, prior harassment, and credible communications while avoiding unnecessary disclosure of security arrangements.

Threat to Immigrant Communities

Immigrants and refugees may be targeted through racialized invasion narratives, harassment, threats, or violence.

National origin, language, religion, or perceived immigration status can become substitutes for direct racial classification.

Threat to LGBTQ+ Communities

Neo-Nazi and accelerationist propaganda may encourage violence against LGBTQ+ people, events, institutions, and advocates.

Specific threats require preservation, reporting, and coordinated protective review.

Threat to Public Officials

Officials may be targeted for civil-rights enforcement, immigration policy, antisemitism legislation, prosecution, education policy, or personal identity.

The February 2026 federal sentencing of a self-identified neo-Nazi for threats mailed to a Jewish state legislator and rabbi illustrates this risk.

Threat to Schools and Youth

Schools can be targets, recruitment environments, or locations where symbols and threats first become visible.

Responses should separate protected expression, disciplinary violations, credible threats, bullying, criminal conduct, and mental-health or safeguarding concerns.

Criminal Activity

Neo-Nazi affiliation may coexist with ordinary criminal activity such as drug trafficking, firearms offenses, fraud, robbery, sexual exploitation, or organized gang crime.

The ideological and criminal enterprises may support one another, remain separate, or overlap only through individual participants.

Firearms Offenses

Federal cases document neo-Nazi and white-supremacist offenders unlawfully possessing, manufacturing, transporting, or acquiring firearms.

Lawful ownership is not proof of extremism.

Relevant evidence includes prohibited status, criminal intent, conspiracy, threatening communications, and linkage to a planned offense.

Vandalism

Vandalism involving swastikas or racial slurs can function as intimidation, propaganda, property damage, or a hate crime depending upon facts and law.

Investigators should preserve images, time, location, camera evidence, fingerprints, witnesses, and related communications.

Doxxing, Swatting, and Cyberstalking

Extremist networks may expose personal information, submit false emergency reports, or conduct repeated online harassment.

These acts can produce armed police responses, employment harm, displacement, and fear without direct physical contact by the offender.

Witness and Informant Intimidation

Members may threaten perceived informants, defectors, journalists, litigants, witnesses, or cooperating defendants.

Retaliation risk increases when identities, home addresses, family information, or legal cooperation become known.

Sexual Exploitation

Neo-Nazi networks are not uniformly organized around sexual crime.

The CVLT prosecution nevertheless demonstrates that extremist branding can merge with coercive child exploitation, self-harm demands, blackmail, and sadistic online control.

Victims require safeguarding rather than ideological suspicion.

Funding

Funding can come from dues, donations, membership fees, events, merchandise, publications, media subscriptions, personal income, online transfers, criminal proceeds, or wealthy patrons.

A payment establishes financial contact, not necessarily knowledge of every organizational purpose.

Merchandise and Publishing

Clothing, flags, books, music, stickers, and digital publications can generate revenue while spreading ideology.

Sellers may operate as organizations, independent businesses, propagandists, or opportunistic vendors.

Cryptocurrency and Digital Payments

Digital currencies and payment systems may support cross-border fundraising, pseudonymous donations, subscriptions, and transfers.

Blockchain records may also create durable transaction evidence.

Financial attribution requires wallet control, exchange records, timing, communications, and purpose.

Foreign Relationships

Neo-Nazi groups may maintain foreign contacts through travel, propaganda, conferences, music, online channels, or personal relationships.

International contact does not automatically establish foreign direction, espionage, or material support to a designated terrorist organization.

State Sponsorship Claims

Claims that a government secretly controls a neo-Nazi group require strong evidence of direction, funding, tasking, protection, or operational command.

Ideological benefit, propaganda amplification, online manipulation, or contact with foreign actors may be relevant without proving complete control.

Law-Enforcement Infiltration

Neo-Nazi organizations have been investigated through informants, undercover personnel, surveillance, search warrants, digital evidence, and cooperating witnesses.

Government penetration may reveal or disrupt a group.

Presence of an informant does not by itself establish that the government created, controlled, or authorized the organization's ideology or independent crimes.

Protected Expression

The First Amendment protects broad categories of offensive political and religious expression.

Government may not criminalize a person merely for holding racist or neo-Nazi beliefs.

True threats, solicitation, conspiracy, stalking, property crime, violence, and other unlawful conduct are not immunized by ideological labeling.

Public Safety and Civil Liberties

Effective protection requires both threat detection and constitutional restraint.

Overbroad surveillance, guilt by association, viewpoint punishment, or false classification can violate rights, degrade intelligence quality, and strengthen extremist propaganda.

Legal Classification

“Neo-Nazi organization” is an ideological and analytical classification, not automatically a statutory status.

A group may be described by government filings as neo-Nazi, white supremacist, racially motivated violent extremist, criminal gang, or terrorist network depending upon evidence and legal context.

Specially Designated Global Terrorists

The United States may designate foreign persons or entities as Specially Designated Global Terrorists under applicable executive and sanctions authorities.

Terrorgram's 2025 designation is specific.

It should not be generalized to unrelated domestic groups or all people sharing parts of its ideology.

Foreign Terrorist Organization Distinction

A Foreign Terrorist Organization designation is a separate statutory process applicable to qualifying foreign organizations.

“Neo-Nazi” does not itself mean FTO.

Analysts must identify the exact entity, designation type, date, legal effect, and current status.

Procedural Status

Legal reporting must distinguish:

  • Investigation.
  • Search or arrest.
  • Complaint.
  • Indictment.
  • Charge.
  • Guilty plea.
  • Trial conviction.
  • Dismissal or acquittal.
  • Sentence.
  • Appeal or resentencing.

Individual Responsibility

A member's act should not be attributed to an entire ideological movement without evidence of authorization, coordination, ratification, or institutional benefit.

An organization should not be shielded when leaders direct, finance, reward, conceal, or systematically encourage unlawful conduct.

Institutional Responsibility Test

  1. Identify the exact entity. Record name, aliases, dates, location, and claimed lineage.
  2. Identify the person. Separate leader, member, associate, recruit, former member, and imitator.
  3. Identify the ideology. Determine whether explicit National Socialist doctrine is established.
  4. Identify the conduct. Separate speech, membership, threat, conspiracy, assistance, and completed offense.
  5. Locate authority. Determine who ordered, approved, funded, or knew of the act.
  6. Trace resources. Identify accounts, communications, travel, property, equipment, and outside support.
  7. Determine current status. Verify whether the organization and affiliation remain active.
  8. Test coercion and deception. Consider manipulation, informants, impersonation, hacking, and false claims.
  9. Preserve procedural status. Distinguish allegation from adjudication.
  10. Assign responsibility proportionately. Attribute findings only to the proven level.

Evidence Classification

  • Adjudicated fact: Established through plea, conviction, final judgment, or court finding.
  • Documented fact: Supported by authenticated records or corroborated evidence.
  • Official allegation: Asserted in a complaint, indictment, affidavit, or government statement but not adjudicated.
  • Organizational admission: Statement by an authorized representative or authenticated publication.
  • Documented association: Verified contact that does not alone establish membership.
  • Independent reporting: Reputable reporting evaluated for sourcing and corroboration.
  • Historical interpretation: Scholarly or archival assessment subject to context.
  • Symbolic indicator: Image, phrase, number, clothing, or tattoo requiring context.
  • Rumor: Unverified claim.
  • Speculation: Inference lacking adequate supporting evidence.

False Identification

False classification may result from:

  • Historical study or military collecting.
  • Religious use of ancient symbols.
  • Provocation, satire, or costume.
  • Hacked or impersonated accounts.
  • Ordinary numbers or clothing.
  • Family, school, workplace, or neighborhood association.
  • Former affiliation or deradicalization.
  • Uncorroborated informant or online claims.

Common Public Misconceptions

  • Every racist is a neo-Nazi.
  • Every neo-Nazi belongs to one organization.
  • Every person displaying a symbol is a member.
  • Every White nationalist is organizationally neo-Nazi.
  • Every skinhead is racist.
  • Every conservative or nationalist is a neo-Nazi.
  • All neo-Nazis are open public activists.
  • Fragmentation means low threat.

Controlled Opposition Assessment

No evidence establishes that governments, Jewish institutions, civil-rights organizations, political parties, or other alleged enemies centrally created and control neo-Nazism as a whole.

Informants, undercover operations, manipulation, factional infiltration, and foreign influence may occur in particular cases.

Those facts do not support a universal controlled-opposition conclusion.

Objective Political Effects

Neo-Nazi activity can objectively:

  • Terrorize targeted communities.
  • Increase security and policing burdens.
  • Damage democratic trust.
  • Polarize political coalitions.
  • Provide propaganda to opposing movements.
  • Redirect alienated people into destructive organizations.

Objective effect does not prove secret direction by whoever benefits.

Proximity to the Malone Network

No direct institutional relationship between neo-Nazi organizations and the Malone Network is established in this dossier.

Proximity may arise through:

  • Threats to Black institutions or leaders.
  • Shared schools, workplaces, neighborhoods, or public events.
  • Online targeting, doxxing, or impersonation.
  • Applicants, contractors, employees, or volunteers concealing active affiliation.
  • Military, law-enforcement, political, or educational overlap.
  • Direct recruitment or infiltration attempts.

Conceptual Relationship to Malone

Malone Doctrine is grounded in institutional development, community protection, ethical authority, and Black advancement.

Neo-Nazism rejects equal dignity and commonly defines Black institutional power as a racial enemy.

The relationship is therefore fundamentally adverse at the ideological level.

Alignment Assessment

Neo-Nazi organizations are not aligned with Malone.

Active advocacy of racial domination, antisemitism, political dictatorship, or targeted dehumanization is incompatible with Malone authority and sensitive institutional roles.

Former adherents should be assessed individually according to current conduct, candor, disengagement, safety, and access.

Hostility Assessment

Neo-Nazism is ideologically hostile to Malone's Black institutional mission.

Specific operational hostility requires evidence of targeting, surveillance, threats, recruitment, infiltration, vandalism, sabotage, or violence involving Malone people or property.

Ideological hostility should inform protection without replacing case-specific proof.

Confusion Assessment

The highest confusion risks are:

  • Ideology versus organization.
  • Member versus sympathizer.
  • Historical symbol versus current affiliation.
  • White nationalism versus explicit neo-Nazism.
  • Skinhead culture versus racist skinhead networks.
  • Prison gang versus political movement.
  • Protected speech versus criminal threat.
  • Domestic-extremist description versus formal terrorist designation.

Public events involving Black leadership, civil rights, Jewish partners, interracial coalitions, immigration, or LGBTQ+ participation may attract hostile attention.

Security planning should include lawful access control, communication, emergency coordination, evidence preservation, and protection of attendees without publicizing tactical vulnerabilities.

Neo-Nazi actors may use impersonation, harassment, doxxing, credential attacks, manipulated media, false event notices, or mass reporting.

Malone should use multifactor authentication, least-privilege access, account recovery controls, verified communications, logging, and incident escalation.

Personnel Security

Personnel assessment should focus on conduct and access rather than private political labels.

Relevant concerns include concealed violent affiliation, threats, unauthorized data access, extremist recruitment, misuse of credentials, harassment, or attempts to compromise investigations.

Disengagement

People can leave neo-Nazi organizations and reject extremist ideology.

Disengagement may involve loss of social network, housing, employment, identity, protection, or family relationships and may create retaliation risk.

Current status should be evaluated through time, conduct, candor, relationships, accountability, and the sensitivity of proposed access.

Deradicalization

Deradicalization concerns changes in belief, while disengagement concerns exit from activity or organization.

A person may disengage without fully changing beliefs or may reject ideology while remaining socially connected.

Institutional decisions should not assume both processes are identical.

Prevention

Prevention can include:

  • Media and historical literacy.
  • Early response to credible threats and targeted harassment.
  • Youth safeguarding and trusted adult intervention.
  • Exit support and family guidance.
  • Community security partnerships.
  • Lawful accountability for crime.
  • Structured belonging, employment, education, and civic participation.

Responsible Analysis

Neo-Nazism should not be minimized as costume, trolling, or obsolete history.

It should also not be used as a careless label for ordinary political disagreement.

The governing rule is: identify the exact ideology and entity, verify current membership and authority, preserve the boundary between protected expression and crime, distinguish domestic-extremist description from formal designation, and assign responsibility only to the level supported by evidence.

Preliminary Malone Classification

The proper preliminary classification is: fragmented transnational extremist ideology and movement rather than one organization; historically derived from German National Socialism and postwar revival movements; internally divided among public parties, propaganda groups, racist skinhead networks, prison-linked formations, accelerationist cells, online networks, and lone adherents; ideologically hostile to Black equality, Jewish life, pluralist democracy, and Malone's institutional mission; external, unaligned, and untrusted; not proven controlled opposition; no direct Malone operation presently established; severe threat wherever verified targeting, access, recruitment, weapons, sabotage planning, intimidation, or violent intent exists.

Distinguishing the Terms

  • Nazism — Ideology and governing system of Hitler's German Nazi Party.
  • Neo-Nazism — Post-1945 revival or adaptation of Nazi ideology.
  • National Socialism — The Nazi Party's self-description; not equivalent to democratic socialism or ordinary public welfare policy.
  • White supremacy — Belief or system asserting White superiority or domination.
  • White nationalism — Advocacy of White political nationhood or collective power.
  • Racially or ethnically motivated violent extremist — Government threat category focused on ideologically motivated unlawful force or violence.
  • Accelerationism — Strategy seeking collapse or intensified conflict to produce revolutionary change.
  • Cell — Small operational group.
  • Associate — Connected person not necessarily holding formal membership.
  • Former member — Person whose past affiliation does not alone establish current status.
  • Domestic terrorism — Statutory conduct-based definition, not a universal organizational designation.
  • SDGT — Specially Designated Global Terrorist under applicable sanctions authority.
  • FTO — Foreign Terrorist Organization designated through a separate statutory process.
