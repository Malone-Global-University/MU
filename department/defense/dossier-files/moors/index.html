Moors

Overview

Moors is a historical and modern term with several distinct meanings. It has been used for Muslim peoples of North Africa and medieval Iberia, for Black American religious and national movements inspired by Moorish history, and for individuals who adopt Moorish identity without belonging to one central organization.

The term does not identify one uniform race, ethnicity, government, religion, or institution. Historical Moors included diverse populations, while modern Moorish-American communities contain separate religious bodies, cultural associations, independent teachers, and unaffiliated individuals.

Accurate analysis therefore requires distinguishing among historical Moors, modern Moorish Americans, the Moorish Science Temple of America, and people who combine Moorish terminology with sovereign-citizen legal theories.

Meaning of the Historical Term

The English word Moor developed from Greek and Latin terms associated with Mauretania, an ancient region of northwestern Africa. European writers later used the term broadly for Muslims, North Africans, people from the Maghreb, and Muslim inhabitants of the Iberian Peninsula.

The term was an external label rather than the normal self-designation of one unified people. Populations called Moors could include Amazigh peoples, Arabs, mixed North African communities, Iberian converts to Islam, sub-Saharan Africans, and other Muslims living within North African or Andalusi societies.

Because European usage varied by century and location, the word could refer to geography, religion, ancestry, appearance, political allegiance, or some combination of these characteristics. It should not be treated as a precise modern racial classification.

North African Background

North Africa contained complex societies long before the Islamic period. Indigenous Amazigh populations lived across regions now associated with Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Mauritania, and surrounding territories. The region also experienced Phoenician, Carthaginian, Roman, Vandal, Byzantine, and other political influences.

Beginning in the seventh century, Muslim armies and settlers expanded across North Africa. Over time, Islam and Arabic became major religious and linguistic forces, while Amazigh identities, languages, dynasties, and local traditions continued to shape the region.

Moors in Iberia

711: Entry into the Iberian Peninsula

In 711, Muslim forces crossed from North Africa into the Iberian Peninsula. Their armies included substantial numbers of Amazigh troops together with Arab commanders and other participants. Within several years, Muslim authority extended across most of the peninsula.

Al-Andalus

Muslim-ruled territories in Iberia became known as al-Andalus. Their borders and political structures changed repeatedly between the eighth and fifteenth centuries.

Major political periods included the Umayyad Emirate and Caliphate of Córdoba, competing taifa kingdoms, and North African Almoravid and Almohad rule. Granada later became the final major Muslim-ruled kingdom in Iberia.

1492: Fall of Granada

In 1492, the Nasrid Kingdom of Granada surrendered to the Catholic monarchs Ferdinand and Isabella. Muslim political rule in Iberia ended, although Muslim communities remained for generations.

Cultural and Intellectual Legacy

Muslim Iberian and North African societies contributed to architecture, agriculture, irrigation, medicine, philosophy, mathematics, astronomy, literature, law, craftsmanship, trade, and urban development.

Cities such as Córdoba, Seville, Toledo, and Granada became centers of administration, scholarship, commerce, and artistic production at different times. Translation and intellectual exchange helped transmit works from Arabic, Greek, Hebrew, and Latin traditions.

The Alhambra in Granada, the Great Mosque of Córdoba, and other surviving structures display architectural traditions often described as Moorish.

Race and the Moors

Historical Moors were not one modern racial group. North Africa has always contained substantial physical, linguistic, and ancestral diversity.

Some Moors would be understood today as Black, some as North African or Amazigh, some as Arab, some as mixed, and some as Iberian converts or their descendants. Claims that all Moors belonged exclusively to one modern racial category are historically overbroad.

Modern Moorish Identity in the United States

In the early twentieth century, some Black Americans adopted Moorish identity as part of a broader effort to recover nationality, religious heritage, dignity, historical consciousness, and collective organization.

These movements developed during the Great Migration, when Black families encountered segregation, labor exploitation, racial violence, and cultural dislocation in northern and midwestern cities.

Moorish identity offered an alternative to degrading racial labels imposed by American society. It presented Black Americans as members of an ancient people possessing nationality, religion, law, civilization, and historical continuity.

Moorish Science Temple of America

Founder

The Moorish Science Temple of America (MSTA) was founded by Noble Drew Ali, whose early life remains partly disputed. He is commonly identified in historical records as having been born Timothy or Thomas Drew in 1886.

Formation

Moorish Science tradition traces the movement to a Canaanite Temple established in Newark, New Jersey, in 1913. Drew Ali later developed congregations in additional cities and established a major base in Chicago during the 1920s.

By 1928, the Moorish Science Temple operated as an incorporated religious organization in Chicago. The movement expanded rapidly before Drew Ali's death in 1929 and later divided into competing branches and successions.

Scripture

The movement's central text is the Holy Koran of the Moorish Science Temple of America, commonly called the Circle Seven Koran. It is distinct from the Qur'an used in mainstream Islam.

Stated Purpose of Moorish Science

Noble Drew Ali's movement sought to give Black Americans a recovered national and religious identity. Its stated and documented objectives included:

  • Teaching that Black Americans possessed a Moorish nationality.
  • Replacing imposed racial labels with a dignified collective identity.
  • Promoting knowledge of self, ancestry, and national origin.
  • Teaching spiritual discipline, moral conduct, and personal transformation.
  • Encouraging economic independence, business development, and mutual support.
  • Promoting civic participation and constructive community conduct.
  • Building organized temples, leadership structures, and social programs.
  • Uplifting Black communities through education, service, and racial pride.

Drew Ali instructed adherents to respect law and government while seeking recognition as Moorish Americans. His program was intended to produce disciplined citizenship and community advancement, not exemption from civil law.

Beliefs and Practices

Moorish Science combines Islamic terminology with Black nationalist, Christian, Masonic, metaphysical, and esoteric influences. Its theology is distinct from Sunni and Shia Islam.

  • Recognition of Noble Drew Ali as prophet or divinely appointed teacher.
  • Use of the Circle Seven Koran.
  • Identification of Black Americans as Moorish Americans or Asiatics.
  • Use of surnames or suffixes such as Bey and El.
  • Use of nationality cards or internal identification documents.
  • Use of the fez and other Moorish symbols.
  • Emphasis on love, truth, peace, freedom, and justice.
  • Instruction in morality, family responsibility, and community uplift.

Organization After Noble Drew Ali

Noble Drew Ali died in 1929 without leaving a succession process accepted by every follower. Competing leaders and temples claimed authority, and the movement divided into multiple branches.

As a result, no single modern organization necessarily represents every Moorish Science adherent or every person who calls themselves Moorish American.

Relationship to Mainstream Islam

Moorish Science uses Islamic vocabulary and identifies itself with Islam, but its scripture, prophetic claims, racial teachings, and ritual system differ substantially from mainstream Sunni and Shia traditions.

Moorish Science remains historically important to the development of Black religious nationalism and Islam-related movements in the United States, regardless of theological classification.

Relationship to the Nation of Islam

The Moorish Science Temple preceded the Nation of Islam and helped establish a religious environment in which Black Americans could claim non-Christian identity, ancient nationality, spiritual authority, and collective economic purpose.

The two organizations are nevertheless distinct. They have different founders, scriptures, doctrines, leadership histories, institutional structures, and contemporary branches.

Moorish Americans Outside the Temple

Not everyone using a Moorish identity belongs to the Moorish Science Temple. Modern self-described Moors may derive their identity from genealogy, nationality teachings, Islam, esoteric religion, indigenous claims, internet research, sovereign-citizen ideology, or combinations of these sources.

Because the field is decentralized, the name of an organization does not establish that it is recognized by the original Moorish Science Temple, Morocco, the United States government, or any tribal nation.

Moorish Sovereign-Citizen Claims

Some individuals combine Moorish identity with the sovereign-citizen belief that a person can become exempt from federal, state, or local law by declaring a special nationality, using particular documents, changing a name, filing commercial forms, or invoking historic treaties.

United States courts do not recognize these theories as a lawful basis for avoiding jurisdiction or ordinary legal duties. Courts have repeatedly rejected attempts to use sovereign-citizen language, fictitious liens, fraudulent deeds, fabricated diplomatic documents, and similar instruments.

The Morocco–United States Treaty

Morocco and the United States concluded a Treaty of Peace and Friendship in 1786, with ratification completed in 1787. The treaty concerns peace, commerce, navigation, diplomatic relations, and the treatment of citizens and vessels of the two countries.

It does not make every self-identified Moor a Moroccan diplomat, create immunity from domestic law, transfer United States territory, or grant private individuals authority to issue government credentials.

Moroccan citizenship and diplomatic status arise through Moroccan law and recognized governmental processes. They are not created by purchasing a nationality card, adding Bey or El to a name, or declaring oneself Moorish.

Property and Document Fraud

Law-enforcement agencies have prosecuted individuals who used Moorish sovereign-citizen theories to file false liens, submit fraudulent tax claims, produce fictitious licenses, occupy homes, or record deeds to property they did not own.

These crimes should not be attributed to all Moorish Americans or to all members of the Moorish Science Temple. Religious or cultural identity is distinct from illegal conduct.

Indigenous and Aboriginal Claims

Some modern Moorish groups claim that Black Americans are the original indigenous inhabitants of the Americas, members of hidden Moorish tribes, or descendants of pre-Columbian North African empires that governed American territory.

Broad claims that all Black Americans are legally indigenous Moors or that a preexisting Moorish government retains sovereignty over the United States are not established by mainstream archaeology, archival evidence, or recognized tribal law.

Native American citizenship and tribal membership are governed by the recognized tribes themselves. Self-declaration as Aboriginal, Moorish, or indigenous does not compel a sovereign tribal nation to recognize membership.

Historical Claims and Evidence Standards

  • Established history: Supported by contemporary records, archaeology, reliable scholarship, or corroborating sources.
  • Religious belief: A matter of faith that should not be presented automatically as verified secular history.
  • Cultural symbolism: A modern use of history or imagery to create identity and solidarity.
  • Genealogical claim: Requires documentary or genetic evidence connecting a person to a particular ancestry.
  • Legal claim: Requires valid constitutional, statutory, treaty, regulatory, or judicial authority.
  • Speculation: A claim lacking sufficient evidence.

Institutional Strengths

  • Recovery of dignity in response to degrading racial classifications.
  • Encouragement of historical study and knowledge of self.
  • Creation of religious institutions under Black leadership.
  • Promotion of moral discipline, family responsibility, and civic conduct.
  • Development of businesses, mutual support, and community networks.
  • Expansion of Black religious and national thought in the United States.

Institutional Risks

  • Unverified teachers may claim offices or powers they do not possess.
  • Religious nationality may be confused with legal citizenship.
  • Historic treaties may be misapplied to private disputes.
  • Fraudulent documents may be sold as lawful credentials.
  • Members may face eviction, fines, imprisonment, or financial loss after relying on false legal theories.
  • Broad racial or indigenous claims may replace disciplined genealogy and scholarship.

Public Perception and Responsible Analysis

The word Moor should not be used as though it automatically identifies a criminal, extremist, fraudulent actor, or member of one religious organization.

At the same time, religious freedom does not validate forged records, false property claims, tax fraud, threats, or obstruction of lawful process. Conduct should be evaluated individually, and organizational responsibility should be assigned only when evidence demonstrates authorization or support.

Distinguishing the Terms

  • Historical Moors — Diverse Muslim populations associated with North Africa, al-Andalus, and the medieval western Mediterranean.
  • Moorish — Relating to the Moors, their cultures, or architectural and artistic traditions.
  • Moorish American — A modern American national, cultural, or religious identity.
  • Moorish Science Temple of America — The religious and national movement founded by Noble Drew Ali.
  • Moorish sovereign citizen — An individual who combines Moorish identity with sovereign-citizen legal theories.
  • Moroccan citizen — A person possessing citizenship under the laws of the Kingdom of Morocco.
