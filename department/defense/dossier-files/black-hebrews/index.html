“Black Hebrews”

Overview

“Black Hebrews”, Black Hebrew Israelites, Hebrew Israelites, and African Hebrew Israelites are overlapping labels applied to a broad family of religious and identity movements whose members identify African Americans, other African-descended peoples, or selected Black, Hispanic, and Indigenous populations as descendants of the biblical Israelites.

The field is not one organization. It includes long-established congregations influenced by Judaism, churches combining the Hebrew Bible and New Testament, the African Hebrew Israelite community centered in Dimona, Israel, street preaching schools and camps, online ministries, independent teachers, and militant or antisemitic splinters.

Some communities seek constructive religious discipline, family stability, health, education, Black dignity, and moral reform. Other factions teach racial hierarchy, apocalyptic domination, collective punishment, misogyny, hostility toward Jews, hostility toward White people, or contempt for other Black institutions.

Accurate analysis requires identifying the exact congregation, school, camp, leader, doctrine, location, and conduct involved.

Core Conclusion

“Black Hebrews” should be treated as a religious and identity ecosystem, not as one coordinated body.

The movement's central ancestry claims are religious, interpretive, and historical claims. Scripture alone cannot establish a complete genealogical chain from ancient Israelites to a modern population.

Modern Jewish populations are also diverse and include Black Jews, Ethiopian Jews, converts, mixed families, and longstanding Jewish communities across Africa, the Middle East, Europe, Asia, and the Americas.

The proper baseline is: separate documented communities from ancestry claims; separate non-hateful congregations from extremist camps; and assign responsibility only to the specific actor supported by evidence.

Not One Group

There is no single founder, headquarters, chief rabbi, bishop, general, council, membership roll, or doctrine governing all Black Hebrew Israelites.

Organizations may disagree about:

  • Whether they are Jews, Israelites, Christians, or a separate people.
  • Whether Jesus is Messiah, divine, human, or rejected entirely.
  • Whether the New Testament is scripture.
  • Whether White Jews are legitimate Jews.
  • Which modern populations correspond to the Twelve Tribes.
  • Whether salvation is open to all peoples.
  • Whether women may teach or lead.
  • Whether polygyny is permitted.
  • Whether political migration, separation, or street preaching is required.
  • Whether Israel is a religious identity, ancestry, nationality, or all three.

Terminology

  • Hebrew Israelite — Broad self-description for a person claiming Israelite identity.
  • Black Hebrew Israelite — Common public label emphasizing African-descended identity.
  • Black Jew — A Black person who is Jewish; not automatically a Hebrew Israelite.
  • African Hebrew Israelite — May describe the Dimona-centered community or a wider African-descended Israelite identity.
  • Camp — A school, assembly, or faction, especially in One West-derived movements.
  • One West or 1West — A Harlem school and lineage from which multiple street-camp factions emerged.
  • Israelite school — A teaching body; not necessarily a synagogue or accredited educational institution.
  • Twelve Tribes chart — A chart assigning modern ethnic populations to biblical tribes.
  • Edomite — Biblical identity frequently assigned by radical factions to White people.
  • Gentile — Non-Israelite; interpretation varies by group.

Black Biblical Identification Before the Movement

Enslaved and free Black Christians frequently identified with biblical Israel. The Exodus story offered a language for understanding slavery, liberation, covenant, judgment, exile, and promised deliverance.

Spirituals, sermons, abolitionist writing, and Black church traditions often described enslaved African Americans as a people in bondage comparable to the Israelites in Egypt.

Symbolic identification did not always involve a claim of literal biological descent. The later Hebrew Israelite movements increasingly converted biblical analogy into ancestry, nationality, and chosen-people claims.

Post-Emancipation Conditions

Hebrew Israelite movements emerged after slavery amid:

  • White supremacy and Jim Crow.
  • Church segregation.
  • Black migration and urban institution building.
  • Holiness and Pentecostal religious experimentation.
  • Pan-Africanism and Ethiopianism.
  • Searches for a dignified history outside White control.
  • Disappointment with American Christianity's defense of slavery and racism.

Israelite identity offered a theological reversal: people treated as cursed and inferior could understand themselves as covenantal, chosen, disciplined, and historically significant.

William Saunders Crowdy

William Saunders Crowdy was a Civil War veteran, preacher, organizer, businessman, and Mason associated with one of the earliest durable Black Israelite institutions.

His church records state that he experienced divine visions in Oklahoma during the 1890s and was commanded to restore Israel from spiritual and mental bondage.

In 1896, he organized the Church of God and Saints of Christ in Kansas.

Church of God and Saints of Christ

The Church of God and Saints of Christ, commonly abbreviated COGASOC, combines Israelite identity with practices and teachings drawn from both the Hebrew Bible and New Testament.

Its traditions include Sabbath observance, Passover, the Hebrew calendar, circumcision, baptism, foot washing, dietary discipline, and belief in Jesus.

The organization established congregations in the United States, the Caribbean, and parts of Africa.

It should not be merged with One West camps merely because both identify Black people with Israel.

COGASOC Structure

COGASOC uses leadership and local-body terminology including chief rabbi, prophet, bishop, elder, and tabernacle.

Its current official materials identify Chief Rabbi Phillip E. McNeil as its senior leader.

Exact authority should be verified through the organization's constitution, leadership records, and tabernacle affiliation.

Wentworth Arthur Matthew

Wentworth Arthur Matthew, an immigrant from Saint Kitts, founded the Commandment Keepers tradition in Harlem during the early twentieth century.

His movement developed in the environment of the Harlem Renaissance, Caribbean migration, Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association, Pan-African thought, and contact with Jewish religious life in New York.

Matthew trained religious leaders and helped establish a network of Black Hebrew congregations in the United States and Caribbean.

Commandment Keepers

The Commandment Keepers Ethiopian Hebrew Congregation was formally incorporated in New York in 1921.

Its own history describes a synagogue organized around Torah observance, Hebrew tradition, Sabbath worship, holy days, education, self-reliance, and community discipline.

Matthew taught that the Israelites led from Egypt were Black and that Black people were returning to their Israelite heritage.

Commandment Keepers history is foundational to several later Black Jewish and Hebrew Israelite congregations.

Black Jewish Congregational Tradition

Some congregations descended from or influenced by Commandment Keepers moved toward recognizable synagogue practice, Hebrew liturgy, Torah study, conversion procedures, rabbinic training, and engagement with mainstream Jewish institutions.

These congregations should not be described automatically as antisemitic, militant, or Christian.

Their members may identify as Black Jews, Ethiopian Hebrews, Israelites, or Jews while disagreeing about the terminology used by outside scholars.

Beth Shalom B'nai Zaken

Beth Shalom B'nai Zaken Ethiopian Hebrew Congregation is a Chicago congregation led by Rabbi Capers C. Funnye Jr.

Its public materials present it as a synagogue with Jewish worship, congregational education, conversion, Hebrew practice, and relationships with wider Jewish institutions.

It represents a different institutional model from militarized street camps.

Black Jews Are Not One Hebrew Israelite Faction

Black Jews include:

  • People born to Jewish families.
  • People with African and Jewish ancestry.
  • Converts accepted by a Jewish community.
  • Ethiopian Jews and their descendants.
  • Members of African, Caribbean, European, Middle Eastern, and American Jewish communities.

A Black Jew does not necessarily believe that African Americans are the lost tribes, that White Jews are impostors, or that the New Testament is scripture.

Beta Israel

Beta Israel is the historic Ethiopian Jewish community. Israel recognizes Beta Israel as part of the Jewish people, and large numbers immigrated to Israel through operations including Moses and Solomon.

Beta Israel history, law, language, and communal development are distinct from American Black Hebrew Israelite movements.

American groups sometimes use Ethiopian identity symbolically. That use does not establish descent from or authority over Beta Israel.

African Hebrew Israelites of Jerusalem

The African Hebrew Israelites of Jerusalem emerged from Chicago during the 1960s under the leadership of Ben Ammi Ben-Israel, born Ben Carter.

Members understood African Americans as descendants of ancient Israelites and interpreted migration as a required return from captivity.

The community is sometimes called the Hebrew Israelites of Dimona, the Black Hebrews of Dimona, or the African Hebrew Israelite Nation of Jerusalem.

Migration Through Liberia

Ben Ammi and followers left the United States for Liberia in 1967. The movement treated Liberia as a period of purification and preparation.

Beginning in 1969, members moved to Israel and established communities centered in Dimona and other locations.

The migration distinguishes this community from most American street camps, which generally remain urban preaching and teaching organizations.

Status in Israel

Israeli authorities did not accept the community's claim that it qualified automatically for immigration as Jews under the Law of Return.

The community and government experienced decades of conflict over entry, residency, deportation, work, citizenship, and legal status.

Many members later obtained permanent residency or citizenship, while unresolved status issues persisted for some families.

Israeli residence or citizenship does not itself prove ancient Israelite ancestry.

Dimona Community Practices

The African Hebrew Israelites of Jerusalem developed a communal culture emphasizing:

  • Vegan or plant-centered diet.
  • Exercise and preventive health.
  • Hebrew names and Israelite identity.
  • Community businesses and cooperative life.
  • Music, dance, clothing, and cultural production.
  • Family and collective discipline.
  • Migration and spiritual return.

Dimona Community Controversies

Critics have raised concerns regarding:

  • Authoritarian spiritual authority.
  • Polygyny.
  • Gender hierarchy.
  • Control over marriage, family, work, and migration.
  • Legal-status uncertainty.
  • Claims surrounding Ben Ammi's religious role.

These concerns should be investigated through named witnesses, governing practices, court or immigration records, and community responses.

The One West Lineage

During the 1960s, a Harlem Israelite school developed at 1 West 123rd Street, an address associated with the Commandment Keepers property.

Abba Bivens, who had connections to the Commandment Keepers environment, established a school that took a more militant, apocalyptic, and racialized direction.

The term One West now identifies a doctrinal and organizational lineage from which numerous camps and splinters emerged.

One West Characteristics

Common characteristics include:

  • Identification of Black, Hispanic, and Indigenous peoples as the Twelve Tribes.
  • Use of the King James Bible, often including the Apocrypha.
  • Street preaching and public confrontation.
  • Military-style ranks and uniforms.
  • Strict male authority.
  • Apocalyptic expectation and divine judgment.
  • Interpretation of slavery as biblical punishment.
  • Rejection of mainstream Judaism and Christianity.
  • Frequent assignment of White people to Edom.

Not every descendant camp applies these teachings identically.

The Twelve Tribes Chart

Many camps use charts assigning modern peoples to biblical tribes.

A common version identifies:

  • American Black people with Judah.
  • West Indians with Benjamin.
  • Haitians with Levi.
  • Puerto Ricans with Ephraim.
  • Mexicans with Issachar.
  • Native Americans with Gad.
  • Other Latin American peoples with additional tribes.

These assignments are doctrinal interpretations. No accepted archaeological, genealogical, linguistic, or genetic method validates the complete chart as a historical population map.

Israelite Church of God in Jesus Christ

The Israelite Church of God in Jesus Christ, abbreviated ICGJC, developed from the One West lineage and has used a hierarchical religious structure with titles such as apostle, chief high priest, bishop, general, captain, and officer.

Its public site continues to invoke the guidance of Jermaine Grant, known as Tazadaqyah, as Holy Apostle and Chief High Priest.

The group should not be confused with every organization using the words Israelite Church of God, Church of Israel, or Israel of God.

ICGJC Financial-Crime Case

In 2019, Jermaine Grant and church treasurer Lincoln Warrington pleaded guilty to conspiring to evade taxes.

The United States Department of Justice stated that they used church funds to pay millions of dollars in Grant's personal expenses and concealed at least $250,000 in taxes owed.

In January 2020, Grant received an eighteen-month federal prison sentence and Warrington received twelve months and one day.

This is documented conduct by identified leaders. It should not be attributed automatically to every Hebrew Israelite group.

Israelite School of Universal Practical Knowledge

The Israelite School of Universal Practical Knowledge, abbreviated ISUPK, is a One West-derived organization led publicly by a figure known as Commanding General Yahanna.

Its official website promotes classes, public debate, podcasts, apparel, and online broadcasting.

Outside monitoring organizations have classified ISUPK as an extremist faction based on racial, antisemitic, anti-LGBT, and militant rhetoric. Such classifications should be attributed to the organization making them and supported with documented statements.

Israel United in Christ

Israel United in Christ, abbreviated IUIC, identifies Black, Hispanic, and Native American peoples as the lost Twelve Tribes of Israel.

It operates classes, online media, public preaching, community events, international outreach, clothing, and organized chapters under the leadership of Bishop Nathanyel Ben Israel.

Its official website declares that it is not affiliated with other Israelite groups, identifies itself as nonviolent, rejects plots to harm others, and instructs members to report threats to authorities.

ADL and SPLC materials have nevertheless criticized IUIC teachings and rhetoric as antisemitic or extremist. The official disclaimer and documented content should both be preserved in the dossier.

Great Millstone

Great Millstone, commonly abbreviated GMS, is a decentralized One West-derived teaching network.

Its public site identifies its members as the true biblical descendants of the Hebrew Israelites.

GMS teachers distribute sermons, street-preaching videos, prophecy analysis, and doctrinal commentary through online platforms and local camps.

Because the network is decentralized, responsibility should be assigned to the specific teacher, camp, channel, or elder whose conduct is documented.

Other Camps and Splinters

Additional organizations and labels have included:

  • House of Israel.
  • Israelite Church of Christ.
  • Israelite Church of God and Jesus Christ.
  • Israelite Saints of Christ.
  • Sicarii and Sicarrii factions.
  • Law Life Israelite School.
  • House of David and local Israelite assemblies.
  • Numerous unaffiliated camps using similar uniforms and charts.

Names change, groups split, channels disappear, and leaders claim succession. Current identity must be verified.

Street Preaching

Street preaching is one of the most visible forms of Hebrew Israelite public activity.

Camps may use:

  • Amplified speech.
  • Uniforms and coordinated ranks.
  • Large signs and tribal charts.
  • Scripture reading and rapid verse citation.
  • Public debate and confrontation.
  • Recorded interactions for online distribution.

Street preaching is generally protected expression subject to neutral laws governing threats, assault, obstruction, noise, permits, and public safety.

Military-Style Organization

Some camps use titles including general, captain, officer, soldier, bishop, and commander.

Members may wear coordinated colors, boots, patches, insignia, body armor, or military-inspired clothing.

Such presentation may create discipline, identity, and recruitment appeal. It does not by itself establish a private army or unlawful militia.

Actual capability must be assessed through weapons, training, orders, operational planning, logistics, and conduct—not appearance alone.

Scriptural Method

Many camps construct doctrine by linking verses from different biblical books and treating later events as fulfillment of prophecy.

Common interpretive moves include:

  • Applying Deuteronomy's curses to Atlantic slavery.
  • Reading “ships” as slave ships.
  • Identifying captivity with the African diaspora.
  • Assigning modern nations to biblical peoples.
  • Reading colonialism and racial hierarchy through Edom and Israel.
  • Interpreting contemporary events as signs of imminent judgment.

Internal scriptural consistency is not the same as independent historical proof.

Jesus and the New Testament

Many Hebrew Israelite camps accept Jesus under names such as Yahawashi, Yeshua, or Yahshua.

Their theology may reject the Trinity, deny universal salvation, emphasize Torah observance, or restrict Christ's mission to Israel.

Other Black Hebrew congregations reject the New Testament or practice forms of Judaism without Christian belief.

Law and Commandments

Common practices may include:

  • Saturday Sabbath.
  • Dietary restrictions.
  • Passover and biblical holy days.
  • Circumcision.
  • Fringes or tassels on clothing.
  • Head coverings.
  • Hebrew names.
  • Restrictions concerning marriage and sexuality.
  • Rejection of Christmas, Easter, and other Christian holidays.

Practice varies substantially.

Identity and the Curses of Deuteronomy

Many groups interpret Deuteronomy 28 as a coded history of African American enslavement, dispossession, family separation, and oppression.

This interpretation is emotionally powerful because it transforms racial suffering into covenantal evidence.

It remains a theological interpretation. Similar passages have been applied to other exiled, conquered, and persecuted populations.

Ancestry Claims

Hebrew Israelite ancestry claims may be based upon:

  • Biblical prophecy.
  • Historical migration theories.
  • African Jewish communities.
  • Names, customs, and oral traditions.
  • Comparisons between slavery and biblical captivity.
  • Claims that ancient Israelites were Black.
  • Claims that modern Jewish populations are converts or impostors.

These arguments vary in quality and should not be treated as one proof.

Ancient Israelites and Race

Ancient Israelites arose in the southern Levant. Modern United States racial categories did not exist in the ancient world.

Ancient Levantine peoples should not be assigned mechanically to the modern American categories Black, White, Hispanic, or Native American.

Art, scripture, skin-color vocabulary, and modern phenotype comparisons cannot establish a complete genealogy from an ancient people to a modern racial population.

Modern Jewish Population History

Jewish populations developed through continuity, diaspora, conversion, migration, intermarriage, community boundaries, and regional admixture.

Genetic studies generally identify shared ancestry among many Jewish populations alongside substantial diversity and local admixture. No single genetic marker defines all Jews.

The diversity of Jewish ancestry does not prove that modern Jews are a fabricated people or that one modern racial population exclusively replaced them.

The Khazar Claim

Some factions claim that Ashkenazi Jews descend primarily or entirely from medieval Khazars and therefore are not connected to ancient Israelites.

Khazar conversion to Judaism is a legitimate historical subject. The blanket replacement claim is not supported by the broader body of genetic and historical research, which generally finds both Middle Eastern and European ancestry among Ashkenazi Jews.

A disputed ancestry theory does not justify collective accusations against living Jews.

Black Jews, African Jews, and Lost-Tribe Claims

Africa contains or has contained communities with Jewish identity, ancestry, or Judaic practice, including Beta Israel, North African Jewish communities, Lemba traditions, Igbo Jewish communities, and others.

Each community has its own history and evidence.

The existence of African Jews demonstrates that Jewish and Black identities are compatible. It does not prove that all African-descended people belong to one biblical tribe.

Replacement Claims

Some factions teach that Black or selected non-White peoples are the true Jews and that today's Jews stole, concealed, or fabricated Israelite identity.

A religious claim of Israelite ancestry is not automatically antisemitic.

It becomes antisemitic where it:

  • Denies Jews collective legitimacy through conspiracy claims.
  • Describes Jews as impostors responsible for hiding Black identity.
  • Attributes collective guilt or secret control to Jews.
  • Calls for degradation, enslavement, expulsion, or violence against Jews.
  • Uses Holocaust denial or celebration.

Antisemitism

ADL and SPLC distinguish the broader Hebrew Israelite field from extremist factions. Both emphasize that not all Hebrew Israelites are antisemitic or violent.

Documented extremist rhetoric has included:

  • Claims that Jews are impostors.
  • Claims that Jews control slavery, banking, media, or government.
  • Collective blame for Black oppression.
  • Religious predictions of Jewish enslavement or destruction.
  • Holocaust minimization or inversion.

The exact speaker, group, date, and statement should be preserved.

Anti-White Teachings

Some camps identify White people collectively as Edomites and teach that they face divine destruction or future servitude.

Other congregations understand Israelite identity without teaching collective White guilt or racial domination.

Criticism of White supremacy and documented oppression is not identical to racial hatred. The distinction depends upon whether responsibility is assigned to conduct and institutions or collectively to every person classified as White.

Teachings About Other Black People

Some camps condemn Black Christians, Muslims, Jews, Africans, immigrants, interracial families, political organizations, or unaffiliated Black people as ignorant, rebellious, cursed, or spiritually dead.

This can convert an identity-restoration message into an internal Black hierarchy in which camp membership becomes the test of legitimacy.

Gender Hierarchy

Many factions impose strict male leadership and female submission.

Practices may include:

  • Male-only teaching authority.
  • Restrictions on women's dress and public speech.
  • Arranged or tightly supervised courtship.
  • Strong obedience requirements within marriage.
  • Acceptance of polygyny in some factions.
  • Discipline or social pressure against women who leave.

Other Black Jewish congregations ordain women or permit wider female leadership. No universal rule applies.

Family and Community Discipline

Supporters credit some Israelite communities with encouraging:

  • Marriage and paternal responsibility.
  • Abstinence from drugs and crime.
  • Dietary discipline and health.
  • Scriptural study.
  • Modest dress.
  • Mutual support and entrepreneurship.
  • Historical interest and Black dignity.

These benefits should be recognized where documented. They do not excuse coercion, financial abuse, hatred, or concealment.

Recruitment

The movement attracts people seeking:

  • A disciplined identity.
  • An explanation for Black suffering.
  • Brotherhood and male structure.
  • Historical importance.
  • Religious certainty.
  • Protection from social disorder.
  • Alternatives to churches viewed as weak or compromised.
  • Community after incarceration, military service, unemployment, or family disruption.

Recruitment may occur through street encounters, prison ministry, social media, family relationships, music, celebrity controversy, and online debate.

Prison Recruitment

Israelite teachings may appeal in prisons because they offer group identity, discipline, dietary rules, scriptural study, protection, and an explanation of oppression.

Prison-based religious exercise should not be treated automatically as gang activity.

Security analysis should examine actual conduct, coercion, contraband, extortion, violence, rank structure, and coordination rather than religious label alone.

Online Expansion

Social media has expanded the movement beyond local congregations.

Online channels permit:

  • Continuous preaching.
  • Rapid creation of splinter ministries.
  • Monetization through donations, merchandise, subscriptions, and classes.
  • Public humiliation and debate content.
  • International recruitment.
  • Circulation of historical and antisemitic conspiracy material.

Online popularity does not prove formal membership or central command.

Celebrities and Public Controversy

Public figures have repeated or promoted claims associated with Hebrew Israelite movements, including arguments that Black people are the true Jews or that modern Jews concealed Black identity.

Celebrity amplification can expose millions of people to the movement without making the celebrity a formal member of any camp.

Membership, endorsement, quotation, and temporary interest should be distinguished.

Hebrews to Negroes

Hebrews to Negroes is a book and film project arguing that Black Americans and other peoples of color are the biblical Israelites.

ADL has criticized the film for promoting antisemitic replacement claims, conspiracy theories, and misrepresentations of Jewish history.

Viewing or sharing the film does not by itself prove membership in a Hebrew Israelite organization. The specific claims endorsed should be identified.

Public Confrontation

Some street camps use insult, provocation, personal targeting, and aggressive debate as preaching tools.

Such tactics may generate attention and online content while increasing:

  • Conflict with residents and businesses.
  • Hostility toward Jews and other groups.
  • Police contact.
  • Noise complaints and civil litigation.
  • Risk of escalation by members or opponents.

Violence and the Movement

The broader Hebrew Israelite movement should not be classified as violent.

Individual offenders and small factions have been associated with violent rhetoric or acts. In some cases, authorities identified interest in Hebrew Israelite ideas without establishing direct membership in a recognized group.

The correct method is to distinguish:

  • Religious belief.
  • Extremist rhetoric.
  • Threat communication.
  • Operational planning.
  • Individual violence.
  • Organizational authorization.

Religious Freedom and Law Enforcement

Hebrew Israelite belief, worship, preaching, clothing, assembly, and publication are protected forms of religious and expressive activity.

Legal protection does not extend to true threats, assault, stalking, fraud, tax evasion, weapons crimes, child abuse, or conspiracies to commit violence.

Government and institutional response should be conduct-based rather than label-based.

Financial Control Risks

Some religious groups require tithes, donations, merchandise purchases, event payments, travel expenses, or labor.

Financial-risk indicators include:

  • Leader lifestyles inconsistent with disclosed income.
  • Church funds paying personal expenses.
  • Unclear ownership of property and businesses.
  • Pressure to give beyond ability.
  • Retaliation for requesting records.
  • Related companies concealing the source of wealth.
  • Lack of independent financial oversight.

Authoritarian-Control Risks

Risk increases where leaders claim:

  • Unique divine appointment.
  • Exclusive access to salvation or truth.
  • Authority over marriage, family, employment, residence, and association.
  • Immunity from criticism.
  • Power to punish dissent spiritually or socially.
  • The right to demand secrecy or total obedience.

Strong religious leadership is not automatically abusive. The test is conduct, consent, transparency, accountability, and freedom to leave.

Relationship to Christianity

Christian churches often criticize Hebrew Israelite doctrine for:

  • Restricting salvation by ancestry.
  • Requiring Mosaic law for salvation.
  • Rejecting the Trinity.
  • Reinterpreting Jesus's identity and mission.
  • Claiming churches concealed Black Israelite identity.

Some Hebrew Israelite groups respond that mainstream Christianity was used to justify slavery, erase biblical law, and disconnect Black people from history.

Relationship to Judaism

Relationships range from synagogue participation and conversion to direct hostility.

Some Black Hebrew congregations seek recognition, dialogue, or integration within wider Jewish life.

Radical camps may reject rabbinic Judaism, the Talmud, conversion authority, and modern Jewish identity while claiming exclusive Israelite legitimacy.

The phrase “relationship to Judaism” therefore requires a named organization.

Relationship to the State of Israel

Factions differ regarding the modern State of Israel.

  • Some seek residence or symbolic return.
  • Some view the state as controlled by false Jews.
  • Some distinguish biblical Israel from modern government.
  • Some support Israel politically.
  • Some are indifferent to modern Zionism.

A claim to Israelite identity is not automatically support for the Israeli government or Zionism.

Relationship to Black Nationalism

Hebrew Israelism overlaps with Black nationalism through:

  • Self-definition outside White institutions.
  • Economic self-help.
  • Historical recovery.
  • Separation and nationhood.
  • Male discipline and family reconstruction.
  • Criticism of integration.

It differs by locating national identity in biblical Israel and divine covenant rather than only African, American, or political categories.

Relationship to African Identity

Some factions affirm African ancestry while claiming Israelite descent within African history.

Others reject African identity and teach that Black Americans were scattered Israelites mistakenly called African.

Rejection of African ancestry can conflict with genetic, genealogical, and historical evidence connecting African Americans to African populations.

Relationship to “Black Native American” Claims

Many Twelve Tribes charts identify Native American and Latin American peoples as Israelites.

Some speakers combine Hebrew Israelite identity with claims that Black people were the original inhabitants of the Americas.

Israelite ancestry, African ancestry, tribal citizenship, and pre-Columbian American ancestry are separate claims requiring separate evidence.

Relationship to Moorish Movements

Hebrew Israelite and Moorish movements may overlap in:

  • Rejection of imposed racial labels.
  • Claims of ancient nationality.
  • Alternative names and identity documents.
  • Criticism of Christianity and American government.
  • Online audiences and sovereignty discussions.

They are not the same movement. Most Hebrew Israelite groups do not recognize Moorish nationality as their central identity.

Relationship to Freemasonry

Some early Black religious leaders, including William Saunders Crowdy, had Masonic involvement. Black fraternal culture influenced leadership, ceremony, institutional organization, and ideas of restored ancient wisdom.

This does not establish that Freemasonry created or controls every Hebrew Israelite movement.

Specific Masonic membership and organizational influence should be documented rather than inferred from titles, clothing, or symbolism.

Community Service

Hebrew Israelite organizations may conduct:

  • Food and clothing distribution.
  • Prison and reentry ministry.
  • Employment assistance.
  • Youth education.
  • Public safety protests.
  • Health and dietary programs.
  • Family counseling.
  • Disaster and community relief.

Service claims should be evaluated through records, recipients, budgets, outcomes, and community partners.

Potential Constructive Contributions

  • Encouragement of discipline and scriptural literacy.
  • Criticism of churches that accommodated racism.
  • Promotion of Black historical inquiry.
  • Family, health, and community standards.
  • Mutual aid and local service.
  • Rejection of criminality and substance abuse in some groups.
  • Creation of independent Black religious institutions.

Potential Harms

  • Antisemitic conspiracy theories.
  • Racial hierarchy and collective punishment teachings.
  • Authoritarian control and leader worship.
  • Financial exploitation.
  • Family separation and coercive gender rules.
  • Hostility toward other Black institutions.
  • Historical misinformation presented as proven genealogy.
  • Public confrontation escalating into threats or violence.
  • Recruitment diversion from evidence-based civic organization.

Institutional Responsibility Test

  1. Identify the exact organization. Name the congregation, camp, school, ministry, channel, or community.
  2. Identify the lineage. Determine whether it descends from Crowdy, Matthew, Ben Ammi, One West, or another tradition.
  3. Identify the leader and office. Verify names, titles, dates, and actual authority.
  4. Identify the doctrine. Separate ancestry belief from antisemitism, racial supremacy, or violence.
  5. Identify the legal entity. Distinguish church, nonprofit, business, property company, school, and media operation.
  6. Trace resources. Determine whose money, buildings, staff, channels, vehicles, or mailing lists supported the conduct.
  7. Locate authorization. Seek sermons, written rules, directives, minutes, financial records, and credible testimony.
  8. Test leadership knowledge. Determine whether leaders approved, concealed, ignored, condemned, or corrected the conduct.
  9. Distinguish belief from action. Religious identity does not prove operational threat.
  10. Assign responsibility proportionately. Do not attribute one faction's conduct to every Black Hebrew or Black Jew.

Proximity to the Malone Network

Hebrew Israelite movements are potentially adjacent to the Malone Network because they recruit from the same Black communities and address identity, history, family, morality, organization, leadership, oppression, and institutional distrust.

Proximity increases where a group:

  • Recruits Malone members, supporters, students, or officers.
  • Seeks to influence Malone history or doctrine.
  • Participates in Malone events, partnerships, or online audiences.
  • Places members inside Malone leadership, education, security, or communications.
  • Claims that Malone must submit to Israelite religious authority.
  • Uses Malone material as an endorsement.

Shared subject matter does not establish embedded access.

Alignment Assessment

No Hebrew Israelite organization is governed by or doctrinally aligned under Malone authority.

Limited cooperation may be possible with specific congregations on community service, family stability, education, health, historical research, or public safety where conduct is compatible.

Alignment should be denied where a group requires:

  • Religious submission as a condition of cooperation.
  • Acceptance of unverified ancestry claims.
  • Antisemitism or racial hierarchy.
  • Loyalty to outside leaders above Malone Doctrine.
  • Coercive gender practices or financial control.
  • Hostility toward Malone's independent authority.

Hostility Assessment

The broader movement is not proven hostile to Malone.

Hostility would require documented conduct such as:

  • Targeted infiltration or capture of Malone institutions.
  • Recruitment designed to remove Malone personnel or redirect their loyalty.
  • False claims that Malone belongs to or endorses a camp.
  • Coordinated obstruction of Malone funding, partnerships, employment, or access.
  • Threats or retaliation against Malone personnel.
  • Deliberate falsification of Malone research or doctrine.
  • Organized reputational, legal, financial, cyber, or physical attack.

Controlled Opposition Assessment

No evidence establishes that the entire Hebrew Israelite ecosystem is centrally controlled by government, Jewish institutions, White organizations, intelligence services, or external donors.

A specific group could function as controlled opposition if evidence shows that it:

  • Claims Black liberation while redirecting members away from effective organization.
  • Receives concealed support from an institution it publicly opposes.
  • Creates predictable racial conflict that benefits outside power.
  • Suppresses evidence while protecting an undisclosed sponsor.
  • Repeatedly fragments independent Black leadership without building durable alternatives.

Dysfunction, extremism, poor strategy, leader ego, or monetization do not independently prove external control.

Confusion Assessment

Confusion is a major baseline explanation for the movement's fragmentation.

Sources of confusion include:

  • Mixing religious truth claims with genealogy.
  • Conflating ancient Israelites, Jews, Hebrews, and modern Israel.
  • Using modern racial categories for ancient peoples.
  • Confusing Black Jews with extremist camps.
  • Treating every group as One West.
  • Interpreting shared symbols as organizational control.
  • Using social-media repetition as historical proof.
  • Assuming that oppression proves one exclusive ancestry theory.

Evidence Classification

  • Documented fact: Supported by corporate records, court files, official publications, archives, financial records, or corroborated reporting.
  • Official religious claim: A belief stated by a named congregation, leader, or doctrinal source.
  • Documented membership: Verified affiliation with a specific body.
  • Documented rhetoric: Preserved audio, video, text, or publication attributable to a speaker.
  • Testimony: A witness account evaluated for access, specificity, bias, credibility, and corroboration.
  • Circumstantial evidence: Facts supporting an inference without directly proving intent or organizational command.
  • Reasonable inference: An analytical conclusion clearly separated from established fact.
  • Speculation: A possibility lacking adequate evidence.
  • Identity conflation: Treating Black Jews, Dimona residents, COGASOC, One West camps, and independent teachers as one actor.

Preliminary Alignment Assessment

“Black Hebrews” are not one organization and cannot receive one universal alignment or threat classification.

Historic congregations and Black Jewish communities should be distinguished from extremist One West-derived factions. Actual Black Jews should not be treated as an external threat merely because of race or religion.

Hebrew Israelite organizations remain external to Malone and untrusted by default until their leadership, doctrine, finances, conduct, and proximity are verified.

The appropriate preliminary classification is: religious and identity ecosystem, not one group; adjacent to Malone; institutionally fragmented; historically significant; potentially constructive in limited settings; untrusted by default; generally confused, doctrinally independent, or competitive rather than proven controlled opposition; selected radical factions present elevated recruitment, antisemitism, authoritarian-control, financial, and confrontation risks; requires branch-specific, organization-specific, leader-specific, and conduct-specific verification.

Responsible Analysis

A rigorous profile must avoid two opposite errors.

The first is to dismiss every Black Hebrew as irrational, hateful, or fake. That erases more than a century of Black religious history and conflates actual Jewish congregations with extremist camps.

The second is to treat every ancestry, conspiracy, or replacement claim as established because it offers dignity or explains oppression.

The correct method is to identify the body, record the doctrine, verify the leader, trace the money, preserve the rhetoric, test the history, distinguish protected belief from harmful conduct, and assign responsibility only where evidence supports it.

The governing rule is: Religious identity does not establish ancestry, ancestry claims do not establish authority, and shared labels do not establish institutional responsibility.
