Crips Gang

Overview

The Crips are a decentralized alliance and shared identity of street and prison gangs that originated in predominantly Black neighborhoods of Los Angeles during the late 1960s and early 1970s.

The alliance is historically associated with Raymond Washington and Stanley “Tookie” Williams. It expanded through neighborhood formation, rivalry, migration, incarceration, imitation, music, family ties, and organized recruitment.

“Crips” does not identify one corporation, one national president, one treasury, one constitution, or one universal chain of command. It identifies a broad gang culture containing autonomous and semi-autonomous sets, neighborhood alliances, prison formations, regional enterprises, splinters, and locally adopted identities.

Federal and state cases document Crip-affiliated enterprises involved in murder, attempted murder, assault, robbery, carjacking, extortion, drug trafficking, firearms offenses, fraud, sex trafficking, witness intimidation, obstruction, and internal violence.

Core Conclusion

The Crips are best classified as: a decentralized gang alliance whose common identity can facilitate belonging, protection, reputation, territorial control, criminal commerce, interstate networking, and violent enforcement without establishing one universal command.

Crip identity developed in communities affected by segregation, poverty, political disinvestment, violence, and conflict with police and rival groups. Those conditions are relevant to historical explanation. They do not excuse later coercion, murder, exploitation, drug distribution, intimidation, or harm imposed upon Black communities.

For Malone purposes, the Crips are external and untrusted. Crip-affiliated structures are operationally adverse wherever gang loyalty, criminal obligations, recruitment, intimidation, weapons, drug commerce, retaliation, or institutional capture threaten Malone personnel, facilities, programs, or participants.

What the Crips Are

The Crips are a networked gang identity composed of separate local organizations.

The identity can create mutual recognition, shared symbolism, inherited rivalries, reputational value, and opportunities for cooperation. The strength of those connections varies by location, set, prison system, and individual.

What the Crips Are Not

The Crips are not one national corporation or one centrally governed army.

A person described as a Crip does not automatically report to a Los Angeles founder, a California set, an incarcerated national leader, or another Crip group in a different state.

Origins in Los Angeles

Government and historical sources generally place the emergence of the Crips in South Los Angeles around 1969.

The early organization grew within a wider environment of neighborhood clubs, youth groups, racial conflict, police pressure, community disinvestment, and the decline or suppression of earlier Black political organizations.

The exact sequence of formation is described differently across official records, participant accounts, and later histories. The broad conclusion is stronger than every detail of any single origin story.

Raymond Washington

Raymond Washington is widely identified as a principal founder of the Crips.

Accounts describe Washington forming an early group in the area around Fremont High School and bringing together neighborhood youth under a larger identity.

Claims about his exact intentions, political purpose, and the original meaning of the name vary. Later criminal developments should not be retroactively attributed to every unverified founding intention.

Stanley “Tookie” Williams

Stanley “Tookie” Williams is widely identified as a prominent co-founder who helped consolidate Crip identity on the west side of Los Angeles.

Williams later renounced gang violence while incarcerated and produced anti-gang writings. His later advocacy does not erase his conviction history, and his conviction history does not make every later public claim about Crip origins automatically accurate.

Name-Origin Disputes

Multiple explanations have been offered for the word Crip, including derivation from “crib,” descriptions of members’ appearance, and later backronyms.

No acronym should be treated as the proven original meaning without contemporaneous evidence. Claims that the name originally meant “Community Revolution in Progress” or another political phrase are commonly repeated but remain disputed.

Early Expansion

Crip identity expanded rapidly as neighborhood groups adopted the name, formed alliances, or were categorized by rivals and law enforcement as Crip sets.

Expansion increased numbers and reputation but also created fragmentation. Sets could share a Crip identity while fighting one another.

The Set as the Basic Unit

A set is a locally named Crip-affiliated group.

  • A neighborhood, street, housing development, or school.
  • A founder or local lineage.
  • A prison or regional formation.
  • A named criminal enterprise.
  • A faction that separated from another set.
  • A local adaptation of a California identity.

Responsibility should be assigned to the exact set and actor involved.

Set Autonomy

Crip sets may cooperate, remain neutral, compete, or fight one another.

Shared Crip identity does not guarantee peace, shared leadership, common finances, or coordinated criminal activity.

Local disputes may involve territory, retaliation, money, drug markets, personal relationships, leadership conflicts, or inherited rivalries.

No Universal National Command

No verified universal leader controls every Crip set in the United States.

Some named organizations possess structured regional or interstate leadership. That authority should not be projected onto unrelated Crip sets.

Neighborhood and Gangster Traditions

Los Angeles Crip identity includes broad traditions commonly described as Neighborhood Crips and Gangster Crips. These are not one national bureaucracy with two departments.

The terms can reflect alliance patterns, inherited rivalries, local culture, and naming conventions. Their meaning must be established in the exact jurisdiction under review.

Neighborhood Crips

Neighborhood Crip identities commonly use “Neighborhood,” “NH,” “Rollin,” or related local language.

Named Neighborhood Crip sets may share traditions and alliances while retaining separate leadership and local priorities.

Neighborhood Crip identity does not establish membership in the Rollin’ 60s or any other particular set.

Gangster Crips

Gangster Crip identities commonly include “Gangster,” “Gangsta,” or “GC” in the set name.

Gangster Crips are not one universal organization. Eight Tray Gangster Crips, Broadway Gangster Crips, Insane Gangster Crips, Playboy Gangster Crips, and other groups require separate attribution.

Rollin’ 60s Neighborhood Crips

The Rollin’ 60s Neighborhood Crips originated in the west side of South Los Angeles and became one of the most widely recognized Crip sets.

Federal cases document Rollin’ 60s enterprises operating in multiple states. Those cases describe localized leadership, districts, units, rules, drug trafficking, robberies, carjackings, shootings, and murder.

A prosecution of a particular Rollin’ 60s enterprise does not prove that every person or faction using the name shares one command.

Rollin’ 90s and Other Rollin Sets

Rollin’ identity appears in several Neighborhood Crip set names, including Rollin’ 90s and other numbered formations.

The number usually reflects a geographic or historical identity. Similar naming does not prove direct command by the Rollin’ 60s.

East Coast Crips

East Coast Crips identifies a family of Los Angeles Crip sets and, in some places, regional groups using related naming.

The phrase does not mean every Crip organization located on the eastern side of the United States.

Grape Street Watts Crips

The Grape Street Watts Crips are historically associated with the Watts area of Los Angeles.

Grape Street identities later appeared in other states. Federal New Jersey prosecutions documented a Grape Street Crips enterprise involved in murder, robbery, extortion, and drug trafficking.

The New Jersey enterprise should not be treated as the governing body of every Grape Street faction.

Eight Tray Gangster Crips

Eight Tray Gangster Crips, also written Eight Trey or ETG, is a major Gangster Crip identity with Los Angeles roots and regional formations outside California.

Federal cases in Baltimore and the Carolinas document structured ETG enterprises involved in drug trafficking, shootings, murder, racketeering, and interstate supply relationships.

ETG authority is not universal Crip authority.

Five Deuce Broadway Gangster Crips

The Five Deuce Broadway Gangster Crips are a South Los Angeles Gangster Crip set.

A federal racketeering case charged dozens of members and associates and documented murders, robberies, narcotics sales, and efforts to control drug commerce near Skid Row.

Insane Gangster Crips

Insane Gangster Crips identities exist in Southern California and other jurisdictions.

The name may identify separate local structures. Evidence should establish the location, leadership, and actual connection before responsibility is assigned.

Playboy Gangster Crips

The Playboy Gangster Crips name is used by multiple local formations.

A federal Detroit prosecution resulted in convictions of sixteen members for racketeering and related offenses. The convicted Detroit enterprise should not be equated automatically with every Playboy Crip group.

Oriental Mafia Crips

The Oriental Mafia Crips are a Southeast Asian Crip-affiliated street gang associated with San Diego.

Their existence demonstrates that Crip identity is not exclusively Black, although the alliance originated primarily in Black Los Angeles communities.

Hyena Crips

The Hyena Crips are a Brooklyn-based gang prosecuted as a racketeering enterprise.

Federal proceedings documented murder, shootings, kidnapping, robbery, fraud, and other crimes. Guilty pleas and trial convictions should be distinguished from charges still pending or subject to appeal.

Four Deuce Oakland Crips

The Four Deuce Oakland Crips name appears in federal cases involving Texas-based activity.

A 2026 federal sentence involved a member convicted on drug and firearms charges. The case concerns the individual and named set, not every Crip organization.

Omaha Crips Enterprise

A 2025 federal indictment charged thirteen alleged Crips members in Omaha with organized-crime offenses including murder, firearms trafficking, assault, and racketeering-related conduct.

An indictment is an allegation. Procedural status must be updated as defendants plead, proceed to trial, receive dismissals, or are convicted.

Hoover Identity and Historical Crip Roots

Hoover groups have historical connections to Crip identity, and some named groups continue to use “Hoover Crips.”

Other groups identify as Hoover Criminals rather than Crips. The current identity of a particular Hoover group must be established rather than inferred from historical lineage.

“Hoover” is therefore not automatically interchangeable with “Crip.”

West Coast and National Adoption

Crip identity began in Los Angeles and later spread nationally.

A set outside California may result from migration, prison ties, authorized expansion, imitation, local rebranding, or independent adoption. Similar names do not prove a verified chain of command back to Los Angeles.

Regional Adaptation

Crip identity has spread through:

  • Migration and family ties.
  • Prison and jail transfers.
  • Neighborhood imitation.
  • Music, film, and popular culture.
  • Organized recruitment.
  • Interstate drug and firearms relationships.
  • Independent adoption of Crip symbols and names.

Regional organizations may develop rules and leadership that differ from their claimed California lineage.

Prison Expansion

Jails and prisons can spread gang identity by concentrating people from different neighborhoods and states.

  • Protection and group alignment.
  • Contraband markets.
  • Debt and enforcement.
  • Racial or regional conflict.
  • Communication with outside associates.
  • Recruitment of people returning to communities.

Street and Prison Authority

Some Crip organizations maintain separate street and prison leadership.

Incarcerated leaders may retain influence through illicit phones, letters, visitors, trusted associates, financial intermediaries, and coded communication.

Specific command must be demonstrated through evidence. Incarceration alone does not prove continuing authority.

Members and Associates

Criminal cases distinguish leaders, members, associates, suppliers, relatives, romantic partners, facilitators, customers, and unaffiliated participants.

Association does not automatically establish membership, criminal intent, or knowledge of every organizational purpose.

Women in Crip-Affiliated Organizations

Women may serve as members, leaders, associates, financial facilitators, recruiters, relatives, partners, or victims.

Gender does not establish minor responsibility, lack of authority, or criminal involvement. Each role requires evidence.

Youth Recruitment

Young people may be recruited through:

  • Neighborhood identity.
  • Family and peer relationships.
  • Protection from violence.
  • Reputation and status.
  • Access to money, drugs, or weapons.
  • Music, social media, and local celebrity.
  • Retaliation following victimization.

Coercive Recruitment

Recruitment is not always voluntary.

  • Threats for refusing alignment.
  • Pressure from relatives or peers.
  • Forced prison or neighborhood alignment.
  • Debt obligations.
  • Fear of rivals.
  • Retaliation for attempting to leave.

Belonging and Identity

Crip identity can offer a substitute family, status system, protection network, historical narrative, and sense of purpose.

These functions help explain persistence. They do not legitimize violent or criminal authority.

Leadership and Rank

Some Crip enterprises use formal or informal titles, seniority systems, councils, shot-callers, generals, or locally defined ranks.

Rank systems vary. A title in one Rollin’ 60s unit does not automatically carry authority in Grape Street, ETG, Broadway, or another Crip organization.

Internal Rules

Federal cases document rules concerning loyalty, attendance, dues, discipline, retaliation, communication, and recognition within particular Crip enterprises.

This dossier does not reproduce internal gang codes, initiation methods, or operational instructions. Their evidentiary relevance is that they may demonstrate organization and authorization in a specific case.

Internal Discipline

Documented reasons for internal punishment include:

  • Disobedience.
  • Failure to share revenue or pay obligations.
  • Cooperation with authorities.
  • Loss of drugs, weapons, or money.
  • Unauthorized departure.
  • Conduct viewed as embarrassing or endangering the set.

Punishment may include assault, robbery, shooting, or murder.

Criminal Activity

Crip-affiliated enterprises have been charged or convicted in cases involving violent crime, narcotics, firearms, fraud, exploitation, and obstruction.

The conduct of one person or enterprise should not be attributed to every Crip set. Organizational responsibility requires proof of authorization, participation, benefit, ratification, or a sustained enterprise purpose.

Violence

Federal and state cases document Crip-affiliated murder, attempted murder, drive-by shootings, retaliatory attacks, assault, kidnapping, robbery, and violence in aid of racketeering.

Violence may serve territory, retaliation, internal discipline, witness suppression, reputation, or protection of criminal commerce.

Murder and Attempted Murder

Murder and attempted murder appear in multiple adjudicated Crip enterprise cases.

Every homicide must be attributed to the exact defendant, set, authorization level, and procedural status. Gang membership alone does not prove responsibility for an unsolved killing.

Retaliation Cycles

Gang labels can convert an individual dispute into collective retaliation.

Retaliation may target a rival, an associate, a neighborhood, a vehicle, a memorial gathering, or uninvolved bystanders. Cycles can persist after the original actors are dead or incarcerated.

Drug Trafficking

Drug trafficking is a repeatedly documented source of revenue for Crip enterprises.

Cases involve cocaine, crack cocaine, heroin, fentanyl, methamphetamine, marijuana, counterfeit pills, and other substances.

Revenue may fund weapons, leadership, incarcerated members, vehicles, legal expenses, and continuing operations.

Fentanyl Risk

Recent Crip-related federal cases include fentanyl distribution and pressed counterfeit pills.

Fentanyl increases the lethality of street drug markets and can kill users who do not know it is present.

Firearms

Firearms are documented in Crip-related murder, assault, robbery, trafficking, retaliation, and intimidation cases.

Some enterprises obtain weapons through straw purchasers, theft, interstate movement, illegal conversion devices, or exchanges with other criminal groups.

Robbery, Carjacking, and Extortion

Federal Crip prosecutions include armed robbery, carjacking, home invasion, extortion, and coercive collection of money or property.

Such conduct extracts resources from the same communities in which gangs may claim to provide protection.

Fraud

Crip-affiliated defendants have been charged or convicted in bank fraud, wire fraud, identity theft, unemployment or benefits fraud, and misuse of nonprofit or program funds.

Fraud can involve participants with no traditional gang appearance and may be concealed inside apparently legitimate activity.

Sex Trafficking

Federal cases have convicted Crip-affiliated individuals of sex trafficking, including exploitation of minors.

Trafficking may involve force, fraud, coercion, transportation, threats, debt, violence, and control over victims’ earnings.

Exploitation of women and children is incompatible with any claim of community protection.

Witness Intimidation

Crip enterprises may threaten, assault, kidnap, or kill witnesses, victims, cooperating defendants, and suspected informants.

Witness intimidation creates private control by making truthful reporting dangerous.

Community Harm

Crip-affiliated violence is often concentrated in Black and low-income neighborhoods.

  • Death and disability.
  • Trauma and retaliation cycles.
  • Business loss and reduced investment.
  • School disruption.
  • Family displacement.
  • Increased surveillance and aggressive policing.
  • Loss of trust among neighbors.
  • Diversion of public resources from constructive development.

Threat to Black Residents

Most people living in Crip-identified neighborhoods are not gang members.

Residents may face pressure from gangs, rival groups, and overbroad law-enforcement response.

Public safety must protect residents without treating the entire community as criminal.

Threat to Black Youth

Youth may be harmed as recruits, victims, witnesses, relatives, students, or bystanders.

Gang identity can redirect intelligence, courage, loyalty, and organizational ability into retaliation and criminal commerce instead of durable Black institutions.

Threat to Families

Family members may experience:

  • Fear of retaliation.
  • Loss through death or incarceration.
  • Pressure to move money, weapons, or messages.
  • Searches and investigations.
  • Financial support burdens.
  • Threats from rivals or fellow members.

Family relationship alone does not prove participation.

Threat to Rival Gangs

Crip rivalries may target Blood sets, other Crip sets, Hoover groups, local gangs, prison organizations, and personal enemies.

The exact rivalry map changes by location and time. Historic labels should not substitute for current evidence.

Relationship to the Bloods

The Blood alliance formed partly in response to expanding Crip power.

Crip–Blood rivalry became one of the most recognized gang conflicts in the United States.

Rivalry is not universal. Local truces, personal relationships, criminal partnerships, peace initiatives, and changing neighborhood conditions also occur.

Crip-on-Crip Conflict

Crip sets may fight other Crip sets.

Shared identity can be outweighed by neighborhood loyalty, money, personal disputes, alliance traditions, leadership conflict, and revenge.

Cross-Gang Cooperation

Crip members may cooperate with Bloods, Hoovers, Gangster Disciples, cartels, prison gangs, or independent traffickers for drugs, fraud, robbery, firearms, or shared criminal objectives.

Cooperation does not prove organizational merger or the end of local rivalry.

Race and Membership

Crip identity originated primarily in Black communities and remains strongly associated with Black membership.

Asian, Latino, White, Pacific Islander, and other Crip-affiliated groups or members have also been documented.

Race alone is never evidence of membership.

Non-Black Crip Sets

Some Asian and multiethnic gangs use Crip identity, including organizations documented in Southern California.

Their relationship to Black Crip sets may involve alliance, shared symbolism, local history, or little operational contact. The relationship must be established rather than assumed.

Colors and Symbols

Blue is widely associated with Crip identity.

Crip-affiliated groups may also use set names, neighborhood numbers, letters, sports-team imagery, bandanas, altered spelling, hand signs, and locally specific symbols.

These are indicators requiring context. They are not conclusive proof.

Color Is Not Membership

Blue clothing is common in ordinary life, schools, sports, religions, employment, and fashion.

A person should not be classified as a Crip from color alone.

Hand Signs and Language

Crip-affiliated groups may use hand signs, greetings, numbers, altered spelling, and local vocabulary.

Popular culture and online imitation make isolated symbols unreliable.

This dossier does not reproduce coded communications or operational gang instructions.

Music and Popular Culture

Crip identity appears in music, fashion, film, social media, sports culture, and celebrity disputes.

  • Actual affiliation.
  • Neighborhood association.
  • Commercial branding.
  • Artistic persona.
  • Imitation.
  • Security or business relationships.

Public performance does not establish command authority.

Celebrity Affiliation Claims

Claims that artists, athletes, entrepreneurs, or public figures belong to Crip sets should be verified through reliable admissions, court records, authenticated relationships, or other strong evidence.

Lyrics, colors, friendships, photographs, and rumors are insufficient by themselves.

Social Media

Social media can support recruitment, threats, reputation, disputes, memorialization, intelligence collection, fraud, and monetization.

Posts may also be false, hacked, performative, copied, manipulated, or taken out of context.

Territory

Some Crip sets claim neighborhoods, blocks, apartment complexes, schools, parks, commercial corridors, or prison units.

Residents and public institutions do not become gang property because a set claims territory.

Private Governance

A gang can function as an unauthorized local government by attempting to control:

  • Who may enter, associate, or conduct business.
  • Drug and underground markets.
  • Debt and punishment.
  • Retaliation and conflict.
  • Neighborhood reputation.
  • Witness behavior.
  • Use of public or community space.

Private coercive order is not legitimate governance.

Protection Versus Predation

A gang may protect selected members while preying upon the wider community.

The proper test is not whether insiders feel protected. The test is whether the institution reduces total harm, respects consent, protects nonmembers, remains accountable, and operates lawfully.

Gang Identity and Black Organization

Crip sets demonstrate real organizational capacities:

  • Recruitment.
  • Identity creation.
  • Mutual recognition.
  • Territorial knowledge.
  • Communication.
  • Resource collection.
  • Discipline.
  • Persistence across generations.

Malone’s objective is not to imitate criminal coercion. It is to redirect organizational talent into lawful, ethical, durable institutions that protect rather than exploit Black communities.

Gang Databases and False Identification

Gang databases can assist investigations and also create false-positive, due-process, and privacy risks.

A database entry may be based on association, field interviews, informants, symbols, or unverified reports. It is not a criminal conviction.

Gang Validation

Law-enforcement or correctional validation may rely on admissions, associations, tattoos, communications, photographs, informants, possession of materials, and conduct.

Validation should be treated as an investigative classification subject to review, not unquestionable proof of membership or every alleged crime.

False Positives

Incorrect Crip identification may result from:

  • Living in a claimed neighborhood.
  • Wearing blue.
  • Family, school, or employment associations.
  • Music and social-media content.
  • Historic or coerced affiliation.
  • Uncorroborated informant claims.
  • Law-enforcement data errors.
  • Confusion between similarly named sets.

2025 Rollin’ 60s Leadership Indictment

In March 2025, federal prosecutors charged Eugene Henley Jr., known as “Big U,” and alleged associates in a forty-three-count indictment involving racketeering, murder, extortion, fraud, misuse of program funds, prostitution-related allegations, tax offenses, and other conduct.

The indictment alleges that Henley led a Rollin’ 60s criminal enterprise while also operating entertainment and community organizations.

These are allegations unless established through plea, trial, or court finding.

Nonprofit and Community-Program Risk

The 2025 Rollin’ 60s case illustrates the institutional risk created when a person holds both public-facing community authority and alleged gang authority.

A nonprofit title, grant, peace program, entertainment business, or community reputation does not prove criminal conduct. It also does not immunize an organization from financial controls, conflict review, or investigation.

2025 Eastside Rollin’ 20s Indictment

A June 2025 federal indictment charged nine alleged members of the Eastside Rollin’ 20s Crips in Virginia with racketeering, murder, attempted murder, armed robbery, narcotics trafficking, identity fraud, firearms offenses, and conspiracy involving pressed fentanyl pills.

The charges are allegations until adjudicated.

2025 Omaha Indictment

A November 2025 federal indictment charged thirteen alleged Crips members in Omaha with violent crimes in aid of racketeering, murder-related offenses, firearms trafficking, assault, and related crimes.

The named Omaha enterprise should not be treated as the governing body of Crips nationally.

2026 Hyena Crips Proceedings

In January 2026, an eighth member of the Brooklyn-based Hyena Crips pleaded guilty to racketeering crimes involving murder.

In April 2026, a federal jury convicted three Crip members of racketeering, murder, kidnapping, robbery, shootings, and government-program fraud in related proceedings.

Convictions are adjudicated facts subject to post-trial motions and appeal.

2026 Four Deuce Oakland Crips Sentence

In March 2026, a federal court sentenced a Four Deuce Oakland Crips member to more than twenty years for drug and firearms offenses.

The sentence concerns the convicted individual and does not establish collective responsibility for every person using the same identity.

Procedural Status

The dossier must distinguish:

  • Investigation.
  • Arrest.
  • Indictment or information.
  • Charge.
  • Guilty plea.
  • Trial.
  • Dismissal.
  • Acquittal.
  • Conviction.
  • Sentence.
  • Appeal and post-conviction review.

Language should be updated when procedural status changes.

Allegations Versus Findings

Official allegations may be supported by extensive evidence and remain unproven until adjudicated.

A guilty plea establishes the admitted offense. A trial conviction establishes guilt under the applicable burden of proof, subject to appeal. A dismissal or acquittal must be recorded and not omitted.

Institutional Responsibility Test

  1. Identify the exact set. Record the local name, territory, claimed lineage, and jurisdiction.
  2. Identify the organization. Separate Neighborhood Crips, Gangster Crips, Rollin’ sets, Grape Street, ETG, Broadway, Hoover, and other structures.
  3. Identify the person. Record legal name, aliases, dates, location, and alleged role.
  4. Distinguish member from associate. Separate leadership, membership, facilitation, coercion, family ties, and imitation.
  5. Identify the conduct. State the crime, order, threat, payment, communication, or institutional act.
  6. Locate authorization. Determine whether conduct was individual, clique-based, set-directed, regional, or independently committed.
  7. Trace resources. Identify money, drugs, weapons, vehicles, phones, accounts, property, nonprofit funds, or outside facilitators.
  8. Test current status and coercion. Determine whether participation is current, inactive, historic, or involuntary.
  9. Separate allegation from adjudication. Preserve procedural status.
  10. Assign responsibility proportionately. Do not attribute one set’s conduct to all Crips or to an entire neighborhood.

Evidence Classification

  • Adjudicated fact: Established through conviction, plea, final judgment, or court finding.
  • Documented fact: Supported by authenticated records, communications, financial evidence, or corroborated sources.
  • Official allegation: Charged or alleged by a named authority but not yet adjudicated.
  • Government classification: An official gang or threat designation subject to scope and methodology limits.
  • Validated affiliation: Law-enforcement or correctional classification, subject to review.
  • Organizational admission: Statement made by the organization or an authorized representative.
  • Documented association: Verified relationship that does not alone establish membership.
  • Witness testimony: An account assessed for access, credibility, incentive, and corroboration.
  • Historical claim: A claim about origins or development requiring source evaluation.
  • Symbolic indicator: Color, tattoo, sign, phrase, number, or image requiring context.
  • Rumor or speculation: Unverified street, prison, media, or online claim.
  • Brand conflation: Treating every Crip set as one national enterprise.

Proximity to the Malone Network

No direct institutional relationship between the Crips alliance and the Malone Network is established in this dossier.

Proximity may arise through:

  • Neighborhood and youth programs.
  • Violence-interruption work.
  • Correctional education and reentry.
  • Family or employment relationships.
  • Individuals claiming both Malone and Crip affiliation.
  • Threats to Malone facilities, personnel, or participants.
  • Attempts to use Malone programs for recruitment, commerce, money movement, or reputation.

Conceptual Proximity to Malone

Crip identity developed partly around needs for safety, belonging, identity, status, and organized protection in Black neighborhoods.

These needs are relevant to Malone’s institutional mission.

The similarity ends where gang authority depends upon criminal commerce, coercion, retaliation, private violence, exploitation, or territorial control.

Alignment Assessment

The Crips are not aligned with or subordinate to Malone.

Active gang command, criminal obligation, undisclosed set loyalty, or willingness to use violence creates a severe conflict with sensitive Malone roles.

Former members and people under coercion should be assessed individually, with attention to current conduct, safety, rehabilitation, truthfulness, and role sensitivity.

Hostility Assessment

No Crip-wide operation directed specifically against Malone is established.

The alliance should nevertheless be classified as operationally adverse wherever gang objectives conflict with Malone safety, authority, programs, or participants.

Direct hostility would require evidence of threats, targeting, infiltration, retaliation, theft, obstruction, violence, or unauthorized claims involving Malone.

Controlled Opposition Assessment

Controlled opposition is not established.

Crip-affiliated violence can objectively benefit oppressive systems by increasing Black death and incarceration, weakening neighborhoods, providing justification for aggressive policing, and redirecting leadership talent into criminal competition.

Those consequences do not prove that an outside institution created, commands, or centrally directs the Crips alliance.

The preliminary finding is: self-directed and fragmented gang organizations whose conduct may reinforce oppression without proof of alliance-wide external control.

Confusion Assessment

Major confusion includes:

  • Crips alliance versus one gang.
  • One set versus national authority.
  • Neighborhood Crips versus Gangster Crips.
  • Rollin’ 60s versus all Rollin’ sets.
  • Eight Tray versus every Gangster Crip.
  • Hoover Crips versus Hoover Criminals.
  • Association versus membership.
  • Neighborhood residence versus affiliation.
  • Music and fashion versus operational command.
  • Historical lineage versus current organization.

Risk Escalation Indicators

  • Claims that a Crip set controls Malone personnel or facilities.
  • Undisclosed active membership in a sensitive Malone role.
  • Use of Malone locations for recruitment, weapons, drugs, fraud, exploitation, or meetings.
  • Threats naming Malone staff, students, participants, or families.
  • Gang pressure affecting a Malone-connected person.
  • Use of Malone funds, vehicles, accounts, data, or nonprofit status for outside activity.
  • Witness intimidation or retaliation.
  • Weapons acquisition linked to a named dispute.
  • Recruitment within youth, correctional, reentry, or violence-prevention programs.
  • Unauthorized claims that Malone recognizes, funds, employs, or protects a set.

Protective Posture for Malone

  • Do not classify people by race, neighborhood, clothing, music, family, or rumor.
  • Require role-appropriate vetting for security, youth, financial, correctional, grant-funded, and reentry work.
  • Separate active gang obligation from past affiliation and rehabilitation.
  • Protect participant records, addresses, schedules, and family information.
  • Use written controls for facilities, visitors, funds, vehicles, grants, contracts, and technology.
  • Require conflict-of-interest and outside-affiliation disclosures for sensitive roles.
  • Provide confidential reporting and anti-retaliation procedures.
  • Coordinate with qualified legal and public-safety professionals for credible threats.
  • Preserve allegation, validation, charge, conviction, and current-status distinctions.
  • Prohibit vigilantism, collective punishment, racial profiling, and unsupported public accusation.

Disengagement and Rehabilitation

A person can leave gang activity and build a constructive life.

Assessment should consider:

  • Current relationships and obligations.
  • Renunciation of criminal conduct.
  • Threats from former associates.
  • Time since active involvement.
  • Truthfulness and accountability.
  • Restitution and constructive community work.
  • The sensitivity of the proposed Malone role.

Violence Prevention

Effective prevention may include:

  • Credible-messenger programs with strong oversight.
  • Trauma treatment.
  • Safe schools and recreation.
  • Employment and trade pathways.
  • Family intervention.
  • Focused protection for people at immediate risk.
  • Conflict mediation.
  • Lawful accountability for serious violence.

Programs must not allow one gang, former leader, contractor, or nonprofit intermediary to become the unofficial government of a neighborhood.

Community Protection Without Gang Rule

Residents need protection from criminal violence and abusive government action.

The solution is not forced dependence upon a gang or unaccountable policing.

Durable safety requires ethical institutions with consent, oversight, trained personnel, equal protection, financial controls, and measurable outcomes.

Security Does Not Justify Criminalizing Black Communities

Crip history does not justify treating Black youth, poor neighborhoods, formerly incarcerated people, musicians, people wearing blue, or relatives of members as gang participants.

  • Overbroad classification creates false intelligence.
  • It destroys community trust.
  • It endangers unaffiliated people.
  • It strengthens gang claims to represent the neighborhood.
  • It discredits legitimate security work.

Documented Organizational Capabilities

  • Durable shared identity across regions.
  • Rapid local recruitment and recognition.
  • Prison-to-street communication.
  • Territorial knowledge and neighborhood presence.
  • Criminal revenue generation.
  • Violent enforcement and retaliation.
  • Adaptation into autonomous regional sets.
  • Use of music, social media, and cultural influence.
  • Ability to operate through legitimate-seeming businesses or organizations in some cases.

These are threat capabilities, not virtues or models for Malone.

Organizational Vulnerabilities

  • Fragmentation and set rivalry.
  • Dependence upon fear and criminal revenue.
  • Exposure through communications, finances, surveillance, and cooperating witnesses.
  • Contradiction between protection claims and community harm.
  • Leadership disputes and internal violence.
  • Law-enforcement infiltration and prosecution.
  • Recruitment dependence upon social disorder.
  • Loss of legitimacy through harm to women, children, families, and neighborhoods.
  • Difficulty controlling independent groups that copy the brand.

Preliminary Malone Classification

The Crips are not aligned with the Malone Network.

Their proper preliminary classification is: decentralized Crip-affiliated gang alliance rather than one organization; historically rooted in Black Los Angeles neighborhood formation and conflict; structurally fragmented across autonomous sets, broad Neighborhood and Gangster traditions, prison formations, regional enterprises, and copied identities; external and untrusted; operationally adverse because of documented murder, drug trafficking, robbery, carjacking, fraud, sex trafficking, witness intimidation, coercive recruitment, and territorial control; not proven controlled opposition; no direct Malone operation presently established; severe threat wherever verified access, recruitment, criminal obligation, retaliation, institutional misuse, or targeting exists.

Monitoring Priorities

  • Current Crip sets operating near Malone programs or facilities.
  • Rollin’ 60s, Eight Tray, Grape Street, Hyena Crips, and other active prosecutions.
  • Youth recruitment and school proximity.
  • Correctional and reentry relationships.
  • Threats, retaliation, weapons, fentanyl, and fraud affecting Malone participants.
  • Use of nonprofits, violence-prevention programs, entertainment businesses, or grants for gang purposes.
  • False claims of Crip or Malone affiliation.
  • Gang-database errors and false identification.
  • Safe disengagement and rehabilitation.
  • Evidence of direct targeting, infiltration, institutional capture, or witness intimidation.

Responsible Analysis

The Crips should not be romanticized as a unified army defending Black neighborhoods.

They should not be used as a pretext to criminalize entire Black communities.

The governing rule is: identify the exact set, distinguish broad identity from operational command, verify membership and current status, separate historical explanation from documented harm, preserve procedural status, and never attribute one faction’s crimes to every person wearing a color or living in a neighborhood.

Distinguishing the Terms

  • Crips — Broad decentralized gang alliance and culture.
  • Set — Locally named gang unit.
  • Neighborhood Crips — Broad family of sets and alliance traditions.
  • Gangster Crips — Broad family of sets and alliance traditions.
  • Rollin’ 60s — Major Neighborhood Crip set with interstate formations.
  • ETG — Eight Tray or Eight Trey Gangster Crips.
  • Grape Street — Watts-rooted Crip identity with regional organizations.
  • BGC — Broadway Gangster Crips in the relevant context.
  • Hoover Criminals — Hoover groups that should not automatically be classified as Crips.
  • Member — Person established as belonging to a specific organization or set.
  • Associate — Person connected to or assisting members without necessarily holding full status.
  • Gang validation — Investigative or correctional classification, not a conviction.
