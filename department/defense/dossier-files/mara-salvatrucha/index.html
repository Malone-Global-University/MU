MS-13

Overview

Mara Salvatrucha, commonly called MS-13, is a transnational criminal gang that originated in Los Angeles during the 1980s among Salvadoran immigrants and refugees.

The gang later expanded into El Salvador, Honduras, Guatemala, Mexico, other parts of the Americas, and selected communities in the United States and Europe.

MS-13 is not one ordinary neighborhood gang. It combines autonomous local cliques with regional programs, prison-based authority, cross-border communication, and senior Salvadoran leadership structures.

Its documented activities include murder, attempted murder, assault, extortion, robbery, kidnapping, drug trafficking, sex trafficking, human smuggling, obstruction, witness intimidation, money laundering, and weapons offenses.

Core Conclusion

MS-13 is best classified as a transnational criminal gang organized through local cliques and higher-level coordinating structures, sustained by violence, fear, territorial influence, prison authority, recruitment, extortion, and cross-border relationships.

Its decentralization does not eliminate organizational responsibility. Federal cases have documented orders, approvals, money transfers, communications, discipline, and murders connecting local activity to leaders in other states and countries.

For Malone purposes, MS-13 is external, unaligned, and operationally hostile wherever its members or structures seek access, recruitment, extortion, intimidation, criminal commerce, or control affecting Malone personnel, facilities, programs, or communities.

Name and Abbreviation

Mara Salvatrucha is the organization’s most widely used name.

MS-13, MS13, and La Mara may be used in official records and public discussion.

Name use alone does not identify a specific clique, rank, location, or current member.

What “Mara” Means

In Central American usage, mara may refer to a gang or group.

The term does not by itself identify MS-13. Other gangs, including Barrio 18, may also be described as maras.

Meaning of “13”

The number 13 is commonly associated with the thirteenth letter of the alphabet and the gang’s historical alignment with the Southern California Sureño system.

The number is an identity indicator. It is not proof of membership when isolated from conduct, admissions, associations, communications, and other evidence.

Not One Local Street Gang

MS-13 cannot be understood solely as a Los Angeles neighborhood gang.

It contains local street cliques, prison-based leaders, regional programs, national structures, and transnational leadership relationships.

Its strength and command structure vary by location.

Not One Perfectly Centralized Corporation

MS-13 does not operate as one ordinary corporation with a universally transparent chart, registered headquarters, and complete command over every participant.

Local cliques may act independently in daily criminal activity while remaining subject to broader identity rules, financial obligations, leadership approvals, or regional coordination.

Origins in Los Angeles

MS-13 formed in Los Angeles during the 1980s among Salvadorans who had fled civil war, political violence, and social disruption.

The early group developed in neighborhoods where immigrant youth encountered established gangs, victimization, exclusion, and pressure for protection.

Historical vulnerability helps explain formation. It does not justify later predation or violence.

Salvadoran Civil War Context

The Salvadoran civil war displaced large numbers of people and contributed to migration to the United States.

Some young migrants arrived with trauma, disrupted families, limited institutional support, and exposure to violence.

These conditions created vulnerability. They did not predetermine gang membership.

Early Cultural Identity

Early MS-13 identity reportedly included Salvadoran nationality, neighborhood solidarity, music and youth culture, and protection from rival groups.

The organization later developed a more durable criminal identity centered on loyalty, status, territorial influence, discipline, and violence.

Sureño Relationship

MS-13 developed within the broader Southern California gang environment and became associated with the Sureño identity.

Sureño affiliation does not mean every Sureño gang reports to MS-13 or that MS-13 controls the Mexican Mafia.

Specific prison and street relationships must be established through evidence.

Relationship to the Mexican Mafia

California gang records have described MS-13 as operating within the Sureño system historically influenced by the Mexican Mafia prison gang.

This relationship should not be simplified into a claim that every MS-13 clique worldwide receives daily orders from one Mexican Mafia body.

Authority is location-specific and must be documented.

Expansion to Central America

MS-13 expanded in El Salvador and neighboring states through migration, deportation, prison organization, recruitment, neighborhood conflict, and communication with United States members.

Central American conditions transformed MS-13 from a Los Angeles-origin gang into a transnational network.

Deportation and Diffusion

United States deportations contributed to the transfer of gang-experienced individuals to countries with limited capacity to absorb them.

Deportation was one factor among several. It does not fully explain MS-13’s growth, leadership, recruitment, or criminal choices.

El Salvador

El Salvador became a major center of MS-13 membership, prison authority, territorial presence, and senior leadership.

United States federal cases have alleged and proven that leaders in El Salvador directed or approved criminal activity in the United States.

Honduras

MS-13 has maintained significant activity in Honduras through local structures, extortion, violence, prison relationships, and transnational connections.

Honduran MS-13 organizations should be distinguished from Salvadoran and United States cliques.

Guatemala

MS-13 operates in Guatemala alongside other gangs and criminal organizations.

Local organization, territorial influence, and relationships with Barrio 18 and state institutions vary by region.

Mexico

Mexico has served as a transit, refuge, operating, and coordination environment for particular MS-13 leaders and members.

MS-13 is not equivalent to a Mexican cartel. Cooperation, conflict, subcontracting, or coexistence with cartels requires case-specific evidence.

United States Presence

MS-13 has operated in multiple United States regions, including California, New York, Maryland, Virginia, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Texas, Ohio, Tennessee, and other states.

Presence is uneven. A national label does not prove an active clique in every city.

Long Island

Long Island has been a major focus of federal MS-13 prosecutions.

Cases have involved local cliques, national leaders, school-age victims, murders, drug distribution, witness retaliation, and communications with leaders outside New York.

Maryland and Northern Virginia

Maryland and Northern Virginia cases have documented cliques involved in extortion, murder, kidnapping, drug trafficking, and witness intimidation.

Some enterprises demanded “rent” from businesses operating in areas claimed as gang territory.

Massachusetts

Federal prosecutions in Massachusetts have documented MS-13 membership, racketeering, murder, attempted murder, internal discipline, and witness-related offenses.

Specific cases should not be generalized to every Salvadoran community in the state.

Texas

Texas cases have documented MS-13 racketeering enterprises involving murder, extortion, drug trafficking, robbery, obstruction, and witness tampering.

Recent federal sentences confirm continuing prosecution of organized MS-13 conduct.

New Jersey

New Jersey prosecutions have documented local and higher-ranking MS-13 leaders involved in racketeering, murder, extortion, drug trafficking, and witness tampering.

Some cases also demonstrate communication between incarcerated leaders and outside participants.

Other International Presence

Official records have identified MS-13 activity or members in other countries in the Americas and Europe.

International presence does not establish identical strength, command, or criminal markets in every country.

Basic Operating Unit: The Clique

A clique is a locally organized MS-13 subgroup.

A clique may control recruitment, meetings, money collection, discipline, criminal activity, and local territorial claims.

Responsibility should be assigned to the exact clique and actors involved.

Clique Naming

Clique names often reference a location, neighborhood, lineage, or identity.

Similar names may exist in different states or countries. A shared name does not always prove one uninterrupted command structure.

Clique Autonomy

Cliques may exercise substantial autonomy over local activity.

Autonomy can coexist with obligations to regional programs or senior leaders.

The proper question is not whether MS-13 is centralized or decentralized in the abstract, but which decisions were controlled at which level.

Programs

Federal cases describe programs that coordinate multiple cliques within a region or operational structure.

Programs may support communication, discipline, money collection, strategic direction, and relationships with senior leaders.

Zones and Regional Structures

In some environments, MS-13 leadership is organized by zones, regions, or national programs.

Terminology and authority may change over time and across countries.

Ranfla Nacional

The Ranfla Nacional has been described in United States indictments as MS-13’s senior Salvadoran leadership structure or board of directors.

Federal charges allege that members directed violence, criminal activity, organizational expansion, and relationships across El Salvador, the United States, Mexico, and elsewhere.

Charges remain allegations unless established through plea, trial, or court finding.

Ranfla in the Prisons

Senior MS-13 authority has often been exercised from Salvadoran prisons.

Incarceration may restrict movement while increasing access to concentrated gang networks, internal discipline, and communication channels.

National Leadership

Federal cases have identified national-level MS-13 leaders in the United States.

National authority may involve coordination, conflict resolution, approval of violence, financial collection, and communication with Central American leadership.

Local Leadership

Local leaders may be described as clique leaders, first word, second word, palabreros, or by other titles depending on the organization and record.

Titles should be interpreted from authenticated evidence rather than internet glossaries.

The “Word”

MS-13 records sometimes describe leadership authority through possession of the “word.”

This indicates recognized decision-making status within a clique or program.

The dossier does not reproduce internal command procedures or initiation rules.

Members

A member is a person established as belonging to a specific MS-13 clique or structure.

Membership may be shown by reliable admissions, testimony, communications, criminal participation, financial obligations, leadership recognition, or corroborated evidence.

Associates

Associates may assist members, provide transportation, store property, transfer money, communicate messages, sell drugs, supply weapons, or participate in particular crimes.

Association does not automatically establish full membership or knowledge of every organizational purpose.

Women and Girls

Women and girls may be members, associates, partners, relatives, facilitators, recruiters, witnesses, or victims.

Gender does not establish either innocence or organizational authority.

Sexual exploitation and coercion require separate analysis from voluntary association.

Youth Membership

MS-13 has recruited adolescents and young adults in schools, neighborhoods, immigrant communities, shelters, detention settings, and social networks.

Young age may increase vulnerability to coercion, manipulation, retaliation, and status-seeking.

Recruitment

Recruitment may rely upon friendship, family connections, shared nationality, neighborhood identity, protection, status, romantic relationships, money, threats, and retaliation.

Recruitment methods vary by clique and location.

School Recruitment

Federal cases on Long Island and elsewhere have documented recruitment involving students and school-age youth.

Schools can become contact environments without becoming organizationally responsible for gang activity.

Recruitment of Recent Migrants

Recent migrants may face language barriers, trauma, unstable housing, limited family supervision, fear of authorities, and pressure from peers.

These vulnerabilities may be exploited by gangs.

Immigration status is not evidence of gang membership.

Coercive Recruitment

Not all participation is freely chosen.

People may face threats, pressure from relatives, neighborhood danger, prison coercion, debt, or retaliation for refusal.

Coercion affects culpability and protective planning but does not make continuing violence harmless.

Initiation

MS-13 cliques have used violent or ritualized initiation and recognition practices.

Specific methods vary and are not reproduced here.

The relevant institutional question is whether membership and current obligation are reliably established.

Internal Discipline

Federal cases document punishment for disobedience, suspected cooperation, unauthorized conduct, failure to remit money, disrespect, desertion, and violation of gang rules.

Punishment may include assault, kidnapping, attempted murder, or murder.

Leaving MS-13

Disengagement can create serious danger from former associates.

A claim of former membership should be assessed through current conduct, relationships, communications, obligations, time, corroboration, and threat conditions.

Prison Authority

Prisons have served as command, recruitment, discipline, and communication environments for MS-13.

Incarcerated leaders may retain influence over outside members through illicit phones, visitors, intermediaries, money transfers, and coded communications.

Communications

MS-13 structures use in-person meetings, telephones, messaging applications, social media, letters, intermediaries, and prison communications.

Specific operational codes are excluded from this dossier.

Cross-Border Direction

Federal prosecutions have documented or alleged local murders and other crimes ordered, approved, or monitored by leaders in El Salvador.

Cross-border direction is a defining feature of the organization’s transnational threat.

Money Transfers

Local cliques may send money to incarcerated members, regional programs, national leaders, or Central American structures.

Transfers may be funded through extortion, drug sales, robbery, member contributions, or other criminal revenue.

Extortion and “Rent”

Extortion is a core documented MS-13 revenue source.

Businesses, transportation operators, residents, vendors, and other victims may be forced to pay recurring “rent” under threat of violence.

Extortion converts territorial fear into private taxation.

Drug Trafficking

MS-13 cliques have participated in street-level and organized distribution of marijuana, cocaine, heroin, fentanyl, methamphetamine, and other drugs.

The organization is not uniformly structured as a wholesale cartel. Drug scale varies by clique and market.

Robbery

Federal cases document robbery used for revenue, weapons acquisition, retaliation, and intimidation.

Robberies may be individual crimes or organizational acts depending on authorization and benefit.

Kidnapping

MS-13 members have been convicted or charged in kidnapping cases involving extortion, retaliation, punishment, and murder.

Procedural status must be preserved in each case.

Human Smuggling

Federal cases have alleged MS-13 participation in alien smuggling and movement of members across borders.

Smuggling, trafficking, voluntary migration, and family assistance are legally and factually distinct.

Human Trafficking

MS-13 members and associates have been charged or convicted in human-trafficking and sex-trafficking cases.

Trafficking depends upon force, fraud, coercion, or legally defined exploitation and should not be inferred from immigration status alone.

Sex Trafficking

Federal cases document sexual exploitation of minors and adults by MS-13 members and associates.

Such conduct should be attributed to the responsible actors and enterprise while recognizing repeated organizational risk.

Money Laundering

Money laundering may conceal the source, ownership, movement, or destination of criminal proceeds.

MS-13 cases have included transfers intended to support gang operations and leaders.

Weapons

MS-13 violence has involved firearms, machetes, knives, blunt objects, and improvised weapons.

Weapon possession is a capability indicator. Intent and access require separate evidence.

Murder

Murder is repeatedly documented in MS-13 prosecutions.

Victims have included rival gang members, suspected informants, witnesses, people accused of disrespect, former associates, and uninvolved residents.

Attempted Murder and Assault

MS-13 cases include attempted murder, shootings, stabbings, beatings, and assaults with dangerous weapons.

Violence may serve retaliation, discipline, territorial control, recruitment, or reputation.

Machete Violence

Machetes are publicly associated with MS-13 because of documented attacks and their symbolic intimidation value.

MS-13 also uses firearms and other weapons. A machete is not a universal or exclusive marker.

Witness Intimidation

Witness intimidation is a major institutional threat.

Federal cases document threats, kidnapping, murder conspiracies, retaliation, and communication intended to silence witnesses or suspected cooperators.

Obstruction of Justice

MS-13 members may conceal evidence, coordinate false accounts, intimidate witnesses, move fugitives, destroy records, or transmit orders from custody.

Obstruction can protect the organization even when the underlying crime was locally initiated.

Territorial Control

Cliques may claim neighborhoods, streets, parks, schools, businesses, or transportation routes.

A gang claim does not create lawful ownership or legitimate government.

Private Taxation

Extortion payments function as unauthorized private taxation.

They force victims to finance the organization threatening them and undermine lawful institutions.

Private Governance

MS-13 may impose unauthorized rules over movement, business activity, relationships, reporting, and perceived cooperation.

This is coercive control rather than legitimate governance.

Protection Versus Predation

MS-13 may offer belonging or protection to selected members while creating danger for members, families, and surrounding communities.

The proper measure is total harm, consent, accountability, and treatment of nonmembers.

Relationship to Barrio 18

MS-13 and Barrio 18 are longstanding rivals in Central America and in some United States communities.

Rivalry is not identical in every location. Local truces, shifting alliances, and shared criminal interests may occur.

Relationship to Other Gangs

MS-13 may fight, cooperate with, supply, or interact with local street gangs, prison gangs, cartels, and smuggling organizations.

Operational cooperation does not establish merger or permanent alliance.

Relationship to Cartels

MS-13 is sometimes described as a cartel.

That description can be misleading. MS-13 is primarily a gang and racketeering network, although particular members may work with drug-trafficking organizations.

Relationship to Government

MS-13 generally operates outside lawful government and uses corruption, intimidation, evasion, and territorial control to weaken state authority.

Government relationships may include prosecution, imprisonment, negotiation, corruption allegations, informant use, deportation, and intelligence collection.

Salvadoran Terrorism Classification

El Salvador has classified MS-13 and Barrio 18 as terrorist organizations under Salvadoran law.

Foreign legal classifications should be identified as government classifications rather than treated as universal definitions.

United States Transnational Criminal Organization Designation

In October 2012, the United States Treasury designated MS-13 as a transnational criminal organization under Executive Order 13581.

The designation restricts property and transactions subject to United States jurisdiction and supports financial disruption.

United States Foreign Terrorist Organization Designation

On February 20, 2025, the United States designated Mara Salvatrucha as a Foreign Terrorist Organization under federal immigration law.

The designation creates material-support, immigration, financial, and enforcement consequences.

Designation does not eliminate the duty to prove individual conduct and knowledge in particular proceedings.

Specially Designated Global Terrorist Status

MS-13 was also designated a Specially Designated Global Terrorist in February 2025.

This status supplements earlier transnational-criminal sanctions and expands counterterrorism-related financial restrictions.

Terrorism Classification Versus Organizational Motive

MS-13’s United States terrorism designation is a legal classification.

The organization’s dominant activities remain criminal control, revenue, status, retaliation, and survival rather than a coherent public political doctrine.

Legal designation and ideological analysis should not be collapsed.

Material Support Risk

Because MS-13 is a designated Foreign Terrorist Organization, knowingly providing prohibited material support or resources can create serious federal criminal exposure.

Humanitarian, legal, journalistic, family, commercial, and coerced interactions require qualified legal analysis rather than assumption.

Sanctions

United States sanctions have targeted MS-13 as an organization and identified leaders, facilitators, and affiliates.

Sanctions are administrative designations and should be distinguished from criminal convictions.

Government Negotiations and Truces

Governments have at times pursued suppression, negotiation, truces, intelligence penetration, or prison-management arrangements involving gangs.

Official allegations of secret dealings or corruption require source-specific treatment.

Negotiation alone does not prove control, and failure of a truce does not establish controlled opposition.

Law-Enforcement Response

United States responses include federal racketeering prosecutions, immigration enforcement, sanctions, terrorism authorities, task forces, state prosecutions, and local gang investigations.

International responses include joint investigations, extradition, intelligence sharing, and prison operations.

Joint Task Force Vulcan

The United States Department of Justice created Joint Task Force Vulcan in 2019 to coordinate efforts against MS-13.

The task force supports investigations and prosecutions involving leadership, finances, immigration, violent crime, and transnational coordination.

Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act

Federal prosecutors frequently use the RICO statute to address MS-13 enterprises.

RICO allows prosecutors to charge participation in an enterprise through a pattern of specified criminal activity.

A RICO allegation is not a conviction.

Violent Crime in Aid of Racketeering

VICAR charges address murder, assault, kidnapping, or threats committed to gain entrance to, maintain, or increase position in a racketeering enterprise.

These charges can reveal the relationship between individual violence and organizational status.

Terrorism Prosecutions of Senior Leaders

Federal indictments have charged senior Ranfla Nacional leaders with terrorism, material-support, narco-terrorism, racketeering, and alien-smuggling conspiracies.

Each defendant’s procedural status must be updated separately.

2025 High-Ranking Leader Proceedings

In 2025, federal authorities announced the arrest and arraignment of high-ranking alleged MS-13 leaders in cases involving terrorism and racketeering charges.

Arrest and indictment establish custody and accusation, not guilt.

2026 Texas Sentences

In April 2026, eight MS-13 members received federal sentences for a Houston-area racketeering conspiracy involving murders, witness tampering, extortion, drug trafficking, robbery, and obstruction.

Court records stated that some murders were ordered or approved by high-ranking leaders in El Salvador.

2026 Queens Murder Sentences

In March and June 2026, federal courts imposed lengthy sentences in Queens murder cases involving MS-13 members and leadership direction.

These judgments are adjudicated evidence concerning the convicted defendants and enterprise.

2026 Massachusetts Sentence

In May 2026, a federal court sentenced an MS-13 member for racketeering connected to a previously unresolved murder.

The sentence demonstrates continued investigation of older gang violence.

2026 Multi-Murder Convictions

In June 2026, federal prosecutors announced convictions of three MS-13 members for nine murders, kidnapping, and racketeering offenses.

Sentencing and appellate status should be monitored.

Historical Long Island Murders

Federal cases have documented murders of teenagers and young adults in wooded areas, parks, streets, schools, and commercial locations on Long Island.

Victims were sometimes misidentified as rivals or accused of disrespect or cooperation.

Community Harm

MS-13 activity can produce death, trauma, displacement, business closure, school disruption, fear, silence, and loss of trust.

Harm often falls heavily upon Salvadoran, Latino, immigrant, and low-income communities.

Threat to Immigrant Communities

MS-13 may prey upon people who fear police, lack lawful status, speak limited English, or have relatives in gang-controlled areas abroad.

Government overreach can compound this harm when entire immigrant communities are treated as suspect.

Threat to Salvadorans

Most Salvadorans are not gang members and are frequently victims of MS-13.

Nationality, language, tattoos, neighborhood, or migration history cannot substitute for individualized evidence.

Threat to Youth

Youth may be targeted as recruits, victims, witnesses, relatives, romantic partners, students, or bystanders.

Gang fear can distort education, recreation, employment, and normal social development.

Threat to Families

Families may face extortion, retaliation, forced relocation, grief, financial burden, pressure to carry messages, and danger from both gangs and enforcement actions.

Family relationship alone does not establish participation.

Threat to Businesses

Businesses may be threatened for recurring payments, free goods, employment access, transportation, storage, or silence.

Extortion risk is elevated where reporting appears unsafe or ineffective.

Threat to Witnesses

Witnesses, informants, victims, and suspected cooperators face severe danger where MS-13 has reach.

Protection planning must include family members, digital exposure, travel, workplace, school, and cross-border threats.

Threat to Schools

Schools may face recruitment, fights, intimidation, social-media threats, weapons, retaliation, and fear among students.

Protective measures should avoid stigmatizing Salvadoran or Latino students.

Threat to Reentry Programs

Correctional and reentry programs may encounter active members, former members, coerced participants, victims, and people falsely classified.

Programs require risk controls without foreclosing rehabilitation.

Threat to Public Institutions

MS-13 can undermine public institutions through corruption, witness suppression, territorial fear, infiltration, and manipulation of services.

Institutional vulnerability rises when records, facilities, funding, vehicles, or vulnerable populations are accessible.

Race, Ethnicity, and Membership

MS-13 is strongly associated with Salvadoran and Central American membership but has included people of different nationalities and backgrounds.

Ethnicity is never proof of membership.

Immigration Status

Members may be citizens, lawful residents, visa holders, asylum applicants, undocumented migrants, or people in removal proceedings.

Immigration status and gang status are separate questions.

Deportation Status

Removal proceedings are civil immigration processes unless accompanied by criminal charges.

Government gang allegations in immigration cases may rely on evidence different from a criminal conviction.

Procedural safeguards and record accuracy remain important.

Gang Databases

Gang databases may contain useful intelligence and false, outdated, duplicated, or poorly substantiated entries.

A database entry is not a conviction and should not be treated as conclusive without corroboration.

Gang Validation

Validation may consider admissions, associations, communications, tattoos, photographs, informants, arrests, and conduct.

Each indicator has limitations and should be tested for reliability, timing, context, and coercion.

False Identification

False MS-13 identification may result from nationality, neighborhood, clothing, tattoos, social media, school associations, family ties, police error, or unreliable informants.

False classification can endanger innocent people and weaken legitimate security work.

Tattoos

Some MS-13 members use tattoos displaying MS, 13, clique names, symbols, or identity claims.

Modern members may avoid conspicuous tattoos.

A tattoo may reflect former, coerced, imitative, or artistic use and requires context.

Hand Signs and Symbols

MS-13 uses signs, numbers, letters, graffiti, and clique identifiers.

These are investigative indicators rather than automatic proof.

This dossier does not reproduce operational signaling instructions.

Graffiti

Graffiti may mark identity, territory, memorials, threats, rivalry, or individual imitation.

One marking does not establish control of a neighborhood or authorization by senior leadership.

Clothing and Appearance

There is no reliable universal MS-13 uniform.

Ordinary clothing, sports apparel, colors, hairstyles, or cultural presentation cannot establish membership.

Social Media

Social media may support recruitment, threats, reputation, memorialization, intelligence gathering, and communication.

Posts may also be false, copied, coerced, hacked, performative, or outdated.

Music and Popular Culture

Media portrayals often emphasize tattoos, machetes, and extreme violence.

These images can reflect documented conduct while still oversimplifying organization, membership, and community context.

Public Misconception: Every Salvadoran Youth Is Suspect

This is false.

Most Salvadoran youth have no MS-13 affiliation and may be direct targets of gang recruitment or violence.

Public Misconception: MS-13 Controls All Latino Gangs

MS-13 does not control every Latino gang, Sureño set, Central American gang, or immigrant neighborhood.

Rival organizations and unaffiliated groups remain distinct.

Public Misconception: Every Clique Receives Every Order from El Salvador

Federal cases document cross-border direction, but local cliques also exercise autonomy.

Authority must be traced for the specific act under review.

Public Misconception: MS-13 Is Only a Foreign Import

MS-13 originated in Los Angeles.

It later became deeply rooted in Central America and returned to the United States through migration, communication, recruitment, and transnational movement.

Public Misconception: Deportation Alone Eliminates the Threat

Deportation may remove an individual from one jurisdiction while transferring risk, preserving communications, or strengthening foreign structures.

Effective strategy requires prosecution, prevention, financial disruption, witness protection, and international coordination.

Public Misconception: Terrorist Designation Proves Every Allegation

A group designation does not prove that a particular person is a member or that every charged act occurred.

Individual evidence and procedural status remain controlling.

Institutional Responsibility Test

  1. Identify the exact person. Record legal name, aliases, dates, location, and alleged role.
  2. Identify the clique. Determine the local subgroup, territory, lineage, and leadership.
  3. Identify higher structures. Determine whether a program, national leader, prison leader, or Ranfla authority was involved.
  4. Distinguish member from associate. Separate leadership, membership, facilitation, coercion, family ties, and imitation.
  5. Identify the conduct. State the act, order, payment, threat, communication, or institutional decision.
  6. Locate authorization. Determine whether conduct was individual, clique-based, program-directed, or transnationally approved.
  7. Trace resources. Identify money, weapons, vehicles, phones, property, accounts, and facilitators.
  8. Test current status. Determine whether affiliation is active, former, coerced, disputed, or falsely attributed.
  9. Preserve procedure. Separate intelligence, arrest, charge, plea, conviction, sentence, and appeal.
  10. Assign responsibility proportionately. Do not attribute one clique’s conduct to every Salvadoran or every alleged member.

Evidence Classification

  • Adjudicated fact: Established through conviction, guilty plea, final judgment, or court finding.
  • Documented fact: Supported by authenticated records, communications, financial evidence, or corroborated sources.
  • Official allegation: Charged or asserted by a named authority but not adjudicated.
  • Government designation: Formal administrative or statutory classification.
  • Organizational admission: Statement by a verified member or leader, evaluated for reliability and context.
  • Witness testimony: Account assessed for access, motive, credibility, and corroboration.
  • Validated affiliation: Law-enforcement or correctional classification subject to review.
  • Documented association: Verified relationship that does not alone establish membership.
  • Symbolic indicator: Tattoo, sign, phrase, number, photograph, or graffiti requiring context.
  • Rumor or speculation: Unverified claim that cannot support an institutional conclusion.

Procedural Status

Legal reporting must distinguish investigation, stop, detention, arrest, immigration custody, indictment, charge, plea, trial, dismissal, acquittal, conviction, sentence, appeal, and removal.

These stages are not interchangeable.

Controlled Opposition Assessment

No sufficient evidence establishes that MS-13 as a whole was created or is centrally controlled by the United States government, a foreign government, or another outside institution to sabotage vulnerable communities.

Government policy, deportation, corruption, covert contact, informant use, failed truces, or unintended benefit do not alone prove control.

The proper finding is: an independently developed transnational gang whose growth has been influenced by war, migration, deportation, prison systems, weak institutions, criminal opportunity, and government responses, without proof of one external controller.

Informants and Cooperating Witnesses

Governments use informants and cooperating witnesses to investigate MS-13.

An informant may remain dangerous, provide false information, act under authorization, exceed authorization, or face retaliation.

Informant status does not automatically make the organization controlled opposition.

Proximity to the Malone Network

No direct institutional relationship between MS-13 and the Malone Network is established in this dossier.

Possible proximity includes shared neighborhoods, schools, youth programs, correctional education, reentry services, immigration assistance, employment programs, community events, facilities, personnel, families, and audiences.

Conceptual Proximity to Malone

MS-13 arose partly from needs for protection, belonging, identity, and organization among vulnerable youth.

Those human needs may overlap with Malone community-building concerns.

The similarity ends where authority depends upon coercion, extortion, criminal commerce, retaliation, and private violence.

Alignment Assessment

MS-13 is not aligned with, subordinate to, or authorized by Malone.

Active membership, concealed obligation, extortion activity, violent enforcement, witness intimidation, or gang-directed recruitment creates an irreconcilable conflict with sensitive Malone roles.

Former members and coerced persons require individualized assessment.

Hostility Assessment

No organization-wide operation specifically targeting Malone is established.

MS-13 should nevertheless be classified as hostile where verified access or conflict exists because intimidation, recruitment, extortion, retaliation, and violence are central documented capabilities.

Confusion Assessment

  • MS-13 identity versus one local clique.
  • Los Angeles origin versus current Salvadoran leadership.
  • Member versus associate.
  • Salvadoran nationality versus gang status.
  • Sureño identity versus MS-13 membership.
  • Gang validation versus conviction.
  • FTO designation versus individual guilt.
  • Local autonomy versus transnational direction.
  • Former affiliation versus current obligation.
  • Victim, coerced participant, and voluntary offender.

Risk Escalation Indicators

  • Threats naming Malone personnel, students, facilities, or partners.
  • Attempts to collect “rent,” fees, or protection payments.
  • Recruitment inside youth, school, correctional, or reentry programs.
  • Use of Malone facilities for meetings, weapons, drugs, trafficking, or money movement.
  • Undisclosed active membership in a sensitive role.
  • Witness intimidation, retaliation, or suspected information leaks.
  • Cross-border threats involving relatives or participants.
  • Weapons acquisition linked to a named dispute.
  • Unauthorized claims that Malone recognizes, protects, or employs a clique.
  • Access to confidential participant records, addresses, schedules, or immigration information.

Protective Posture for Malone

  • Do not classify people by nationality, language, neighborhood, clothing, tattoos, or rumor.
  • Use role-appropriate vetting for security, youth, finance, immigration, correctional, and reentry work.
  • Separate active gang obligation from former affiliation and rehabilitation.
  • Protect addresses, immigration records, family information, schedules, and witness identities.
  • Control visitors, facilities, vehicles, funds, technology, and data access.
  • Maintain confidential reporting and anti-retaliation procedures.
  • Escalate credible threats to qualified legal and public-safety professionals.
  • Preserve investigation, allegation, validation, conviction, and current-status distinctions.
  • Prohibit vigilantism, collective punishment, ethnic profiling, and unsupported public accusation.
  • Review potential material-support exposure before providing resources to known organizational structures.

Facility Security

Facilities serving youth, immigrants, former prisoners, or high-risk neighborhoods require access controls, visitor procedures, incident reporting, secure records, and emergency plans.

Security should protect participants without turning educational or community programs into punitive environments.

Information Security

Addresses, family identities, immigration status, case information, schedules, phone numbers, and witness details may create severe risk if disclosed.

Access should be limited by role and logged where appropriate.

Financial Controls

Programs should use documented payments, separation of duties, vendor review, cash controls, donation screening, and investigation of coercive collections.

Unusual transfers do not prove gang financing but require review.

Youth Protection

Youth programs should train staff to recognize coercion, grooming, retaliation, sudden behavioral changes, and credible threats.

Intervention should involve guardians, qualified professionals, and law enforcement when legally and safely appropriate.

Witness Protection

Threat reporting must account for family exposure, digital footprints, school routes, employment, housing, and cross-border connections.

Do not promise protection beyond institutional capability.

Disengagement and Rehabilitation

People can leave gang activity and build lawful lives.

Assessment should consider current obligations, truthfulness, time since activity, retaliation risk, criminal accountability, family safety, rehabilitation, and role sensitivity.

Prevention

Prevention may include stable housing, trauma treatment, school support, recreation, employment, language services, mentorship, family support, safe reporting, and focused protection.

Prevention is not a substitute for lawful accountability for serious violence.

Community Protection Without Collective Suspicion

Communities need protection from MS-13 violence and from indiscriminate enforcement.

Overbroad suspicion alienates victims, reduces cooperation, produces false intelligence, and strengthens gang claims that lawful institutions are hostile.

Immigration Services

Organizations serving migrants should separate legal assistance, humanitarian services, criminal reporting, and security screening.

Staff should obtain legal advice concerning confidentiality, mandatory reporting, sanctions, and material-support restrictions.

Lawful Cooperation With Authorities

Cooperation with authorities should be governed by law, institutional policy, safety planning, and protection of due process.

Malone personnel should not conduct unauthorized surveillance, interrogation, detention, or enforcement.

Responsible Public Communication

Public statements should identify the exact person, clique, allegation, procedural status, and source.

Do not publish accusations that expose victims, witnesses, families, or unaffiliated communities without a compelling lawful purpose.

Monitoring Priorities

  • MS-13 cliques operating near Malone facilities or partner communities.
  • Current Ranfla Nacional terrorism proceedings and extraditions.
  • 2025 terrorism-designation implementation and material-support enforcement.
  • Youth recruitment and school proximity.
  • Correctional and reentry relationships.
  • Extortion of businesses and families.
  • Witness retaliation and cross-border threats.
  • Use of social services, transportation, housing, or employment for gang purposes.
  • False gang identification and database errors.
  • Evidence of direct targeting, infiltration, or institutional capture involving Malone.

Documented Organizational Capabilities

  • Durable identity across countries and generations.
  • Local clique formation and adaptation.
  • Prison-to-street and cross-border communication.
  • Recruitment from vulnerable populations.
  • Extortion and distributed revenue collection.
  • Violent enforcement and witness retaliation.
  • Ability to transmit orders across jurisdictions.
  • Use of family, social, and neighborhood relationships.
  • Resilience after arrests and leadership disruption.

Organizational Vulnerabilities

  • Dependence upon fear, secrecy, and criminal revenue.
  • Exposure through communications, money transfers, phones, and cooperating witnesses.
  • Fragmentation among cliques and leaders.
  • Leadership arrests, extradition, sanctions, and long sentences.
  • Contradiction between protection claims and harm to the same communities.
  • Dependence upon vulnerable youth recruitment.
  • Loss of secrecy through digital evidence.
  • Community resistance and witness cooperation when protection is credible.

Preliminary Malone Classification

MS-13 is not aligned with the Malone Network.

Its preliminary classification is: Los Angeles-origin transnational criminal gang; organized through autonomous cliques, regional programs, prison structures, and senior Salvadoran leadership; designated by the United States as a transnational criminal organization, Foreign Terrorist Organization, and Specially Designated Global Terrorist; operationally hostile because of documented murder, extortion, kidnapping, drug trafficking, human and sex trafficking, obstruction, witness intimidation, coercive recruitment, and cross-border direction; not proven controlled opposition; no direct Malone operation presently established; severe threat wherever verified access, recruitment, extortion, intimidation, or targeting exists.

Responsible Analysis

MS-13 should not be romanticized as an immigrant-protection organization.

It should also not be used as a pretext to criminalize Salvadorans, Central Americans, immigrants, asylum seekers, youth, or entire neighborhoods.

The governing rule is: identify the exact clique and person, distinguish local autonomy from higher direction, verify current membership, preserve procedural status, protect victims and witnesses, and never substitute nationality or fear for evidence.

Distinguishing the Terms

  • Mara Salvatrucha / MS-13 — Transnational gang and criminal organization.
  • Clique — Local operating unit.
  • Program — Coordinating structure linking cliques.
  • Ranfla Nacional — Senior Salvadoran leadership structure described in federal cases.
  • Palabrero — Leadership term appearing in some records.
  • Member — Person established as belonging to a specific clique or structure.
  • Associate — Connected person who may assist without full status.
  • Sureño — Broader Southern California gang alignment.
  • Barrio 18 — Separate rival gang.
  • TCO designation — United States transnational-criminal-organization sanctions classification.
  • FTO designation — United States Foreign Terrorist Organization classification.
  • SDGT designation — Specially Designated Global Terrorist classification.
  • Gang validation — Investigative classification, not a conviction.
