Prehistory

Explore the human past before written records, from early hominins and stone tools to agriculture, permanent settlements, and the first recorded societies.

Overview

Prehistory is the study of the human past before the widespread use of written records. It encompasses millions of years of biological evolution, migration, technological change, environmental adaptation, social development, and cultural innovation.

During prehistory, early hominins developed increasingly sophisticated tools, controlled fire, migrated across continents, created symbolic and artistic traditions, domesticated plants and animals, established permanent settlements, and eventually formed increasingly complex societies. These developments laid the foundations for the cities, states, economic systems, and written civilizations of the ancient world.

Prehistory does not end at the same date everywhere. Writing emerged independently in some regions while other societies continued to preserve knowledge through oral traditions, archaeology, material culture, and other non-written forms of evidence.

Chronological Framework

Malone Global University uses the following broad framework for organizing prehistoric history. Dates are approximate and may overlap because technological and social changes occurred at different times in different regions.

  • Human Origins: c. 7 million BCE onward
  • Paleolithic: c. 3.3 million–10,000 BCE
  • Mesolithic: c. 10,000–8,000/5,000 BCE, depending on region
  • Neolithic: c. 10,000–3,000 BCE, depending on region
  • Chalcolithic: generally c. 5,000–3,000 BCE, varying considerably by region
  • Transition to Recorded History: broadly c. 4,000–3,000 BCE in the earliest literate societies

Human Origins

Human origins examines the evolutionary history of hominins and the development of the biological, behavioral, and social characteristics associated with later human populations. The earliest known hominins lived in Africa millions of years before the emergence of the genus Homo.

The study of human origins draws upon fossils, genetics, archaeology, comparative anatomy, paleoenvironmental evidence, and stone-tool assemblages. Major questions include the development of bipedalism, changing brain size, tool manufacture, dietary adaptation, migration, social cooperation, and the emergence of anatomically modern humans.

Paleolithic

c. 3.3 million–10,000 BCE

The Paleolithic, or Old Stone Age, covers the longest portion of human technological and cultural development. Paleolithic societies generally depended on hunting, gathering, scavenging, fishing, and other forms of mobile subsistence.

During this period, hominin populations developed increasingly complex stone industries, controlled fire, expanded into new environments, created art and symbolic objects, developed long-distance social networks, and populated much of Africa, Eurasia, Australia, and eventually the Americas.

  • Explore the Paleolithic

Mesolithic

Beginning around c. 10,000 BCE, with dates varying by region

The Mesolithic, or Middle Stone Age in some regional classification systems, describes a transitional period between Paleolithic hunter-gatherer lifeways and the widespread adoption of agriculture and permanent settlement.

Mesolithic communities frequently developed highly specialized hunting, fishing, gathering, watercraft, microlithic tools, and seasonal settlement systems. In some regions, populations became increasingly sedentary before the full development of agriculture.

The term is used differently across archaeological traditions and is not equally applicable to every region of the world.

  • Explore the Mesolithic

Neolithic

Beginning around c. 10,000 BCE, varying significantly by region

The Neolithic, or New Stone Age, is associated with major transformations in subsistence, settlement, technology, and social organization. In several parts of the world, communities began domesticating plants and animals and increasingly relied on food production rather than exclusively on hunting and gathering.

Agricultural development contributed to population growth, permanent villages, food storage, craft specialization, expanding trade networks, property systems, and increasingly complex forms of social organization. Agriculture nevertheless developed independently in multiple regions and followed different trajectories.

  • Explore the Neolithic

Chalcolithic

Generally c. 5,000–3,000 BCE, depending on region

The Chalcolithic, or Copper Age, describes societies in which copper metallurgy developed alongside continued use of stone tools. It represents an important transitional period in several regions between Neolithic village societies and the more complex urban and state-level societies associated with the Bronze Age.

Chalcolithic communities often displayed expanding trade networks, craft specialization, social differentiation, fortified settlements, religious centers, and increasingly complex economic organization.

Transition to Recorded History

The transition from prehistory to recorded history occurred gradually and unevenly. In parts of Mesopotamia and Egypt, administrative systems, accounting practices, monumental institutions, and early writing emerged during the fourth millennium BCE.

Writing did not immediately replace archaeological evidence or oral traditions. Early historical societies continued to be studied through architecture, burials, artifacts, environmental data, economic remains, and other forms of material evidence alongside written records.

Regions without indigenous written traditions may remain archaeologically prehistoric for organizational purposes even when they had contact with literate societies elsewhere.

Major Developments in Prehistory

Human Evolution

Prehistory includes the emergence and diversification of hominin species, the development of anatomically modern humans, and the eventual global expansion of Homo sapiens.

Technology

Stone tools, fire, clothing, shelters, hunting equipment, watercraft, pottery, weaving, agriculture, metallurgy, and other technologies transformed how human populations interacted with their environments.

Migration

Human populations expanded outward from Africa over long periods, adapting to widely different climates and environments and eventually inhabiting nearly every major region of the world.

Symbolic Culture

Cave paintings, carvings, ornaments, burials, figurines, music, ritual practices, and other forms of symbolic behavior provide evidence for increasingly complex social and cultural life.

Food Production

The domestication of plants and animals altered human settlement, population density, economic organization, labor patterns, environmental relationships, and political development.

Permanent Settlement

Sedentary communities created increasingly complex villages and towns, some of which eventually developed into urban centers with specialized occupations, organized leadership, and long-distance exchange networks.

Evidence and Sources

Because prehistoric societies left few or no written records, historians and archaeologists rely heavily on material and scientific evidence.

  • Archaeological Sites: settlements, camps, hearths, workshops, monuments, and burials.
  • Artifacts: tools, pottery, weapons, ornaments, figurines, and household objects.
  • Human Remains: fossils, skeletons, teeth, and preserved tissue.
  • Genetics: ancient and modern DNA used to reconstruct ancestry and migration.
  • Environmental Evidence: pollen, sediments, animal remains, plant remains, and climate records.
  • Rock and Cave Art: paintings, engravings, carvings, and symbolic markings.
  • Dating Methods: radiocarbon dating, stratigraphy, dendrochronology, and other scientific techniques.
  • Oral Traditions: inherited narratives that may preserve information about migrations, environments, and past communities.

Historiography and Interpretation

Interpretations of prehistory change as new archaeological discoveries, dating methods, genetic evidence, and analytical techniques become available. Older models sometimes presented prehistoric development as a simple progression from primitive to advanced societies. Modern scholarship generally recognizes a much greater diversity of social systems and historical paths.

Terms such as Paleolithic, Mesolithic, Neolithic, and Chalcolithic remain useful, but they were originally developed within particular archaeological traditions and do not describe every society equally well. MGU therefore uses them as organizational frameworks rather than as universal stages through which every society necessarily passed.

Historical Significance

Prehistory contains the foundations of nearly every later dimension of human history. Language, technology, migration, family organization, trade, religion, art, warfare, agriculture, property, settlement, political authority, and environmental modification all have roots extending deep into the prehistoric past.

Understanding these developments helps explain how small and often mobile human communities eventually produced villages, cities, states, empires, and the recorded civilizations studied in Ancient History.

Summary

Prehistory spans the immense period before written documentation became widespread. During this time, hominin populations evolved, migrated, developed technologies, created symbolic cultures, domesticated plants and animals, formed permanent communities, and developed increasingly complex social systems. These transformations established the biological, technological, economic, and social foundations upon which recorded history was built.
