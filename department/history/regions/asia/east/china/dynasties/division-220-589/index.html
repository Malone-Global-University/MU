Lesson Orientation

The collapse of the Han Dynasty in 220 CE did not mark the collapse of Chinese civilization.

It marked the collapse of a particular imperial government.

During the following centuries, political authority fragmented among competing kingdoms, military regimes, regional dynasties, aristocratic families, and states founded by peoples with different cultural and geographic backgrounds.

Historians often describe the years from 220 to 589 as a Period of Division.

Parts of this era are also described through terms such as:

  • Three Kingdoms;
  • Western and Eastern Jin;
  • Sixteen Kingdoms;
  • Northern and Southern Dynasties;
  • Six Dynasties.

These names refer to overlapping political periods and historiographical traditions rather than a single uninterrupted dynasty.

The period was frequently violent, but it was not simply an age of decline.

It was also an era of:

  • large-scale migration;
  • political experimentation;
  • development of southern China;
  • interaction among different peoples;
  • rapid expansion of Buddhism;
  • new artistic and intellectual traditions;
  • changes in warfare and government;
  • the formation of political institutions later used by the Sui and Tang.

The broad transformation can be represented as:

Han collapse → competing states → northern and southern political worlds → institutional transformation → Sui reunification

What Was the Period of Division?

The Period of Division refers broadly to the centuries between the formal end of the Han Dynasty in 220 and the reunification of the major northern and southern political regions under the Sui Dynasty in 589.

During these centuries, no single dynasty continuously controlled the territory formerly governed by the Han Empire.

Instead, numerous states rose, expanded, divided, conquered one another, and disappeared.

A Complex Chronology

The major political phases include:

  • Three Kingdoms: 220–280
  • Western Jin: 265–316
  • Eastern Jin: 317–420
  • Sixteen Kingdoms: approximately 304–439 in northern China
  • Northern and Southern Dynasties: broadly 386–589

These periods overlap because different governments often ruled different regions at the same time.

Political Division, Cultural Connection

Political fragmentation did not mean that the regions became completely isolated from one another.

Warfare, migration, trade, diplomacy, religion, marriage, scholarship, and population movement continued to connect northern and southern China.

The Collapse of Han Authority

During the final decades of the Eastern Han, imperial authority weakened dramatically.

Rebellion, court conflict, powerful landholding families, factional struggles, and military decentralization reduced the emperor's ability to control the empire.

Regional commanders gained increasingly independent armies and political bases.

By the beginning of the third century, several powerful military leaders controlled large territories while the Han emperor retained little practical authority.

In 220 CE, the final Han emperor surrendered the throne.

The imperial system survived as an ideal, but no single government could immediately restore control over the former Han territories.

The Three Kingdoms

The political struggles surrounding the collapse of Han eventually produced three major rival states:

  • Wei in the north;
  • Shu Han in the southwest;
  • Wu in the southeast.

Each claimed political legitimacy and sought to control additional territory.

Wei

Wei controlled much of northern China and possessed the largest population and resource base of the three principal states.

Its political foundations were closely associated with Cao Cao, although the formal Wei dynasty was established by his son.

Shu Han

Shu Han was established in the southwest and claimed continuity with the fallen Han imperial house.

Its rulers governed from the Sichuan Basin, a region protected by difficult geography and supported by substantial agricultural resources.

Wu

Wu controlled much of the lower Yangtze region and southeastern China.

Its development contributed to the growing political and economic importance of southern China.

More Than a Famous War Story

The Three Kingdoms period later became one of the most famous settings in Chinese literature and popular historical memory.

Historians, however, distinguish the historical period from later literary retellings of its people and events.

Western Jin and Temporary Reunification

The political balance among the Three Kingdoms did not last permanently.

Wei conquered Shu Han in 263.

Shortly afterward, the Sima family replaced the Wei ruling house and established the Jin Dynasty in 265.

Jin forces then conquered Wu in 280.

For the first time since the final breakdown of Han authority, most of the former imperial territory was again governed by one dynasty.

This reunited state is known as the Western Jin.

A Brief Reunification

Western Jin reunification demonstrated that political unity remained an achievable goal.

Yet the dynasty was unable to create long-term political stability.

The Collapse of Western Jin

Western Jin suffered from severe conflict within the ruling family and among powerful political elites.

Members of the imperial clan controlled substantial military resources and competed for influence.

Internal warfare weakened the dynasty and damaged the central government's ability to respond to broader military and social pressures.

Northern Political Change

Northern China contained many communities with different political, cultural, and geographic backgrounds.

Some had long served within Han and Jin military systems or lived within imperial territory.

As Western Jin weakened, new political leaders established independent states.

By the early fourth century, the Western Jin government had lost control of much of northern China.

The dynasty's northern capital fell, and surviving members of the court moved south.

Migration and the Development of Southern China

One of the most consequential developments of the period was large-scale migration from northern China toward the south.

Warfare and political instability encouraged members of the court, aristocratic families, officials, farmers, craftspeople, soldiers, and other communities to relocate.

The Yangtze Region

Southern China had already contained important populations, agricultural regions, and political centers before these migrations.

The arrival of additional people, skills, institutions, and capital accelerated the development of the Yangtze River basin and regions farther south.

Over the long term, this contributed to a major shift in Chinese economic geography.

Not an Empty South

Migration should not be understood as northern settlers simply entering unoccupied territory.

Southern regions already contained established communities and diverse populations.

The resulting societies developed through interaction among older southern populations and newer migrants.

Eastern Jin

Members of the Jin ruling house established a new court in southern China in 317.

This government is known as the Eastern Jin.

Its principal capital was at Jiankang, near modern Nanjing.

A Southern Imperial Court

Eastern Jin emperors continued to claim imperial legitimacy even though they did not control northern China.

Powerful aristocratic families played major roles in politics, administration, military affairs, and culture.

North and South

The distinction between north and south became one of the defining political features of the era.

Northern China experienced repeated political competition among states founded by different ruling groups, while southern dynasties developed around the lower Yangtze political center.

The Sixteen Kingdoms

Northern China entered a period of intense political fragmentation during the fourth and early fifth centuries.

Historians commonly refer to much of this era as the Sixteen Kingdoms period.

The name does not mean that exactly sixteen states existed at all times.

It is a conventional historical label for a complex period in which many regimes competed for territory and legitimacy.

Diverse Ruling Groups

Northern states were established by rulers associated with several different peoples and political traditions.

These governments should not be understood simply as foreign states replacing a culturally uniform Chinese population.

Northern society had already been shaped by centuries of migration, military recruitment, intermarriage, trade, frontier interaction, and political exchange.

The new states combined institutions and practices from multiple cultural and political traditions.

Northern and Southern Dynasties

By the fifth century, the political pattern increasingly developed into a broad division between dynasties controlling northern China and dynasties ruling the south.

The Southern Dynasties

After the Eastern Jin ended in 420, a succession of dynasties governed southern China from the Jiankang region.

These included:

  • Song;
  • Southern Qi;
  • Liang;
  • Chen.

The Northern Dynasties

Northern China experienced a different political sequence centered first upon the rise of the Northern Wei and later upon several successor states.

These included:

  • Northern Wei;
  • Eastern Wei;
  • Western Wei;
  • Northern Qi;
  • Northern Zhou.

The northern and southern regimes remained politically separate but culturally, militarily, commercially, and religiously connected.

Case Study: Northern Wei

The Northern Wei became one of the most important states of the Period of Division.

It was founded by the Tuoba, a ruling group associated with the Xianbei peoples of the northern frontier and steppe world.

Northern Wei rulers gradually conquered competing states and reunified much of northern China by 439.

Governing a Diverse Population

Northern Wei rulers governed populations with different languages, cultural traditions, social structures, and political histories.

Their government therefore combined practices associated with northern military traditions and earlier Chinese imperial administration.

Political and Cultural Change

Over time, the Northern Wei court adopted many institutions and practices associated with established Chinese imperial government.

Court reforms included changes in administration, dress, language, marriage patterns, landholding, and elite political culture.

These developments are sometimes described as sinicization.

That term can be useful, but it should not imply that cultural change moved in only one direction.

Northern military traditions, frontier institutions, and the political practices of different peoples also influenced the states that eventually reunified China.

Move to Luoyang

In the late fifth century, the Northern Wei court moved its capital to Luoyang.

The move placed the dynasty within one of the historic political centers of northern China and reflected the government's changing imperial ambitions.

Political and Military Transformation

Centuries of political competition forced governments to experiment with new methods of administration and military organization.

Military Power

States depended heavily upon cavalry, fortified cities, regional armies, frontier forces, and mobile military commands.

Northern states were especially influenced by military traditions associated with steppe and frontier societies.

Land and Population

Governments needed reliable methods for registering households, distributing or taxing land, obtaining labor, and supporting armies.

Political competition therefore encouraged administrative reform.

Institutional Legacy

Several institutions associated with later Sui and Tang government developed or matured during these centuries.

The Period of Division was therefore not an administrative interruption between two functioning empires.

It was one of the environments in which the next imperial system was constructed.

The Expansion of Buddhism

Buddhism had entered China before the fall of Han, but it expanded dramatically during the Period of Division.

Monks, travelers, translators, merchants, rulers, aristocratic families, and local communities all contributed to its growth.

Why Buddhism Expanded

The religion offered developed traditions concerning:

  • suffering;
  • ethical conduct;
  • karma;
  • rebirth;
  • salvation;
  • meditation;
  • monastic life;
  • the nature of existence.

These teachings entered a society experiencing political uncertainty, migration, warfare, and cultural change.

Translation

Buddhist texts were translated into Chinese on an increasing scale.

Translation required scholars to develop new vocabulary and methods for communicating concepts that had originated in different linguistic and philosophical traditions.

Political Patronage

Several northern and southern rulers supported Buddhist monasteries, translation projects, temples, sculpture, and religious institutions.

Buddhism could provide rulers with prestige and a religious framework that crossed older regional and lineage boundaries.

Buddhist Art

Monumental Buddhist cave complexes, sculpture, painting, and architecture became increasingly important.

Artistic traditions from China, Central Asia, India, and other regions interacted to create new forms of Buddhist visual culture.

Culture and Intellectual Life

Political division did not prevent cultural innovation.

Literature, calligraphy, painting, philosophy, religious scholarship, and historical writing developed substantially during these centuries.

Southern Elite Culture

Southern courts and aristocratic families became important centers of literary and artistic culture.

Calligraphy and painting gained increasing prestige as forms of elite personal expression.

Philosophical Discussion

Scholars continued to interpret earlier Confucian and Daoist traditions while also responding to Buddhist thought.

Intellectual life therefore developed through interaction among several philosophical and religious traditions.

Historical Memory

Scholars also wrote histories of earlier dynasties and contemporary political regimes.

These works helped preserve historical traditions that remain central to the study of the period.

Economy and Society

The economic history of the Period of Division differed significantly by region and century.

Warfare disrupted some areas while migration and political investment encouraged development elsewhere.

Southern Agricultural Expansion

Population growth and migration contributed to expanded cultivation in the Yangtze River basin and farther south.

Agricultural development strengthened southern states and increased the long-term economic importance of regions outside the traditional northern political centers.

Aristocratic Families

Powerful families could control land, labor, political connections, and access to government office.

Their influence was particularly important in several southern dynasties.

Cities and Trade

Major cities remained centers of government, craft production, religion, scholarship, and commerce.

Long-distance trade continued to connect China with Central Asia, the Korean Peninsula, Southeast Asia, and other regions.

A More Regionally Integrated China

One of the most important long-term consequences of the Period of Division was the increased integration of regions beyond the old northern imperial heartland.

Han imperial government had already extended far beyond the Central Plains, but the centuries after 220 accelerated major demographic, economic, and political shifts.

North

Northern political systems were transformed by interaction among agricultural populations, frontier communities, military settlers, and peoples with connections to the steppe.

South

Southern China gained population, cities, cultivated land, political institutions, and economic importance.

A Changed Political Geography

When China was eventually reunified, the resulting empire was not simply a restoration of the Han political world.

It governed a society that had been substantially transformed during more than three centuries of division.

How Do We Know?

Historians reconstruct the Period of Division through a broad combination of written and archaeological evidence.

Historical Records

Official histories and other written works preserve information about rulers, wars, political institutions, diplomacy, officials, rebellions, migrations, and court politics.

Archaeology

Cities, tombs, fortifications, settlements, temples, cave complexes, workshops, ceramics, weapons, coins, and other remains provide evidence about political and social life.

Religious Sites

Buddhist cave temples, sculptures, inscriptions, monasteries, and religious objects reveal the scale and geographic spread of Buddhist patronage.

Tombs

Burials provide evidence concerning regional identity, social status, migration, clothing, diet, artistic exchange, religious beliefs, and changing funerary practices.

Art and Material Culture

Ceramics, metalwork, sculpture, painting, calligraphy, textiles, and other objects reveal extensive cultural interaction among regions and peoples.

Comparing Evidence

The period is therefore reconstructed through:

historical texts + archaeology + inscriptions + religious sites + tombs + material culture

Think Like a Historian

Consider the following question:

If China lacked a single imperial government for more than three centuries, should the period be described primarily as an age of decline?

Political fragmentation certainly created major costs.

Warfare destroyed communities, displaced populations, weakened dynasties, and repeatedly disrupted government.

Yet other historical developments occurred at the same time.

During the period:

  • southern China became more economically and politically important;
  • Buddhism expanded dramatically;
  • new political institutions developed;
  • different peoples became increasingly integrated;
  • military systems changed;
  • art and literature flourished in several regions;
  • new imperial centers emerged;
  • institutions used by the Sui and Tang took shape.

A historian should therefore distinguish between:

political unity and historical development.

A society can experience political fragmentation while simultaneously undergoing substantial cultural, economic, demographic, and institutional transformation.

The Rise of Sui

During the sixth century, northern political competition gradually produced stronger and more consolidated states.

The Northern Zhou defeated the Northern Qi and controlled most of northern China.

In 581, Yang Jian replaced the Northern Zhou ruling house and established the Sui Dynasty.

He became Emperor Wen of Sui.

A Strong Northern Base

The Sui inherited institutions, military systems, officials, and political experience developed through the northern dynasties.

The new dynasty therefore did not begin from nothing.

It emerged from centuries of institutional development within the divided political world.

Reunification in 589

When the Sui Dynasty was founded in 581, southern China remained under the Chen Dynasty.

The Sui government consolidated its northern position and prepared for a major southern campaign.

In 589, Sui forces defeated the Chen Dynasty.

The major northern and southern political regions were again governed by a single imperial dynasty.

More than three centuries of sustained political division had ended.

Reunification Was Not Restoration

The Sui did not simply recreate the Han Empire exactly as it had existed before 220.

The political world of 589 had been transformed by:

  • centuries of migration;
  • southern development;
  • northern military institutions;
  • interaction among different peoples;
  • Buddhist expansion;
  • new administrative systems;
  • changing regional economies.

Sui reunification therefore created a new imperial order from the institutions and societies produced during the Period of Division.

Historical Significance

The Period of Division was one of the great transitional eras of Chinese history.

Its historical significance includes:

  • the transformation of the political order inherited from Han;
  • the rise and fall of numerous regional states;
  • large-scale migration from north to south;
  • accelerated development of the Yangtze region;
  • interaction among peoples from northern China, the frontier, and the steppe;
  • the dramatic expansion of Buddhism;
  • important developments in art, literature, calligraphy, and philosophy;
  • changes in military organization and territorial administration;
  • the development of institutions later used by the Sui and Tang;
  • the creation of a more geographically diverse imperial society.

The period therefore should not be treated merely as the time between two successful empires.

The Sui and Tang political orders were possible partly because the centuries of division had transformed the societies, economies, and institutions that they later governed.

The broader historical sequence is:

Han imperial order → political fragmentation → regional transformation → Sui reunification

Key Takeaways

  1. The Period of Division extended from the fall of Han rule in 220 to Sui reunification in 589.
  2. The Three Kingdoms of Wei, Shu Han, and Wu emerged from the political struggles surrounding the collapse of Han.
  3. Western Jin temporarily reunified most of China in 280 but collapsed during the early fourth century.
  4. Large-scale migration contributed to the economic and political development of southern China.
  5. Eastern Jin and later southern dynasties governed from the lower Yangtze region while northern China experienced a succession of competing states.
  6. Northern Wei reunified much of northern China and became one of the most important northern dynasties.
  7. Northern states combined institutions and traditions from multiple cultural and political backgrounds.
  8. Buddhism expanded dramatically and became a major component of Chinese religious, intellectual, and artistic life.
  9. The period produced substantial political, demographic, cultural, and institutional development despite repeated warfare.
  10. The Sui Dynasty conquered the southern Chen Dynasty in 589 and reunited the major northern and southern political regions.

Key Terms

Period of Division
The broad historical period from 220 to 589 during which no single dynasty continuously controlled the major regions formerly governed by the Han Empire.
Three Kingdoms
The political period following the collapse of Han in which Wei, Shu Han, and Wu competed for control.
Wei
The northern and most populous of the Three Kingdoms, established after the formal end of Han rule.
Shu Han
A southwestern state of the Three Kingdoms period whose rulers claimed political continuity with the Han Dynasty.
Wu
The southeastern state of the Three Kingdoms period centered in the lower Yangtze region.
Western Jin
The dynasty that reunited most of China in 280 before collapsing during the early fourth century.
Eastern Jin
The southern continuation of Jin rule established in 317 after the loss of the dynasty's northern political centers.
Sixteen Kingdoms
A conventional name for the politically fragmented era in northern China during much of the fourth and early fifth centuries.
Northern and Southern Dynasties
The broad period during which separate dynastic states governed northern and southern China before Sui reunification.
Northern Wei
A major northern dynasty founded by the Tuoba that reunified much of northern China by 439.
Xianbei
A broad designation associated with several northern peoples and ruling groups that played important political roles during the Period of Division.
Jiankang
The major southern capital near modern Nanjing used by Eastern Jin and several succeeding southern dynasties.
Buddhism
A religious and philosophical tradition originating in South Asia that expanded substantially throughout China during the Period of Division.
Sinicization
A term sometimes used to describe the adoption of Chinese language, institutions, customs, and political practices by ruling groups, though the actual process often involved multidirectional cultural exchange.
Sui Dynasty
The dynasty established in northern China in 581 that conquered the southern Chen Dynasty in 589 and reunited the major political regions.

Continue the Story

The Sui reunification of 589 ended more than three centuries of sustained political division.

Yet the reunified empire was not simply a restoration of the Han Dynasty.

The political and social world had changed substantially.

Southern China was more populous and economically important, Buddhism had become a major religious tradition, northern governments had developed new military and administrative institutions, and centuries of interaction had transformed relationships among different peoples and regions.

The Sui would bring these developments into a single imperial system.

Like the Qin before it, the Sui Dynasty would be short-lived but institutionally important.
