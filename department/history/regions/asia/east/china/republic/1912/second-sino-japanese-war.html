Lesson Orientation

The war between China and Japan became the most destructive crisis of the Republican era.

Japanese military expansion had already transformed Manchuria after 1931, but the fighting that began near the Marco Polo Bridge in July 1937 escalated into a full-scale war that lasted until Japan's surrender in 1945.

The war involved:

  • large conventional battles;
  • guerrilla warfare;
  • strategic bombing;
  • occupation governments;
  • collaboration and resistance;
  • mass killing and sexual violence;
  • refugee displacement;
  • famine and economic dislocation;
  • foreign aid and international diplomacy;
  • the integration of China into the wider Second World War after 1941.

Japan captured many of China's largest cities and much of the coast.

It did not compel the Nationalist government to surrender.

Chinese resistance continued from the interior and from guerrilla base areas behind Japanese lines until the collapse of the Japanese war effort in 1945.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, the learner should be able to:

  • Distinguish the Manchurian crisis beginning in 1931 from the full-scale Sino-Japanese War beginning in 1937.
  • Explain the Mukden Incident and the creation of Manchukuo.
  • Describe the Marco Polo Bridge Incident and the escalation of hostilities.
  • Explain the strategic and political significance of the Battle of Shanghai.
  • Describe the fall of Nanjing and the mass violence committed after the city's capture.
  • Explain why casualty estimates for the Nanjing Massacre vary across historical studies.
  • Describe the Nationalist government's retreat inland and the creation of a wartime capital at Chongqing.
  • Explain Nationalist and Communist forms of resistance to Japan.
  • Describe the Second United Front and explain why cooperation remained tense.
  • Explain the political and social complexity of occupation and collaboration.
  • Describe the effects of war on refugees, food supply, health, and civilian survival.
  • Explain the importance of the Henan famine within the wartime context.
  • Describe the role of the Burma Road, Flying Tigers, and American aid.
  • Explain how Pearl Harbor connected China's war more directly to the global Second World War.
  • Describe China's participation in Allied diplomacy and the China-Burma-India theater.
  • Explain the importance of Operation Ichi-Go.
  • Describe the combination of events that brought the war to an end in 1945.
  • Explain how wartime political and military changes shaped the renewed Chinese Civil War.

When Did the War Begin?

The standard international chronology dates the Second Sino-Japanese War from July 1937 to 1945.

Chinese historical narratives often place the conflict within a longer War of Resistance Against Japan beginning with the Japanese seizure of Manchuria in 1931.

Both chronologies identify real historical turning points.

The 1931 crisis marked the beginning of sustained Japanese territorial expansion in northeastern China.

The 1937 Marco Polo Bridge fighting marked the escalation into a much larger and sustained interstate war.

This lesson therefore begins in 1931 while treating 1937 as the start of full-scale war.

Manchuria Before 1931

Manchuria was strategically and economically important to both China and Japan.

Japan had acquired major railway and treaty interests in the region after the Russo-Japanese War.

The Kwantung Army guarded Japanese interests around the South Manchuria Railway.

Chinese political authority in the northeast was associated with the regional military government of Zhang Zuolin and later his son Zhang Xueliang.

Zhang Xueliang recognized the Nanjing National Government in 1928, but the northeast retained substantial military and administrative autonomy.

The Mukden Incident

On September 18, 1931, an explosion damaged a section of the South Manchuria Railway near Mukden, now Shenyang.

Officers of Japan's Kwantung Army used the incident as justification for rapid military expansion across Manchuria.

Japanese forces occupied major cities and extended control far beyond the immediate railway zone.

The Nationalist government protested internationally while attempting to avoid a larger war for which it believed China was not yet prepared.

Documentary reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — documents on the Japanese occupation of Manchuria .

Manchukuo

In 1932, Japanese authorities supported the establishment of Manchukuo in the occupied northeast.

Former Qing emperor Puyi was placed at the head of the new state, first as chief executive and later as emperor.

Manchukuo possessed formal governmental institutions, but Japanese military and political power decisively shaped its security, diplomacy, and strategic direction.

The Nationalist government did not recognize the loss of Manchuria as legitimate.

Many foreign governments also refused to recognize Manchukuo.

The League of Nations and International Response

China appealed to the League of Nations after the Manchurian crisis.

The League created the Lytton Commission to investigate the conflict.

The resulting report rejected recognition of Manchukuo as the product of a normal local independence movement and challenged the legitimacy of the Japanese territorial settlement.

The League lacked an effective mechanism to reverse the occupation.

Japan withdrew from the League after the organization adopted the report's conclusions in 1933.

Background reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — The Mukden Incident and the Manchurian Crisis .

The 1932 Shanghai Conflict

Fighting between Chinese and Japanese forces also erupted in Shanghai in early 1932.

The conflict demonstrated that the crisis was not confined to Manchuria.

Japanese naval and ground forces fought Chinese troops in and around the city, causing substantial destruction and civilian displacement.

An armistice ended the immediate fighting, but the larger conflict between the two states remained unresolved.

Shanghai would become a major battlefield again in 1937.

Growing Pressure in North China

During the mid-1930s, Japanese political and military influence expanded beyond Manchuria into parts of North China.

Agreements, demilitarized zones, local political arrangements, and military pressure weakened Nationalist authority in several northern areas.

Chinese public opinion increasingly criticized the Nationalist government for appearing to prioritize internal political consolidation and anti-Communist campaigns over resistance to Japan.

This criticism became especially important during the Xi'an crisis of 1936.

Xi'an and the Second United Front

The detention of Chiang Kai-shek during the Xi'an Incident helped shift political priorities toward a renewed anti-Japanese coalition.

Nationalists and Communists remained deeply distrustful of one another.

Nevertheless, negotiations reduced direct hostilities and created the political basis for renewed cooperation once full-scale war broke out in 1937.

This wartime alliance became known as the Second United Front.

It coordinated resistance against Japan without eliminating the separate armies, political organizations, territories, or long-term goals of the KMT and CCP.

The Marco Polo Bridge Incident

On the night of July 7–8, 1937, Chinese and Japanese forces clashed near the Marco Polo Bridge southwest of Beijing.

The immediate responsibility for the first shots has never been established with certainty.

What began as a local military confrontation escalated through reinforcement, failed negotiations, and wider political decisions.

Fighting soon spread through North China.

Documentary references: U.S. Office of the Historian — outbreak of hostilities at Marco Polo Bridge ; U.S. diplomatic report on the initial clash and competing claims .

From Local Clash to Full-Scale War

Chinese and Japanese governments initially explored ways to contain the North China crisis.

Neither side succeeded in restoring the earlier status quo.

Japanese forces expanded operations around Beijing and Tianjin.

Chinese leaders moved additional forces into threatened regions.

By August 1937, the conflict had spread to Shanghai, transforming the crisis into a national war.

The Battle of Shanghai

The Battle of Shanghai began in August 1937 and continued for roughly three months.

Chiang Kai-shek committed some of the Nationalist government's best-trained divisions to the defense of the city.

Chinese leaders hoped that prolonged resistance in an internationally visible city would demonstrate national determination and perhaps encourage stronger foreign intervention.

Japanese land, naval, and air power eventually forced a Chinese withdrawal.

The battle inflicted severe casualties on both sides and destroyed large parts of the city outside the foreign concessions.

Documentary reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — Chinese government account of the Shanghai campaign .

The Road to Nanjing

After the fall of Shanghai, Japanese forces advanced toward the Nationalist capital.

The Chinese government began moving institutions and personnel inland.

Nanjing remained defended, but the military situation deteriorated rapidly.

Japanese forces entered the city on December 13, 1937.

What followed became one of the most extensively documented episodes of mass violence in the war.

The Nanjing Massacre

After Japanese forces captured Nanjing, soldiers committed large-scale atrocities against Chinese civilians and captured or disarmed soldiers.

The violence included:

  • mass executions;
  • widespread rape and sexual assault;
  • looting;
  • arson;
  • killings in homes, streets, and execution sites.

The massacre is documented through Chinese testimony, foreign eyewitness accounts, photographs, diaries, burial records, Japanese military records, postwar trials, and later historical research.

The fact of large-scale atrocity is firmly established.

The exact death toll remains debated because scholars and institutions use different geographic boundaries, date ranges, categories of victims, and source sets.

Background reference: United States Holocaust Memorial Museum — World War II in the Pacific .

What Can Historians Say About the Death Toll?

Historical estimates for deaths associated with the Nanjing Massacre vary significantly.

Differences can result from:

  • whether only the city or a wider Nanjing region is counted;
  • whether the period is defined as several weeks or a longer span;
  • how captured soldiers and civilians are categorized;
  • incomplete records and destroyed evidence;
  • different uses of burial records, eyewitness testimony, military documents, and demographic evidence.

A responsible historical account should therefore avoid presenting one disputed number as though it were universally accepted.

The uncertainty surrounding a precise total does not undermine the extensive evidence for mass killing, sexual violence, looting, and destruction.

The Nanjing Safety Zone

Foreign residents and Chinese associates created the Nanjing Safety Zone in an effort to shelter civilians.

Hospitals, schools, and other buildings housed large numbers of refugees.

Members of the international committee documented killings, rape, looting, and repeated violations of the zone.

Their diaries, letters, photographs, and reports became important primary sources for historians and postwar investigations.

The 1938 Yellow River Flood

In June 1938, Nationalist authorities deliberately breached Yellow River dikes in an attempt to slow the Japanese advance.

The diversion created an enormous flooded region across parts of Henan, Anhui, and Jiangsu.

The military effect was limited and temporary.

The human and environmental consequences were catastrophic.

Large populations were displaced, farmland was destroyed, and the river remained diverted for years.

Historical estimates of deaths and displacement vary, making it important to distinguish the well-established scale of the disaster from uncertain precise totals.

Scholarly reference: Social Science History — the 1938 Yellow River diversion and its aftermath .

Xuzhou and the Battle for Wuhan

Japanese forces continued major offensives during 1938.

Fighting around Xuzhou and later Wuhan drew large Chinese forces into extended conventional campaigns.

Chinese forces won important tactical successes in some engagements, but Japan retained substantial superiority in air power, heavy weapons, and mobility.

Wuhan fell in October 1938.

Yet the loss of another major political and industrial center still did not produce Chinese surrender.

Why Japan Could Not Force Chinese Surrender

Japan won many of the largest conventional battles during the opening phase of the war.

It captured major coastal cities, railways, river corridors, and industrial centers.

But territorial occupation did not automatically produce political victory.

China's enormous geographic scale allowed the Nationalist government to retreat inland.

Chinese armies continued to exist.

Communist and other guerrilla forces operated behind Japanese lines.

Japanese forces therefore faced the difficult task of defending occupied cities and transport networks while trying to destroy political resistance across a vast territory.

Cambridge military history emphasizes this central paradox: repeated Japanese battlefield victories did not compel China to surrender.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge History of the Second World War — “Campaigns in China, 1937–1945” .

Government, Industry, and Universities Move Inland

One of the most important Chinese responses to invasion was the relocation of institutions away from the threatened coast.

Government offices, factories, machinery, universities, schools, archives, professionals, and skilled workers moved toward interior provinces.

This evacuation preserved important parts of China's administrative, industrial, educational, and cultural capacity.

The movement also transformed inland cities that received large numbers of refugees and institutions.

Wartime China therefore became geographically reorganized around a new interior political and economic network.

Chongqing: Wartime Capital

Chongqing became the principal wartime capital of the Nationalist government.

Located deep in the southwest, the city was difficult for Japanese ground forces to reach.

Government ministries, foreign embassies, military headquarters, schools, factories, and refugees concentrated there and in surrounding regions.

Modern scholarship emphasizes Chongqing's role not only as a place of refuge but as an international wartime capital from which China conducted diplomacy with the Allied powers.

Scholarly reference: Modern Asian Studies — “Redefining Wartime Chongqing” .

The Bombing of Chongqing

Japanese aircraft repeatedly bombed Chongqing and other inland cities.

Air raids targeted military, industrial, political, and transportation objectives but also caused extensive civilian casualties and urban destruction.

Civilians adapted through:

  • air-raid shelters;
  • blackouts;
  • evacuation;
  • fire-control organizations;
  • emergency medical services.

The survival of the wartime capital under prolonged bombing became an important symbol of continued resistance.

The Nationalist War Effort

The Nationalist government carried the main burden of China's large conventional battles against Japan.

National Revolutionary Army forces defended major cities, transportation corridors, and central and southern provinces.

The government also had to:

  • finance a long war;
  • replace military losses;
  • maintain administration in the interior;
  • manage refugees;
  • relocate industry;
  • negotiate foreign aid;
  • contain Communist expansion and regional military autonomy.

These simultaneous pressures placed extraordinary strain on Nationalist institutions.

The Second United Front in Practice

The Second United Front temporarily aligned the Nationalists and Communists against Japan.

Communist armed forces were formally incorporated into the broader Chinese resistance structure under new designations.

Cooperation remained limited.

The two parties retained:

  • separate leadership structures;
  • separate political programs;
  • different military strategies;
  • competing territorial ambitions;
  • deep memories of the violence that followed the 1927 split.

The alliance was therefore both a real anti-Japanese coalition and a temporary political arrangement between rival revolutionary movements.

The Eighth Route Army

The principal Communist force in North China was reorganized as the Eighth Route Army.

Its forces operated behind Japanese lines and developed rural base areas in provinces including Shanxi, Hebei, Shandong, and Henan.

Communist strategy emphasized:

  • guerrilla warfare;
  • political organization;
  • local militias;
  • mobilization of rural populations;
  • attacks on transportation and communication networks.

These operations helped expand Communist political influence during the war.

The New Fourth Army

Communist forces remaining in central and eastern China were reorganized into the New Fourth Army.

The army operated in areas south of the main Eighth Route Army zones and developed guerrilla networks in regions fragmented by Japanese occupation.

Like the Eighth Route Army, it combined military operations with political organization among rural populations.

Its relationship with nearby Nationalist armies was often tense.

Communist Base Areas Behind Japanese Lines

Japanese occupation rarely meant uniform control over every village and rural district.

Communist forces used this uneven geography to build base areas behind or between Japanese positions.

These areas contained:

  • party committees;
  • local governments;
  • militias;
  • tax systems;
  • schools and political education;
  • land and rent policies;
  • support networks for guerrilla forces.

The war thus became an important period of Communist organizational expansion.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — Communist base areas during the War of Resistance .

The Hundred Regiments Offensive

In 1940, the Eighth Route Army launched a major coordinated offensive against Japanese and collaborationist forces in North China.

The campaign targeted railways, communication lines, fortified positions, and other infrastructure.

Communist sources described a very large number of engagements across several months.

The offensive demonstrated the capacity of Communist forces to conduct larger coordinated operations beyond small-scale guerrilla attacks.

It also provoked strong Japanese countermeasures in North China.

Scholarly reference: Modern Asian Studies — “The Battle of the Hundred Regiments” .

Tensions Within the United Front

Nationalist and Communist cooperation deteriorated as the war continued.

Both parties accused the other of using the anti-Japanese struggle to expand its own political and territorial power.

Sources of tension included:

  • control of local governments;
  • army recruitment;
  • tax collection;
  • Communist base expansion;
  • Nationalist restrictions on Communist activity;
  • conflicting military commands.

The alliance never fully restored the cooperation that had existed during the First United Front.

The New Fourth Army Incident

In January 1941, Nationalist and Communist forces clashed in southern Anhui during what became known as the New Fourth Army Incident.

Nationalist forces destroyed much of the Communist column involved in the confrontation.

The CCP condemned the action as an attack on the anti-Japanese united front.

Nationalist leaders argued that Communist forces had failed to comply with military orders and territorial arrangements.

The incident demonstrated how close the wartime coalition came to renewed open civil war even while Japan remained the principal external enemy.

Life Under Japanese Occupation

Japanese military occupation varied greatly by region.

Major cities, ports, railway corridors, and strategically important regions were often under tighter control than remote rural districts.

Civilians living under occupation confronted:

  • military regulations;
  • economic controls;
  • food shortages;
  • political surveillance;
  • forced labor or requisitions in some regions;
  • violence and counterinsurgency;
  • competing demands from Japanese, collaborationist, Nationalist, Communist, and local forces.

Survival often required decisions that cannot be reduced easily to a simple resistance-versus-collaboration binary.

Economic Control and Wartime Extraction

Occupation authorities attempted to use Chinese agricultural, industrial, transportation, and financial resources to support the Japanese war effort.

Railways, mines, factories, ports, and commercial systems were reorganized around wartime requirements.

Currency manipulation, requisitions, shortages, and disrupted trade contributed to severe economic instability.

Economic control also became part of counterinsurgency because armies depended on grain, transport, labor, and local taxation.

Collaboration Under Occupation

Japanese occupation depended partly on Chinese officials, police, soldiers, merchants, administrators, and local intermediaries.

Historians use the term collaboration for many forms of political or administrative cooperation with occupying authorities.

Motivations varied.

They could include:

  • ideological agreement;
  • political rivalry with Chiang Kai-shek;
  • career ambition;
  • economic survival;
  • protection of local communities;
  • coercion;
  • attempts to preserve institutions under occupation.

Distinguishing among these circumstances does not erase the political consequences of collaboration, but it prevents historians from treating every form of accommodation as identical.

The Wang Jingwei Regime

Wang Jingwei, a senior Nationalist politician and former rival of Chiang Kai-shek, broke with the Chongqing government and pursued a negotiated settlement with Japan.

In 1940, a Japanese-backed Reorganized National Government of the Republic of China was established at Nanjing under Wang's leadership.

The regime claimed to represent Republican nationalism and presented itself as a government seeking peace and reconstruction.

In practice, it operated within the constraints of Japanese military occupation and possessed limited sovereignty.

Historians increasingly study the regime not only through elite diplomacy but through the everyday institutions and populations living under its authority.

Scholarly references: Comparative Studies in Society and History — the Wang Jingwei regime ; Modern Asian Studies — everyday life and memory under the occupation government .

Resistance Inside Occupied China

Occupied areas also contained extensive resistance networks.

Resistance included:

  • Nationalist intelligence and underground organizations;
  • Communist guerrillas and rural base networks;
  • student activism;
  • labor resistance;
  • assassination and sabotage;
  • clandestine newspapers and information networks;
  • local groups protecting communities or resisting requisitions.

The boundaries between resistance, survival, neutrality, and collaboration could shift under wartime pressure.

Refugees and Mass Displacement

The war displaced enormous numbers of people.

Civilians fled:

  • front lines;
  • bombed cities;
  • occupied territories;
  • flood zones;
  • famine areas;
  • counterinsurgency campaigns.

Refugees moved into cities, mountain regions, western provinces, foreign concessions, villages, monasteries, schools, and temporary camps.

Refugee movement reshaped the population of interior China and placed enormous pressure on food, housing, sanitation, schools, and local government.

Civilian Survival

Most wartime civilians experienced the conflict through daily struggles rather than battlefield strategy.

Survival could depend upon:

  • finding food;
  • maintaining family networks;
  • avoiding military recruitment or violence;
  • locating shelter;
  • moving between occupied and unoccupied zones;
  • obtaining medicine;
  • protecting children and elderly relatives;
  • navigating multiple political authorities.

Wartime social history therefore examines people whose experiences cannot be understood primarily through party membership or military service.

The Henan Famine of 1942

Henan suffered a devastating famine during 1942–1943.

The disaster developed from multiple interacting causes:

  • drought and poor harvest conditions;
  • wartime disruption of markets and transport;
  • heavy military demands on food supplies;
  • taxation and requisitioning;
  • inadequate and delayed relief.

The province was divided among Japanese, Nationalist, and Communist zones, making food distribution and administration especially difficult.

Scholarship emphasizes the interaction between environmental shock and wartime political decisions rather than attributing the famine to one cause alone.

Scholarly reference: Modern Asian Studies — “A Quantitative Description of the Henan Famine of 1942” .

Inflation and the Wartime Economy

Maintaining a large army and displaced government without access to many of China's richest coastal tax bases created severe financial pressure.

The Nationalist government increasingly relied on currency issuance, borrowing, taxation, and foreign aid.

Wartime inflation accelerated.

Rising prices reduced real incomes and damaged the living standards of soldiers, civil servants, teachers, workers, and urban families.

Economic strain contributed to corruption, black markets, declining morale, and growing political dissatisfaction by the final years of the war.

Foreign Aid Before Pearl Harbor

China sought foreign military, financial, and technical assistance throughout the war.

The Soviet Union provided important military aid and advisers during the earlier years of full-scale conflict.

The United States initially avoided direct war with Japan but gradually expanded credits and material support to China.

American policy hardened as the Japanese war expanded and Japan moved toward closer alliance with Germany and Italy and deeper military activity in Southeast Asia.

Background reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — Japan, China, the United States, and the Road to Pearl Harbor .

The Burma Road

After Japanese occupation cut many coastal supply routes, inland transportation became strategically vital.

The Burma Road connected British-controlled Burma with southwest China.

Trucks carried weapons, fuel, machinery, and other supplies toward Kunming and the Chinese interior.

Japanese strategy increasingly sought to isolate China from external supply.

The fall of Burma in 1942 temporarily closed the road, creating a major Allied logistical crisis.

The Flying Tigers

The American Volunteer Group, popularly known as the Flying Tigers, was organized under Claire Chennault with American pilots and aircraft for service in support of China.

The unit entered combat around the time the wider Pacific War began.

Its operations in China and Burma became highly visible symbols of American assistance to China.

The Flying Tigers were later absorbed into the formal U.S. military structure.

Pearl Harbor and the Globalization of the War

Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941 transformed the strategic environment.

The United States entered the Pacific War.

China formally declared war on Japan, Germany, and Italy in December 1941.

The Sino-Japanese conflict was now fully integrated into the global Allied war against the Axis powers.

American military personnel, aircraft, supplies, loans, and advisers became increasingly important to the Chinese war effort.

China as an Allied Power

China's long resistance to Japan gave it a major place within the wartime Allied coalition.

Chiang Kai-shek participated in high-level Allied diplomacy.

China's wartime status contributed to its inclusion among the principal Allied powers and later among the permanent members of the United Nations Security Council.

This diplomatic elevation coexisted with serious military, economic, and political difficulties inside China itself.

The China-Burma-India Theater

After 1941, Allied strategy linked the Chinese war to military operations in Burma and India.

American commanders and Chinese leaders disagreed frequently over priorities, command structures, training, and the allocation of limited supplies.

The theater included:

  • Chinese armies in Burma;
  • American air forces in China;
  • transport aviation from India;
  • construction of roads and airfields;
  • efforts to reopen an overland route to China.

Logistics became one of the greatest constraints on Allied military operations in China.

The Hump and Wartime Logistics

After the Burma Road was cut, Allied aircraft transported supplies from India across the eastern Himalayas into southwest China.

This dangerous air route became known as the Hump.

Aircraft carried:

  • fuel;
  • weapons;
  • ammunition;
  • medical supplies;
  • communications equipment;
  • other high-priority cargo.

Air transport could not initially replace the carrying capacity of an open land route, making the reconquest of northern Burma strategically important.

Chinese Forces in Burma

Chinese expeditionary forces fought in Burma alongside British and later American forces.

Campaigns in Burma sought both to resist Japanese expansion and to restore a land supply connection to China.

Chinese units were also retrained and reequipped in India with American support.

Operations in Burma demonstrated that China's war effort extended beyond the territorial boundaries of China itself.

The End of U.S. and British Extraterritoriality

The wartime alliance also transformed China's diplomatic relationship with Western powers.

In January 1943, the United States and Britain signed treaties relinquishing long-standing extraterritorial privileges in China.

These treaties ended major legal structures associated with the unequal-treaty era.

The change represented an important wartime gain in formal Chinese sovereignty.

Documentary reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — treaty relinquishing American extraterritorial rights in China .

The Cairo Conference

In November 1943, Chiang Kai-shek met U.S. President Franklin D. Roosevelt and British Prime Minister Winston Churchill at the Cairo Conference.

The conference discussed future military operations against Japan and the postwar settlement in Asia.

The Cairo communiqué stated Allied intentions concerning territories seized by Japan and affirmed that Korea should become independent in due course.

The meeting symbolized China's status as one of the major Allied powers.

Primary-source reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — Cairo Conference communiqué, November 26, 1943 .

Operation Ichi-Go

In 1944, Japan launched Operation Ichi-Go, the largest Japanese offensive of the war in China.

The campaign extended across a vast corridor from Henan through Hunan and Guangxi.

Japanese goals included:

  • linking railways across occupied territory;
  • destroying or capturing airfields used by American aircraft;
  • weakening Nationalist armies;
  • improving communications between northern and southern occupied zones.

The offensive inflicted severe damage on Nationalist forces and exposed major weaknesses in wartime administration and military organization.

It did not force China out of the war.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge History of the Second World War — Operation Ichi-Go and the China campaigns .

War Exhaustion and Political Strain

By the final years of the war, Nationalist-controlled China had endured prolonged military losses, refugee pressure, inflation, conscription, corruption, food shortages, and administrative breakdown in some regions.

Communist base areas had meanwhile expanded their political and military networks in important rural regions.

The shared struggle against Japan therefore changed the balance between the Nationalists and Communists.

Both movements survived the war, but they emerged from it with very different strengths, territorial networks, reputations, and strategic problems.

The War Ends in 1945

Japan's position deteriorated rapidly during 1945.

Allied advances in the Pacific brought the war closer to the Japanese home islands.

The United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August.

The Soviet Union entered the war against Japan and attacked Japanese forces in Manchuria.

Japan announced acceptance of the Allied surrender terms on August 15, 1945.

The formal surrender was signed on September 2.

Soviet Entry Into the War Against Japan

In August 1945, Soviet forces entered Manchuria and rapidly defeated the Japanese Kwantung Army.

The collapse of Japanese power in the northeast created an immediate political problem for both Chinese Nationalists and Communists:

who would occupy and govern the territories being evacuated by Japanese forces?

Manchuria would soon become one of the most important theaters of the renewed Chinese Civil War.

Japanese Surrender

Japanese military forces across China were ordered to surrender.

Nationalist authorities sought to reestablish control over major cities, railways, ports, and territories that had been occupied for years.

Communist forces attempted to expand into areas evacuated by Japanese and collaborationist armies.

The surrender therefore ended one war while immediately reopening the unresolved struggle over China's political future.

From Allied Victory to Renewed Civil War

Victory over Japan did not resolve the conflict between the Kuomintang and Chinese Communist Party.

The war had transformed both.

The Nationalist government retained international recognition, major cities, conventional armies, and formal state institutions.

The Communists had expanded rural base areas, armed forces, local governments, and political organization.

Both parties claimed to represent China's legitimate national future.

The central problem after August 1945 became:

how would occupied territory, Japanese weapons, railways, cities, and political authority be transferred in a country where the wartime allies remained domestic rivals?

How Do We Know?

The war is documented through Chinese, Japanese, American, Soviet, British, and other governmental archives; military records; newspapers; diaries; photographs; films; refugee records; burial reports; diplomatic correspondence; court records; memoirs; local histories; economic statistics; and oral testimony.

Military Records

Operational orders, unit histories, casualty reports, maps, and intelligence records help reconstruct campaigns and troop movements.

Civilian Testimony

Diaries, letters, interviews, refugee accounts, and local records reveal the experience of occupation, bombing, famine, flight, and mass violence.

Occupation Archives

Records from Japanese authorities and Chinese occupation governments reveal systems of administration, policing, economic control, propaganda, and collaboration.

Diplomatic Archives

Foreign diplomatic documents preserve contemporary observations of battles, negotiations, atrocities, international aid, and Allied strategy.

Use Numbers Carefully

Wartime casualty estimates are especially difficult.

Records were destroyed, populations moved, military and civilian categories overlap in some sources, and later political narratives have sometimes used casualty statistics for commemorative or ideological purposes.

Historians therefore compare sources, explain definitions, and distinguish minimum documented counts from broader demographic estimates.

Think Like a Historian

Consider the following question:

How should historians compare Nationalist and Communist resistance to Japan?

The two movements fought the same occupying power but often in different ways.

Nationalist forces:

  • fought most of the largest conventional battles;
  • defended and lost major cities during the opening campaigns;
  • maintained the internationally recognized wartime government;
  • operated large standing armies;
  • coordinated much of China's diplomacy and foreign military aid.

Communist forces:

  • specialized increasingly in guerrilla warfare;
  • created base areas behind Japanese lines;
  • organized militias and rural governments;
  • attacked transportation and occupation networks;
  • expanded political influence among rural populations during the war.

These forms of resistance are not directly interchangeable.

A useful comparison asks what kinds of warfare each organization conducted, where it fought, what resources it possessed, what political institutions it built, and how those wartime experiences shaped the postwar balance of power.

Historical Significance

The Second Sino-Japanese War transformed modern China.

Its historical significance includes:

  • ending the relative peace and state-building possibilities of the Nanjing Decade;
  • causing enormous military and civilian losses;
  • destroying cities, infrastructure, farms, and industrial capacity;
  • producing mass migration and refugee displacement;
  • moving major government, industrial, and educational institutions into China's interior;
  • making Chongqing an international wartime capital;
  • placing millions of Chinese under occupation governments;
  • creating complex patterns of resistance, collaboration, and survival;
  • expanding Communist rural political and military networks;
  • placing extraordinary fiscal and administrative strain on the Nationalist government;
  • connecting China directly to the global Second World War;
  • strengthening China's formal international status among the Allied powers;
  • ending important foreign extraterritorial privileges;
  • creating the military and territorial conditions for the renewed Chinese Civil War after 1945.

Key Takeaways

  1. Japanese expansion in Manchuria began in 1931, while the full-scale Second Sino-Japanese War is conventionally dated from July 1937.
  2. The Mukden Incident provided the immediate pretext for the Japanese Kwantung Army's occupation of Manchuria.
  3. Manchukuo was established under Japanese domination and was not recognized as legitimate by the Chinese National Government or many foreign states.
  4. The Marco Polo Bridge Incident escalated from a local clash into full-scale interstate war.
  5. The Battle of Shanghai demonstrated strong Chinese resistance but ended in Japanese victory and severe Chinese losses.
  6. Japanese forces committed large-scale killing, sexual violence, looting, and destruction after the capture of Nanjing; exact death estimates remain debated.
  7. The Nationalist government survived by moving political, industrial, and educational institutions inland.
  8. Chongqing became the principal wartime capital and endured prolonged Japanese bombing.
  9. Nationalists fought most of the major conventional campaigns, while Communist forces developed guerrilla warfare and rural base areas behind Japanese lines.
  10. The Second United Front never eliminated political conflict between the KMT and CCP.
  11. Japanese occupation produced multiple systems of collaboration, administration, resistance, and local survival.
  12. The 1938 Yellow River diversion and the 1942 Henan famine demonstrate how military decisions, environment, food supply, and civilian suffering became interconnected.
  13. The Burma Road and later air transport over the Hump became vital supply links to inland China.
  14. After Pearl Harbor, China's war became fully integrated into the global Allied war against Japan.
  15. China's Allied status contributed to the abolition of major U.S. and British extraterritorial privileges in 1943.
  16. Operation Ichi-Go in 1944 inflicted severe military and political damage but did not force Chinese surrender.
  17. Japan's surrender in 1945 ended the war while reopening the unresolved Nationalist-Communist struggle for control of China.

Key Terms

Second Sino-Japanese War
The full-scale war between China and Japan conventionally dated from July 1937 to Japan's surrender in 1945.
War of Resistance Against Japan
A Chinese term for the anti-Japanese struggle, often placed within a longer chronology beginning with the 1931 Manchurian crisis.
Mukden Incident
The September 1931 railway explosion used by Japan's Kwantung Army as a pretext for rapid military expansion across Manchuria.
Manchukuo
A Japanese-dominated state established in occupied Manchuria in 1932 with Puyi as its formal head.
Kwantung Army
The Japanese military force responsible for protecting and expanding Japanese interests in Manchuria and a principal actor in the 1931 occupation.
Marco Polo Bridge Incident
The July 1937 clash between Chinese and Japanese forces near Beijing that escalated into full-scale war.
Battle of Shanghai
The major August–November 1937 urban battle in which Chinese forces resisted Japanese attack before withdrawing.
Nanjing Massacre
The period of mass killing, rape, looting, and other atrocities committed by Japanese forces after the capture of Nanjing in December 1937.
Nanjing Safety Zone
An emergency refuge area organized by foreign residents and Chinese associates to shelter civilians during the Japanese capture and occupation of Nanjing.
Yellow River Flood of 1938
The catastrophic flooding caused when Nationalist authorities breached Yellow River dikes in an attempt to slow the Japanese advance.
Chongqing
The principal inland wartime capital of the Nationalist government during the war against Japan.
Second United Front
The wartime Nationalist-Communist coalition formed to resist Japan while the two parties retained separate organizations and long-term goals.
Eighth Route Army
The principal Communist-led military force operating in North China during the anti-Japanese war.
New Fourth Army
A Communist-led military force operating mainly in central and eastern China during the war.
Hundred Regiments Offensive
A large Communist-led offensive in North China during 1940 targeting Japanese and collaborationist forces and transportation networks.
Wang Jingwei
A former senior Nationalist leader who headed the Japanese-backed Reorganized National Government at Nanjing from 1940 until his death in 1944.
Reorganized National Government
The Japanese-backed collaborationist government established at Nanjing under Wang Jingwei in 1940.
Burma Road
An overland supply route linking Burma with southwest China before Japanese conquest of Burma cut the road in 1942.
Flying Tigers
The American Volunteer Group of pilots and aircraft organized under Claire Chennault to support China against Japan.
The Hump
The Allied air-supply route from India across the eastern Himalayas into southwest China.
China-Burma-India Theater
The Allied military theater connecting operations and logistics in China, Burma, and India during World War II.
Operation Ichi-Go
The major Japanese offensive of 1944 across central and southern China intended to connect occupied territories, seize airfields, and weaken Nationalist armies.
Henan Famine
The severe 1942–1943 famine produced by the interaction of drought, wartime disruption, military demands, taxation, and inadequate relief.

Continue the Story

Japan's surrender created victory, relief, and a major political vacuum.

The Nationalist government sought to return from Chongqing and reestablish administration over the major cities, transport networks, and occupied regions.

Communist armies and local governments moved to expand their control across northern China and toward Manchuria.

Large quantities of Japanese weapons and military infrastructure now had to be surrendered to someone.

American and Soviet military actions affected how rapidly the rival Chinese forces could reach strategic territories.

Negotiations continued briefly, but the political and military conflict suspended by the anti-Japanese war soon returned on a much larger scale.
