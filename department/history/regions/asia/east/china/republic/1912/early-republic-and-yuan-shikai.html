Lesson Orientation

The Republic of China was created in 1912 after the abdication of the Qing emperor ended China's imperial political system.

Yet establishing a republic on paper was not the same as creating a stable republican state.

The new political order inherited major problems from the late Qing period:

  • powerful provincial military organizations;
  • uncertain relations between central and provincial authority;
  • foreign treaty rights and international pressure;
  • fiscal weakness and dependence on loans;
  • conflict over constitutional government;
  • competing revolutionary, reformist, and conservative political programs;
  • the unresolved question of how political legitimacy would work after the monarchy.

Between 1912 and 1916, these tensions centered increasingly on Yuan Shikai, the military and political leader who helped negotiate the end of Qing rule and then became president of the new republic.

The broad historical sequence can be represented as:

1911 Revolution → provisional republic → Qing abdication → Yuan presidency → constitutional experiment → parliamentary conflict → Second Revolution → presidential centralization → attempted monarchy → provincial resistance → Yuan's death → intensified fragmentation

  • Describe the major principles of the 1912 Provisional Constitution.
  • Explain how political parties and parliamentary elections shaped the early republic.
  • Describe the political importance of Song Jiaoren and explain why his assassination became a national crisis.
  • Explain the causes and outcome of the Second Revolution of 1913.
  • Describe how Yuan reduced the power of the National Assembly and centralized authority.
  • Explain the importance of Japan's Twenty-One Demands in 1915.
  • Describe Yuan's attempted restoration of monarchy and the resistance it generated.
  • Explain why Yuan's death in 1916 did not restore a strong unified constitutional republic.
  • Evaluate competing interpretations of the early republic as both a failed political experiment and a period of genuine constitutional innovation.

What Was the Early Republic?

The early Republic of China was the first national political order created after the end of Qing dynastic rule.

For this lesson, the early republic refers primarily to the period from 1912 to 1916, when Yuan Shikai occupied the central presidency and the new state struggled to define the relationship between constitutional institutions and military-administrative power.

The period cannot be understood simply as an immediate descent from empire into warlordism. Historians have also emphasized the real political experimentation of these years: elections were held, parties organized, legislatures met, constitutions were debated, newspapers expanded political discussion, and new concepts of citizenship and sovereignty entered public life.

At the same time, these institutions operated in a country where military forces, provincial authorities, foreign powers, and competing political networks possessed substantial independent power.

From Revolution to Republic

The Wuchang Uprising of October 1911 triggered a wider revolutionary crisis in which a number of provinces declared independence from Qing authority.

Revolutionary leaders and provincial representatives established a provisional political center at Nanjing. Their immediate challenge was not merely to overthrow the Qing court but to create a government capable of claiming national authority.

The revolution therefore produced two simultaneous political problems:

  • ending the Qing monarchy;
  • constructing a government that could bring revolutionary and northern military forces into one political order.

Those problems made negotiation with Yuan Shikai unavoidable.

Sun Yat-sen and the Nanjing Provisional Government

Provincial representatives meeting at Nanjing elected Sun Yat-sen provisional president on December 29, 1911. He was inaugurated on January 1, 1912.

The Nanjing government symbolized the revolutionary claim that political authority should no longer derive from a ruling dynasty.

Yet Sun and the revolutionary coalition did not command the strongest military forces in the country. Much of the military power around Beijing remained connected to the northern forces associated with Yuan Shikai.

The survival of the republic therefore depended upon a political settlement that could persuade the Qing court to abdicate and bring Yuan into the new order.

Source note: U.S. Office of the Historian, memorandum on the formation of the provisional government .

The Abdication Settlement

On February 12, 1912, the Qing throne abdicated. The settlement ended the dynasty and formally accepted a republican political order.

The transition was negotiated rather than produced by the complete military destruction of one side by the other.

Sun Yat-sen had indicated that he would yield the presidency to Yuan Shikai if Yuan helped secure abdication and accepted the republic.

The agreement therefore linked the revolutionary movement in the south with a northern military-political establishment that had developed during the late Qing.

A Compromise With Long-Term Consequences

The compromise allowed the monarchy to end without a prolonged final war between the revolutionary coalition and Yuan's northern forces.

But it also meant that the new republic began with substantial political and military power concentrated in the hands of a leader who had not emerged from the revolutionary movement itself.

Why Yuan Shikai Mattered

Yuan Shikai had built his career within the late Qing political and military system. He possessed experience in diplomacy, administration, military reform, and central government.

Most importantly, he was closely associated with the powerful Beiyang Army, one of the most modernized military forces created during the final decades of Qing rule.

Yuan therefore occupied an unusual position:

  • the Qing court needed his military strength;
  • revolutionaries needed his cooperation to secure abdication;
  • foreign governments regarded him as an important figure in maintaining central authority;
  • provincial leaders understood that the new republic could not ignore northern military power.

Yuan's strength made him useful to the founding of the republic, but that same strength also created a continuing imbalance between constitutional institutions and presidential military power.

From Nanjing to Beijing

The Nanjing assembly elected Yuan Shikai provisional president in February 1912, and Sun's resignation took effect when Yuan assumed office.

Yuan was inaugurated in Beijing on March 10, 1912.

The decision to govern from Beijing was politically important. Revolutionary leaders had wanted the new republic to represent a break with the old imperial center, while Yuan's power base was concentrated in the north.

Beijing remained the national capital, and many institutions and officials from the late Qing state continued to operate within the new republican framework.

The republic was therefore simultaneously a revolution in political legitimacy and a state that depended upon significant institutional continuity.

Case Study: The 1912 Provisional Constitution

The Provisional Constitution of March 1912 attempted to define the new republic through constitutional law.

Its most important principle appeared near the beginning:

“The sovereignty of the Chinese Republic is vested in the people.”

The document distributed state authority among an advisory legislature, the provisional president, the cabinet, and the judiciary.

It also called for a national assembly that would create a permanent constitution and elect a regular president.

A Constitutional Constraint on Executive Power

The provisional arrangements reflected concern that the presidency should not simply replace the emperor as an unrestricted center of authority.

This became one of the central political conflicts of the early republic:

parliamentary government + cabinet responsibility + constitutional law versus presidential centralization + military-administrative authority

Source note: Translation of the Provisional Constitution of the Republic of China .

Citizenship and Popular Sovereignty

Republican political language introduced a major conceptual change.

Under the imperial system, political legitimacy had centered on the emperor, dynasty, and inherited institutions of monarchy.

The provisional constitution instead declared sovereignty to rest with the people and described citizens as equal before the new republican order without racial, class, or religious distinction.

These principles did not mean that political participation was universal. Electoral qualifications restricted the electorate, and political rights remained uneven in practice.

The significance lay in the constitutional claim that state authority was now justified through citizenship and public sovereignty rather than dynastic rule.

Political Parties and Parliament

The early republic produced one of the most active periods of competitive party politics in modern Chinese history.

Revolutionary and constitutionalist networks reorganized into political parties that attempted to influence the new National Assembly.

The most important of these was the Kuomintang (Nationalist Party), formed from revolutionary political organizations and associated with leaders including Sun Yat-sen and Song Jiaoren.

Other parties and political groupings represented former constitutionalists, provincial elites, officials, reformers, and competing ideological currents.

Different Visions of Republican Government

Party leaders did not all agree about how power should be organized.

Some favored a parliamentary system in which a cabinet dependent upon the legislature would exercise substantial executive power.

Yuan and his allies placed greater emphasis on a strong presidency and centralized administration.

The 1912–1913 Elections

National parliamentary elections were held during 1912 and early 1913.

The electorate was limited by qualifications involving factors such as property, taxation, education, and status, so the elections did not represent universal mass suffrage.

Even with these limitations, the elections represented a major experiment in competitive national politics.

The Kuomintang emerged as the largest political grouping in the new parliament. Song Jiaoren hoped that electoral strength could support a cabinet responsible to parliament rather than a government dominated by the president.

The National Assembly formally convened in Beijing in April 1913.

Source note: Journal of Asian Studies, “Political Participation and Political Elites in Early Republican China” .

Song Jiaoren and the Crisis of Parliamentary Politics

Song Jiaoren became one of the most important advocates of parliamentary party government in the early republic.

In March 1913, after the Nationalist electoral success, Song was shot at a Shanghai railway station and died shortly afterward.

The assassination immediately became a national political crisis.

Who Was Responsible?

Investigations and political accusations connected the assassination to figures associated with Yuan Shikai's administration, and many contemporaries believed Yuan was responsible.

Historians should distinguish that widespread suspicion from a simple claim that Yuan's personal order has been conclusively demonstrated. The precise chain of responsibility has remained debated.

What is not disputed is that Song's death severely damaged trust between Yuan's government and Nationalist leaders and intensified the struggle over the future of constitutional government.

The Reorganization Loan

The new republic faced severe financial problems. Central government revenue was limited, military expenses were high, and the state inherited foreign obligations from the Qing period.

Yuan's government negotiated a large international Reorganization Loan with a foreign banking consortium in 1913.

Supporters regarded outside financing as necessary to stabilize government finances and maintain administration.

Nationalist opponents argued that Yuan had bypassed parliamentary authority and used foreign-backed finance to strengthen the presidency against its domestic political rivals.

The loan therefore became part of a larger constitutional dispute:

Who possessed the legitimate power to borrow, tax, command armies, and define national policy in the new republic?

The Second Revolution

Political conflict became military conflict during the summer of 1913.

Nationalist leaders and several southern provincial forces rebelled against Yuan in what became known as the Second Revolution.

The revolt was not a unified national uprising comparable in scale to the revolutionary crisis of 1911.

Yuan's forces defeated the rebellion within a relatively short period. Sun Yat-sen and other opponents again went into exile.

Political Consequences

The defeat of the uprising shifted the balance of power sharply toward Yuan.

Armed opposition had failed, and the president now possessed greater freedom to weaken the party and parliamentary institutions that had challenged him.

Yuan Becomes Permanent President

Despite the political crisis, the constitutional process continued long enough for parliament to enact a presidential election law.

On October 6, 1913, Yuan Shikai was elected regular president of the Republic of China. He was inaugurated on October 10.

This moment illustrates the central contradiction of the early republic: constitutional institutions continued to operate even as the political balance was moving decisively toward presidential power.

Source note: U.S. Office of the Historian, chronology of constitutional developments, 1912–1914 .

The Expulsion of the Nationalists and Dissolution of Parliament

In November 1913, Yuan ordered Nationalist members expelled from the National Assembly.

Their removal deprived parliament of the quorum needed to continue its work on a permanent constitution.

In January 1914, the National Assembly was dissolved. Provincial assemblies and local representative bodies were also dissolved in the following months.

The political system established in 1912 had therefore changed fundamentally.

Republican institutions remained, but meaningful legislative constraints on the presidency had been greatly reduced.

Constitutional Revision and Centralization

Yuan did not abolish constitutional language immediately.

Instead, his government created new advisory and constitutional bodies and promulgated revised constitutional arrangements that strengthened presidential authority.

The political transformation was therefore not simply:

constitution → no constitution

It was more accurately:

parliamentary constitutionalism → executive-centered constitutional order

This distinction matters because the struggle concerned not only whether China would possess a constitution, but what kind of constitutional state it would become.

China and the First World War Context

The outbreak of the First World War in 1914 transformed the international environment surrounding China.

European powers became preoccupied with the war, while Japan expanded its position in East Asia and seized German-controlled holdings in Shandong.

China's government therefore confronted domestic political centralization at the same time that the international balance of power around the country was changing rapidly.

This context became especially important in 1915.

The Twenty-One Demands

In January 1915, the Japanese government presented Yuan's government with a series of proposals known collectively as the Twenty-One Demands.

The demands concerned Japanese interests and privileges in areas including Shandong, South Manchuria, and eastern Inner Mongolia, along with additional economic and political questions.

Chinese negotiators resisted a number of the proposals. Japan eventually issued an ultimatum, and China accepted a reduced set of demands in May 1915.

The episode became politically damaging inside China because it intensified fears that national sovereignty remained vulnerable despite the end of the Qing.

It also demonstrated that building a modern republic required confronting an international treaty and power system that the revolution of 1911 had not removed.

Source note: U.S. Office of the Historian, Chinese statement and documents on the Twenty-One Demands .

The Monarchical Movement

By 1915, political organizations and officials connected with the central government were openly debating whether China should return to monarchy.

Advocates argued that a monarchy could provide greater stability, clearer succession, and stronger central authority than the troubled republican system.

Opponents argued that restoration would betray the revolution, destroy the legitimacy of the republic, and concentrate political power around Yuan personally.

Contemporary diplomatic records also questioned whether the formal procedures used to demonstrate support for monarchy reflected free public opinion.

Historians therefore should not treat official petitions and votes as simple proof of unanimous national support.

The Attempted Empire

Late in 1915, Yuan accepted the movement to establish a new monarchy with himself as emperor.

The proposed imperial era was associated with the name Hongxian.

The project was intended to replace the republican presidency with a new hereditary monarchical order.

Yuan's move did not restore the Qing dynasty. It attempted to create a new monarchy centered on Yuan and his political system.

Rather than solving the problem of political unity, the proposal intensified opposition among important military and provincial leaders.

The National Protection War

Opposition became armed resistance beginning in Yunnan late in 1915.

Leaders including Cai E and Tang Jiyao opposed the monarchical project and organized forces defending the republic.

The conflict became known as the National Protection War.

Additional provinces and military leaders increasingly distanced themselves from Yuan.

The crisis revealed a structural weakness of Yuan's centralization: although he had weakened parliament and opposition parties, the central government still depended heavily on provincial military cooperation.

Source note: U.S. Office of the Historian, report on the Yunnan opposition movement .

The Monarchy Is Abandoned

As opposition widened, Yuan's political position deteriorated.

On March 22, 1916, Yuan announced the cancellation of the measures preparing for monarchy.

The republic formally remained in place.

Abandoning the monarchy, however, did not restore Yuan's earlier political authority. Several opponents continued to demand that he leave the presidency.

Source note: U.S. Office of the Historian, report and translated mandate cancelling the monarchy .

Yuan Shikai's Death

Yuan Shikai died in June 1916.

Vice President Li Yuanhong succeeded him as president, and political leaders attempted to restore the earlier constitutional framework.

But Yuan had not created a durable political institution capable of replacing his personal position at the center of the military and administrative system.

The Beiyang military establishment contained multiple powerful commanders and factions, while provinces retained substantial independent military strength.

From Centralization to Fragmentation

Yuan had attempted to solve China's post-revolutionary instability by concentrating authority in the presidency.

Yet the political methods used to create that concentration weakened many of the constitutional and party institutions that might have mediated disputes after his death.

At the same time, Yuan remained dependent on military commanders whose loyalty could not simply be transferred to a successor.

After 1916, national politics increasingly revolved around:

  • competing Beiyang military factions;
  • provincial military governments;
  • constitutional disputes in Beijing;
  • regional political alliances;
  • continuing Nationalist revolutionary activity in the south;
  • foreign diplomatic and financial influence.

The next period is commonly described as the Warlord Era, although that term must be used carefully because constitutional institutions, civil government, commerce, education, and urban development continued alongside military fragmentation.

How Do We Know?

The early republic is documented through an unusually rich combination of constitutional texts, official records, newspapers, political writings, diplomatic correspondence, memoirs, election materials, and international archives.

Constitutional and Government Documents

The Provisional Constitution, presidential mandates, election laws, government gazettes, parliamentary records, and administrative documents reveal how the new political system was supposed to operate.

Political Party Records

Party platforms, speeches, correspondence, campaign activity, and organizational records help reconstruct the competing programs of Nationalists, constitutionalists, and other political groupings.

Newspapers and Public Debate

Chinese and foreign-language newspapers provide evidence about elections, constitutional disputes, political accusations, public campaigns, the monarchy movement, and changing political language.

Diplomatic Records

American, British, Japanese, Russian, and other diplomatic archives preserve detailed reports on government formation, loans, recognition, foreign relations, the Twenty-One Demands, and the monarchical crisis.

Use Diplomatic Sources Carefully

Foreign diplomats were observers with their own assumptions, interests, and limited access. Their reports are valuable evidence, but they should not be treated as neutral descriptions of Chinese politics.

Historical Scholarship

Modern historians use these sources to debate whether the early republic should be understood primarily as the beginning of political fragmentation or as a serious, if unsuccessful, period of constitutional and participatory experimentation.

Think Like a Historian

Consider the following question:

Did Yuan Shikai destroy China's first republican experiment?

There is substantial evidence for emphasizing Yuan's role in weakening republican institutions.

Under his presidency:

  • Nationalist legislators were expelled from parliament;
  • the National Assembly was dissolved;
  • provincial representative bodies were dissolved;
  • executive authority was strengthened;
  • a monarchical restoration centered on Yuan was attempted.

Yet a complete explanation cannot stop with Yuan's personal ambitions.

The republic also faced deeper structural problems:

  • the revolutionary coalition lacked control over all major armed forces;
  • provincial military power had expanded during the late Qing;
  • the central state faced severe fiscal problems;
  • foreign treaty systems constrained sovereignty;
  • political parties and representative institutions were new and fragile;
  • different groups disagreed fundamentally over whether parliamentary or centralized executive government could best preserve national unity.

The strongest historical interpretation therefore examines the interaction between Yuan's decisions and the institutional conditions in which those decisions were made.

The early republic was neither destined to fail nor guaranteed to succeed. Its outcome emerged through political choices made within a highly unstable post-revolutionary environment.

This is consistent with modern scholarship that treats the era as both the beginning of intensified military fragmentation and a period of genuine experimentation with representative government.

Historical Significance

The early republic transformed the language and institutions of Chinese politics.

Its historical significance includes:

  • ending dynastic monarchy as the recognized national political system;
  • establishing the Republic of China;
  • declaring popular sovereignty as a constitutional principle;
  • creating national elections, political parties, and parliamentary institutions;
  • revealing the tension between representative government and military centralization;
  • demonstrating the continuing importance of provincial military power;
  • showing how foreign finance and international pressure affected domestic politics;
  • intensifying nationalism through the crisis of the Twenty-One Demands;
  • demonstrating the political limits of Yuan's attempt to replace republican government with a new monarchy;
  • setting the conditions for the fragmented political order that followed Yuan's death.

The period therefore connects two major transformations:

the destruction of the imperial political order and the emergence of the prolonged struggle over what kind of modern Chinese state would replace it.

Key Takeaways

  1. The Republic of China was established in 1912 after the Qing abdication ended imperial rule.
  2. Sun Yat-sen became provisional president at Nanjing but yielded the presidency as part of the settlement that brought Yuan Shikai into the republic.
  3. Yuan's military and political position made him essential to the transition but also gave the presidency power beyond the new constitutional institutions.
  4. The 1912 Provisional Constitution declared that sovereignty rested with the people and attempted to divide authority among republican institutions.
  5. Competitive party politics and national elections created a genuine but restricted experiment in representative government.
  6. Song Jiaoren's assassination deepened distrust between Nationalists and Yuan's government; Yuan's personal responsibility remains historically debated rather than conclusively established.
  7. The failed Second Revolution of 1913 allowed Yuan to weaken organized political opposition.
  8. Yuan's government expelled Nationalist legislators, dissolved parliament, and created a more executive-centered political order.
  9. Japan's Twenty-One Demands demonstrated the continuing vulnerability of Chinese sovereignty in the international system.
  10. Yuan's attempted monarchy generated provincial and military resistance and was abandoned in March 1916.
  11. Yuan's death in June 1916 removed the central figure of the early republic without producing a strong replacement political center.
  12. The years after 1916 moved into a more fragmented political environment commonly called the Warlord Era.

Sun Yat-sen

Yuan Shikai

Continue the Story

Yuan Shikai's death in 1916 ended the period in which one dominant political figure stood at the center of the new republic.

It did not end the Republic of China, and it did not eliminate constitutional politics.

Instead, the national government at Beijing continued while military and political power became increasingly divided among regional commanders and factions.

The next period therefore requires students to study both sides of the same system:

constitutional government in Beijing + regional militarism in the provinces
