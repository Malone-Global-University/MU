Lesson Orientation

Yuan Shikai's death in June 1916 removed the strongest individual political figure in the early Republic of China.

It did not end the republic.

A national government continued to operate in Beijing. Presidents, premiers, cabinets, ministries, courts, legislatures, diplomats, and civil officials remained part of the political system. Foreign governments generally continued to treat the Beijing government as the government representing China internationally.

At the same time, effective political and military authority became increasingly divided among regional commanders, provincial governments, military coalitions, southern political movements, and competing national institutions.

The period from 1916 to 1928 is therefore commonly called the Warlord Era, but the name can be misleading if it suggests that China had no government, no institutions, no economy, or no social development during these years.

A stronger historical framework is:

constitutional national government + regional military power + competing political centers + uneven provincial state-building + continuing social, intellectual, and economic change

What Was the Warlord Era?

The term Warlord Era is commonly used for the period from Yuan Shikai's death in 1916 through the Nationalist Northern Expedition and the political reorganization of 1928.

Military commanders controlled or competed for control of provinces, transportation corridors, tax revenues, and the national government at Beijing. Political alliances were often personal and unstable, and military conflict repeatedly altered the balance of power.

Yet Cambridge historian James E. Sheridan emphasized that the period must be viewed from both the provinces and the national center. Regional militarism explains one side of the era, while constitutional and military struggles surrounding the Beijing government explain another.

This two-level framework is essential because the Republic of China continued to exist as a state even when its central government could not exercise uniform authority across all provinces.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge History of China — “The Warlord Era: Politics and Militarism under the Peking Government, 1916–28” .

After Yuan Shikai's Death

Yuan Shikai had concentrated political authority around the presidency and his personal relationships with senior military officers.

When he died in June 1916, there was no equivalent figure capable of commanding the same combination of constitutional office, military influence, administrative experience, and personal loyalty.

Vice President Li Yuanhong became president, while Duan Qirui, an important Beiyang military leader, remained a central figure in government.

The political problem became increasingly clear:

the republic possessed national institutions, but those institutions depended heavily on military leaders who commanded forces outside ordinary civilian control.

The Beijing Government Continues

The death of Yuan did not cause the national government to disappear.

The Beijing government continued to maintain ministries, conduct diplomacy, issue laws and orders, negotiate loans, appoint officials, and claim authority over the Republic of China.

Andrew J. Nathan's study of the period describes the Beijing government as continuing to symbolize national sovereignty and the possibility of national unity even while military factions repeatedly influenced or controlled it.

This distinction matters. A weak central government is not the same as the absence of a central government.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge History of China — “A Constitutional Republic: The Peking Government, 1916–28” .

Li Yuanhong and Duan Qirui

President Li Yuanhong and Premier Duan Qirui represented different sources of power within the post-Yuan political order.

Li possessed the presidency and was associated with restoration of the earlier constitutional framework. Duan was a powerful military and political figure with strong connections to the Beiyang establishment.

Their disagreements became especially serious over China's response to the First World War.

Duan favored entering the war against Germany, while political opponents challenged both his policy and the methods used to secure support.

The dispute developed into a constitutional crisis involving the president, premier, parliament, and military leaders.

The 1917 Restoration Attempt

In the summer of 1917, the political crisis opened the way for Zhang Xun, a military commander known for his loyalty to the fallen Qing dynasty, to intervene in Beijing.

Zhang attempted to restore the former Qing emperor Puyi to the throne in July 1917.

The restoration was brief and lacked broad political support. Duan Qirui and opposing forces moved against Zhang, and the restored monarchy collapsed within days.

The episode demonstrated that monarchist political currents had not disappeared, but it also showed that restoring the Qing dynasty was no longer a viable basis for national political authority.

Primary-source reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — diplomatic report and translated documents on the July 1917 restoration attempt .

China Enters the First World War

After the restoration attempt failed, Duan returned to political prominence.

On August 14, 1917, the Republic of China declared that a state of war existed with Germany and Austria-Hungary.

Chinese participation in the wider Allied war effort was connected to several goals, including strengthening China's international position and seeking revision of foreign privileges and arrangements inherited from the imperial era.

The war also provided opportunities for the Beijing government to seek international loans and political support.

Primary-source reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — Chinese declaration of war, August 14, 1917 .

The Constitutional Protection Movement

Political conflict between northern leaders and defenders of the earlier constitutional order produced a competing political movement in the south.

Members of the dissolved parliament moved south, and a Military Government of the Republic of China was organized at Canton in 1917.

Sun Yat-sen became one of the principal leaders of the Constitutional Protection Movement.

The southern movement argued that the constitution and lawful republican institutions had to be defended against militarist control in Beijing.

The existence of rival political centers meant that fragmentation was not simply a division among military commanders. It also involved competing claims about which institutions legally represented the republic.

Primary-source reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — Sun Yat-sen's account of the Constitutional Protection Movement .

What Did “Warlord” Mean?

The English term warlord is commonly used to describe military leaders who commanded personally loyal armed forces, controlled territory or sought to control it, and exercised substantial authority independently of ordinary civilian institutions.

The Chinese term junfa developed strongly negative political associations and often implied selfish militarism.

Some historians therefore use the more neutral term regional militarist when analyzing the period.

The distinction is useful because the leaders grouped together as warlords differed greatly in:

  • political goals;
  • administrative ability;
  • regional support;
  • economic policy;
  • military organization;
  • relationships with civilian elites;
  • relationships with foreign governments;
  • commitment to national reunification.

The category describes a form of political-military authority, not a single ideology or identical type of leader.

Regional Militarism

Regional military power had developed before Yuan Shikai's death.

Late Qing reforms had created modernized provincial and regional military formations. The 1911 Revolution further strengthened the political role of provincial military commanders.

Yuan attempted to manage these forces through appointment, patronage, military hierarchy, finance, and personal relationships.

After 1916, those relationships no longer revolved around one dominant leader. Commanders increasingly constructed regional coalitions and competed for influence over Beijing.

Military power therefore became both regional and national:

control a province → secure revenue → maintain an army → build alliances → influence Beijing → claim national political authority

Major Northern Military Cliques

Historians commonly organize northern military politics around several major groupings usually called cliques.

These were not disciplined modern political parties.

They were shifting networks of commanders, protégés, political allies, administrators, financiers, and military units connected by regional interests, shared careers, personal loyalties, and temporary strategic agreements.

The three most important northern groupings were:

  • the Anhui Clique;
  • the Zhili Clique;
  • the Fengtian Clique.

The names came from older provincial names associated with prominent leaders, not from rigid territorial borders.

The Anhui Clique

The Anhui Clique was closely associated with Duan Qirui and political-military networks emerging from the Beiyang establishment.

Duan's influence over the Beijing government, his connections with military commanders, and his pursuit of foreign loans helped strengthen the grouping during the late 1910s.

Critics accused Duan and his allies of using national institutions and foreign finance to expand their own military position.

The clique's dominance was broken militarily in the Zhili–Anhui War of 1920.

The Zhili Clique

The Zhili Clique included important commanders such as Feng Guozhang, Cao Kun, and Wu Peifu.

The grouping became increasingly powerful after the defeat of the Anhui Clique in 1920.

Zhili leaders presented themselves at different times as defenders of constitutional government or national reunification, but they also relied on military force and factional control of Beijing politics.

The clique's power reached a high point in the early 1920s before being weakened during the Second Zhili–Fengtian War and Feng Yuxiang's 1924 coup in Beijing.

The Fengtian Clique

The Fengtian Clique was centered in Manchuria and led by Zhang Zuolin.

Zhang developed a powerful regional military and political base in the northeast, where control of railways, commercial resources, industrial development, and relations with Japan shaped regional politics.

Fengtian forces repeatedly intervened in national struggles for control of Beijing.

The clique fought the Zhili faction in 1922 and again in 1924. After the second conflict, Zhang became one of the most powerful military figures in northern China.

Why Alliances Changed So Quickly

Military coalitions during the Warlord Era were often temporary.

Leaders made and broke alliances in response to:

  • control of provinces and transportation routes;
  • access to tax revenue and loans;
  • personal rivalries;
  • appointments in the Beijing government;
  • fear of a rival becoming too powerful;
  • foreign support or diplomatic opportunity;
  • changes in the military balance;
  • regional political interests.

A commander who was an ally in one conflict could become an opponent in the next.

These shifting relationships make the period difficult to understand as a simple sequence of permanent factions.

Provincial Government and State-Building

Regional militarism did not always mean the complete disappearance of government.

Some provincial regimes attempted to build more centralized fiscal and administrative institutions, improve military organization, develop industry, establish schools, or strengthen transportation systems.

Historian Huaiyin Li describes the period as involving the rise of regional fiscal-military states in addition to warfare and fragmentation.

Regions that could bureaucratize revenue collection, organize armies, and gain access to modern economic resources could become more competitive.

This does not erase the violence or coercion of warlord rule. It does show that political fragmentation could coexist with attempts at regional state-building.

Scholarly reference: Modern Asian Studies — “Centralized Regionalism: The Rise of Regional Fiscal-Military States in China, 1916–28” .

Taxation, Finance, and Military Power

Armies required money.

Regional military governments therefore competed for access to:

  • land taxes;
  • commercial taxes;
  • salt and other established revenue systems;
  • transportation revenue;
  • customs-related income where accessible;
  • bank loans and political financing;
  • local levies and emergency assessments.

Military spending often consumed a large share of available public revenue.

Soldiers were not always paid regularly, and unpaid troops could become a major source of insecurity for civilians and local governments.

Fiscal weakness therefore reinforced political weakness:

weak revenue → unpaid armies → coercive extraction → local disruption → weaker civilian administration

Railways, Communications, and Strategic Geography

Modern transportation systems made geography increasingly important to military and political competition.

Railways could move troops, supplies, officials, mail, and commercial goods over long distances.

Control of major rail corridors therefore offered:

  • military mobility;
  • access to commercial centers;
  • transportation revenue;
  • communication with Beijing or treaty ports;
  • strategic leverage over neighboring provinces.

Telegraph systems and modern communications also made it possible for military leaders and governments to coordinate political action across large areas.

Many major conflicts of the period followed railway corridors and focused on control of transport nodes rather than simple occupation of abstract provincial territory.

Rural Society and Local Power

Most people in China still lived in rural communities.

The effect of militarized politics varied greatly from region to region.

Rural populations might experience:

  • new taxes and military levies;
  • conscription or recruitment;
  • requisitioning of food, animals, or transport;
  • banditry and local insecurity;
  • damage from military campaigns;
  • continued operation of village institutions and local elites;
  • commercial opportunities in areas connected to expanding markets.

The phrase Warlord Era should therefore not imply that every rural community experienced the same level of warfare or political disruption.

Cities, Commerce, and Economic Growth

Some Chinese cities expanded economically and culturally during the same years that national politics remained fragmented.

Shanghai, Tianjin, Guangzhou, Wuhan, and other urban centers participated in expanding networks of finance, manufacturing, trade, publishing, education, professional life, and mass communication.

The First World War reduced some foreign competition and created opportunities for Chinese-owned industries in certain sectors.

Urban development was uneven and vulnerable to military conflict, but the existence of political fragmentation did not stop all modernization or commercial growth.

This is one reason historians distinguish the weakness of national political unity from the broader social and economic history of Republican China.

Foreign Powers, Loans, and Recognition

Foreign governments remained deeply involved in China's political environment.

Treaty rights, foreign concessions, customs arrangements, railway interests, loans, banking networks, and diplomatic recognition all affected the balance between Chinese governments and military factions.

Foreign powers did not control every internal political development, and their policies were not uniform.

But weak central authority created opportunities for outside governments and financial interests to negotiate with different Chinese political and regional actors.

Diplomatic recognition of the Beijing government also gave it an international status that exceeded its effective domestic control in many periods.

The Paris Settlement and the Shandong Question

China entered the First World War hoping that participation in the Allied cause would strengthen its position in the postwar settlement.

At the Paris Peace Conference, however, the treatment of former German rights in Shandong became a major political controversy when those rights were assigned to Japan rather than immediately returned to Chinese control.

The decision produced intense anger among Chinese students, intellectuals, merchants, and political activists.

The resulting May Fourth protests belong to a broader intellectual and cultural transformation and are examined in the next lesson.

For the political history of the Warlord Era, the episode demonstrated the connection between domestic governmental weakness, foreign policy, and rising mass nationalism.

The Zhili–Anhui War

By 1920, conflict among the major northern military coalitions produced open warfare.

The Zhili and Fengtian groupings cooperated against the Anhui Clique associated with Duan Qirui.

The resulting Zhili–Anhui War was brief but politically important.

Anhui forces were defeated, and Duan's faction lost its dominant position in Beijing.

A contemporary American diplomatic report described the central government as struggling with severe administrative and financial weakness while military leaders continued to compete for supremacy.

Primary-source reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — report on Chinese political conditions in 1920 .

The First Zhili–Fengtian War

The coalition that defeated the Anhui Clique did not remain united.

Tensions developed between the Zhili faction and Zhang Zuolin's Fengtian forces.

In 1922, the two groupings fought the First Zhili–Fengtian War.

Zhili forces defeated Zhang's army, and the Fengtian faction withdrew toward its northeastern base.

Zhili leaders then exercised greater influence over the Beijing government.

The result illustrates a recurring feature of the period:

one faction's victory did not produce permanent national unification; it temporarily changed which military coalition held the strongest position at the center.

Cao Kun and the 1923 Presidency

Cao Kun, one of the principal Zhili leaders, became president of the Republic of China in 1923.

His election became politically notorious because legislators were widely accused of accepting payments in connection with the presidential vote.

The episode damaged the legitimacy of parliamentary politics and strengthened the public perception that constitutional institutions had become instruments of factional and military power.

At the same time, the event shows why historians should not describe the Beijing system as purely military dictatorship. Political leaders still sought the legal authority and symbolic legitimacy associated with constitutional offices.

Documentary reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — documents on the 1923 collapse of Li Yuanhong's government and Cao Kun's presidential election .

The Second Zhili–Fengtian War

Conflict between the Zhili and Fengtian factions resumed in 1924.

The Second Zhili–Fengtian War became another major struggle for influence over northern China and the Beijing government.

During the conflict, events inside the Zhili coalition altered the military balance.

General Feng Yuxiang, previously associated with the Zhili side, turned against the existing Beijing leadership.

Feng Yuxiang and the Beijing Coup

In October 1924, Feng Yuxiang's forces seized control of Beijing in a coup sometimes called the Beijing Coup or Capital Revolution.

President Cao Kun was removed from effective power.

The coup weakened Wu Peifu and the Zhili position while creating a new political arrangement involving Feng, Zhang Zuolin, and other leaders.

A contemporary diplomatic report described the coup as the work of several military leaders who claimed they sought national reunification and an end to destructive militarist warfare.

Such declarations should be read critically. Military leaders frequently justified intervention in national politics as a necessary step toward unity, constitutionalism, reform, or peace.

Primary-source reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — diplomatic report on the October 1924 Beijing coup .

Duan Qirui's Provisional Government

After the 1924 coup, Duan Qirui returned to the center of national politics as Provisional Chief Executive.

His new government attempted to function above competing military factions and proposed political reorganization and national consultation.

In practice, however, the provisional government remained dependent on military coalitions whose interests did not always align.

Administrative continuity survived, but the government's capacity to enforce decisions outside territory controlled by allied military forces remained limited.

Primary-source reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — proclamation organizing the 1924 provisional government .

The March Eighteenth Incident

Political instability was accompanied by growing nationalist protest and conflict between demonstrators and government authorities.

On March 18, 1926, demonstrators in Beijing protesting foreign pressure and government policy confronted the guards of Duan Qirui's government.

Government forces fired on the crowd, killing and wounding demonstrators.

The incident further damaged the legitimacy of Duan's government and became part of a wider political culture in which student activism, nationalism, anti-imperialism, and criticism of militarist government increasingly overlapped.

Documentary reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — political review describing the March 18, 1926 incident .

The Southern Nationalist Challenge

While northern military coalitions competed for Beijing, the political movement associated with Sun Yat-sen continued developing in southern China.

During the early 1920s, the Nationalist Party reorganized its institutions, built a stronger military structure, developed cooperation with the Soviet Union, and entered into a temporary alliance with the newly founded Chinese Communist Party.

Guangzhou became the center of an alternative revolutionary state-building project.

This southern movement differed from the northern military cliques because it sought to combine party organization, political ideology, mass mobilization, and military force into a new national revolutionary movement.

Detailed treatment of the Nationalist-Communist alliance appears later in the lesson on Nationalists, Communists, and the Northern Expedition.

The Northern Expedition Begins

In 1926, the Nationalist government in Guangzhou launched the Northern Expedition.

The campaign sought to defeat or incorporate major regional military powers and create a new national government under Nationalist leadership.

Nationalist armies advanced through central and eastern China, while some regional commanders changed allegiance rather than fight to destruction.

By late 1926, contemporary diplomatic reporting described the southern armies as having extended control across a wide belt of territory reaching the Yangtze region.

Documentary reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — report on Nationalist territorial gains during the Northern Expedition .

The End of the Beijing Government

By 1927, Zhang Zuolin had become the dominant military leader in Beijing and formally assumed the title of generalissimo.

Nationalist advances continued during 1927 and 1928.

In 1928, Zhang withdrew from Beijing as Nationalist forces moved north. He was killed in June when officers of Japan's Kwantung Army bombed his train near Mukden.

Nationalist forces entered Beijing, and the Nationalist government at Nanjing became the principal national government claiming authority over China.

Later in 1928, Zhang Zuolin's son Zhang Xueliang accepted the Nationalist government's national flag and recognized the Nanjing government, helping complete the conventional political transition from the Beijing government to the Nationalist state.

Why 1928 Is a Conventional Boundary

Historians commonly end the Warlord Era in 1928 because the Northern Expedition ended the old Beijing government's position as the principal national political center and established the Nationalist government at Nanjing as the new national regime.

But 1928 did not eliminate regional militarism.

Many former warlords retained:

  • regional armies;
  • provincial political influence;
  • control of local revenue;
  • negotiated relationships with the Nationalist government;
  • the ability to resist or bargain with central authority.

The 1928 boundary therefore represents a major change in the structure of national politics, not the disappearance of every military regionalist.

Rethinking “Chaos”

Older summaries often describe the Warlord Era as little more than political chaos.

There are strong reasons for emphasizing disorder:

  • repeated wars among military coalitions;
  • weak central enforcement capacity;
  • heavy military taxation;
  • soldier indiscipline in some regions;
  • rapid government turnover;
  • foreign vulnerability;
  • uneven security and administration.

But the word chaos becomes misleading when it erases everything else happening during the same period.

Between 1916 and 1928:

  • the Beijing government continued to function as a national political institution;
  • constitutional language remained politically important;
  • southern governments claimed alternative constitutional legitimacy;
  • some provincial governments built stronger fiscal and administrative institutions;
  • major cities expanded economically and culturally;
  • universities, newspapers, publishing, and intellectual movements flourished;
  • new political parties and social movements developed;
  • the Nationalist movement created a new model of party-led state-building.

A more accurate interpretation therefore treats the period as one of fragmented state power rather than the absence of political order.

How Do We Know?

The Warlord Era is documented through government records, military correspondence, newspapers, diplomatic archives, provincial documents, tax records, memoirs, business records, maps, photographs, and later historical scholarship.

National Government Records

Presidential mandates, cabinet records, parliamentary documents, ministries, diplomatic correspondence, and legal texts reveal the continuing operation of national institutions at Beijing.

Provincial and Military Sources

Orders, military telegrams, provincial budgets, tax records, and administrative correspondence help historians reconstruct regional power and state-building.

Newspapers and Public Debate

Newspapers reported military conflicts, cabinet changes, political scandals, student protests, foreign pressure, and public criticism of warlord government.

Foreign Diplomatic Records

Diplomatic archives provide detailed observations of rapidly changing governments and military coalitions.

These sources must be used carefully. Foreign diplomats wrote from the perspective of their own governments and often focused heavily on issues affecting foreign property, treaties, commerce, and security.

Modern Historical Scholarship

Scholarship has moved beyond a simple “warlord chaos” narrative by examining constitutional politics, provincial state-building, economic growth, intellectual change, and the institutional foundations of later Nationalist consolidation.

Think Like a Historian

Consider the following question:

Was China during the Warlord Era a failed state with no meaningful national government?

Evidence for severe political weakness is substantial.

The Beijing government frequently lacked the military power to enforce decisions across the country. Regional commanders collected revenue, controlled armies, and fought wars. Cabinets changed rapidly, and national institutions could be manipulated by whichever military coalition held Beijing.

Yet the opposite claim — that no meaningful state existed — is also too simple.

The national government:

  • maintained ministries and diplomatic representation;
  • issued laws and official appointments;
  • negotiated with foreign powers;
  • preserved constitutional offices and legal claims;
  • served as an important symbol of national sovereignty.

At the provincial level, some military regimes also built fiscal and administrative institutions rather than merely destroying them.

The stronger historical conclusion is therefore not that the state disappeared, but that state authority became fragmented, militarized, and contested.

This framework helps explain why the later Nationalist revolution was able to present itself not as the creation of China from nothing, but as a project to reunify and reorganize an already existing republic.

Historical Significance

The Warlord Era reshaped the political development of the Republic of China.

Its historical significance includes:

  • demonstrating the weakness of civilian control over regional military forces;
  • preserving constitutional politics even while constitutional institutions were repeatedly manipulated or overridden;
  • creating competing northern and southern claims to national legitimacy;
  • turning provincial finance and military organization into central elements of state power;
  • making railways and modern communications strategically important to political control;
  • showing how foreign loans, treaty rights, and diplomatic recognition interacted with domestic fragmentation;
  • providing the political setting for the New Culture and May Fourth movements;
  • encouraging new forms of nationalism and anti-imperialist politics;
  • creating the environment in which the Nationalist and Communist parties expanded;
  • demonstrating that regional state-building could coexist with national political fragmentation;
  • setting the stage for the Northern Expedition and the establishment of the Nanjing government.

Key Takeaways

  1. The Warlord Era is conventionally dated from Yuan Shikai's death in 1916 to the Nationalist political reorganization of 1928.
  2. The Republic of China and its national government did not disappear after 1916.
  3. The Beijing government remained an important constitutional, administrative, diplomatic, and symbolic national institution.
  4. The 1917 Qing restoration attempt was brief and failed to reestablish monarchy as a viable national political system.
  5. China declared war on Germany and Austria-Hungary in August 1917.
  6. The Constitutional Protection Movement created a competing political center in Canton and challenged the legitimacy of northern militarist rule.
  7. Warlord or regional militarist describes a type of military-political authority, not one uniform ideology or social program.
  8. The Anhui, Zhili, and Fengtian groupings were shifting military-political coalitions rather than modern disciplined parties.
  9. Regional power depended heavily on armies, taxation, transportation, finance, and access to economically productive territory.
  10. Some provincial military regimes also engaged in regional state-building and administrative centralization.
  11. Political fragmentation did not prevent all urban growth, commerce, education, publishing, or intellectual development.
  12. The Zhili–Anhui War and two Zhili–Fengtian wars repeatedly changed which military coalition dominated Beijing.
  13. Feng Yuxiang's 1924 Beijing Coup removed Cao Kun from effective power and transformed the northern political balance.
  14. The Nationalist movement in Guangzhou developed an increasingly powerful alternative model of party-led political and military organization.
  15. The Northern Expedition undermined the old Beijing political order and helped establish the Nationalist government at Nanjing.
  16. The year 1928 marks a major political transition but did not eliminate regional military power.

Continue the Story

The Warlord Era was not only a story of armies and competing governments.

During the same years, Chinese students, writers, professors, journalists, workers, merchants, and activists were debating what political and cultural transformation should mean.

Universities and publishing centers became sites of intellectual experimentation. Reformers challenged inherited literary conventions, family structures, gender expectations, political traditions, and older educational practices.

The Shandong settlement at the Paris Peace Conference then helped transform intellectual criticism into mass political protest.

The chronology therefore overlaps deliberately:

fragmented political authority + New Culture intellectual change + May Fourth nationalism
