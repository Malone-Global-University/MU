Lesson Orientation

China in 2026 is a far more urban, educated, mobile, digitally connected, and long-lived society than it was at the beginning of reform. At the same time, the population has begun to shrink, the share of older people is rising quickly, fertility remains low, housing and education costs weigh heavily on families, and younger adults face a more difficult labor market than earlier reform-era generations expected.

These changes are forcing social policy to move in new directions. The state that once tried to slow population growth now subsidizes childrearing; the retirement system is gradually raising statutory retirement ages; long-term care insurance is being built nationally; and a new 2026 policy seeks to provide more basic public services according to where people actually live rather than tying them as closely to hukou registration.

This lesson examines those changes as connected parts of one social transformation. Demography affects pensions and labor supply; housing affects marriage and fertility; migration affects education and welfare access; technology reshapes employment and public life; and aging changes health, caregiving, and family responsibilities. The goal is not to describe one 'Chinese lifestyle,' but to understand how different generations, regions, classes, migrants, families, and communities experience the current period differently.

Current-History Status

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, the learner should be able to explain China's current population decline and aging; describe fertility policy and the new childcare-subsidy system; explain the gradual retirement-age reform and expansion of long-term-care insurance; describe urbanization, migration, and hukou reform; explain current youth and graduate employment pressures; identify major changes in education, housing, health care, digital life, culture, and religion; describe inequality and social mobility; explain how internet governance shapes public life; and evaluate why contemporary Chinese society combines greater mobility and consumption with tighter demographic, economic, and institutional pressures.

The Population Snapshot

China's mainland population is now both very large and demographically mature. The 2025 one-percent population sample survey estimated a population of 1.40545 billion. It counted 214.36 million people aged 0–14, 869.87 million aged 15–59, and 321.22 million aged sixty or above. People aged sixty and above therefore made up 22.86 percent of the population, while those aged sixty-five and above made up 15.87 percent.

The same survey estimated 514.65 million family households with an average household size of only 2.52 persons. That small household size reflects decades of low fertility, urban living, smaller families, migration, and changing marriage and residential patterns.

Primary source: National Bureau of Statistics — 2025 one-percent population sample survey.

Population Decline and the End of Demographic Expansion

China has entered a period in which the mainland population is no longer growing automatically from year to year. Births remain below deaths, and the long-term effects of earlier fertility decline now shape the age structure. This does not mean the population will suddenly collapse, but it changes the assumptions that guided decades of development policy.

A shrinking working-age population can slow labor-force growth and increase the number of older people supported by each working-age cohort. It can also reduce future demand for some kinds of housing and schooling while increasing demand for health care, pensions, rehabilitation, accessibility, and eldercare.

The demographic challenge is therefore not one problem. It simultaneously affects labor, consumption, local finance, family formation, social insurance, regional settlement, and the pace at which productivity must rise.

From Birth Restrictions to Birth Support

Chinese population policy has reversed direction. Earlier reform-era governments tried to limit births; current policy seeks to make marriage, childbirth, and childrearing less financially difficult. The universal three-child policy adopted in 2021 remains part of this shift, but by 2025–2026 the emphasis had moved beyond permission toward direct support.

A nationwide childcare subsidy now provides a baseline of 3,600 yuan per child per year for eligible children under age three. By July 2026, more than twenty-five million children had already received 2026 subsidy payments. The government also began waiving childcare and education fees for the final year of kindergarten, and in August 2026 officials said roughly twenty-four million children had benefited from that measure.

These policies are historically significant because they show that low fertility is now treated as a structural social-policy problem rather than simply a matter of family choice.

Current references: National Health Commission — childcare subsidy rules; Chinese government — 2026 childcare and kindergarten support.

Why More Birth Support Does Not Automatically Raise Fertility

Low fertility cannot be explained by one policy or one cultural preference. Urban housing costs, education expenses, childcare availability, job insecurity, delayed marriage, long working hours, women's employment concerns, and the opportunity cost of raising children all shape family decisions.

Modern Chinese families invest heavily in children's schooling, tutoring, housing, health, and future opportunities. As household size has fallen, expectations surrounding each child have often risen rather than disappeared. That can make a second or third child feel financially and emotionally difficult even when legal birth restrictions have been removed.

Current policy therefore links fertility to housing, maternity insurance, parental leave, childcare, preschool, employment protection, and broader efforts to build what official documents call a birth-friendly society.

Marriage, Households, and Changing Family Life

Marriage remains a major social institution, but people generally marry later than earlier generations and a growing share of young adults postpone marriage. Housing expectations, labor-market uncertainty, education, migration, and changing ideas about personal autonomy all influence family formation.

Smaller households do not necessarily mean weaker family ties. Grandparents often provide childcare, adult children frequently remain involved in eldercare, and families continue to pool housing, savings, education spending, and medical support across generations. Yet geographic mobility means that family members may live in different cities or provinces.

Contemporary family life therefore combines strong intergenerational obligations with smaller families, greater geographic mobility, and increasing tension between caregiving expectations and individual work lives.

Population Aging

Population aging is now one of the defining social facts of contemporary China. The 2025 population survey counted more than 321 million people aged sixty or above. The sheer size of this older population creates enormous demand for pensions, primary health care, chronic-disease treatment, rehabilitation, accessible housing, community services, and long-term care.

Aging is uneven across regions. Some northeastern, rural, and interior areas have older populations because younger adults have migrated away, while major cities can attract working-age migrants even as their registered populations age. Families also differ greatly in their ability to purchase private care.

Official policy increasingly describes aging not only as a burden but as the basis of a 'silver economy' involving health services, assistive technology, tourism, finance, housing adaptation, home care, robotics, and elder-oriented consumer goods.

The Gradual Retirement-Age Reform

China began a gradual increase in statutory retirement ages on January 1, 2025. Over fifteen years, the statutory age for men is scheduled to rise from sixty to sixty-three. The previous female retirement ages of fifty and fifty-five are scheduled to rise gradually to fifty-five and fifty-eight respectively.

The reform is deliberately incremental. Men and women in the fifty-five-year category generally see the statutory age rise by one month for every four months of birth cohort; women in the fifty-year category see a faster monthly adjustment. Flexible retirement rules also permit some workers to retire earlier or later within defined limits.

The change reflects longer life expectancy, population aging, labor-supply concerns, and pension financing. It also raises difficult questions about physically demanding jobs, age discrimination, youth employment, health differences, and whether all occupations can reasonably sustain longer working lives.

Primary sources: NPC Standing Committee decision on gradual retirement-age reform; Flexible retirement implementation rules.

Long-Term Care and Eldercare

Traditional family caregiving remains important, but smaller families, migration, disability, dementia, and longer life expectancy make family care alone increasingly difficult. In March 2026, central authorities called for creation of a nationwide long-term-care insurance system over roughly three years.

The new system is designed to provide services or financial support for people with long-lasting disability who need assistance with daily living and closely related medical care. During the initial stage, policy emphasizes people with severe disability, with benefits and financing differing according to the insurance category through which people participate.

This reform is historically important because it creates a new social-insurance pillar for a problem that had often been handled by families, medical insurance, local pilots, or direct household payment.

Primary references: National Healthcare Security Administration — 2026 provincial long-term-care implementation; Central guidance on long-term-care insurance.

An Urban Society

China is now decisively urban. The 2025 population sample survey estimated 952.08 million people living in urban areas, or 67.74 percent of the mainland population, compared with 453.37 million rural residents.

This represents a profound reversal of the social geography that characterized China for most of the twentieth century. Employment, universities, hospitals, commercial services, finance, technology, and higher-income jobs are increasingly concentrated in cities and metropolitan regions.

Urbanization has also become less about moving everyone into a few megacities and more about improving city clusters, county seats, smaller cities, transit, urban renewal, public services, and the integration of migrants into the places where they actually live.

Primary source: National Bureau of Statistics — 2025 urban and rural population.

Migration and the Floating Population

The same 2025 survey estimated China's floating population at 357.88 million. This category includes people living away from their place of household registration and demonstrates that internal migration remains one of the largest forces shaping Chinese society.

Migrants work in construction, manufacturing, logistics, retail, restaurants, domestic services, delivery platforms, technology, offices, health care, and education. They are not one homogeneous working class: today's mobile population includes factory workers, professionals, university graduates, entrepreneurs, students, and families moving between cities.

Migration has helped create national labor and consumer markets, but it has also exposed the long-standing tension between physical mobility and locally tied public benefits.

Hukou and the 2026 Residence-Based Public-Service Reform

In May 2026, the State Council issued new guidelines calling for basic public services to be provided increasingly according to people's actual place of residence rather than being tied as closely to hukou registration.

The guidelines call for stronger schooling support for migrant children, wider public-rental-housing eligibility for qualified non-hukou residents, removal of hukou restrictions on participation in employee social insurance, and gradual extension of services involving health, social assistance, disability, childcare, and eldercare.

This does not abolish the hukou system, nor does it instantly equalize access in every city. But it is a significant social-policy change because it explicitly aims to narrow the difference between people who reside in a city and people who possess that city's hukou.

Primary source: State Council — basic public services based on residence, 2026.

Housing After the Property Boom

Homeownership remains central to household wealth and family expectations, but the social meaning of housing has changed as the long property boom has weakened. Falling or stagnant home values can reduce household confidence even when they make some markets more affordable to new buyers.

For younger adults, housing is closely connected to marriage, family formation, access to good schools, commuting, and long-term financial security. For older urban households, an apartment may be the family's largest asset. Migrants and lower-income residents may depend more heavily on rental housing, employer dormitories, informal rentals, or public housing.

Current housing policy therefore faces multiple goals at once: completing unfinished homes, stabilizing property markets, expanding affordable and rental housing, renovating older neighborhoods, and preventing housing from again becoming an unsustainable engine of debt-driven growth.

Employment and the Changing Labor Market

China's overall urban labor market remains large and comparatively stable, but the composition of employment is changing. Manufacturing continues to matter, while services, logistics, health care, digital platforms, advanced industry, eldercare, tourism, education, and technology employ growing shares of workers.

Automation and AI create new technical jobs while potentially reducing demand for some routine office and factory tasks. Platform employment has created flexible work in delivery, ride-hailing, online retail, and other services but has also raised questions about labor protection, scheduling, income volatility, and occupational injury coverage.

Employment policy in 2026 emphasizes skills training, graduate hiring, support for small firms, public employment services, and expansion of occupational-injury protection to newer forms of work.

Youth and Graduate Employment

Younger workers face one of the clearest social pressures of the current period. The official urban unemployment rate for people aged sixteen to twenty-four excluding students reached 17.9 percent in July 2026, according to National Bureau of Statistics data reported by Reuters. The figure rose just as another large graduating class entered the labor market.

The government has responded with repeated national campaigns connecting graduates to private firms, SOEs, public-service positions, grassroots work, internships, vocational training, entrepreneurship, and targeted recruitment. Ministries launched spring, summer, and second-half employment drives specifically for 2026 graduates and unemployed youth.

The difficulty is partly structural: China has produced a far more educated generation at the same time that property, traditional white-collar hiring, and some private industries have slowed, while advanced manufacturing and service employers may seek different skills than graduates expected to use.

Current references: Reuters — July 2026 youth unemployment; Ministry of Human Resources — 2026 youth employment services.

Income, Consumption, and Household Security

Household living standards are far higher than during the early reform era, but current consumption remains cautious. In the first half of 2026, nationwide per-capita consumption expenditure was 14,836 yuan, up 3.7 percent nominally and 2.7 percent after adjusting for prices.

Food, housing, transport and communications, education and recreation, and health care remain major components of household spending. Rural consumption expenditure was lower than urban expenditure but grew more quickly during the first half of the year.

Consumption decisions reflect more than current wages. Families also consider mortgages, future medical costs, schooling, eldercare, job stability, pension security, and the value of housing assets. These concerns help explain why social policy is increasingly connected to the government's broader goal of strengthening domestic demand.

Primary source: National Bureau of Statistics — household income and consumption, first half 2026.

Inequality and Social Mobility

China's reform era created broad gains in income and consumption but also substantial inequalities between regions, occupations, ownership sectors, property owners and renters, registered urban residents and migrants, and households with different educational resources.

Today, social mobility often depends on access to strong schools, university credentials, city residence, property, professional networks, and fast-growing industries. Large cities offer unusually dense opportunity but also some of the highest housing and education costs.

Government policy increasingly uses ideas such as common prosperity, equalization of public services, rural revitalization, regional development, and social assistance to address these gaps. The continuing challenge is that redistributing opportunity can be harder than raising average income.

Economic development changes what people expect from life. Younger generations often expect higher-quality education, professional employment, safe food, cleaner environments, better health care, leisure, consumer choice, privacy, personal autonomy, and opportunities unavailable to earlier generations.

Those rising expectations can create satisfaction when opportunities expand but frustration when housing, jobs, school competition, caregiving, or income fail to keep pace. Public concern increasingly centers not only on absolute poverty but on fairness, mobility, security, and whether hard work still produces upward movement.

This helps explain why demographic, employment, housing, and social-policy questions have become central to governance even after extreme poverty has fallen dramatically.

A Mass Education System

China's education system has become enormous. In 2025, the country had about 440,700 schools of all types, 280.4 million students in formal education, and 18.7 million full-time teachers.

Higher education is now a mass system rather than an elite one. The 2025 gross higher-education enrollment rate reached 61.3 percent. Ordinary undergraduate and vocational colleges enrolled nearly 39.54 million students, while graduate enrollment reached about 4.30 million.

This expansion has raised human capital and widened university access, but it has also intensified credential competition. A university degree no longer guarantees the kind of employment security that earlier generations sometimes associated with higher education.

Primary source: Ministry of Education — 2025 national education statistics.

Examinations, Credentials, and Family Pressure

The gaokao remains a major gateway to higher education, and families invest heavily in schooling, tutoring, extracurricular activities, housing near desirable schools, and preparation for competitive admissions.

Educational competition is connected to demographic change in a paradoxical way. Families may have fewer children but invest more intensively in each child's education. This can raise the perceived cost of childrearing and reinforce low fertility.

Policy has tried to reduce excessive homework and tutoring pressure and to strengthen vocational education, but competition for prestigious universities and attractive urban careers remains intense.

Health Care and Medical Security

China now operates a very large basic medical-insurance system. At the end of 2025, basic medical insurance covered about 1.33 billion people, roughly 95 percent of the population.

The health system has expanded rapidly, but patients still face differences in hospital quality, regional resources, reimbursement levels, and access to top specialists. Major urban hospitals attract patients from wide areas, while policy seeks to strengthen county hospitals, community clinics, pediatrics, mental health, and primary care.

In August 2026, the National Healthcare Security Administration issued a five-year plan for 2026–2030 covering basic insurance, catastrophic illness protection, medical assistance, maternity insurance, commercial health insurance, and long-term-care insurance.

Current references: National Healthcare Security Administration — 2025 medical-insurance statistics; 2026–2030 medical-security plan.

Public Health and Longer Lives

Longer life expectancy is one of the achievements driving population aging. The 2026 government work report states that average life expectancy reached 79.25 years by the end of the Fourteenth Five-Year Plan period.

Current public-health challenges are increasingly dominated by chronic disease, cancer, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, mental health, dementia, and the health effects of aging, even though infectious disease surveillance remains important after the experience of SARS and COVID-19.

Public health therefore increasingly emphasizes prevention, community care, vaccination, chronic-disease management, rehabilitation, health education, and integration between medical treatment and eldercare.

Pensions, Social Insurance, and the Welfare State

China has built the world's largest social-insurance systems by number of participants. Official statistics report basic pension participation above 95 percent and basic medical-insurance participation at roughly the same level.

The welfare system remains multi-tiered. Urban employees, rural and non-employed residents, civil servants, migrants, and people with different contribution histories can receive different benefits. The challenge is therefore not simply coverage but adequacy, portability, and equality.

Current reforms emphasize national pension coordination, personal pension accounts, unemployment and occupational-injury coverage, easier transfer of benefits across regions, and better access for migrants and flexible workers.

Women, Gender, Work, and Care

Women participate extensively in higher education, professional work, government, science, entrepreneurship, services, and manufacturing. At the same time, family formation can create unequal career costs because women continue to carry a large share of pregnancy, childcare, and eldercare responsibilities.

Low fertility has made this tension more visible. Policies that encourage childbirth can conflict with women's employment interests if employers expect mothers to take more leave or carry greater caregiving responsibilities. Effective birth support therefore depends partly on childcare, paternal involvement, anti-discrimination enforcement, and affordable public services rather than appeals to fertility alone.

Gender expectations are also changing culturally. Younger adults encounter both traditional expectations surrounding marriage and family and more individualistic ideas about careers, relationships, personal identity, and life planning.

A Highly Digital Society

China is one of the world's largest digital societies. At the end of 2025, official statistics counted 1.125 billion internet users, including 1.121 billion mobile internet users. Internet penetration reached 80.1 percent nationally and 69.5 percent in rural areas.

Mobile payments, e-commerce, short video, livestreaming, messaging, online games, food delivery, ride-hailing, telemedicine, government services, and online education are woven into ordinary life. There were also more than 1.2 billion 5G mobile subscribers and almost 2.9 billion mobile internet-of-things terminals.

Digitalization reduces distance and transaction costs but also makes access to smartphones, platforms, digital identification, and data systems increasingly important to participation in modern social and economic life.

Primary source: National Bureau of Statistics — 2025 telecommunications and internet statistics.

Internet Public Life and Regulation

Online platforms create enormous spaces for entertainment, consumer discussion, local complaints, celebrity culture, news circulation, professional networking, public debate, and social mobilization. Yet this public sphere operates under extensive content and platform regulation.

In September 2026, new rules governing multichannel internet-content distribution took effect, imposing responsibilities on platforms, content agencies, and public-account operators concerning identity, prohibited content, online violence, false traffic, misleading marketing, and information management. In July, regulators also released a draft comprehensive revision of internet-information-service rules for public comment.

Chinese authorities describe these measures as necessary for cybersecurity, lawful content, protection of users, minors, and social order. Critics of China's information system argue that regulatory powers can also restrict political expression and independent debate. These are competing interpretations that should be attributed when specific cases are examined.

Current references: Cyberspace Administration — 2026 multichannel content-distribution rules; Draft revised internet-information-service rules.

Culture, Entertainment, and Tourism

Public life includes a large commercial and public cultural sector. At the end of 2025, China had 3,253 public libraries and 3,517 cultural centers, while public libraries recorded nearly 1.47 billion visits during the year. Film, television, animation, publishing, streaming, online literature, games, music, live performance, museums, heritage sites, and tourism all form part of a vast national cultural economy.

Digital culture increasingly crosses borders. Official statistics describe online literature, online film and television, and online games as important new cultural exports. Tourism also became a major consumption sector after the pandemic-era disruption, and the State Council approved a 2026–2030 plan intended to expand and upgrade the tourism industry.

Culture is simultaneously commercial, social, and regulated. Entertainment markets respond to consumer demand, while censorship, licensing, broadcasting rules, platform policy, heritage protection, and official cultural priorities shape what can be produced and distributed.

Sources: National Bureau of Statistics — 2025 culture statistics; State Council — tourism plan for 2026–2030.

Religion and Spiritual Life

China's Constitution states that citizens enjoy freedom of religious belief and that the state protects what it defines as normal religious activities. It also states that religion may not be used to disrupt public order, impair health, interfere with the state education system, or operate under control of foreign forces.

In practice, Buddhism, Daoism, Islam, Protestant Christianity, Catholicism, folk religious practices, ancestral rituals, temples, mosques, churches, pilgrimage, meditation, and other forms of spiritual life remain part of Chinese society. Religious activity is also governed through registration, approved organizations, clergy rules, property regulation, education rules, online-content regulation, and security law.

Religious life therefore occupies a space that is both socially significant and institutionally regulated. A neutral historical treatment should distinguish constitutional guarantees, official regulatory categories, everyday religious practice, and external criticisms of restrictions in particular communities.

Primary source: Constitution, Article 36.

Social Organizations, Charity, and Volunteering

Public life also includes charities, foundations, professional associations, social-service organizations, neighborhood groups, volunteer networks, industry associations, disability organizations, environmental groups, and other forms of organized social activity.

These organizations can provide services, disaster relief, eldercare, disability support, poverty assistance, education, environmental work, and community programs. Their institutional independence varies considerably, and many cooperate closely with local government or operate within formal registration and party-building requirements.

Contemporary Chinese civil society is therefore neither absent nor fully autonomous from the state. It is better understood as a diverse field ranging from heavily state-linked organizations to more independent service and advocacy groups operating within regulatory boundaries.

Community Governance and Everyday Public Participation

Urban neighborhoods and rural villages are important arenas of public life. Residents committees, villagers committees, homeowners associations, property-management companies, social workers, neighborhood party organizations, volunteers, and local government offices all participate in community affairs.

Residents encounter public institutions through schools, health clinics, neighborhood disputes, eldercare, sanitation, parking, housing management, public safety, petitions, online government platforms, and local consultation. These interactions are often more immediate than national politics.

Community governance therefore helps explain how state administration and everyday society meet: through services and problem-solving, but also through registration, monitoring, dispute resolution, and public-order responsibilities.

Leisure, Sports, and Everyday Consumption

Rising incomes and urbanization have expanded tourism, fitness, dining, shopping, gaming, concerts, cinemas, domestic travel, outdoor recreation, and spectator sports. Official statistics reported that 38.52 percent of people aged seven and above exercised regularly in 2025 and that per-capita sports-venue space reached 3.1 square meters.

These activities reflect the broader move from a society organized primarily around work-unit and household necessities toward one in which leisure and experience-based consumption matter more.

Yet access remains unequal. Income, location, working hours, caregiving duties, health, and migration status shape who can participate in commercial leisure and who relies more heavily on low-cost public facilities.

Regional, Rural, and Urban Differences

National averages conceal enormous geographic variation. Major coastal and interior metropolitan regions can provide high-level universities, hospitals, transit, entertainment, and professional employment, while smaller cities and rural counties may face population loss, fewer specialized services, and older populations.

At the same time, rural living standards, roads, internet access, housing, health coverage, and consumer markets are far higher than in earlier decades. County-level economies and rural e-commerce connect many villages more closely to national markets than before.

The social divide is therefore no longer a simple contrast between modern cities and isolated villages. China contains a hierarchy of global cities, provincial capitals, industrial towns, county seats, suburbs, peri-urban villages, and rural communities connected by migration and digital networks.

The 2026 Social-Policy Turn

Several policies make 2026 a useful current-history snapshot. The government is implementing gradual retirement-age increases, distributing nationwide childcare subsidies, waiving final-year kindergarten fees, expanding residence-based access to public services, and building a national long-term-care-insurance framework.

The 2026 government work report also calls for higher basic pensions for urban and rural residents, wider social-insurance coverage, stronger support for disabled and vulnerable people, more eldercare capacity, maternity and childcare support, and development of the silver economy.

These measures show a social-policy system adapting to problems created by China's own transformation: smaller families, longer lives, higher urbanization, greater migration, expensive childrearing, and an economy in which household confidence matters increasingly to national growth.

Primary source: 2026 Government Work Report.

How Do We Know?

Current social history relies on population censuses and sample surveys, household-income surveys, education statistics, health-insurance records, labor-market releases, migration data, internet statistics, housing records, social-policy documents, ethnography, interviews, media, court cases, platform data, and independent reporting.

Each source has limits. Official statistics provide national scale but may use changing definitions. Administrative records show policy coverage but not always user experience. Surveys can measure attitudes but depend on question design and sampling. Online discourse is abundant but shaped by platform demographics and censorship.

The strongest method combines these different forms of evidence and dates claims carefully because current conditions change rapidly.

Think Like a Historian

Contemporary China is not accurately described as either a single prosperous urban middle-class society or a society frozen in Mao-era institutions.

It contains extremely wealthy households and low-income workers; globally connected technology professionals and elderly villagers; homeowners with substantial assets and migrants with insecure rentals; highly educated graduates and workers whose skills were shaped by older industrial systems.

Likewise, greater consumer choice and personal mobility do not automatically imply unrestricted political or informational freedom, just as regulation and political control do not eliminate social diversity, cultural creativity, private aspiration, or everyday negotiation with institutions.

Is China's current demographic problem mainly about too few births?

Low fertility is important, but it is only one part of the transformation. The same demographic structure also reflects longer life expectancy, decades of earlier fertility decline, urbanization, education, migration, housing costs, and changing family preferences.

Policies that increase births today would not quickly solve near-term aging because newborn children take many years to enter the labor force. That is why current policy simultaneously addresses childcare, employment, retirement ages, pensions, eldercare, migration, health care, and productivity.

A historical explanation should therefore treat population decline as a long-term structural transition rather than a short-term policy failure with one simple solution.

Historical Significance

China's social history in 2026 shows the mature consequences of nearly half a century of reform.

The country is urban, digitally connected, highly educated, and substantially wealthier, with broad health and pension coverage and vastly improved infrastructure. At the same time, it is aging and beginning to shrink, and many young adults face intense competition for jobs, housing, and family security.

The most important current change is that social policy is being redesigned around these new conditions. Birth subsidies, free preschool support, delayed retirement, long-term-care insurance, residence-based public services, and expanded eldercare all address problems that barely existed in their present form when reform began.

The central social question of the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan period is therefore whether institutions built during rapid-growth decades can adapt to slower growth, smaller families, an older population, higher expectations, and a society whose citizens are more mobile and digitally connected than ever before.

Key Terms

Population Aging: increase in the share of older people caused by low fertility and longer life expectancy.

Natural Population Decline: condition in which deaths exceed births over a period.

Childcare Subsidy: national cash benefit currently set at a baseline of 3,600 yuan per eligible child per year through age three.

Gradual Retirement-Age Reform: policy implemented from 2025 that incrementally raises statutory retirement ages over fifteen years.

Long-Term Care Insurance: social-insurance system being expanded nationally to help cover care for people with long-term disability.

Hukou: household-registration system linking legal registration to locality and historically shaping access to public services.

Floating Population: people living away from their place of household registration.

Residence-Based Public Services: 2026 policy direction seeking to provide more basic services according to actual place of residence rather than hukou alone.

Youth Unemployment: labor-market measure tracking unemployment among younger workers; current official series excludes students for the 16–24 age group.

Gaokao: national college entrance examination.

Mass Higher Education: stage in which university and college participation reaches a large share of the age cohort rather than a small elite.

Basic Medical Insurance: public insurance system covering most of the population through employee and resident schemes.

Silver Economy: industries and services serving older people, including care, health, finance, housing adaptation, leisure, and assistive technology.

Digital Public Sphere: online spaces in which citizens consume information, communicate, discuss events, entertain themselves, and interact with institutions.

Social Organization: broad category including associations, charities, foundations, service groups, and other organized social bodies.

Birth-Friendly Society: current policy language for reducing economic and institutional barriers to marriage, childbirth, and childrearing.

Continue the Story

Demographic and social change cannot be separated from the physical environment in which people live. Cities require enormous amounts of electricity, water, transportation, housing, and materials, while an aging and increasingly affluent population expects cleaner air, safer water, reliable energy, and protection from climate and disaster risks.

China's next major current-history problem is therefore the relationship among development, energy security, pollution control, climate policy, renewable technology, and environmental governance.
