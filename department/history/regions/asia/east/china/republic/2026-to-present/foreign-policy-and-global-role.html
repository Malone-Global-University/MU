Lesson Orientation

China's foreign policy in 2026 operates at the intersection of national development, security, trade, technology, diplomacy, military power, and global governance. The People's Republic of China is a permanent member of the United Nations Security Council, the world's second-largest economy by nominal GDP, one of the largest trading partners for dozens of countries, a major source of development finance and infrastructure investment, and a central actor in climate, technology, energy, and security debates.

Chinese leaders describe the current approach as 'major-country diplomacy with Chinese characteristics.' Official policy emphasizes sovereignty, non-interference, peaceful development, multipolarity, support for the United Nations, opposition to what Beijing calls hegemonism and unilateral coercion, deeper relations with the Global South, and economic openness under conditions of greater security competition.

At the same time, China's global role is contested. The United States, European Union, Japan, India, the Philippines, and other governments cooperate with China in some fields while criticizing or resisting Chinese trade, security, technology, territorial, or political policies in others. A useful historical account therefore distinguishes China's own diplomatic doctrine from how other states respond to Chinese power.

Current-History Status

Verified through: September 16, 2026.

This lesson incorporates the May 2026 Xi Jinping–Donald Trump summit in Beijing, Vladimir Putin's May 2026 state visit to China, the September 2026 BRICS summit in New Delhi, current EU-China trade tensions, the continuing India-China diplomatic thaw, 2026 ASEAN-China South China Sea negotiations, and China's current Middle East diplomacy.

Foreign policy can change very quickly. Planned summits, military deployments, sanctions, trade restrictions, wars, diplomatic recognition, and international agreements should be dated when future revisions are made.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, the learner should be able to explain China's current foreign-policy doctrine and decision-making structure; describe the importance of head-of-state diplomacy; explain the current U.S.-China relationship; describe China-Russia relations and China's position on the Ukraine war; explain current EU-China economic tensions; describe China's relations with Japan, Korea, ASEAN, India, and Central Asia; explain the South China Sea dispute; identify the roles of APEC, BRICS, the Shanghai Cooperation Organization, and the United Nations; explain the Global Development, Security, Civilization, and Governance initiatives; describe the Belt and Road Initiative and debates over development finance; explain China's current roles in Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East; and assess how China's global role combines interdependence, competition, diplomacy, and security.

Major-Country Diplomacy and Foreign-Policy Institutions

Chinese official foreign-policy doctrine places Communist Party leadership and President Xi Jinping's diplomatic strategy at the center of external affairs. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs describes the goal as creating an international environment favorable to China's development while defending sovereignty, security, and development interests.

Foreign policy is shaped through the top Communist Party leadership, especially the general secretary, Politburo Standing Committee, Central Foreign Affairs Commission, Central Military Commission, and other central party bodies. The State Council, Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Ministry of Commerce, defense institutions, party international departments, security organs, financial regulators, and state enterprises also carry out international policy. Wang Yi's simultaneous role as foreign minister and director of the Office of the Central Commission for Foreign Affairs links state diplomacy with party-level coordination.

In December 2025, Wang Yi described the 2026 agenda as including more stable major-country relations, deeper relations with neighboring countries and the Global South, reform of global governance, stronger Belt and Road cooperation, defense of China's national interests, and support for the opening year of the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan. This is an official strategic framework, not a neutral description of how every other government experiences Chinese foreign policy.

Primary source: Ministry of Foreign Affairs — 2026 diplomatic priorities.

Head-of-State Diplomacy

Chinese officials describe head-of-state diplomacy as the 'anchor' of the country's diplomacy. Xi Jinping's personal meetings with foreign leaders are used to manage major-power relations, establish strategic partnerships, launch initiatives, and resolve or contain disputes that working-level officials cannot easily settle.

In 2026, this approach has been visible in Xi's meetings with Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin, Narendra Modi, BRICS leaders, and other heads of government. High-level diplomacy also serves a domestic purpose by demonstrating that China's leadership can shape the international environment rather than simply react to it.

Primary source: Wang Yi on head-of-state diplomacy, March 2026.

Sovereignty, Security, and “Core Interests”

Sovereignty and territorial integrity are recurring organizing concepts in Chinese diplomacy. Taiwan, Hong Kong, Xinjiang, Tibet, maritime claims, border questions, and opposition to foreign sanctions or political pressure are often framed through sovereignty and national-security language.

Beijing distinguishes between issues it considers ordinary international disputes and matters it regards as internal affairs or core interests. Foreign governments frequently disagree with China's legal or political framing, especially regarding Taiwan, the South China Sea, human rights, sanctions, and maritime jurisdiction.

The resulting disputes are often difficult to compromise because the parties disagree not only about policy but about which legal framework should govern the issue.

Multipolarity and the International Order

Chinese diplomacy consistently argues that the international system should become more multipolar and that developing countries should have greater representation in global governance. Beijing supports the United Nations as the central multilateral institution while calling for changes to financial, development, security, and economic institutions created during earlier periods of Western dominance.

Chinese officials often criticize 'bloc confrontation,' unilateral sanctions, and what they describe as hegemonic or coercive practices. Western and some Asian governments, by contrast, argue that China sometimes seeks to rewrite rules or institutions in ways that favor its own political and strategic interests.

The historical issue is therefore not whether China participates in the existing international order—it does extensively—but how it seeks to change the distribution of influence inside that order.

The United States: Competition and Interdependence

The U.S.-China relationship remains China's most consequential bilateral relationship. The two economies remain deeply connected through trade, investment, finance, supply chains, universities, technology markets, and global institutions while also competing over semiconductors, artificial intelligence, military power, industrial policy, Taiwan, maritime security, and political influence.

Neither government describes complete economic separation as a realistic near-term objective. Yet both have expanded national-security screening, export controls, investment restrictions, sanctions, industrial subsidies, and supply-chain resilience policies.

The result is a relationship in which strategic rivalry and economic interdependence operate simultaneously rather than one replacing the other.

The May 2026 Trump-Xi Summit

U.S. President Donald Trump made a state visit to China in May 2026 and met Xi Jinping in Beijing. Chinese accounts presented the summit as an effort to stabilize relations and create a new period of engagement after years of trade and technology conflict.

Trade, agricultural purchases, aviation, technology restrictions, rare-earth access, Taiwan, and the Middle East were among the issues surrounding the summit. The visit did not remove strategic competition, but it demonstrated that both governments still saw value in direct leader-level management of the relationship.

By September 2026, further high-level economic and strategic discussions were continuing while artificial intelligence and semiconductor access remained central disputes.

Sources: Chinese Foreign Ministry — Xi-Trump talks; Reuters — summit issues.

Trade, AI, Chips, and Critical Technology

Technology competition increasingly defines the economic side of U.S.-China relations. The United States restricts exports of selected advanced semiconductors and manufacturing equipment to China and uses investment screening and sanctions against some Chinese firms. Beijing criticizes those measures as technology containment and has expanded domestic substitution, export controls, security reviews, and industrial support.

In September 2026, Reuters reported that artificial intelligence was becoming a central issue in renewed Xi-Trump discussions, with disputes involving chip access, model development, technology transfer, AI military risks, and regulation.

Technology diplomacy therefore increasingly overlaps with national security, making commercial disputes harder to separate from strategic rivalry.

Current reference: Reuters — U.S.-China AI rivalry, September 16, 2026.

China and Russia

China and Russia describe their relationship as a comprehensive strategic partnership of coordination. Political ties have deepened substantially since the 2010s, especially as both governments have criticized U.S.-led alliances, sanctions, and aspects of the existing international security system.

Vladimir Putin visited Beijing in May 2026, and the two governments agreed to extend the Treaty of Good-Neighborliness and Friendly Cooperation and signed agreements covering trade, education, science, technology, and other fields. Xi and Putin also issued statements supporting a more multipolar international system.

The partnership is close but not identical to a formal military alliance. The two countries retain different economic interests and bargaining positions, as illustrated by continuing negotiation over major energy projects.

Primary source: Xi-Putin talks, May 2026.

China and the War in Ukraine

China has not joined Western sanctions against Russia and has expanded trade with Russia during the war. Beijing states that it is not a party to the conflict, calls for negotiations, opposes unilateral sanctions, and argues that security concerns of all parties should be addressed.

Western governments have criticized China's economic support for Russia and have sanctioned some Chinese companies accused of supplying dual-use goods. Beijing rejects accusations that it is fueling the conflict and says normal trade with Russia is legitimate.

China's position on Ukraine therefore combines formal advocacy of negotiations with a strategic relationship with Moscow that has continued throughout the war. Those two facts should be analyzed together rather than treating either as the whole policy.

China and the European Union

The European Union is simultaneously one of China's largest economic partners and a major source of trade and regulatory pressure. European governments continue to seek Chinese market access and cooperation on climate, technology, and global issues while also pursuing 'de-risking' strategies in critical minerals, telecommunications, clean technology, and strategic manufacturing.

By September 2026, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen was publicly demanding a reduction in the EU's very large goods-trade deficit with China and warning about industrial dependence and deindustrialization. China argues that its export strength reflects competitiveness and that European protectionism risks damaging global trade.

EU-China relations therefore differ from a simple alliance or adversarial model: trade remains enormous even as industrial and security policy becomes more defensive.

Current reference: Reuters — EU-China trade tensions, September 16, 2026.

China and Japan

China and Japan are deeply connected economically but divided by strategic, territorial, and historical disputes. Major areas of friction include the East China Sea, the Diaoyu/Senkaku Islands, Taiwan, Japanese defense policy, U.S.-Japan military cooperation, and competing interpretations of twentieth-century history.

Relations were particularly tense in 2026 amid stronger Japanese security policies and Chinese criticism of what Beijing called renewed militarism. Japan in turn protested some Chinese and joint China-Russia military activity near Japanese-claimed maritime zones.

At the same time, trade, investment, tourism, and regional economic cooperation remain substantial, giving both governments incentives to prevent security disputes from destroying the broader relationship.

Current reference: Reuters — China-Japan security tensions, September 16, 2026.

The Korean Peninsula

China is North Korea's largest economic partner and historically its most important diplomatic relationship, but Beijing does not control Pyongyang's decisions. North Korea's growing ties with Russia after the invasion of Ukraine created new complexity for Chinese policy.

In 2025 and 2026, China and North Korea increased high-level contact, cross-border trade, transport, and political engagement after a period of relative distance. Beijing continues to oppose instability or war on the Korean Peninsula and formally supports a political settlement of nuclear and security disputes.

South Korea is also a major Chinese trading partner and technology economy, but Seoul's alliance with the United States, missile-defense questions, semiconductor controls, and North Korea policy create recurring strategic friction.

Current background: Reuters — China-North Korea relations in 2026.

China and ASEAN

Southeast Asia is one of China's most important neighboring regions. ASEAN countries trade extensively with China and participate in manufacturing supply chains, Belt and Road projects, tourism, education, digital commerce, and infrastructure cooperation.

Relations are complicated by South China Sea disputes involving several ASEAN members. China therefore pursues both regional economic integration and separate negotiations over maritime disputes.

In 2026, China and ASEAN continued formal negotiations on a South China Sea Code of Conduct, with repeated senior-official and working-group meetings aimed at narrowing differences.

Primary source: ASEAN-China Code of Conduct talks, August 2026.

The South China Sea

China claims sovereignty over island groups and maritime rights across large parts of the South China Sea. Brunei, Malaysia, the Philippines, Vietnam, and Taiwan hold overlapping claims in different areas.

The 2016 arbitral tribunal in the Philippines v. China case rejected several legal foundations for China's expansive maritime claims under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. Beijing rejected the award and continues to argue that the tribunal lacked jurisdiction and that China's rights and sovereignty are historically and legally grounded.

In July 2026, the dispute was again highlighted on the tenth anniversary of the award. China reiterated its rejection while the Philippines, United States, Japan, Australia, and several European governments supported the award's legal significance.

Current PRC position: Chinese Foreign Ministry, July 12, 2026.

China and India

China and India are both major Asian powers, nuclear-armed states, large economies, BRICS members, and neighbors with a long disputed Himalayan border. Relations deteriorated sharply after deadly border clashes in 2020.

A 2024 disengagement agreement reduced the immediate military standoff at the final major friction points, and bilateral diplomacy began to recover. In September 2026, Xi Jinping visited India for the BRICS summit and met Prime Minister Narendra Modi, with both leaders supporting stronger business and transport ties and continued border stabilization.

Trade remains heavily imbalanced in China's favor, and large military forces remain positioned along the disputed frontier. The relationship is therefore improving from a low point without having resolved its structural security problems.

Current reference: Reuters — Xi-Modi meeting, September 2026.

Central Asia and the Shanghai Cooperation Organization

Central Asia has become increasingly important to China for energy, trade corridors, border security, investment, and relations with Russia. Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan connect China geographically with western Eurasia.

The Shanghai Cooperation Organization provides a wider framework for Chinese relations with Russia, India, Pakistan, Iran, Central Asian states, and other members or partners. Chinese officials emphasize counterterrorism, security, connectivity, trade, digital development, and governance cooperation.

In July 2026, Wang Yi called for stronger SCO security institutions, economic cooperation, Belt and Road connectivity, AI and digital cooperation, and a larger role in reforming global governance.

Primary source: SCO foreign ministers meeting, July 2026.

China as APEC Host in 2026

China is hosting the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation process in 2026. The year's theme is 'Building an Asia-Pacific Community to Prosper Together,' with priorities of openness, innovation, and cooperation.

APEC allows China to present itself as a supporter of regional trade, economic integration, and open markets at the same time that geopolitical rivalry is fragmenting technology and supply chains.

The process began with senior-official meetings in Guangzhou in February and is scheduled to culminate in an Economic Leaders' Meeting later in the year. As of this lesson's cutoff, that final meeting had not yet occurred.

Primary source: Chinese Foreign Ministry — APEC China Year 2026.

BRICS and the Expanded Global South

BRICS has expanded beyond its original Brazil-Russia-India-China-South Africa membership to include additional major developing countries and partner states. China supports this expansion as part of a broader effort to increase developing-country influence in global economic and political governance.

At the September 2026 BRICS summit in New Delhi, Xi Jinping called for deeper trade, AI, finance, service-economy, and development cooperation across what Chinese officials call 'Greater BRICS.'

BRICS is not a military alliance and its members have important disagreements. Its significance lies in creating an additional institutional forum outside the traditional Western-led alliance structure.

Sources: Reuters — 2026 BRICS summit; Chinese Foreign Ministry — Xi at BRICS.

China and the “Global South”

Chinese diplomacy increasingly presents China as a member and partner of the Global South despite China's status as a major power and the world's second-largest economy. Beijing uses this identity to emphasize shared development concerns, opposition to colonialism and external interference, infrastructure needs, debt and finance reform, and representation in global institutions.

Many developing governments welcome Chinese trade, infrastructure, technology, investment, and diplomatic alternatives. Others also negotiate hard over debt, local employment, environmental standards, market access, or strategic dependence.

The term 'Global South' therefore describes a broad diplomatic constituency rather than a politically unified bloc.

China and the United Nations

China is one of five permanent members of the UN Security Council and therefore has veto power. It is also one of the largest assessed financial contributors to the UN system and to peacekeeping operations.

Chinese diplomacy emphasizes the UN Charter, sovereign equality, non-interference, and the central role of the United Nations. Beijing also supports reform of global governance to increase representation for developing countries.

China's UN voting and diplomacy can nevertheless differ sharply from Western governments on sanctions, humanitarian intervention, sovereignty, human rights, and conflicts involving Russia or other Chinese partners.

References: UN assessment records, 2026; UN Peacekeeping — assessed contributions.

The Four Global Initiatives

Xi Jinping has introduced a set of broad diplomatic concepts: the Global Development Initiative, Global Security Initiative, Global Civilization Initiative, and Global Governance Initiative. Together they present China's preferred language for development cooperation, security, cultural relations, and institutional reform.

The Global Development Initiative is linked to the UN Sustainable Development Goals. The Global Security Initiative emphasizes common, comprehensive, cooperative, and sustainable security. The Global Civilization Initiative stresses respect for different political and cultural systems. The Global Governance Initiative, launched in 2025, emphasizes sovereign equality, international law, multilateralism, a people-centered approach, and practical action.

These initiatives are official Chinese frameworks. Their international influence depends on how other governments interpret and adopt them.

Primary source: Wang Yi — Global Governance Initiative, May 2026.

The Belt and Road Initiative in Its Second Decade

The Belt and Road Initiative remains one of China's largest international economic programs. It includes ports, railways, roads, power projects, mining, industrial parks, digital infrastructure, telecommunications, finance, logistics, and smaller social or development projects.

By 2026, the BRI had evolved from its early emphasis on very large sovereign infrastructure loans. Chinese policy increasingly promotes a combination of major strategic projects and smaller 'small and beautiful' projects, green and digital infrastructure, equity investment, standards cooperation, and greater involvement by Chinese private companies.

Reuters reported that BRI deal volume reached a record level in 2025, suggesting that the initiative had not disappeared despite earlier expectations that lending stress would permanently shrink it.

Sources: Reuters — Belt and Road in 2026; Chinese Foreign Ministry — high-quality BRI.

Development Finance, Debt, and Infrastructure

Chinese banks, policy institutions, state enterprises, and private firms finance or construct infrastructure in many developing economies. These projects can improve transport, electricity, telecommunications, and industrial capacity, but they can also create debt, environmental, procurement, governance, and commercial risks.

The World Bank's research on Belt and Road transport corridors concluded that better infrastructure can increase trade and investment but that benefits depend heavily on transparency, debt sustainability, trade reform, environmental protection, and stronger institutions.

Debate over 'debt-trap diplomacy' has often been politically polarized. Evidence varies substantially by country and project, making project-level analysis more useful than assuming either that all Chinese finance is predatory or that all projects are commercially successful.

Independent reference: World Bank — Belt and Road Economics.

China and Africa

China's relations with African states include trade, infrastructure, mining, energy, telecommunications, finance, health, education, military cooperation, and diplomacy. The Forum on China-Africa Cooperation, or FOCAC, provides the principal multilateral framework.

The current Beijing Action Plan covers 2025–2027 and includes cooperation in trade, agriculture, health, digital technology, green development, security, people-to-people exchange, and infrastructure. A senior officials meeting was scheduled for 2026 ahead of the next ministerial conference in the Republic of the Congo in 2027.

Chinese firms remain major contractors and technology suppliers across the continent, while the United States and Europe increasingly treat African infrastructure and telecommunications as part of their own strategic competition with China.

Primary sources: FOCAC Beijing Action Plan 2025–2027; FOCAC follow-up, 2026.

China and Latin America and the Caribbean

China has become a major trade, investment, infrastructure, energy, mining, agriculture, and technology partner across Latin America and the Caribbean. Brazil is especially important because of its economic scale and BRICS role, but Chinese relations also extend across South America, Central America, and the Caribbean.

The China-CELAC Forum provides a regional diplomatic framework. Its 2025 Beijing meeting adopted an action plan covering 2025–2027 and cooperation in infrastructure, trade, investment, technology, health, green energy, education, and political consultation.

Relations also intersect with U.S. strategic interests, especially concerning ports, telecommunications, energy, minerals, and diplomatic recognition of Taiwan.

Primary source: China-CELAC Forum, 2025.

China and the Middle East

China's Middle East relationships are shaped by energy imports, trade, investment, Belt and Road infrastructure, technology, diplomacy, and the region's security crises. Beijing maintains partnerships with Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Iran, Egypt, Israel, and other states that are themselves geopolitical rivals.

This broad network means Chinese policy usually avoids binding alliance commitments to one regional bloc. Beijing emphasizes sovereignty, non-interference, economic development, support for Palestinian statehood under a two-state framework, and diplomatic settlement of regional disputes.

China's ability to maintain relations with rival governments gives it diplomatic access but also limits how strongly it can support one side without damaging relations with another.

Iran, the Gulf, and Energy Security

China's dependence on energy imports makes Gulf security a direct economic concern. The 2026 escalation involving Iran, the United States, and regional actors threatened shipping routes and especially the Strait of Hormuz, which is central to global oil and gas flows.

On September 16, Wang Yi met Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi in Beijing and called for restraint, renewed negotiations, and restoration of safe navigation through the Strait of Hormuz. China also sought to protect its commercial and energy interests while avoiding direct military involvement.

Reuters reporting has described continued Chinese-Iranian trade despite U.S. sanctions and allegations involving sanctions-evasion mechanisms, which Beijing and Tehran reject as illegitimate applications of unilateral U.S. law.

Current reference: Reuters — China-Iran diplomacy, September 16, 2026.

Military Diplomacy and the Xiangshan Forum

China's global role includes defense diplomacy, military exercises, naval port visits, arms sales, officer exchanges, peacekeeping, and strategic-security forums. Joint exercises with Russia and other partners demonstrate operational cooperation without necessarily creating treaty alliances.

The Beijing Xiangshan Forum has become one of China's principal international security-dialogue platforms. The September 2026 forum brought more than two thousand military officials, diplomats, scholars, and other participants from roughly one hundred countries.

Chinese Defense Minister Dong Jun used the forum to criticize provocative behavior and argue for an egalitarian security order, while regional disputes over Taiwan, Japan, and the South China Sea remained prominent.

Current reference: Reuters — Xiangshan Forum, September 16, 2026.

Overseas Citizens, Businesses, and Security

China's global economic presence has created new responsibilities for protecting citizens, companies, ships, workers, and investments abroad. Evacuations from conflict zones, maritime escort missions, consular protection, anti-piracy operations, and security agreements have therefore become more important than they were during China's earlier period of limited overseas exposure.

Beijing maintains a military support base in Djibouti and deploys naval forces for Gulf of Aden anti-piracy missions. It generally rejects the idea that it is building a traditional U.S.-style alliance and base network, but its overseas security footprint has expanded as Chinese interests have expanded.

This is a broader historical pattern: economic globalization creates security obligations even for a state whose official doctrine emphasizes non-interference.

Trade, Manufacturing, and Critical Supply Chains

China's global power derives heavily from manufacturing scale and its position in international supply chains. It is a leading producer of machinery, electronics, batteries, solar panels, electric vehicles, chemicals, steel, and many other goods.

Foreign governments increasingly view dependence on Chinese semiconductors, batteries, rare-earth processing, telecommunications equipment, pharmaceuticals, and green technologies through a national-security lens. China likewise worries about dependence on foreign advanced chips, aerospace technology, energy routes, and financial infrastructure.

The resulting policies—tariffs, export controls, industrial subsidies, stockpiles, investment screening, and supply-chain diversification—are making globalization more strategically managed rather than ending it.

Climate, Development, and Global Public Goods

China's global role is not limited to security competition. It is also central to climate policy because China is the world's largest greenhouse-gas emitter and the dominant manufacturing base for many clean-energy technologies.

Chinese solar panels, batteries, electric vehicles, and power equipment have helped reduce global technology costs. At the same time, trade disputes have intensified over subsidies, manufacturing concentration, and dependence on Chinese supply chains.

Climate diplomacy therefore captures the broader pattern of China's international position: other countries may depend on Chinese capacity while simultaneously seeking to reduce strategic dependence on China.

The Global Position in September 2026

As of September 16, 2026, China is simultaneously managing a competitive but actively negotiated relationship with the United States, a close strategic partnership with Russia, serious economic tensions with the European Union, difficult security relations with Japan and the Philippines, a cautious improvement with India, extensive economic integration with ASEAN, and expanding institutional engagement through BRICS, SCO, APEC, FOCAC, CELAC, and the United Nations.

That combination makes simple labels such as 'isolated,' 'hegemonic,' 'nonaligned,' or 'allied' inadequate. China's relationships differ greatly by region and issue.

The current pattern is best described as broad global engagement under conditions of intensified strategic competition.

How Do We Know?

Current foreign-policy history uses diplomatic communiqués, summit statements, treaties, trade statistics, military records, sanctions lists, international-organization documents, development-finance databases, company filings, speeches, parliamentary records, news reporting, satellite imagery, and academic analysis.

Government sources are essential for understanding official policy but frequently present events in ways favorable to the issuing government. Independent reporting can reveal negotiations or disagreements but may rely on anonymous sources. Trade data can show economic relationships without proving political motives.

Strong analysis therefore separates observable actions from official justifications and outside interpretations.

Think Like a Historian

Is China's global role primarily cooperative or revisionist?

That question is too broad to produce one historically useful answer. China supports many existing institutions, including the UN, WTO, IMF, World Bank, Paris climate framework, and APEC, while also promoting new or expanded forums such as BRICS, the SCO, Belt and Road institutions, and its own global initiatives.

Likewise, China can cooperate on trade, climate, development, or conflict diplomacy while simultaneously contesting maritime boundaries, export controls, sanctions, alliance structures, or human-rights policies.

A better historical method evaluates which parts of the international order China supports, which it seeks to change, and how other states respond.

Historical Significance

China's rise from a relatively isolated and economically weak state in the mid-twentieth century to a central actor in nearly every major global economic and security system is one of the largest changes in modern international history.

By 2026, China does not simply participate in institutions created by others. It hosts and shapes major summits, funds infrastructure, leads or co-leads diplomatic forums, promotes concepts for global governance, maintains expanding military and security relationships, and influences global markets through the scale of its manufacturing and consumption.

The central question for the current period is how China, the United States, other major powers, and the Global South manage this redistribution of economic and strategic power without allowing competition to destroy the international connections on which all of them continue to depend.

Key Terms

Major-Country Diplomacy with Chinese Characteristics: official Chinese framework for contemporary foreign policy under CCP leadership.

Head-of-State Diplomacy: foreign-policy practice centered on direct engagement between national leaders.

Multipolarity: distribution of global power among several major centers rather than one dominant state or bloc.

Global South: broad diplomatic term referring primarily to developing and emerging economies, though it does not describe a unified political bloc.

BRICS: grouping of major emerging economies that has expanded beyond its original five members.

Shanghai Cooperation Organization (SCO): Eurasian organization focused on security, political coordination, and economic cooperation.

APEC: Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum; China is the host economy in 2026.

Belt and Road Initiative (BRI): Chinese international connectivity and development initiative launched in 2013.

FOCAC: Forum on China-Africa Cooperation.

China-CELAC Forum: multilateral framework linking China with Latin American and Caribbean states.

Global Development Initiative (GDI): Chinese initiative promoting development cooperation and UN Sustainable Development Goals.

Global Security Initiative (GSI): Chinese security framework emphasizing common, comprehensive, cooperative, and sustainable security.

Global Civilization Initiative (GCI): Chinese framework emphasizing dialogue and respect among civilizations and political systems.

Global Governance Initiative (GGI): Chinese framework emphasizing sovereign equality, international law, multilateralism, people-centered governance, and practical action.

De-Risking: strategy of reducing strategic economic dependence without necessarily ending trade.

Strategic Partnership: flexible diplomatic label China uses for important bilateral relationships; it does not automatically mean a military alliance.
