Lesson Orientation

The Great Leap Forward was an attempt to accelerate China's transition to socialism and compress decades of industrial and agricultural development into a few years.

Beginning in 1958, Mao Zedong and other Chinese Communist leaders promoted mass mobilization, decentralized industrialization, people's communes, ambitious production targets, and the belief that political enthusiasm could overcome material and technical constraints.

The campaign initially generated extraordinary activity and optimism. It also produced distorted reporting, labor diversion, administrative coercion, inefficient industrial production, excessive grain procurement, and a severe breakdown in agricultural incentives and information.

From 1959 through the early 1960s, China experienced a catastrophic famine. The existence of mass excess mortality is well established; the precise national death toll remains debated because demographic methods, date ranges, baseline assumptions, and available records differ.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, the learner should be able to explain why the CCP launched the Great Leap Forward; distinguish people's communes from the earlier agricultural cooperatives; describe backyard steel and mass labor mobilization; explain exaggerated crop reporting and procurement pressure; describe the Lushan Conference and Peng Dehuai controversy; identify the major institutional, policy, environmental, and international pressures contributing to famine; explain why scholarly mortality estimates differ; describe regional and urban-rural variation; explain the policy retreat of 1960–1962; and connect the Great Leap crisis to later divisions within the CCP leadership.

From the First Five-Year Plan to the Great Leap

The First Five-Year Plan had produced substantial industrial growth but also left major problems: agriculture grew more slowly than planners desired, heavy industrial projects required large capital investments, Soviet-style planning appeared bureaucratic to some leaders, and consumer supply remained constrained.

In 1958, the leadership pursued a different strategy that placed far greater emphasis on political mobilization, local initiative, labor-intensive development, and simultaneous industrial and agricultural expansion.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge History of China — “The Great Leap Forward and the split in the Yenan leadership”.

The Search for a Distinctive Chinese Road

The Great Leap was partly a response to the perceived limits of simply repeating the Soviet industrialization model.

Chinese leaders hoped that a country with abundant labor but limited capital could develop through mass mobilization, local industry, water-control projects, organizational innovation, and ideological commitment.

The campaign was therefore not only a set of production targets. It represented a theory of development in which revolutionary politics could accelerate economic transformation.

The Anti-Rightist Political Context

The Great Leap followed the Hundred Flowers and Anti-Rightist campaigns of 1957.

The punishment of critics during the Anti-Rightist Campaign created a political environment in which officials, specialists, and local cadres had strong incentives to avoid appearing pessimistic or politically conservative.

This climate became important when unrealistic production claims began circulating during the Leap.

Launching the Great Leap Forward

During the spring and summer of 1958, Great Leap policies expanded rapidly across industry, agriculture, local administration, education, and labor organization.

Targets were repeatedly raised as local success stories appeared to demonstrate that extraordinary increases in production were possible.

Competition among provinces, counties, communes, and work units encouraged officials to announce increasingly ambitious results.

The General Line

Great Leap rhetoric emphasized building socialism through the coordinated expansion of industry and agriculture.

Campaign language stressed speed, quantity, self-reliance, and the mobilization of human labor on a scale far beyond the conventional investment program of the First Five-Year Plan.

Political commitment was increasingly treated as an economic resource.

“Walking on Two Legs”

One Great Leap principle was often summarized as “walking on two legs”: combining modern large-scale industry with smaller local production, and central investment with decentralized initiative.

In theory, local industry could produce tools, fertilizer, construction materials, and simple machinery without waiting for expensive national projects.

Some decentralized production was useful. Other campaigns—especially indiscriminate small-scale iron and steel production—generated enormous waste.

The Creation of People's Communes

In 1958, existing agricultural cooperatives were rapidly merged into much larger people's communes.

A commune could contain thousands of households and combine economic, administrative, social, and militia functions.

Communes were much larger and more ambitious institutions than the higher-stage cooperatives formed during the First Five-Year Plan.

How a People's Commune Was Organized

Communes were generally divided into smaller production brigades and production teams.

Authority over land, labor, production, and distribution became increasingly collective and administrative.

The exact organization changed during the crisis, and later reforms shifted important accounting and decision-making functions downward from the commune level.

Communal Dining Halls

Many communes established collective dining halls that provided meals outside individual households.

Early abundance in some places encouraged the belief that collective provisioning had overcome scarcity.

When production estimates proved false and food supplies tightened, dining halls could accelerate consumption of available stocks and weaken households' ability to manage food independently.

Experiences varied widely across regions and over time.

Nurseries, Household Labor, and Collective Life

Communes also experimented with collective nurseries, laundries, mess halls, and other services intended to release household labor for production.

These initiatives aimed to reorganize daily life as well as farming.

Some services reduced domestic labor burdens, especially for women; others were temporary, poorly supplied, or disruptive to family routines.

Private Plots and Household Autonomy

Private plots, household sidelines, and local markets were sharply reduced or eliminated in many areas during the radical phase of the Leap.

This removed supplementary food and income sources that had previously helped rural households manage risk.

Later recovery policies restored some household plots and rural market activity.

Mass Labor Mobilization

Great Leap campaigns moved enormous numbers of people into irrigation, reservoirs, roads, earthworks, local industry, and construction.

Mass labor could complete useful infrastructure, but it also diverted workers from farming and imposed severe physical demands.

Projects undertaken too quickly or without adequate engineering could fail or provide less benefit than claimed.

Water Conservancy Projects

Large-scale irrigation and water-control projects were central to Great Leap agricultural strategy.

Officials hoped that reservoirs, canals, terracing, and irrigation systems would sharply increase yields.

Some projects became durable infrastructure. Others were poorly designed, unfinished, or built at great human and economic cost.

Backyard Steel Furnaces

In 1958, the leadership called for a dramatic increase in steel output.

Small furnaces were established in communes, neighborhoods, schools, factories, and other work units.

People collected scrap metal and fuel and were mobilized into iron and steel campaigns, frequently without metallurgical expertise.

Much of the resulting material was low quality or unusable, and the effort diverted labor, fuel, tools, and attention from agriculture and productive industry.

Production Targets and the Politics of Quantity

The steel campaign illustrates a larger Great Leap problem: political success became associated with meeting rapidly rising numerical targets.

Officials who warned that targets were unrealistic risked appearing conservative or insufficiently revolutionary.

That incentive structure encouraged quantity claims that were not always connected to usable output.

Radical Agricultural Experiments

Great Leap agriculture promoted techniques such as extremely close planting and deep plowing, sometimes justified by poorly tested theories and exaggerated demonstrations.

Local officials could feel pressure to adopt methods endorsed politically even when farmers or agricultural specialists doubted their value.

The consequences varied, but some practices wasted labor or damaged crops.

“Sputnik” Yields and Exaggerated Harvest Claims

During 1958, newspapers and local reports announced spectacular grain yields from selected fields.

Some demonstrations involved unusually concentrated inputs or manipulated presentation and were not representative of ordinary production.

Exaggerated local reports traveled upward through the administrative system and helped convince higher officials that much larger harvests existed than were actually available.

The Breakdown of Reliable Information

The planning system depended on accurate reports from villages, communes, counties, and provinces.

During the Great Leap, officials faced incentives to report success and hide shortages.

This created a dangerous information problem: central and provincial authorities could set procurement or investment policy using production figures that were substantially overstated.

Fear of political criticism made correction more difficult.

Grain Procurement

The state required grain to feed cities, industrial workers, the military, and other priority groups.

When reported harvests were exaggerated, procurement demands could be based on grain that did not actually exist.

Local officials then faced pressure to meet state deliveries from real village stocks, leaving rural households with sharply reduced food supplies.

Modern economic research identifies procurement institutions and distorted production information as central mechanisms in the famine.

Institutional Causes of the Famine

Scholars have examined how collective organization, procurement rules, restrictions on mobility, weak feedback, and political incentives interacted to turn production problems into mass starvation.

One influential economic literature argues that institutional mechanisms determining grain procurement and redistribution help explain both the scale and regional variation of famine mortality.

Research references: Meng, Qian, and Yared — “The Institutional Causes of China’s Great Famine, 1959–1961”; Kung and Lin — “The Causes of China’s Great Leap Famine”.

Weather and Natural Disasters

Parts of China experienced drought, flooding, and other adverse weather during the famine years.

Natural disasters worsened conditions in some regions, but the famine cannot be explained adequately as a weather event alone.

The national crisis emerged from the interaction of agricultural decline and disruption with procurement policy, labor diversion, collective institutions, reporting failures, and delayed policy correction.

The Soviet Withdrawal of Experts

Relations between China and the Soviet Union deteriorated during the Great Leap period.

In 1960, the Soviet Union withdrew many specialists and disrupted technical cooperation on industrial projects.

This worsened industrial difficulties and the broader economic crisis.

It should not be treated as the primary explanation for the rural famine, which was already rooted in domestic Great Leap institutions and policies.

Scholarly reference: Journal of Contemporary China — “The Great Leap Forward, the People’s Commune and the Sino-Soviet Split”.

Food Availability and Rural Hunger

By 1959 and especially 1960, food availability deteriorated severely in many rural regions.

Households exhausted reserves, reduced meals, substituted inferior foods, sold or consumed possessions where possible, and attempted to migrate despite restrictions.

In the worst affected areas, severe malnutrition and starvation became widespread.

Why the Countryside Suffered More

Urban residents were generally protected more effectively by ration systems and state grain distribution than rural producers.

Rural communities had fewer protected claims on state food supplies and were the main source from which procurement was collected.

This contributed to a profound urban-rural difference in famine exposure.

Mobility Controls and Famine

Migration could be a traditional survival strategy during food crises.

By the late 1950s, household-registration and rationing institutions increasingly restricted rural access to cities and urban food systems.

Enforcement varied, and people still fled famine regions, but the institutional barriers reduced the ability of many rural residents to escape scarcity.

When Was the Famine?

The most severe national mortality crisis is generally concentrated in 1959–1961, although hardship began in some places in 1958 and continued into 1962.

For that reason, historical works use overlapping labels such as the Great Leap Famine, the 1959–1961 famine, or the 1958–1962 famine.

Students should check the date range used by any demographic estimate before comparing numbers.

How Many People Died?

There is no single universally accepted death toll.

Demographic studies commonly identify excess deaths in the tens of millions. Earlier demographic reconstructions such as the work of Basil Ashton and colleagues and Judith Banister produced estimates around roughly 30 million excess deaths, while other studies have produced lower or substantially higher totals.

Different estimates use different census reconstructions, expected mortality baselines, provincial records, and date ranges.

A careful textbook should therefore describe the famine as one of the deadliest in modern history without treating one disputed estimate as settled fact.

Demographic references: Ashton et al. — “Famine in China, 1958–61”; Peng Xizhe — “Demographic Consequences of the Great Leap Forward in China’s Provinces”.

Lost and Postponed Births

The demographic impact extended beyond excess deaths.

Fertility fell sharply during the crisis as malnutrition, family separation, uncertainty, and physiological stress reduced births.

Later population recovery included a rebound in births, complicating simple comparisons of population totals before and after the famine.

Regional Variation

Famine severity differed dramatically among provinces, counties, communes, and villages.

Local crop conditions mattered, but so did leadership behavior, procurement pressure, reporting practices, access to transportation, migration opportunities, and the timing of policy correction.

Regional variation is one reason national averages alone cannot explain how the famine operated.

The Role of Local Cadres

Local officials occupied a difficult and powerful position.

They were responsible for implementing production targets, procurement, collective dining, labor mobilization, and political campaigns.

Some falsified reports or used coercion to meet targets. Others attempted to protect local populations, reduce deliveries, hide grain, or correct radical policies.

The system rewarded compliance with ambitious central goals, which could punish honest reporting.

Coercion and Rural Violence

Famine-era rural governance could involve detention, beatings, public punishment, confiscation, and other forms of coercion against people accused of hiding grain, avoiding labor, stealing food, or resisting commune rules.

The prevalence and intensity varied across localities.

These practices show that famine mortality cannot be understood only through aggregate food-production statistics.

Everyday Survival

Families adopted many survival strategies: reducing meals, eating wild plants or substitutes, exchanging possessions, seeking relatives, leaving villages, concealing food, or bargaining with local officials.

These choices were constrained by collective institutions, poverty, mobility controls, and the collapse of local food availability.

Survivor testimony provides evidence that national statistics cannot capture.

Children, the Elderly, and Vulnerability

Severe food scarcity did not affect all people equally.

Children, elderly people, sick individuals, and people without strong household or political networks could face especially high risk.

Households themselves had to make painful decisions about how limited food was distributed.

Early Attempts to Correct the Leap

By late 1958 and early 1959, central leaders had begun recognizing serious problems in inflated targets, commune organization, and industrial mobilization.

Some policies were adjusted before the full magnitude of the famine became clear.

This is important because the Great Leap was not implemented as one unchanging policy from 1958 to 1962.

The Lushan Conference of 1959

Senior leaders met at Lushan in the summer of 1959 to assess the Great Leap.

Defense Minister Peng Dehuai wrote Mao a letter that acknowledged achievements but criticized exaggeration, economic imbalance, and what he saw as serious errors in implementation and policy.

Mao interpreted the criticism as a political challenge and turned the meeting into a confrontation over the direction of the Leap.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge History of China — Lushan and the Great Leap leadership split.

Peng Dehuai

Peng Dehuai was a veteran revolutionary commander and minister of national defense.

His criticism at Lushan was not a rejection of socialism or industrial development. It challenged excess, false reporting, and the economic consequences of the Great Leap.

Peng was removed from the defense portfolio and replaced by Lin Biao.

The Anti-Right Opportunist Campaign

After Lushan, criticism of Great Leap policies became politically dangerous.

A campaign against “right opportunism” targeted cadres associated with skepticism or retreat.

This undermined corrective efforts at a crucial moment and encouraged officials to reaffirm policies even as famine conditions worsened.

A Renewed Radical Push

The political result of Lushan contributed to renewed Great Leap pressure in late 1959 and 1960.

Some commune and production policies that had begun to be moderated were reasserted.

This helped delay a more comprehensive response to the crisis.

Industrial Disruption

Great Leap industrial expansion produced rapid increases in employment and construction but also serious shortages, poor-quality output, unfinished projects, and imbalances.

When the crisis deepened, industrial investment and urban employment had to be reduced sharply.

Cambridge economic history treats 1958–1965 as a period in which the economy moved from overexpansion to severe retrenchment and recovery.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge History of China — “The Chinese economy under stress, 1958–1965”.

Central Planning and Incentive Failure

Economic studies of the Great Leap emphasize that the disaster did not result simply from central planning in the abstract.

Specific institutional features mattered: unrealistic targets, politically distorted information, compulsory procurement, weak local incentives, administrative competition, and limited mechanisms for correcting error.

Economic reference: Li Wei and Dennis Tao Yang — “The Great Leap Forward: Anatomy of a Central Planning Disaster”.

The Policy Retreat Begins

By late 1960, the scale of economic and food-supply problems forced broader retrenchment.

Investment was cut, industrial expansion slowed, commune institutions were revised, and greater attention was given to restoring agricultural production.

The retreat developed unevenly and continued through 1961 and 1962.

Reforming the Communes

Decision-making and accounting were increasingly shifted downward from very large communes toward smaller production units.

Communal dining halls were reduced or closed in many areas.

Household sidelines and private plots were restored to a limited degree, and incentives were adjusted to connect work more closely with material reward.

Private Plots and Rural Markets Return

Recovery policies permitted renewed household plots, sideline production, and some rural market exchange.

These measures did not abolish collective agriculture.

They represented pragmatic efforts to restore incentives, food production, and household survival within the socialist system.

Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, and Chen Yun

During economic recovery, leaders including Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, and Chen Yun played major roles in policies that reduced Great Leap excesses and restored more cautious economic management.

Mao remained CCP chairman and a central political leader; it is misleading to say that he simply disappeared from power.

However, the crisis strengthened leaders who favored pragmatic recovery policies and sharpened later disagreements over China's socialist direction.

The Seven Thousand Cadres Conference

In January and February 1962, more than seven thousand officials gathered in Beijing for an enlarged central work conference usually called the Seven Thousand Cadres Conference.

The meeting reviewed the economic crisis and problems of leadership, implementation, and political decision-making.

It became an important moment of internal reassessment after the Great Leap catastrophe.

Debating Responsibility

Responsibility for the famine has remained politically and historically contested.

Explanations have emphasized different combinations of Mao's leadership, collective decision-making, local cadre behavior, procurement institutions, political repression of criticism, natural disasters, and the Sino-Soviet rupture.

These explanations are not equally supported in every case or region, but scholarship strongly rejects an account that treats the disaster as the result of weather alone.

Mao's Role

Mao was the principal political advocate of the Great Leap strategy and repeatedly pushed for accelerated development and mass mobilization.

He was not the only leader who supported the Leap in 1958; broad sections of the senior leadership participated in its launch.

His authority became especially consequential at Lushan, where the attack on Peng Dehuai helped suppress criticism and delayed broader policy correction.

A historically responsible account therefore distinguishes Mao's central leadership responsibility from the participation of other leaders and the autonomous actions of provincial and local officials.

Official Retrospective Assessments

Later CCP assessments acknowledged serious errors in the Great Leap period and placed substantial responsibility on policy mistakes while also mentioning natural disasters and the deterioration of Sino-Soviet relations.

Official terminology and emphasis have changed over time, and public discussion of famine mortality remains politically sensitive.

Historians therefore compare official retrospective narratives with demographic, archival, and local evidence.

Social Consequences

The famine damaged families and communities through death, illness, postponed births, migration, separation, loss of property, and erosion of trust.

Collective institutions that had been promoted as symbols of rapid socialist advancement became associated in many places with deprivation and coercion.

These experiences influenced rural attitudes toward later state campaigns.

Environmental Consequences

Great Leap projects also had environmental effects.

Tree cutting for fuel, poorly planned water projects, intensive earth moving, and attempts to transform landscapes rapidly could damage local ecosystems.

The scale and persistence of environmental effects varied considerably by project and region.

Gender and Communal Labor

Women were mobilized into agricultural, industrial, construction, and public labor on an unprecedented scale in many communities.

Collective dining and childcare were intended partly to reduce household burdens and make more labor available for production.

Yet food scarcity and the collapse of services often intensified women's responsibilities for obtaining food, caring for children, and sustaining households.

Memory and Historical Silence

Famine memory has often been preserved through families, village stories, local histories, memoirs, demographic research, and archival reconstruction.

For long periods, open public discussion inside China was limited and official language emphasized economic “difficulties” rather than the full demographic catastrophe.

Historical study therefore depends heavily on combining statistical reconstruction with local and personal evidence.

Why Did the Famine Become So Severe?

No single factor is sufficient.

A useful causal chain is: unrealistic targets → exaggerated production reports → excessive procurement expectations → rural food depletion, combined with labor diversion, weakened household incentives, collective-dining problems, mobility restrictions, coercive enforcement, adverse weather in some regions, and delayed policy correction.

The crisis became catastrophic because these mechanisms reinforced one another inside a political system in which reporting failure and fear of criticism impaired feedback.

What the Great Leap Was Not

The Great Leap was not simply a plan to build backyard furnaces.

It was not identical to the famine, although Great Leap institutions and policy failures were central causes of the famine.

It was not implemented uniformly across China.

And it was not a policy imposed by Mao alone without support from other senior leaders in 1958, even though Mao's leadership and later suppression of criticism were central to the campaign's trajectory.

Think Like a Historian

Was the Great Leap famine primarily an economic failure, a political failure, or an institutional failure?

Economic evidence shows falling agricultural output, wasteful investment, and disrupted allocation. Political evidence shows inflated targets, pressure to display enthusiasm, and punishment of criticism. Institutional evidence shows distorted reporting, compulsory procurement, restricted mobility, and weak mechanisms for correcting local shortages.

The strongest interpretation treats these dimensions as interconnected rather than competing explanations.

How Do We Know?

Historians reconstruct the Great Leap through population censuses, provincial mortality data, grain statistics, procurement records, party directives, local archives, memoirs, oral histories, diplomatic reports, commune documents, and retrospective interviews.

National statistics must be compared carefully because official production figures were themselves distorted during the Leap.

Demographic reconstruction is especially important because direct death registration was incomplete and because fertility collapse must be separated from excess mortality.

Historical Significance

The Great Leap Forward was a turning point in PRC history.

It produced one of the deadliest famines of the twentieth century, exposed severe weaknesses in campaign-style economic governance, weakened confidence in radical development policies, and created major tensions within the CCP leadership.

Recovery policies of 1961–1962 restored more pragmatic economic practices, but disagreements over those measures contributed to the political struggles that later culminated in the Cultural Revolution.

Key Takeaways

The Great Leap Forward began in 1958 as an attempt to accelerate industrial and agricultural development through mass mobilization.

People's communes were larger and more comprehensive than the agricultural cooperatives of the First Five-Year Plan.

Backyard steel campaigns diverted labor and resources and often produced poor-quality output.

Exaggerated harvest reports undermined the accuracy of state planning.

Grain procurement based on inflated production claims contributed directly to rural food shortages.

Natural disasters and the Soviet withdrawal worsened the crisis but do not adequately explain the famine by themselves.

The most severe famine mortality occurred mainly from 1959 through 1961, with hardship extending beyond those dates.

Scholarly estimates of excess deaths vary widely; a responsible account presents a range rather than a single uncontested number.

Peng Dehuai criticized Great Leap excesses at Lushan in 1959 and was removed from the defense ministry after Mao turned the criticism into a political confrontation.

The campaign against right opportunism weakened criticism and delayed correction.

From late 1960 through 1962, leaders reduced Great Leap policies, reformed communes, restored limited household incentives, and cut industrial overexpansion.

The disaster reshaped leadership politics and contributed to tensions that later became central to the Cultural Revolution.

Key Terms

Great Leap Forward: the development and mass-mobilization campaign launched in 1958 to accelerate industrialization and agricultural transformation.

People's Commune: a large rural organization combining agricultural production with administrative, social, militia, and sometimes industrial functions.

Backyard Furnace: a small-scale furnace promoted during the 1958 steel campaign, frequently producing low-quality or unusable metal.

Communal Dining Hall: a collective eating institution established in many communes during the radical phase of the Leap.

Grain Procurement: state acquisition of agricultural grain for cities, industry, government, and other uses.

Sputnik Yield: an informal label for spectacular and often exaggerated crop-yield claims publicized during the Great Leap.

Peng Dehuai: defense minister whose criticism of Great Leap excesses at the 1959 Lushan Conference led to his political downfall.

Lushan Conference: the 1959 leadership meeting that shifted from correcting Great Leap problems to a political confrontation over Peng Dehuai's criticism.

Anti-Right Opportunist Campaign: the post-Lushan campaign targeting officials accused of opposing the Great Leap.

Great Leap Famine: the catastrophic famine associated with Great Leap policies, institutional failures, agricultural disruption, adverse weather in some regions, and delayed correction, with the worst mortality concentrated in 1959–1961.

Seven Thousand Cadres Conference: the large 1962 CCP conference reviewing the crisis and problems of leadership and policy.

Recovery Policies: the 1960–1962 retreat from radical Great Leap measures through lower investment, revised communes, restored household incentives, and more cautious economic management.

Continue the Story

By 1962, the Great Leap had been substantially dismantled as an economic strategy. Commune institutions survived in revised form, but the most radical production, dining, industrial, and procurement practices had been curtailed.

Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yun, Zhou Enlai, and other leaders supported policies aimed at restoring agricultural output, improving incentives, reducing industrial overexpansion, and rebuilding economic stability.

The crisis did not end political conflict inside the CCP.

Mao increasingly worried that pragmatic recovery policies could move China away from revolutionary socialism, while relations with the Soviet Union continued to deteriorate.
