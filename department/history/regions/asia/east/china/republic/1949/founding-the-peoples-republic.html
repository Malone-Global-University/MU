Lesson Orientation

The Communist victory of 1949 did not automatically create a functioning national government. The new leadership inherited a country marked by decades of invasion, civil war, hyperinflation, damaged transport, population displacement, fragmented administration, and unresolved territorial conflict.

The central problem of the founding period was therefore larger than announcing a new state: the Chinese Communist Party had to transform a revolutionary movement with armies and regional base governments into the leadership of a nationwide government.

Early consolidation combined reconstruction, institution-building, social revolution, mass mobilization, and intense political coercion.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, the learner should be able to explain the CPPCC and Common Program; describe the Central People's Government; distinguish New Democracy from later socialist transformation; explain urban takeover, monetary stabilization, land reform, the Marriage Law, political campaigns, territorial consolidation, the Sino-Soviet alliance, and the Korean War; and evaluate both the administrative achievements and coercive costs of early PRC consolidation.

What Does “Founding the PRC” Mean?

The phrase can refer narrowly to the political events of September and October 1949 or more broadly to the process through which the new government converted revolutionary victory into effective state authority.

October 1 created a new central government. Consolidation required that government to make its authority effective across territory, cities, villages, markets, institutions, and armed forces.

From Revolutionary War to State Power

Communist base areas had provided experience in taxation, local administration, land policy, military recruitment, mass mobilization, and political education.

Governing all of mainland China required those practices to be adapted to large cities, national ministries, universities, banks, factories, railways, and regions that had never been Communist bases.

The Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference

In September 1949, representatives met in Beijing for the first plenary session of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference (CPPCC).

The conference included CCP representatives as well as allied political parties, mass organizations, military forces, regional delegations, minority representatives, overseas Chinese, and other public figures.

It selected the leadership of the new government and adopted the framework under which the new state would initially operate.

The Common Program

On September 29, 1949, the CPPCC adopted the Common Program. It addressed state power, military organization, economic policy, culture and education, nationalities policy, and foreign relations.

Because the PRC did not yet have a permanent constitution, the Common Program functioned as an interim constitutional framework until the 1954 Constitution.

Documentary reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — adoption of the Common Program.

New Democracy

The founding framework did not announce the immediate abolition of all private business and individual farming. It described a transitional order often called New Democracy.

The early economy contained a growing state sector alongside cooperatives, individual production, markets, and regulated private capitalist enterprise.

This distinction matters because systematic socialist transformation accelerated later and belongs primarily to the next lesson.

October 1, 1949

On October 1, 1949, Mao Zedong publicly proclaimed the establishment of the People's Republic of China in Beijing.

That day the Central People's Government Council took office, accepted the Common Program as its course of action, and announced major appointments.

Primary-source reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — October 1 proclamation.

The Central People's Government

The new state included the Central People's Government Council, Government Administration Council, People's Revolutionary Military Council, Supreme People's Court, and the office of the People's Procurator General.

These institutions converted revolutionary authority into national administration while operating under decisive Chinese Communist Party leadership.

Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai

Mao became chairman of the Central People's Government and chairman of the People's Revolutionary Military Council while remaining the preeminent CCP leader.

Zhou Enlai became premier of the Government Administration Council and foreign minister, making him central to civil administration, diplomacy, and coordination among ministries.

A Broader Founding Coalition

The formal founding leadership also included non-Communist figures such as Soong Ching-ling, Li Jishen, and Zhang Lan.

Their inclusion reflected the united-front language of New Democracy, although effective political leadership remained centered in the CCP.

The CCP and the New State

Party committees influenced appointments, military command, political campaigns, mass organizations, and major policy decisions.

Formal government bodies mattered, but the CCP's leadership over the state became a defining institutional feature of the People's Republic.

Military and Regional Administration

In recently conquered regions, military and civilian administration often overlapped. Large regional structures helped govern areas where local government had collapsed or armed opposition remained.

Over time, national and provincial civilian administration became more standardized.

Taking Over China's Cities

Running a city required more than military victory. The new authorities had to maintain food supplies, water, electricity, factories, banks, hospitals, schools, transport, and public order.

Communist officials therefore retained many technicians, clerks, engineers, doctors, teachers, and other specialists while replacing or screening politically sensitive leadership.

Case Study: Governing Shanghai

Shanghai was the country's largest commercial and industrial metropolis and an early test of Communist urban governance.

Officials confronted inflation, food supply, private banking, industrial production, speculation, unemployment, and a large professional and business population unfamiliar with CCP rule.

The government's ability to stabilize the city strengthened its claim to administrative competence.

The Economic Crisis of 1949

The new government inherited hyperinflation, disrupted taxation, damaged transport, shortages, war-damaged factories, and regional monetary fragmentation.

Economic stabilization became one of the most urgent tests of the new state.

Currency and Hyperinflation

The government expanded use of the renminbi through the People's Bank of China and centralized fiscal and monetary policy.

By 1950 it had made substantial progress against runaway inflation through taxation, banking control, commodity management, spending discipline, and campaigns against speculation.

Success against hyperinflation became one of the early regime's most visible administrative achievements.

Private Business and the Expanding State Sector

Private manufacturers and merchants continued to operate during the New Democratic period, but they faced growing regulation over credit, taxation, raw materials, state contracts, and markets.

Meanwhile the government controlled major banks, railways, utilities, mines, and enterprises associated with the former Nationalist state or large bureaucratic-capital interests.

This mixed structure provided the foundation for later socialist transformation.

Workers and the New Government

Communist ideology described the working class as the leading class of the new order. Trade unions and workplace organizations gained new political importance.

At the same time, the government emphasized production discipline, economic recovery, and incorporation of unions into party-state institutions.

The Rural Majority

Most people still lived in rural communities, so state consolidation depended heavily on village transformation.

The government sought to redistribute land, reorganize local authority, establish party and government institutions, and extend taxation and grain procurement.

The Agrarian Reform Law of 1950

The Agrarian Reform Law of 1950 extended land redistribution into areas newly brought under Communist rule.

It targeted the landlord system and redistributed land and other rural property, building on earlier Communist agrarian campaigns in revolutionary base areas.

Land Reform in Practice

Cadres organized village investigations, class classification, peasant associations, accusation meetings, confiscation, and redistribution.

Poor and land-poor villagers could gain property and political influence, while people classified as landlords lost property and local authority.

Violence and Coercion in Land Reform

Land reform also involved substantial coercion. Campaigns could include public denunciation, beatings, imprisonment, and executions, with intensity varying by region and phase.

Historians emphasize that mass accusation and coercion were often used deliberately to destroy older systems of local authority and establish new political relationships.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — Morality, Coercion and State Building by Campaign.

The Marriage Law of 1950

The Marriage Law challenged forced marriage, concubinage, and unequal marital status and formally emphasized greater equality and consent.

Implementation was uneven. Women sometimes used the law to seek divorce or resist arranged marriages, but local family structures and officials could limit change.

Mass Organizations

Organizations for workers, women, youth, peasants, professionals, and cultural groups helped connect the party-state to society.

They represented constituencies but also transmitted policy, recruited activists, and organized campaigns.

Government by Campaign

Political campaigns became a characteristic instrument of early PRC governance.

They combined propaganda, meetings, investigation, public accusation, administrative enforcement, and mass participation. Campaigns could implement policy quickly but also generate coercion, local excess, and unstable political pressure.

Suppression of Counterrevolutionaries

Beginning in 1950, the Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries targeted people and organizations defined as threats to the new political order, including armed remnants, underground networks, secret societies, spies, and other opponents.

The campaign involved arrests, public trials, imprisonment, and executions and became a major instrument of regime consolidation.

Scholarly references: Comparative Studies in Society and History and The China Quarterly.

Territorial Consolidation After October 1949

The PRC did not control every territory it claimed when it was proclaimed. Nationalist forces still held Hainan, Taiwan, and several offshore islands; Tibet remained outside effective Communist administration.

State consolidation therefore continued through military, political, and diplomatic means.

Hainan and Xinjiang

PLA forces captured Hainan from Nationalist control in 1950. In Xinjiang, political authorities shifted allegiance during the final Civil War and PLA forces entered without a major campaign comparable to the battles in eastern China.

These developments extended PRC authority but did not eliminate every regional political problem.

Tibet and the Seventeen Point Agreement

PLA forces entered eastern Tibetan areas in 1950. In 1951, PRC and Tibetan representatives signed the Seventeen Point Agreement, which recognized PRC sovereignty while formally promising continuity in important Tibetan institutions.

The circumstances and legitimacy of that agreement later became deeply contested. Later Tibetan policy and conflict belong to subsequent chapters.

Taiwan and the Continuing ROC Government

The Republic of China government had transferred its seat to Taipei in December 1949. Taiwan therefore remained outside PRC control.

Two rival governments claimed to represent China: the PRC in Beijing and the ROC in Taipei. This unresolved division became a major East Asian international issue.

International Recognition

The PRC declared itself the sole legal government representing China, but foreign recognition occurred unevenly.

Communist states recognized the new government quickly, while the United States continued to recognize the Republic of China government.

The Sino-Soviet Treaty of 1950

On February 14, 1950, the PRC and Soviet Union signed a thirty-year Treaty of Friendship, Alliance, and Mutual Assistance.

Associated agreements addressed the Chinese Changchun Railway, Port Arthur, Dairen, and a Soviet credit of $300 million over five years.

Documentary reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — Sino-Soviet treaty and agreements.

“Leaning to One Side”

Mao's phrase signaled alignment with the Soviet Union and socialist camp in the emerging Cold War.

The alliance was close but did not mean that Soviet and Chinese interests were identical; disputes over sovereignty, assistance, and regional influence remained possible.

Relations with the United States and the UN

The United States did not recognize the PRC and continued relations with the Republic of China government.

The ROC delegation continued to hold China's United Nations seat, creating a representation dispute that remained unresolved in the ROC's favor until 1971.

The Korean War

The Korean War began in June 1950. As United Nations forces advanced northward toward the Chinese border, PRC leaders increasingly regarded the conflict as a direct security threat.

In October 1950, Chinese forces entered the war under the designation Chinese People's Volunteers, bringing the PRC into direct combat with U.S. and other UN forces.

How the Korean War Changed Consolidation

The Korean War intensified domestic mobilization, military production, political loyalty campaigns, and anti-American propaganda.

It also increased pressure against suspected internal opposition and strengthened the campaign-centered character of early PRC governance.

U.S. naval intervention in the Taiwan Strait also reduced the immediate possibility of a PLA invasion of Taiwan.

Achievements and Costs of Early Consolidation

The early PRC created functioning central institutions, stabilized currency, restored production and transport, redistributed land, and extended administration across most of the mainland.

Those achievements came with major costs: political consolidation relied heavily on coercive campaigns, public denunciation, imprisonment, executions, and the destruction of organized opposition.

A balanced historical account must examine administrative effectiveness and coercive political transformation together.

Why 1949 Was Not Yet Full Socialist Transformation

The PRC's founding placed the CCP in national power, but the economy was not immediately collectivized or fully nationalized.

Private businesses, individual peasant farming, markets, and a growing state sector coexisted during the New Democratic recovery period.

The shift toward systematic socialist transformation accelerated after recovery and became central to the First Five-Year Plan.

How Do We Know?

The founding period is documented through CCP records, government laws and proclamations, CPPCC materials, economic statistics, land-reform reports, court records, diplomatic archives, memoirs, newspapers, local histories, and later scholarship.

Political terms such as “liberation,” “counterrevolutionary,” and “bureaucratic capitalist” were categories used by the new regime. Historians should explain them rather than automatically adopt them as neutral descriptions.

Think Like a Historian

Was the early PRC primarily a period of reconstruction or revolution?

Evidence supports both. Currency stabilization, reopening railways and factories, and rebuilding administration were reconstruction. Land reform, suppression campaigns, mass organizations, and the construction of a new party-state were revolutionary transformations.

The strongest interpretation treats them as interconnected: reconstruction strengthened the revolutionary state, while revolutionary campaigns became instruments for extending and rebuilding state authority.

Historical Significance

The founding period established the political framework that governed mainland China after 1949.

It created the Central People's Government, consolidated CCP leadership, stabilized the economy, transformed rural property and local authority, expanded legal claims for women, aligned the PRC with the Soviet Union, intensified conflict with the United States, and laid the institutional foundation for socialist transformation.

Key Takeaways

October 1, 1949 was the founding of the PRC, not the completion of every consolidation task.

The Common Program served as an interim constitutional framework.

New Democracy allowed a mixed economy during the initial recovery period.

Land reform redistributed property and political power but also involved major coercion and violence.

Political campaigns became a central method of governance.

The PRC extended authority across most of the mainland while Taiwan remained governed by the ROC.

The 1950 Sino-Soviet treaty formalized the PRC's early alignment with the Soviet Union.

The Korean War intensified both external conflict and domestic political mobilization.

The founding period created the foundations for the socialist transformation that followed.

Key Terms

People's Republic of China (PRC): the state proclaimed in Beijing on October 1, 1949.

CPPCC: the united-front conference that adopted the Common Program and helped organize the new government.

Common Program: the interim constitutional framework adopted in September 1949.

New Democracy: the early transitional order combining CCP leadership with a united front and mixed economy.

Renminbi: the currency expanded as the unified money of the new state.

Agrarian Reform Law: the 1950 law providing the national framework for land redistribution.

Marriage Law of 1950: the law formally challenging forced marriage, concubinage, and unequal marital status.

Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries: the early-1950s campaign targeting groups and individuals defined as threats to the new order.

Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship, Alliance, and Mutual Assistance: the February 1950 alliance between the PRC and USSR.

Chinese People's Volunteers: the designation used for Chinese forces entering the Korean War.
