Lesson Orientation

The famine and economic crisis of the Great Leap Forward forced the People's Republic of China into a period of recovery and reassessment.

From 1961 through the mid-1960s, senior leaders reduced many Great Leap policies, restored agricultural incentives, cut industrial overexpansion, and tried to rebuild production. Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yun, Zhou Enlai, and other officials played important roles in this readjustment.

Recovery did not produce political consensus. Mao Zedong increasingly worried that pragmatic economic policies, bureaucratic privilege, and reliance on experts could push China toward what he called revisionism.

At the same time, the alliance with the Soviet Union deteriorated into an open ideological and geopolitical split. Domestic disagreement and the Sino-Soviet conflict reinforced Mao's fear that socialist systems could change direction from within.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, the learner should be able to explain the post-famine recovery policies; describe the roles of Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yun, Zhou Enlai, and Mao Zedong; explain the Seven Thousand Cadres Conference and the Tenth Plenum; describe the Socialist Education Movement and Four Cleanups; distinguish economic recovery from political radicalization; trace the Sino-Soviet split from alliance tensions to open polemics; explain disputes over de-Stalinization, peaceful coexistence, nuclear weapons, and revolutionary strategy; identify the importance of the 1960 Soviet withdrawal of experts, the 1963 polemics, the Partial Test Ban Treaty, China's 1964 nuclear test, and Khrushchev's removal; and explain how these conflicts helped create the political setting for the Cultural Revolution.

China After the Great Leap Famine

By 1961, the Great Leap economy was in severe distress. Agricultural output had fallen, famine had caused mass mortality, industrial projects were being abandoned or scaled back, and state finances were under pressure.

Recovery required a retreat from policies that had treated political enthusiasm as a substitute for reliable information, incentives, technical expertise, and material constraints.

The Readjustment Program

Beginning in 1960 and accelerating in 1961–1962, the leadership adopted a program commonly summarized by the priorities of readjustment, consolidation, filling out, and raising standards.

Industrial investment was cut, unrealistic construction projects were postponed, workers were transferred out of overexpanded urban industries, and greater attention was given to agriculture and basic supply.

Scholarly reference: A Social History of Maoist China — “The Post-Famine Years: From Readjustment to the Socialist Education Campaign”.

Agricultural Recovery

Rural recovery depended on restoring incentives and reducing the administrative scale of collective farming.

Accounting and work organization shifted downward from the giant commune level toward smaller production brigades and especially production teams.

Private household plots, sideline activities, and limited rural market exchange were restored or expanded in many areas.

The Production Team as the Basic Accounting Unit

One of the most important institutional adjustments was the strengthening of the production team as the basic unit for accounting and income distribution.

This reduced the degree to which very large communes pooled resources across unrelated villages and gave smaller local groups more direct responsibility for work and distribution.

The people's commune remained in existence, but its radical 1958 form was substantially modified.

Household Incentives and Local Flexibility

Recovery policies allowed households more opportunity to cultivate private plots, raise animals, engage in sideline production, and participate in local markets.

In some localities, forms of household responsibility or task contracting also appeared experimentally.

These measures were designed to restore production, but Mao later worried that they encouraged private economic tendencies.

Industrial Retrenchment

The state reduced investment in heavy industry and closed or consolidated inefficient enterprises created during the Great Leap.

Urban employment was cut substantially, and many recent migrants to cities returned to the countryside.

Industrial recovery increasingly emphasized efficiency, maintenance, quality, and completion of viable projects rather than spectacular headline targets.

Chen Yun and Economic Pragmatism

Chen Yun became an important advocate of cautious economic management.

He emphasized material balances, reliable statistics, agricultural recovery, fiscal stability, and the use of limited market mechanisms within a socialist framework.

His approach did not reject state planning. It argued that planning had to respect supply constraints and economic information.

Liu Shaoqi

Liu Shaoqi, chairman of the People's Republic and one of the CCP's most senior leaders, became closely associated with post-famine recovery and institutional repair.

He supported policies that reduced Great Leap excesses and strengthened regular administrative procedures.

Later Cultural Revolution narratives portrayed Liu as the principal representative of a “capitalist road,” but that retrospective label should not be treated as a neutral description of his policies during the early 1960s.

Deng Xiaoping

As CCP general secretary, Deng Xiaoping played a major role in daily party administration and economic recovery.

His reputation for organizational pragmatism grew during the readjustment years.

Like Liu and Chen Yun, Deng remained committed to socialism and CCP rule; the central disagreement concerned how socialist development should be organized, not whether China should become a liberal capitalist system.

Zhou Enlai

Zhou Enlai helped coordinate economic reconstruction, state administration, foreign relations, and increasingly difficult relations with the Soviet Union.

Zhou often operated between competing priorities: maintaining political loyalty to Mao, supporting technical reconstruction, and preserving diplomatic room for maneuver.

Mao's Position After the Great Leap

It is inaccurate to say that Mao simply lost power after the Great Leap.

He remained CCP chairman and retained exceptional political and ideological authority.

Other leaders gained greater responsibility for economic management, but Mao continued to shape major political questions and increasingly warned that recovery policies could weaken revolutionary commitment.

The Seven Thousand Cadres Conference

In January and February 1962, more than seven thousand officials attended an enlarged central work conference in Beijing.

The meeting reviewed the crisis produced by the Great Leap and discussed responsibility, economic conditions, cadre behavior, and recovery.

Liu Shaoqi gave a major report acknowledging that policy errors had played a substantial role in the disaster, while Mao made a self-critical statement but retained his central political position.

The Limits of Post-Leap Self-Criticism

The leadership acknowledged serious mistakes, but there was no open institutional process assigning responsibility comparable to an independent inquiry.

Discussion remained inside a one-party political system in which Mao's authority placed limits on how far criticism could go.

The conference nevertheless provided more room for internal acknowledgment of failure than had existed during the radical phase of the Leap.

The Tenth Plenum of 1962

In September 1962, the CCP Central Committee met at its Tenth Plenum.

Mao renewed emphasis on class struggle and warned that class contradictions continued under socialism.

This shift marked a movement away from the relative political relaxation that had accompanied economic readjustment.

Cambridge reference: Cambridge History of China — Mao’s renewed emphasis on class struggle after the Tenth Plenum.

Class Struggle After Socialist Transformation

Official doctrine had earlier presented socialist transformation as eliminating the economic foundations of the old exploiting classes.

Mao increasingly argued that class struggle could continue after formal changes in ownership because bourgeois ideas, bureaucratic privilege, and political tendencies could emerge inside socialist institutions themselves.

This argument shifted attention from former landlords and capitalists toward people within the party-state.

What Did Mao Mean by “Revisionism”?

In Maoist political language, revisionism meant altering Marxism-Leninism in ways that abandoned revolutionary struggle while preserving the name of socialism.

Mao increasingly used the Soviet Union under Nikita Khrushchev as the leading international example.

The fear of revisionism also became a domestic argument: if the Soviet Communist Party could change direction, Mao believed the Chinese Communist Party might do the same.

The Socialist Education Movement

Beginning in the early 1960s and expanding from 1963, the Socialist Education Movement attempted to correct problems in rural party organizations and revive socialist political commitment.

The movement investigated local cadres, collective finance, labor accounting, property, and political behavior.

It became one of the most important bridges between post-famine recovery and the Cultural Revolution.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — Socialist Education Movement.

The Four Cleanups

The rural campaign became widely associated with the Four Cleanups, which focused on problems such as accounts, warehouses, property, and work points or related areas of collective administration.

The exact formulation changed during the campaign.

At the practical level, work teams investigated corruption, misuse of collective resources, local privilege, and alleged capitalist tendencies.

Was the Problem Local Cadres or Party Leadership?

A major internal disagreement concerned where the political danger was located.

Some approaches focused on correcting corrupt or abusive grassroots cadres through investigation and organizational discipline.

Mao increasingly argued that the deeper problem could involve higher-ranking people inside the party who were taking a capitalist road.

This change in emphasis pointed toward the much broader political targets of the Cultural Revolution.

The Twenty-Three Articles of 1965

In January 1965, new directives known as the Twenty-Three Articles redirected the Socialist Education Movement.

The formulation associated with Mao emphasized that the main target was people in authority within the party who were taking the capitalist road.

This language foreshadowed one of the defining slogans and political categories of the Cultural Revolution.

Intellectuals and Political Suspicion

Universities, writers, artists, scientists, and other intellectuals had already experienced severe political pressure during the Anti-Rightist Campaign and Great Leap.

During the early 1960s, technical expertise regained some practical importance because recovery required specialists.

At the same time, Mao's renewed class-struggle emphasis encouraged suspicion that professional authority and cultural autonomy could shelter bourgeois political tendencies.

Education: Expertise Versus Revolution

Educational policy reflected the broader conflict.

Recovery required higher academic standards and trained specialists, but Maoist critics warned that schools could reproduce social privilege and separate students from workers and peasants.

Debates over examinations, curriculum, academic authority, and work-study programs therefore became political questions.

Culture and Historical Allegory

Historical drama, literature, opera, and scholarship became increasingly politicized.

By the mid-1960s, criticism of works such as Wu Han's play about the Ming official Hai Rui became connected to struggles inside the Beijing party and cultural establishment.

That controversy belongs most directly to the opening of the Cultural Revolution, but its roots lay in the ideological disputes of the recovery period.

Roots of the Sino-Soviet Split

The Sino-Soviet split did not begin with one event.

Tensions grew from disagreements over ideology, nuclear strategy, foreign policy, economic aid, relations with the United States, leadership of the international communist movement, and competing national interests.

By the early 1960s, what had been a close alliance in the 1950s had become an increasingly open rivalry.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge History of China — “The Sino-Soviet split”.

Khrushchev and De-Stalinization

In 1956, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev denounced aspects of Joseph Stalin's rule in his famous Secret Speech.

Chinese leaders had their own criticisms of Stalin, but Mao objected to the political implications of Khrushchev's attack and to what he regarded as Soviet claims to ideological authority over other communist parties.

De-Stalinization became one source of the widening dispute.

Peaceful Coexistence

Khrushchev emphasized the possibility of avoiding world war and competing with capitalist states through peaceful coexistence.

Chinese leaders argued that Soviet policy risked underestimating anti-imperialist and revolutionary struggle.

The disagreement was partly doctrinal and partly strategic: the Soviet Union and China faced different security environments and had different ideas about how communist movements should respond to the United States and colonial conflict.

Nuclear Weapons and Revolutionary Strategy

Nuclear weapons intensified the dispute.

Soviet leaders emphasized the catastrophic danger of nuclear war and greater control over escalation.

Maoist rhetoric often argued that fear of nuclear weapons should not paralyze revolutionary movements or national liberation struggles.

Public statements could be highly militant, but actual Chinese foreign policy also involved calculations of security and state interest.

The 1957 Moscow Meetings

At international communist meetings in Moscow in 1957, Chinese and Soviet leaders publicly emphasized unity.

Privately, differences over revolutionary strategy and nuclear politics were already visible.

The meetings demonstrated that formal alliance language could coexist with growing disagreement.

The Great Leap and Soviet Doubts

The Great Leap widened economic and political differences.

Soviet leaders were skeptical of commune organization, backyard industrialization, and Mao's claims that China could accelerate rapidly toward communism.

Chinese leaders increasingly regarded Soviet criticism as evidence of ideological conservatism and condescension.

The 1959 Nuclear-Aid Dispute

The Soviet Union had earlier agreed to provide nuclear-related assistance to China, but in 1959 it canceled an agreement involving prototype atomic-bomb technology and related technical support.

The decision deepened Chinese suspicions that Moscow wanted to constrain China's strategic independence.

Chinese nuclear development continued without the promised level of Soviet assistance.

The 1959 Sino-Indian Border Crisis

Border clashes between China and India created another source of Sino-Soviet tension.

Moscow attempted to preserve relations with both Beijing and New Delhi rather than automatically support China's position.

Chinese leaders interpreted Soviet neutrality and later Soviet support for India as further evidence that Moscow placed state diplomacy above communist solidarity.

The Soviet Withdrawal of Experts in 1960

In 1960, the Soviet Union abruptly withdrew large numbers of technical advisers from China and terminated or disrupted many cooperative arrangements.

Projects lost specialists, drawings, and technical coordination during an already severe Chinese economic crisis.

The withdrawal imposed real industrial costs and became a lasting symbol in Chinese accounts of Soviet unreliability.

Documentary background: U.S. Office of the Historian — later assessment of the Sino-Soviet rupture.

Competition Inside the International Communist Movement

Beijing and Moscow increasingly competed for influence among communist parties in Asia, Europe, Africa, and Latin America.

The dispute concerned who possessed the more correct interpretation of Marxism-Leninism and what strategy revolutionary movements should follow.

Smaller communist parties were pressured to navigate between the two centers.

Albania and the Communist Bloc Dispute

Albania became an important symbol of the split because its leadership aligned increasingly with Beijing against Khrushchev.

Soviet criticism of Albania and Chinese defense of it turned a bilateral dispute into a visible conflict inside the broader communist bloc.

The 1960 Moscow Conference

At the 1960 meeting of communist and workers' parties in Moscow, ideological differences became increasingly difficult to conceal.

Public documents preserved formal unity, but Chinese and Soviet representatives contested questions of peaceful coexistence, revolution, party leadership, and relations with capitalist states.

The international communist movement was no longer organized around uncontested Soviet leadership.

The Great Polemic

By 1963, the Sino-Soviet dispute had become openly public through letters, editorials, and ideological essays.

Chinese and Soviet parties accused one another of betraying Marxism-Leninism.

The argument addressed war and peace, revolution, colonial liberation, party leadership, Stalin's legacy, and the meaning of socialist internationalism.

The 1963 Open-Letter Exchange

In 1963, Soviet and Chinese party documents transformed the dispute into a sustained public polemic.

Chinese publications attacked Soviet revisionism, while Soviet statements criticized Chinese dogmatism and adventurism.

Foreign observers by this time increasingly treated China and the USSR as separate powers with fundamentally conflicting policies.

Contemporary documentary reference: U.S. Office of the Historian — January 1963 assessment of the Sino-Soviet split.

The Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty of 1963

In 1963, the Soviet Union, United States, and United Kingdom signed the Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty, prohibiting nuclear tests in the atmosphere, outer space, and underwater.

China opposed the agreement, arguing that it helped existing nuclear powers preserve strategic advantages while China remained without its own nuclear weapon.

The treaty became another highly visible symbol of the divergence between Beijing and Moscow.

China's First Atomic Bomb

On October 16, 1964, China successfully tested its first atomic bomb.

The test demonstrated that the PRC had developed an independent nuclear capability despite the earlier breakdown of Soviet nuclear cooperation.

It also strengthened Beijing's claim to strategic autonomy within the communist world.

Khrushchev's Removal in 1964

Nikita Khrushchev was removed from Soviet leadership in October 1964.

Chinese leaders initially explored whether relations might improve under Leonid Brezhnev and Alexei Kosygin.

The fundamental ideological and strategic disagreements remained unresolved.

Contemporary assessment: U.S. Office of the Historian — Soviet policy toward China after Khrushchev.

Zhou Enlai's 1964 Moscow Visit

Zhou Enlai attended Soviet commemorations after Khrushchev's removal and held discussions with the new leadership.

The visit did not restore the alliance.

Both governments had accumulated disputes over ideology, security, international communist leadership, and national interests that could not be resolved by a change in Soviet leader alone.

Vietnam and Competing Revolutionary Strategy

The escalating Vietnam War gave both China and the Soviet Union strong reasons to support North Vietnam while also creating new opportunities for rivalry.

Beijing and Moscow differed over military aid, diplomacy, relations with the United States, and leadership of anti-imperialist struggle.

North Vietnamese leaders attempted to obtain support from both communist powers without becoming fully subordinate to either.

China and Revolutionary Movements in the Global South

Chinese foreign policy increasingly emphasized support for national liberation and revolutionary struggles in Asia, Africa, and Latin America.

Beijing presented its own revolutionary experience as especially relevant to countries confronting colonialism, rural poverty, and weak industrial bases.

This message directly challenged Soviet claims to leadership of the global communist movement.

Diplomacy Beyond the Soviet Bloc

The PRC also pursued state-to-state diplomacy with nonaligned and newly independent countries.

Chinese leaders used the language of anti-imperialism and the Bandung tradition alongside revolutionary rhetoric.

This demonstrated that Chinese foreign policy was not reducible to ideological polemic alone.

How the External Split Affected Domestic Politics

The Sino-Soviet split became a domestic political lesson for Mao.

He argued that the Soviet Union demonstrated how a socialist state could become revisionist after a change in leadership and policy.

That interpretation strengthened his suspicion of Chinese officials who favored material incentives, bureaucratic regularity, expert authority, or less confrontational foreign policy.

The Emerging Idea of the “Capitalist Road”

By the mid-1960s, Mao increasingly framed policy disagreements as evidence that people inside the Communist Party could follow a capitalist road.

This language transformed ordinary disagreements over administration or incentives into questions of class struggle and political loyalty.

It became central to the Cultural Revolution.

Mao and Liu Shaoqi

Mao and Liu had worked together for decades, and their relationship should not be rewritten as permanent opposition.

During recovery, however, disagreements widened over rural policy, party discipline, the Socialist Education Movement, and how to interpret the danger of capitalist restoration.

These conflicts became much more severe by 1965–1966.

Mao and Deng Xiaoping

Deng supported party discipline, organizational efficiency, and pragmatic recovery policies.

Mao later interpreted such approaches as insufficiently revolutionary when separated from continuing class struggle.

Like Liu, Deng would become a major target during the Cultural Revolution, but his early-1960s policies should be understood in the context of famine recovery rather than through later political labels.

Lin Biao and the PLA

Lin Biao had replaced Peng Dehuai as defense minister after Lushan.

During the early 1960s, the People's Liberation Army became increasingly associated with political education, Mao-study campaigns, discipline, and promotion of revolutionary values.

The military's ideological role expanded before the Cultural Revolution.

“Learn from the PLA”

Campaigns encouraged civilian organizations to learn from the discipline, political study, and collective ethos attributed to the People's Liberation Army.

The PLA was increasingly presented not simply as a military institution but as a model for socialist political behavior.

Lei Feng and Model Political Behavior

In the early 1960s, national propaganda promoted model figures such as Lei Feng as examples of self-sacrifice, discipline, modest living, and devotion to Mao and the collective.

Model campaigns helped shift political culture from post-famine pragmatism toward renewed ideological mobilization.

Dazhai and Agricultural Self-Reliance

The agricultural community of Dazhai was promoted as a model of self-reliance, collective labor, and political determination.

Its national prominence grew in the mid-1960s.

Model emulation encouraged localities to treat political commitment as an important measure of economic performance.

Daqing and Industrial Self-Reliance

The Daqing oil field became an industrial model associated with self-reliance, hard work, political study, and reduced dependence on Soviet expertise.

Its propaganda significance reflected the connection between the Sino-Soviet split and domestic campaigns for technological and ideological independence.

Results of Economic Recovery

By the mid-1960s, agricultural and industrial production had recovered substantially from the Great Leap collapse.

More cautious investment, restored incentives, improved supply, and reduced overexpansion helped stabilize the economy.

Recovery demonstrated the effectiveness of pragmatic adjustments, but it also intensified Mao's fear that material incentives could displace revolutionary politics.

The Recovery Paradox

The recovery created a political paradox.

Policies that helped restore production could be criticized as ideologically dangerous because they relied on expertise, differentiated rewards, household incentives, and administrative regularity.

The better the recovery worked, the more it could reinforce Mao's concern that China was moving away from revolutionary transformation.

Consequences of the Sino-Soviet Split

The split reduced access to Soviet expertise and aid, forced China toward greater technological self-reliance, divided international communist movements, and increased China's strategic isolation.

It also encouraged Beijing to develop independent nuclear capability and cultivate political relationships outside the Soviet bloc.

Over the longer term, hostility toward the Soviet Union would contribute to China's unexpected strategic opening toward the United States in the 1970s.

The Split Was Not Caused by One Issue

The Sino-Soviet split cannot be explained only by Mao's personality, Khrushchev's Secret Speech, nuclear weapons, or national rivalry.

It developed through an interaction of ideology, security interests, economic disagreements, leadership competition, historical grievances, foreign-policy disputes, and conflicting visions of world revolution.

Contemporary China Quarterly analysis emphasized that ideological dispute and concrete diplomatic conflict developed together: “Diplomacy and Revolution: The Dialectics of a Dispute”.

Toward the Cultural Revolution

By 1965, economic recovery had not resolved Mao's political concerns.

The Socialist Education Movement had expanded the search for capitalist tendencies inside the party. Cultural criticism became increasingly political. The PLA and model campaigns promoted stronger Mao-centered ideological discipline.

These developments did not make the Cultural Revolution inevitable, but they created institutions, language, conflicts, and political expectations that helped make the upheaval of 1966 possible.

How Do We Know?

Historians reconstruct this period through CCP documents, economic statistics, provincial records, Soviet and Chinese publications, diplomatic archives, memoirs, Socialist Education Movement materials, educational and cultural records, and international communist-party documents.

Sino-Soviet polemics are unusually rich sources because both governments published long arguments against one another. They must be read as political documents designed to persuade international audiences, not as neutral summaries.

Later Cultural Revolution accusations against Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, Peng Zhen, and others must also be separated from evidence about what those leaders actually advocated during the recovery years.

Think Like a Historian

Did economic recovery and ideological radicalization contradict one another?

Economically, the early 1960s moved away from Great Leap extremism toward incentives, technical expertise, smaller accounting units, and more regular administration.

Politically, Mao increasingly argued that those very tendencies could recreate inequality, privilege, and revisionism.

The period therefore combined economic moderation with growing ideological conflict. That combination is essential for understanding why improved economic performance did not lead to political liberalization.

Historical Significance

The years between the Great Leap and Cultural Revolution transformed both domestic politics and China's international position.

Recovery restored production and demonstrated the practical value of more cautious economic management. Mao's renewed class-struggle theory challenged that pragmatism. The Socialist Education Movement brought political investigation back into rural governance.

Internationally, the Sino-Soviet alliance collapsed into rivalry, forcing China toward greater self-reliance and a more independent foreign policy.

Together, these developments created the ideological and institutional background for the Cultural Revolution.

Key Takeaways

Post-famine recovery reduced Great Leap excesses and restored agricultural and industrial production.

Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yun, Zhou Enlai, and other officials played major roles in pragmatic readjustment, while Mao remained CCP chairman and retained exceptional authority.

The Seven Thousand Cadres Conference reviewed Great Leap failures, but did not remove Mao from political leadership.

At the Tenth Plenum in 1962, Mao renewed emphasis on class struggle.

The Socialist Education Movement and Four Cleanups investigated rural corruption, collective administration, and alleged capitalist tendencies.

By 1965, Mao increasingly defined the political danger as people in authority inside the party taking the capitalist road.

The Sino-Soviet split developed gradually from ideological, security, diplomatic, economic, and leadership disputes.

Khrushchev's de-Stalinization and peaceful-coexistence strategy became major points of disagreement.

The Soviet withdrawal of experts in 1960 damaged Chinese industrial cooperation and became a symbol of the rupture.

The dispute became openly public in the early 1960s and intensified through the 1963 ideological polemics.

China opposed the 1963 Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty and tested its own atomic bomb in 1964.

Khrushchev's fall in 1964 did not repair the alliance.

Domestic fear of Soviet-style revisionism strengthened Mao's campaign against perceived capitalist tendencies inside China.

By 1965, recovery, ideological struggle, cultural conflict, and Sino-Soviet rivalry had created much of the political setting for the Cultural Revolution.

Key Terms

Readjustment: the post-Great Leap economic program that cut overexpansion, restored incentives, and prioritized recovery.

Seven Thousand Cadres Conference: the large 1962 CCP meeting that reviewed the Great Leap crisis and leadership responsibility.

Tenth Plenum: the September 1962 Central Committee meeting at which Mao renewed emphasis on class struggle.

Revisionism: in Maoist usage, alteration of Marxism-Leninism in ways said to abandon revolutionary struggle while preserving socialist language.

Socialist Education Movement: the 1962–1966 campaign to investigate and politically rectify rural and later urban institutions.

Four Cleanups: a major phase of the Socialist Education Movement focused on problems in collective administration and cadre behavior.

Capitalist Roader: a Maoist political category for people in authority accused of directing socialism toward capitalism.

Sino-Soviet Split: the breakdown of the PRC-USSR alliance into ideological, strategic, and geopolitical rivalry.

Peaceful Coexistence: the Soviet strategy emphasizing the possibility of avoiding world war while competing with capitalist powers.

Great Polemic: the public Sino-Soviet ideological dispute of the early 1960s carried through letters, editorials, and theoretical essays.

Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty: the 1963 U.S.-UK-Soviet treaty prohibiting nuclear tests in the atmosphere, outer space, and underwater; China opposed it.

Daqing: an oil-production center promoted as a model of industrial self-reliance and political discipline.

Dazhai: a rural community promoted as a model of collective agricultural self-reliance.

Lei Feng: a soldier promoted in national propaganda as a model of self-sacrifice and devotion to Mao and the collective.

Continue the Story

By 1965, China had largely recovered from the Great Leap famine, but political tensions had intensified rather than disappeared.

Mao believed that Soviet revisionism demonstrated the danger of bureaucratic degeneration inside a socialist state. He increasingly applied that lesson to China, arguing that powerful people inside the CCP itself could take the capitalist road.

Disputes over the Socialist Education Movement, intellectuals, education, cultural works, and party authority converged during 1965–1966.
