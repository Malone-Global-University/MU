Lesson Orientation

The Cultural Revolution was a decade-long political upheaval launched by Mao Zedong in 1966 and conventionally dated through 1976.

It began as an attack on alleged bourgeois, revisionist, and bureaucratic tendencies inside the Chinese Communist Party, schools, cultural institutions, and society. It rapidly expanded into mass political mobilization, Red Guard activism, purges, factional violence, attacks on cultural heritage, disruption of education and administration, and prolonged conflict over who legitimately represented Mao's revolutionary line.

The period was not ten identical years of chaos. The most uncontrolled mass violence was concentrated especially in 1966–1968. Thereafter, the PLA and Revolutionary Committees restored a degree of order, while political campaigns, purges, succession conflict, and ideological mobilization continued until 1976.

Roots of the Cultural Revolution

The Cultural Revolution grew out of conflicts already visible during the post-famine recovery. Mao feared that material incentives, expert management, bureaucratic authority, and pragmatic economic policy could generate a new privileged class inside socialism.

The Sino-Soviet split reinforced this fear. Mao increasingly treated the Soviet Union as evidence that a communist party could become revisionist after a change in leadership. The question became whether similar political tendencies existed inside the CCP itself.

The May 16 Notification

On May 16, 1966, the CCP issued a document that helped define the movement's political targets. It warned that representatives of the bourgeoisie had entered party, state, military, and cultural institutions.

The language widened the campaign from criticism of particular writers and officials into a search for hidden political enemies within the socialist system itself.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — “The Cultural Revolution as a Crisis of Representation” .

The Rise of the Red Guards

Student groups identifying themselves as Red Guards spread rapidly during the summer of 1966. Mao publicly supported the movement and appeared at enormous rallies in Beijing.

Red Guards attacked teachers, officials, cultural institutions, and people accused of revisionism or bad class background. They were never one unified national organization: rival Red Guard factions frequently interpreted Maoist politics differently and sometimes fought one another.

Attacking the Four Olds

Activists were encouraged to attack the so-called Four Olds: old ideas, old culture, old customs, and old habits.

The category was broad and locally interpreted. Campaigns targeted religious sites, temples, books, artworks, genealogies, clothing, private collections, signs, place names, and household objects.

Some cultural heritage was destroyed or damaged, while other materials survived because curators, local residents, officials, or military units protected them.

Struggle Sessions and Political Violence

Public denunciation meetings became a defining practice. Accused people could be forced to confess, wear signs, adopt humiliating postures, or endure verbal and physical abuse.

Violence escalated sharply in many localities in 1966. Beatings, killings, forced suicides, raids, expulsions, and imprisonment affected teachers, intellectuals, officials, religious figures, former landlords and capitalists, and members of rival revolutionary factions.

The Fall of Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping

Liu Shaoqi, president of the People's Republic and previously one of the CCP's most senior leaders, became the most prominent high-level target. He was accused of taking the capitalist road, removed from office, imprisoned under harsh conditions, and died in custody in 1969.

Deng Xiaoping was also purged as a leading representative of pragmatic administration. Deng survived and later returned to political leadership.

The Breakdown of Party and Government Institutions

Existing party committees and government offices were attacked as centers of revisionism. Officials became targets of competing rebel organizations, and ordinary administrative processes were badly disrupted.

Contemporary foreign assessments described severe damage to the party machinery and national leadership even while Mao retained overwhelming symbolic authority.

Contemporary source: U.S. Office of the Historian — 1967 National Intelligence Estimate .

The January Storm in Shanghai

In January 1967, rebel workers and activists challenged Shanghai's existing party and municipal authorities in what became known as the January Storm.

Some radicals advocated a Paris Commune-style institution. The more durable result was the later creation of Revolutionary Committees that combined representatives of mass organizations, experienced cadres, and military personnel.

Revolutionary Committees

Revolutionary Committees became the principal new local governing form from 1967 onward.

Their actual composition differed by locality. Military officers frequently held great influence because the PLA possessed the organizational capacity to restore transport, production, policing, and administrative order.

The Wuhan Incident

In July 1967, conflict in Wuhan exposed serious tensions among local military authorities, rival mass organizations, and central Cultural Revolution leaders.

The confrontation demonstrated that even PLA units disagreed over how the Cultural Revolution should be interpreted and enforced.

Factional Warfare

During 1967 and 1968, rival organizations fought in many provinces and cities. Some conflicts involved firearms, seized arsenals, fortified buildings, and substantial casualties.

Factional alignments could reflect political loyalties, workplace grievances, regional rivalries, generational divisions, and competition over institutional power.

PLA Intervention and the Restoration of Order

As violence threatened transport, factories, and administration, the PLA took an increasingly large political role. Military units disarmed factions, protected strategic facilities, supervised new governing bodies, and helped restore basic order.

This was not a simple restoration of the pre-1966 system. It produced a new political arrangement in which military influence became unusually strong.

Background: Office of the Historian — U.S. assessments of China, 1967–1968 .

The Cleansing of the Class Ranks

From 1968, political purification became more organized. Campaigns targeted alleged spies, traitors, counterrevolutionaries, and people with suspect political histories.

Investigations frequently relied upon confession, denunciation, detention, and coercion, and they produced new waves of persecution even as open Red Guard warfare declined.

The Sent-Down Youth Movement

Millions of urban young people were sent to rural and frontier areas during the late 1960s and 1970s for labor and political reeducation.

The policy also helped disperse Red Guard organizations and reduce pressure on cities. For many participants, rustication meant interrupted education, family separation, difficult work, and years of uncertain career prospects.

Education and Intellectual Life

Universities and many schools experienced prolonged disruption. Entrance examinations were suspended or transformed, faculty were criticized or reassigned, and political credentials became more important in admissions and promotion.

Schools later reopened with curricula emphasizing politics, labor, practical experience, and reduced deference to academic specialization.

Barefoot Doctors and Rural Services

The period also saw programs that expanded basic rural health provision. Village-level workers often called barefoot doctors provided preventive care, simple treatment, health education, and referrals.

The program did not erase inequalities in medical expertise, but it became one of the most durable examples of low-cost rural public-health expansion during the Mao era.

The Ninth Party Congress

The Ninth CCP Congress in April 1969 reconstructed national party leadership after years of upheaval.

Mao's authority was reaffirmed, Lin Biao was formally identified as his successor, and military leaders occupied an unusually prominent place in the new hierarchy.

Lin Biao and the Politics of Succession

Lin Biao had become defense minister after Peng Dehuai's fall and was closely associated with Mao-centered political education in the PLA.

His elevation as designated successor made later disagreements over military influence, state offices, and succession especially consequential.

The Cult of Mao

Mao's political authority combined formal leadership, revolutionary prestige, propaganda, and mass symbolism.

Quotations from Mao circulated on an enormous scale, and public recitation, badges, posters, songs, portraits, and loyalty rituals became features of everyday political life.

The 1969 Sino-Soviet Border Conflict

Armed clashes occurred along the Sino-Soviet border in 1969, including fighting around Zhenbao/Damansky Island.

The crisis reinforced Chinese fears of Soviet military pressure and contributed to the strategic logic of improving relations with the United States.

The Death of Lin Biao

In September 1971, Lin Biao died in an airplane crash in Mongolia.

Chinese official accounts later accused Lin and associates of plotting against Mao and attempting to flee after a failed coup plan. Important details remain debated because the evidence is incomplete and was shaped by later political narratives.

What is firmly established is that Mao's designated successor died in the crash, producing a major leadership shock.

Contemporary documentation: U.S. Office of the Historian — Lin Biao developments .

Rebuilding Party and State Institutions

During the early 1970s, party committees, ministries, schools, universities, and administrative institutions were gradually rebuilt.

Many experienced cadres returned. Reconstruction did not end Cultural Revolution politics; restored institutions became new arenas for conflict over how much of the pre-1966 administrative order should return.

The PRC Takes China's UN Seat

In October 1971, United Nations voting changed the representation of China. The PRC delegation took China's seat, including the permanent Security Council seat, while the ROC delegation left the organization.

The change was a major diplomatic victory for Beijing.

Background: U.S. Office of the Historian — rapprochement with China .

Nixon's 1972 Visit and the Shanghai Communiqué

U.S. President Richard Nixon visited the PRC from February 21 to 28, 1972. He met Mao Zedong and held extensive talks with Zhou Enlai.

The Shanghai Communiqué stated both areas of agreement and continuing disagreement. The opening reflected strategic concern about the Soviet Union as well as a desire to reduce long-term U.S.-China hostility.

Primary source: Shanghai Communiqué, February 27, 1972 .

Deng Xiaoping Returns

Deng Xiaoping gradually returned to senior positions during the early 1970s. By 1975, he played a major role in efforts to restore railway performance, industrial management, science, education, and administrative discipline.

His return reflected the government's need for experienced officials, but it also reopened ideological conflict with radical Cultural Revolution leaders.

The Four Modernizations

Zhou Enlai emphasized modernization of agriculture, industry, national defense, and science and technology.

At the Fourth National People's Congress in January 1975, these goals became particularly prominent. They later became central to post-Mao reform, although their meaning in 1975 remained embedded in Mao-era politics.

The Gang of Four

The label Gang of Four refers to Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan, and Wang Hongwen.

They were prominent supporters of Cultural Revolution radicalism and held important positions in propaganda, culture, and national politics.

The label became standard after their arrest in 1976, when the new leadership attributed many Cultural Revolution abuses to them.

The Death of Zhou Enlai

Zhou Enlai died on January 8, 1976. Public mourning was widespread.

His death intensified the leadership succession problem and sharpened conflict between officials associated with administrative restoration and radical leaders defending Cultural Revolution politics.

The 1976 Tiananmen Incident

During the Qingming festival, large crowds gathered at Tiananmen Square to mourn Zhou Enlai and leave wreaths, poems, and political messages.

Many expressions also criticized radical Cultural Revolution politics. Authorities removed memorial objects and suppressed demonstrations on April 5.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — Qingming demonstrations of 1976 .

Deng's Second Purge

Deng was blamed for the political atmosphere surrounding the April demonstrations and was again removed from his leading posts in 1976.

His second fall showed that the leadership struggle remained unresolved only months before Mao's death.

Hua Guofeng and the Succession Problem

Hua Guofeng rose rapidly during the final Mao years and became premier after Zhou Enlai's death.

Mao assigned Hua increasing leadership responsibility. Hua's position placed him between veteran cadres seeking institutional restoration and radical leaders associated with the Cultural Revolution.

The Tangshan Earthquake

On July 28, 1976, a devastating earthquake struck Tangshan and surrounding areas. It killed very large numbers of people and destroyed much of the city.

The disaster occurred during an extraordinary year already marked by leadership deaths, factional struggle, public protest, and uncertainty about succession.

Mao Zedong's Death

Mao Zedong died on September 9, 1976.

His death ended the personal leadership that had dominated CCP and PRC politics for decades and created an immediate succession crisis.

Scholarly record: The China Quarterly — Hua Guofeng and the Arrest of the Gang of Four .

The Arrest of the Gang of Four

On October 6, 1976, Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan, and Wang Hongwen were arrested in a leadership move organized by Hua Guofeng, Ye Jianying, Wang Dongxing, and allies.

Their arrest removed the most prominent remaining radical leadership faction and became a decisive marker for the end of the Cultural Revolution.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — “Dethroning the Mao-era Elite” .

When Did the Cultural Revolution End?

The Cultural Revolution is conventionally dated from 1966 to 1976.

Mao's death removed the movement's central political authority, while the October 6 arrest of the Gang of Four destroyed the principal radical leadership faction. The two events together mark the closing of the Mao-era Cultural Revolution.

Death, Injury, and Persecution

The Cultural Revolution produced large-scale killing, physical injury, imprisonment, forced displacement, suicide, and political persecution.

There is no single universally accepted nationwide death toll. Archival and local studies produce different totals depending on region, date range, category, and treatment of indirect deaths.

A responsible historical account therefore emphasizes the well-documented breadth of violence without presenting one disputed national number as settled fact.

Minority Regions, Religion, and Cultural Destruction

Campaigns in minority regions could combine Cultural Revolution ideology with local ethnic, religious, and security conflicts.

Religious institutions and cultural practices were attacked in Tibet, Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, and elsewhere. Temples, monasteries, churches, shrines, texts, and ritual objects experienced closures, confiscation, or destruction.

Economy, Science, and Technology

The early Cultural Revolution severely disrupted production, management, education, research, and transportation.

The decade was not one continuous economic collapse: industrial administration recovered unevenly after 1968, and strategic programs such as nuclear weapons and missiles continued under state protection.

Arts and Model Works

Cultural production was heavily restricted during the radical phase. A small set of approved revolutionary operas, ballets, films, and musical works received extraordinary national prominence.

These model works, often associated with Jiang Qing's cultural policies, combined selected traditional forms with modern staging and revolutionary themes.

Everyday Life and Family Politics

The Cultural Revolution entered daily life through political study, workplace meetings, quotations, badges, wall posters, loyalty rituals, factional labels, personnel files, and family-background classifications.

Political campaigns could divide families. A relative's class background or accusation might affect education, work, or marriage prospects. At the same time, families often quietly protected one another and preserved documents or possessions.

The 1981 Historical Resolution

After Mao's death and Deng Xiaoping's return, the CCP formally reassessed the Mao era.

The 1981 historical resolution characterized the Cultural Revolution as a grave error initiated by Mao and exploited by radical groups while preserving Mao's larger revolutionary status.

The resolution remains influential but should itself be read as a political document produced during consolidation of the post-Mao leadership.

Why Did Mao Launch the Cultural Revolution?

No single motive is sufficient.

Factors included Mao's fear of Soviet-style revisionism, concern about bureaucratic privilege, conflict with pragmatic recovery leaders, belief in continuing class struggle under socialism, desire to revive revolutionary participation, and a struggle over the direction and control of the party.

Ideology and personal power were intertwined rather than mutually exclusive explanations.

Why Did It Spread So Rapidly?

Mao's endorsement gave legitimacy to attacks on established authority. Students and workers also brought local grievances into the movement.

Broad political labels allowed institutional, generational, workplace, and personal conflicts to be reframed as revolutionary struggles, while weakened institutional constraints enabled competing groups to claim direct loyalty to Mao.

Why Did It End?

By the mid-1970s, repeated campaigns, factional conflict, aging leadership, and the practical need for stable administration had exhausted the political system.

Mao's death removed the unique authority that radical factions invoked. The swift arrest of the Gang of Four showed that senior party and military leaders could organize against continued radical rule.

How Do We Know?

Historians use party documents, Red Guard newspapers, wall posters, local chronicles, provincial archives, personnel files, photographs, diplomatic records, memoirs, oral histories, court records, and demographic research.

Political labels such as capitalist roader, counterrevolutionary, rebel, revisionist, or black element should be treated as historical political categories, not as neutral descriptions of the people who received them.

Regional studies are especially important because violence and political chronology varied substantially across China.

Think Like a Historian

Consider the question:

Was the Cultural Revolution a mass rebellion against bureaucracy, a purge directed from above, or a struggle among political factions?

Evidence supports parts of all three interpretations.

Mao initiated and repeatedly redirected the movement. Millions of students and workers nevertheless acted with genuine agency and brought local grievances into the conflict. Senior officials and military commanders also competed for power.

The strongest interpretation therefore treats the Cultural Revolution as an interaction between central political struggle and decentralized mass mobilization whose effects often escaped the control of those who launched it.

Historical Significance

  • Established Mao-centered ideological politics at extraordinary intensity.
  • Destroyed or severely weakened many party, government, educational, and cultural institutions.
  • Purged senior leaders including Liu Shaoqi and repeatedly displaced Deng Xiaoping.
  • Mobilized millions of students, workers, soldiers, cadres, and rural residents.
  • Produced widespread violence, persecution, imprisonment, displacement, and social trauma.
  • Expanded the political role of the PLA and created Revolutionary Committees.
  • Disrupted education and sent millions of urban youth to rural and frontier regions.
  • Changed China's international position through the Sino-Soviet conflict, UN representation change, and opening to the United States.
  • Left the party-state weakened and divided at Mao's death.
  • Created the political opening for post-Mao rehabilitation, reconstruction, and reform.

Continue the Story

The arrest of the Gang of Four ended the principal radical leadership faction, but it did not immediately determine what would replace Mao-era politics.

Hua Guofeng initially succeeded Mao and defended much of Mao's legacy. Veteran officials returned, and Deng Xiaoping was rehabilitated again in 1977.

The central argument shifted toward how China should rebuild its economy, restore institutions, evaluate Mao's legacy, and define socialism after a decade of political upheaval.
