Lesson Orientation

Reform and global integration changed far more than China's economic institutions. They transformed where people lived, how families were organized, what kinds of work people performed, how education and health care were financed, how citizens communicated, and how environmental costs were distributed.

This lesson follows those social consequences across the reform era, with particular emphasis on the 1990s and 2000s. It examines urbanization, migration, the hukou system, housing, demographic change, family planning, aging, gender, higher education, consumer culture, the internet, public health, environmental degradation, civil society, and the emergence of new social classes.

The result was not one uniform national experience. Coastal and inland regions, registered urban residents and migrants, men and women, older state workers and younger private-sector employees, and wealthy and poor households often experienced reform very differently.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, the learner should be able to explain China's rapid urbanization; describe the hukou system and migrant labor; explain housing commercialization and the rise of homeownership; describe the one-child policy and its uneven implementation; explain fertility decline and population aging; identify changing gender and family patterns; describe the expansion of higher education; explain the rise of consumer culture and a new urban middle class; trace the spread of the internet and mobile communications; explain the social significance of SARS and later health reforms; describe the environmental costs of industrialization; and assess how social change created new inequalities, opportunities, and forms of public participation.

From a Rural Society Toward an Urban Society

For most of the twentieth century, China remained predominantly rural.

Reform accelerated the movement of population, investment, industry, education, and services toward cities and urbanizing towns.

By the early 2010s, China had crossed the threshold at which more than half of the population lived in urban areas, marking a major demographic transformation.

The Speed of Urbanization

Urbanization accelerated especially after the 1990s as export manufacturing, construction, services, real-estate development, universities, and government investment expanded.

Cambridge research on the Hu–Wen period notes that the urban share rose from about 39 percent in 2002 to 53 percent in 2012.

The pace of change meant that urban growth was not simply demographic; it involved massive construction of roads, housing, transit, industrial zones, schools, hospitals, and commercial districts.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — urbanization, migration, aging, and inequality during the Hu–Wen era.

Megacities and Urban Regions

Cities such as Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Chongqing, Tianjin, Chengdu, and Wuhan expanded dramatically.

Urban development also increasingly took the form of metropolitan regions and city clusters rather than isolated city centers.

The Pearl River Delta and Yangtze River Delta became especially important examples of densely connected urban-industrial regions.

Urban Expansion and Land Conversion

Urban expansion depended heavily on conversion of collectively owned rural land into state urban land.

Local governments often played a central role in land acquisition, infrastructure construction, and development finance.

This created powerful incentives for urban growth but also generated disputes over compensation, displacement, and the distribution of gains from rising land values.

The Hukou System

China's household-registration system continued to classify people by place of registration and historically by agricultural or non-agricultural status.

Reform reduced direct barriers to physical movement, but hukou status continued to shape access to public education, subsidized housing, health care, pensions, and other local services.

The result was a society in which millions could live and work in cities without possessing the same social rights as locally registered urban residents.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — the hukou system in the 2000s.

Mass Rural-to-Urban Migration

Market reform created unprecedented internal migration.

Rural workers moved toward export factories, construction sites, restaurants, domestic service, transport, retail, and other urban jobs.

By 2012, research cited in The China Quarterly counted approximately 263 million migrant workers, illustrating how central migration had become to China's labor force and urban economy.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — migrant workers and social transformation.

Migrant Workers and the Urban Economy

Migrant labor powered construction, manufacturing, logistics, restaurants, domestic work, retail, and municipal services.

Employers benefited from a large mobile labor force, while cities benefited from workers whose education and childhood costs had often been borne by rural families and localities.

This arrangement helped industrialization but transferred many social costs back to migrants and their home communities.

Dormitory and Factory Life

In major export zones, many young migrants lived in factory dormitories tied directly to employment.

Long hours, managerial discipline, crowded housing, and separation from family became characteristic experiences for parts of the manufacturing workforce.

These labor systems were central to China's emergence as a global manufacturing center.

Left-Behind Children and Families

Because migrant parents often could not easily transfer urban welfare and school access to children, many families remained geographically divided.

Children could remain in rural communities with grandparents or one parent while working-age adults migrated.

Migration therefore increased household income for many families while also creating new emotional, educational, and caregiving burdens.

Hukou Reform Without Full Abolition

Local governments gradually relaxed registration rules, especially in smaller cities and towns.

Large and economically attractive cities generally maintained more restrictive systems because local hukou carried valuable welfare and education benefits.

Reform therefore changed the operation of hukou without eliminating its role in social stratification.

The End of Welfare Housing Allocation

Urban housing underwent a fundamental transformation during the reform era.

Under the planned economy, many urban workers received heavily subsidized rental housing through state work units.

In 1998, the government ended the nationwide welfare-housing allocation system and pushed urban housing toward commercialization and household ownership.

Scholarly reference: Journal of East Asian Studies — China's 1998 housing reform and homeownership.

The Rise of Homeownership

Urban employees were often allowed to purchase former public housing at discounted prices.

Private developers and state-linked development firms then expanded commercial housing on a vast scale.

Research cited by Cambridge notes that urban family homeownership rose dramatically during the 1990s, making housing one of the most important forms of household wealth.

The Property Boom

Rapid urbanization, credit expansion, local-government land finance, and household demand contributed to a long property boom.

Homeownership created new wealth for many established urban families but also increased the cost of entry for younger households and migrants.

Housing prices therefore became central to class formation, marriage expectations, household savings, and intergenerational inequality.

New Neighborhoods and Gated Communities

Commercial housing produced new residential forms, including privately managed apartment compounds and gated communities.

Homeowners' committees, property-management companies, and neighborhood disputes created new arenas of urban social organization.

These communities differed sharply from older work-unit neighborhoods in which employment and residence had been institutionally linked.

The Decline of the Danwei as a Total Social Institution

The urban work unit had historically linked employment to housing, health care, pensions, schooling, political supervision, and social life.

SOE reform and housing commercialization weakened this integrated system.

Urban residents increasingly depended on labor markets, commercial housing, insurance systems, and municipal institutions rather than a single employer for most aspects of life.

The Consumer Revolution

Rising incomes and commercialization transformed consumption.

Urban households purchased televisions, refrigerators, washing machines, air conditioners, computers, mobile phones, cars, branded clothing, restaurant meals, tourism, and entertainment on a scale unimaginable in the early reform era.

Cambridge research describes this as a consumer revolution that brought both expanded choice and sharper social differentiation.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — urban consumer culture.

The Emergence of an Urban Middle Class

Professionals, managers, entrepreneurs, skilled technicians, civil servants, property owners, and successful private-sector employees formed new middle-income groups.

Homeownership, education, white-collar employment, cars, travel, and spending on children became important markers of status.

This new middle class emerged alongside continued poverty, migrant marginality, and large regional differences rather than replacing them.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge University Press — formation of China's urban middle class.

Rising Income Inequality

Market reform widened income differences among regions, occupations, ownership sectors, and households.

Cambridge research estimates that the national Gini coefficient rose from roughly 0.44 in 2000 to about 0.49 in 2008 before easing somewhat by 2012.

Inequality coexisted with broad improvements in average living standards, making reform simultaneously a history of mass advancement and sharper stratification.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — social policy and income inequality.

The Rural-Urban Divide

Urban households generally gained earlier and more complete access to pensions, hospitals, universities, infrastructure, and high-paying formal employment.

Rural households benefited from agricultural reform and migration income but often faced weaker public services and fewer social protections.

Reducing the rural-urban gap became a major policy concern during the 2000s.

Regional Inequality and Western Development

Coastal provinces benefited disproportionately from foreign investment and export manufacturing.

Beginning around 2000, the central government promoted a Western Development strategy intended to improve infrastructure and investment in inland and western regions.

The program expanded roads, railways, energy projects, and transfers but did not eliminate regional inequality.

Population Policy and Modernization

Population policy became closely tied to the post-Mao modernization project.

Leaders feared that rapid population growth would absorb gains in food supply, education, housing, and income.

Beginning in 1979–1980, the state promoted a very low-fertility regime widely known as the one-child policy.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — development of the one-child policy.

The “One-Child Policy” Was Not One Uniform Rule

The phrase one-child policy is useful shorthand but can obscure regional and social variation.

Rules changed over time, and many rural families, ethnic minorities, and households meeting particular conditions were permitted additional births.

Implementation also differed sharply among provinces and local governments.

Coercion and Family-Planning Enforcement

Family planning relied on propaganda, incentives, fines, workplace pressure, administrative monitoring, and in some periods and localities coercive reproductive interventions.

Historical research documents increasingly compulsory methods during the early years of the program.

A balanced account should distinguish national goals from local implementation while acknowledging the documented coercive dimensions of enforcement.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — population policy and coercive enforcement, 1978–1983.

Fertility Decline

China experienced a major decline in fertility over the late twentieth century.

Family-planning policy contributed to that decline, but urbanization, women's education, later marriage, rising child-rearing costs, housing constraints, and changing family preferences also mattered.

By the 2000s, low fertility was increasingly embedded in social and economic behavior rather than produced only through direct administrative pressure.

Son Preference and the Sex Ratio

Long-standing son preference interacted with fertility restrictions and access to prenatal sex identification to produce distorted sex ratios at birth in many regions.

The imbalance created long-term demographic consequences for marriage markets and family formation.

Government campaigns later sought to discourage sex-selective abortion and discrimination against girls.

The Only-Child Generation

Millions of urban children grew up without siblings under the low-fertility regime.

Families often concentrated educational spending, housing resources, and parental expectations on a single child.

Research on reform-era parenting describes increasingly intensive investment in children's education and perceived 'quality,' particularly among aspiring middle-class households.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — only-child parenting, class, and education.

Population Aging

Low fertility and longer life expectancy accelerated population aging.

By the 2010 census, people aged sixty and above accounted for 13.3 percent of the population, according to figures cited in Cambridge research.

Aging created pressure on pensions, health services, family caregiving, and the labor force.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — aging in the 2000s.

The “4-2-1” Family Structure

Public discussion increasingly focused on families in which one adult child might eventually share responsibility for supporting two parents and four grandparents.

The actual experience varied greatly, but the '4-2-1' image captured anxiety about low fertility and aging.

It also highlighted the tension between traditional expectations of family eldercare and a more mobile urban labor market.

Women, Work, and Market Reform

Market reform expanded opportunities for women in higher education, entrepreneurship, professional work, and private enterprise.

It also weakened some employment guarantees and exposed women to new forms of labor-market discrimination.

Employers sometimes treated maternity and childcare as private costs, while commercial culture reintroduced or intensified gendered expectations about appearance and family roles.

Marriage and Family Change

Marriage remained nearly universal for much of the reform era, but people generally married later than earlier generations.

Urban housing costs, education, employment competition, migration, and individual expectations increasingly shaped marriage decisions.

Divorce became more common as legal procedures and social attitudes changed.

Private Life and Changing Social Norms

Reform expanded the social space for personal relationships, fashion, dating, entertainment, and private consumption.

Commercial media exposed audiences to new ideas about romance, sexuality, lifestyle, and self-expression.

These changes did not eliminate state regulation or conservative social expectations, but everyday life became less completely organized around the work unit and collective political campaigns.

The Expansion of Higher Education

China began a major expansion of higher education in 1999.

Cambridge research notes that new college admissions increased from roughly 1.1 million in 1999 to about 6.4 million a decade later.

The transition from elite to mass higher education reshaped the labor market, family spending, youth expectations, and competition for credentials.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — higher education expansion after 1999.

Educational Expansion and Unequal Access

Mass higher education increased opportunity but did not erase inequality.

Urban students, families with stronger schools, and households able to finance tutoring and relocation often retained advantages.

Research finds that rural youth from poorer areas remained much less likely to enter university and especially elite institutions.

The Gaokao and Educational Competition

The national university entrance examination remained a major gateway to higher education.

Because educational credentials became increasingly important in labor markets, preparation for the gaokao intensified family investment in schooling and tutoring.

Examination success could provide mobility, but competition also generated substantial pressure on students and parents.

Graduates and a Changing Labor Market

University expansion produced millions of new graduates seeking professional employment.

The older model in which the state assigned jobs weakened, and graduates increasingly searched through labor markets.

This created new opportunities but also underemployment, credential inflation, and anxiety about the value of degrees.

The Arrival of the Internet

Internet access began on a very small scale in the 1990s and expanded rapidly during the 2000s.

Cambridge research notes growth from roughly one hundred thousand Chinese internet users in 1996 to more than 120 million by 2006.

The internet introduced new spaces for news, entertainment, commerce, personal expression, public discussion, and social organization.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge University Press — China's media boom and cyber culture.

Mobile Phones and Everyday Connectivity

Mobile telephones spread even faster than personal computers in many parts of China.

Workers, migrants, entrepreneurs, and families could coordinate travel, employment, remittances, business, and personal relationships across long distances.

Mobile communication reduced some of the social isolation created by migration while accelerating the pace of commercial and urban life.

The Beginnings of E-Commerce

Internet growth supported new technology firms, online portals, messaging services, digital marketplaces, and later large e-commerce platforms.

Consumers increasingly used the internet to compare products, communicate with sellers, and participate in national markets.

Digital commerce would become one of the defining features of Chinese economic life in the following decade.

An Online Public Sphere

Internet forums, blogs, and later social-media platforms provided new ways to discuss scandals, disasters, corruption, public policy, culture, and local grievances.

Online communication could amplify issues that received limited treatment in traditional media.

Researchers also found that civic associations used the internet for publicity, networking, and information exchange.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — civic associations and the internet.

Internet Growth and Information Control

The expansion of digital communication was accompanied by increasingly sophisticated censorship and surveillance.

Internet companies were required to comply with content restrictions, and access to some foreign sites was blocked.

Thus digital technology widened public communication while also creating new instruments of state information control.

The 2003 SARS Crisis

Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome emerged as a major public-health crisis in 2002–2003.

Early problems in reporting and surveillance contributed to domestic and international criticism.

After the crisis became publicly acknowledged at the highest level, authorities implemented aggressive measures including surveillance, quarantine, hospital controls, public information, and large-scale mobilization.

What SARS Revealed

SARS demonstrated the dangers of delayed information and weak disease-surveillance systems in a highly mobile society.

WHO concluded in 2003 that the outbreak exposed substantial weaknesses in China's disease-surveillance system while also showing that strong political commitment and public-health measures could rapidly improve control.

The crisis contributed to later investment in public-health monitoring and emergency response.

Primary reference: World Health Organization — lessons from the 2003 SARS outbreak in China.

Health Care After Market Reform

The collapse of communes and restructuring of work units weakened older systems of collective health financing.

Out-of-pocket costs rose in many areas, and access to quality care became strongly shaped by income and location.

By the 2000s, rebuilding social health protection became a major national policy priority.

The New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme

Beginning in the early 2000s, the government rebuilt rural medical-insurance coverage through the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme.

The program differed from the Mao-era commune system because it relied heavily on public subsidies and operated in a marketized health environment.

WHO research describes the new scheme as part of a broader effort to restore rural financial protection after the older collective system had collapsed.

Reference: World Health Organization — health-care financing in rural China.

The 2009 Health-Care Reform

In 2009, China launched a major health-system reform intended to build broad basic medical coverage and improve affordability.

By 2012, WHO reported that the three main insurance schemes covered about 95 percent of the rural and urban population.

Insurance expansion improved financial access while major challenges remained in cost, quality, hospital incentives, and regional inequality.

Reference: World Health Organization — China's health-care reform progress by 2012.

From Infectious Disease to Chronic Disease

As incomes rose and life expectancy increased, China's disease burden shifted increasingly toward chronic illnesses such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, and respiratory disease.

Urban lifestyles, smoking, air pollution, changing diets, and aging all contributed.

Public health therefore had to manage both infectious-disease threats and the long-term consequences of modernization.

The Environmental Cost of Rapid Growth

Industrialization and urbanization imposed severe environmental costs.

Coal combustion, factory emissions, vehicle growth, untreated wastewater, mining, chemical production, and construction contributed to air, water, and soil pollution.

A 2007 World Bank study described pollution as imposing large economic and health costs after decades of rapid growth.

Reference: World Bank — Cost of Pollution in China.

Air Pollution

Coal remained central to China's energy system, while electricity generation, steel, cement, manufacturing, and motor vehicles expanded rapidly.

Many cities experienced severe particulate and sulfur pollution.

Air quality became both a health issue and a visible symbol of the environmental consequences of growth.

Water Pollution and Scarcity

Industrial discharge, agricultural runoff, municipal wastewater, and uneven treatment infrastructure damaged rivers, lakes, and groundwater.

Northern China also faced severe water scarcity.

Large engineering and conservation projects attempted to manage these pressures, but environmental burdens remained geographically unequal.

Environmental Governance Changes

China had environmental laws and agencies before the 2000s, but local implementation was often weakened by growth incentives.

Under the Eleventh Five-Year Plan for 2006–2010, the central government introduced binding targets for pollutants such as sulfur dioxide and chemical oxygen demand.

This represented a shift toward integrating environmental performance more directly into local-government evaluation.

Scholarly reference: Cambridge University Press — changing environmental governance and binding targets.

Energy, Climate, and Global Responsibility

China's industrial rise made it a major consumer of energy and a central participant in global climate politics.

Policymakers faced the challenge of sustaining development while reducing energy intensity and expanding non-fossil energy.

Climate policy increasingly connected domestic pollution control with international diplomacy.

Environmental Protest

Pollution also generated local protest.

Residents challenged chemical plants, waste facilities, polluted rivers, mining projects, and other perceived threats to health and property.

Research shows that environmental mobilization depended on local political conditions, framing, community relationships, and the source of pollution.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — pollution and environmental protest.

NGOs and New Forms of Social Organization

Market reform weakened some older state-organized social structures while creating room for charities, professional associations, environmental groups, foundations, volunteer organizations, and informal networks.

These organizations operated under regulatory constraints and often depended on cooperation with government agencies.

Their growth nevertheless broadened the number of institutions through which citizens could address social problems.

The 2008 Wenchuan Earthquake

On May 12, 2008, a devastating earthquake struck Wenchuan and surrounding areas of Sichuan.

The disaster produced enormous loss of life and displacement and prompted a massive state relief operation.

It also generated an extraordinary wave of private donations, volunteerism, media attention, and participation by grassroots organizations.

Earthquake Relief and Civil Society

Research on the Wenchuan response found that participation in relief strengthened the capacity, visibility, and government relationships of some civil-society organizations.

At the same time, regulatory and trust limitations constrained longer-term independent development.

The earthquake therefore revealed both the possibilities and boundaries of organized civic action.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — civil society after the Wenchuan earthquake.

The 2008 Beijing Olympics

The Beijing Olympic Games symbolized China's transformed international position, new infrastructure, urban redevelopment, and growing confidence.

Massive investment reshaped parts of Beijing and showcased modern architecture, transit, telecommunications, and organizational capacity.

The Games also drew international attention to questions of displacement, censorship, protest, and political rights.

The Expansion of Social Policy in the 2000s

During the 2000s, the leadership increased spending and policy attention on rural health, pensions, minimum-income support, education, and poverty reduction.

These initiatives partly responded to inequalities created by market reform and the erosion of older work-unit and commune welfare institutions.

The shift did not eliminate inequality but represented a broader state effort to rebuild social protection.

Abolition of the Agricultural Tax

China abolished the centuries-old agricultural tax nationwide in the mid-2000s after earlier rural tax-and-fee reforms.

The change reduced one important fiscal burden on farmers and symbolized a shift toward greater central support for rural households.

It formed part of the broader policy effort to address rural discontent and the urban-rural divide.

Local Protest and “Mass Incidents”

Rapid development produced large numbers of localized disputes over land, labor, pollution, corruption, unpaid wages, and administrative abuse.

Chinese official discourse often grouped collective protests and disturbances under the category of 'mass incidents.'

Research cited in The China Quarterly indicates that reported incidents rose sharply during the 2000s, showing that social stability coexisted with frequent local contention.

Scholarly reference: The China Quarterly — social inequality and mass incidents.

A More Stratified Society

By the early twenty-first century, social categories based primarily on revolutionary class labels had given way to a much more complex hierarchy of income, education, occupation, property, migration status, and political connections.

Private entrepreneurs, professionals, white-collar workers, migrant laborers, laid-off industrial workers, farmers, government employees, and wealthy property owners occupied different positions in the reform economy.

This new stratification altered both everyday expectations and the political challenges facing the state.

Entrepreneurs and the Communist Party

The rise of private capital raised the question of whether entrepreneurs could be incorporated into the CCP.

Under Jiang Zemin's 'Three Represents,' the party broadened its ideological justification for admitting people associated with advanced productive forces, including private entrepreneurs.

The change illustrated the party's adaptation to a social structure produced by its own market reforms.

Youth Culture

Younger Chinese increasingly grew up in a society shaped by commercial media, examinations, consumer brands, the internet, travel, popular music, and global entertainment.

Their experiences differed sharply from generations socialized during Mao-era political campaigns.

Yet education pressure, employment competition, housing costs, and family expectations also created new forms of anxiety.

Religion and Spiritual Life

Religious practice revived in many regions during the reform era.

Buddhist temples, Daoist sites, churches, mosques, popular religious practices, qigong movements, and other forms of spirituality attracted participants.

The state permitted officially recognized religious institutions while regulating organization, clergy, publication, and activity outside approved structures.

Television, Advertising, and Mass Media

Television became a nearly universal medium and increasingly commercialized through advertising, entertainment, sports, drama, and consumer programming.

Satellite television, imported media, music videos, and later the internet diversified the cultural environment.

State ownership and censorship remained important, producing a media system that mixed commercial incentives with political control.

Automobility

Private automobile ownership expanded rapidly during the 2000s.

Cars increased personal mobility and supported suburban growth, road building, tourism, and consumer industries.

They also contributed to congestion, air pollution, petroleum demand, and changing patterns of urban land use.

Railways and Mobility

Rail investment connected cities, migrant workers, students, tourists, and regional economies.

By the end of the 2000s, China had begun building a major high-speed rail network.

Faster mobility reinforced the emergence of large urban regions and national labor and consumer markets.

Global Integration in Everyday Life

WTO membership, overseas study, tourism, multinational firms, foreign brands, global media, and international sporting and cultural events made external connections increasingly ordinary.

Globalization was experienced not only through trade statistics but through jobs, clothing, food, language study, education, travel, entertainment, and digital communication.

At the same time, public debate increasingly asked how China should preserve cultural identity and national autonomy in a globalized world.

Social Change Was Uneven

There was no single Chinese reform-era lifestyle.

A migrant worker in Dongguan, a university student in Beijing, a farmer in Gansu, a homeowner in Shanghai, a laid-off worker in the northeast, and an entrepreneur in Zhejiang occupied different social worlds.

National averages are useful, but regional, class, gender, generational, and hukou differences are essential to understanding reform-era society.

How Do We Know?

Historians and social scientists study these changes through national censuses, household surveys, labor and migration data, health statistics, environmental monitoring, ethnography, interviews, urban planning records, internet archives, media, court cases, demographic research, and local-government documents.

Administrative categories such as migrant, urban, rural, hukou status, unemployment, middle class, and private enterprise changed over time, so statistics must be interpreted carefully.

Social history is especially important because the consequences of reform cannot be reconstructed from GDP growth or policy documents alone.

Think Like a Historian

Did reform make Chinese society freer or more controlled?

Different forms of change moved in different directions.

People gained substantially more freedom in employment, migration, consumption, education, housing, travel, personal relationships, and access to information than under the Mao-era command system.

At the same time, hukou restrictions, workplace exploitation, family-planning enforcement, censorship, political limits, and unequal access to public services remained powerful.

The strongest interpretation therefore distinguishes among economic choice, social autonomy, legal rights, and political power rather than treating 'freedom' as one single measure.

Historical Significance

The social transformations of the reform era remade China as profoundly as market reform itself.

Urbanization and migration created new cities and labor systems; housing reform created a property-owning urban population; low fertility and longer lives accelerated aging; higher education expanded dramatically; and internet and mobile technology changed communication and commerce.

At the same time, inequality, migrant exclusion, pollution, health costs, educational competition, and demographic pressures became major policy challenges.

By the early 2010s, China had become an urbanizing, digitally connected, increasingly middle-income society whose social structure differed fundamentally from that of the Mao era.

Key Takeaways

Rapid urbanization transformed China's population geography and built vast new metropolitan regions.

The hukou system became more flexible but continued to shape unequal access to urban public services.

Rural-to-urban migrants became essential to manufacturing, construction, services, and city growth.

The 1998 housing reform accelerated commercialization and homeownership while weakening the older work-unit housing system.

Property ownership became a major source of household wealth and inequality.

The one-child policy varied across time, locality, ethnicity, and rural-urban status and included documented coercive enforcement.

Fertility decline reflected both state policy and broader social changes such as urbanization, education, and rising child-rearing costs.

Low fertility and longer life expectancy accelerated population aging.

Higher education expanded dramatically after 1999 but retained important rural-urban and class inequalities.

Consumer culture and an urban middle class expanded alongside growing income inequality.

Internet users increased rapidly from the late 1990s through the 2000s, widening communication while producing new systems of censorship and surveillance.

The 2003 SARS outbreak exposed weaknesses in public-health reporting and surveillance and contributed to later institutional reform.

China rebuilt health-insurance coverage during the 2000s and launched a major health reform in 2009.

Industrialization imposed severe air, water, and ecological costs, leading to stronger environmental policy during the 2000s.

The 2008 Wenchuan earthquake produced both massive state mobilization and unusually visible volunteer and NGO participation.

Reform-era society became more stratified by property, education, occupation, hukou, region, and political connections.

By the early 2010s, social transformation had created both broader personal opportunities and major new governance challenges.

Key Terms

Urbanization: the increasing concentration of population and economic activity in cities and urban regions.

Hukou: household-registration system linking legal residence status to place and historically to rural or urban classification.

Migrant Worker: worker living and working outside the locality of household registration, often without full local welfare rights.

Floating Population: administrative term for people living away from their registered place of residence.

Danwei: urban work unit that historically linked employment with housing, welfare, health care, and social administration.

Housing Reform: transition from subsidized work-unit housing toward commercial housing and household ownership, especially after 1998.

One-Child Policy: shorthand for the low-fertility family-planning regime introduced around 1979–1980, with substantial variation in rules and enforcement.

Population Aging: increase in the share of older people caused by low fertility and longer life expectancy.

Gaokao: national college entrance examination central to access to higher education.

Middle Class: diverse urban social groups associated with professional employment, education, property ownership, and higher consumption.

SARS: Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome, whose 2003 outbreak exposed major public-health and information-system weaknesses.

New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme: publicly subsidized health-insurance system introduced in the 2000s for rural residents.

Mass Incident: official Chinese category commonly used for localized collective protests, disturbances, or disputes.

Civil Society: associations, charities, NGOs, volunteer groups, and other forms of social organization outside direct state administration but often regulated by it.

Environmental Governance: laws, institutions, targets, monitoring systems, and enforcement mechanisms used to control pollution and ecological damage.

By the early 2010s, China had become an urbanizing, globally connected, increasingly digital society whose institutions and social structure were far removed from those of the Mao era.

Market growth and WTO integration had produced new wealth and technological capacity, while demographic aging, environmental stress, inequality, migration, housing costs, and demands for stronger social protection created new policy pressures.
