Lesson Orientation

By the end of the Neolithic period, several regions of prehistoric China contained societies far more complex than the early farming villages from which they had developed.

Large political centers dominated surrounding settlements. Elite groups controlled prestige goods, ritual institutions, specialized craft production, and increasingly large labor forces. Fortifications, ceremonial compounds, monumental construction, water-management systems, differentiated burials, and organized warfare demonstrate that political authority was becoming broader and more durable.

Archaeologists describe some of these societies as early states.

A state differs from a small village or chiefdom not simply because it has a powerful leader. State-level political organization generally involves institutions capable of governing substantial populations, coordinating labor, controlling territory, collecting or directing resources, maintaining social hierarchy, and organizing activities that extend far beyond individual communities.

State formation did not happen once in a single location. Liangzhu, Taosi, Shimao, and Erlitou demonstrate different regional pathways toward political centralization.

This lesson therefore represents the final stage of prehistoric China in the MGU sequence.

It ends at the threshold between archaeology and dynastic tradition, where the Erlitou state becomes central to the historical debate over the Xia dynasty.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, the learner should be able to:

  • Explain what archaeologists mean by an early state.
  • Distinguish state-level political organization from smaller chiefdom-level societies.
  • Explain the importance of territorial authority and settlement hierarchy.
  • Describe how urban centers concentrated political, economic, ritual, and productive activities.
  • Explain how specialized craft production could reinforce elite power.
  • Describe the political importance of bronze technology.
  • Explain how ritual authority and warfare contributed to early state formation.
  • Identify the historical importance of Liangzhu, Taosi, Shimao, and Erlitou.
  • Explain why Erlitou cannot automatically be identified with the Xia dynasty.
  • Describe how early states created the institutional foundations for dynastic civilization.

What Is a State?

A state is a political organization capable of exercising sustained authority over a substantial population and territory through institutions that extend beyond individual families, villages, or personal relationships.

States vary enormously, but archaeologists often look for combinations of evidence including:

  • politically dominant regional centers;
  • large populations;
  • settlement hierarchies;
  • territorial organization;
  • social classes or strongly ranked social groups;
  • specialized political or ritual institutions;
  • large-scale public works;
  • specialized craft production;
  • organized military power;
  • systems for mobilizing labor and resources.

Writing is not required for a society to be considered a state.

Many early states around the world developed substantial political institutions before surviving written administrative records appeared.

Archaeologists therefore reconstruct early states primarily through the physical evidence of political organization.

From Chiefdom to State

Chiefdoms had already created regional political systems in which powerful leaders and elite groups could influence multiple communities.

State formation carried this process further.

Political authority became more capable of coordinating large territories and populations. Regional capitals could contain political, ceremonial, productive, and residential districts. Specialists could work for elites or political institutions. Large projects could mobilize labor from many communities.

A simplified developmental model might appear as:

villages → ranked regional centers → chiefdoms → early states

This sequence is useful for teaching, but actual political development was not always linear.

Some states collapsed. Some chiefdoms remained stable for long periods. Some regional centers developed state-level organization independently, while others were absorbed into expanding political systems.

Multiple Pathways to State Formation

Early Chinese states did not develop through one universal pathway.

Different regions emphasized different combinations of political, economic, environmental, military, and ritual institutions.

Liangzhu developed extensive water management, rice agriculture, monumental earthworks, elite ritual systems, and highly differentiated cemeteries.

Taosi developed a large regional center, settlement hierarchy, substantial social inequality, organized construction, and elite ceremonial institutions.

Shimao developed massive stone fortifications, a large urban center, sharp social hierarchy, specialized production, and evidence of human sacrifice.

Erlitou developed a powerful Central Plains political center characterized by large palace-temple compounds, planned roads, elite burials, bronze production, turquoise working, and an extensive regional cultural influence.

These cases demonstrate that state formation in prehistoric China was multiregional.

Territory and Political Authority

A major difference between local leadership and state-level authority is the ability to coordinate communities across a broader territory.

Archaeologists investigate territorial organization by examining:

  • settlement distribution;
  • differences in settlement size;
  • regional road or communication networks;
  • fortifications;
  • resource movement;
  • standardized elite objects;
  • regional ceremonial systems;
  • the spread of material culture from major centers.

A political center may exercise direct control over some communities and looser influence over others.

Early states therefore should not always be imagined as possessing sharply defined borders like modern nation-states.

Political authority could weaken gradually with distance from a central settlement.

Urbanization and Political Centers

State formation was closely connected with the rise of increasingly large and functionally complex settlements.

An early city or proto-urban center might contain:

  • elite compounds;
  • ceremonial precincts;
  • residential districts;
  • specialized workshops;
  • major storage facilities;
  • roads;
  • walls and gates;
  • water-control systems;
  • cemeteries;
  • public construction.

Urbanization therefore involved more than population size.

A major political center concentrated different activities and social groups within an organized settlement.

The physical layout of such centers can provide evidence of political planning and social separation.

Administration Without Written Records

Mature historical states often leave written laws, tax records, censuses, official titles, and administrative archives.

The earliest Chinese states have not left comparable surviving written bureaucratic records.

This does not mean they lacked administration.

Large political systems required mechanisms for organizing:

  • labor;
  • construction;
  • food supplies;
  • craft production;
  • ceremonial events;
  • defense;
  • movement of resources;
  • relationships among settlements.

Archaeologists therefore infer administrative capacity from the scale, repetition, organization, and coordination visible in material remains.

Administration should not automatically be imagined as a later imperial bureaucracy. Early states could govern through elite households, lineages, ritual institutions, military authority, personal retainers, and other mechanisms.

Specialized Production

As political centers expanded, craft production became increasingly specialized.

Skilled workers could concentrate upon producing:

  • bronze objects;
  • jade ornaments;
  • turquoise objects;
  • fine pottery;
  • stone tools;
  • bone artifacts;
  • textiles;
  • wooden objects.

Specialized production required raw materials, tools, knowledge, labor, and often food supplied by people who were not directly producing their own subsistence.

Political elites could strengthen their authority by controlling access to workshops, imported materials, skilled craftspeople, and prestige goods.

Workshops located near elite compounds are particularly important because they may indicate close relationships between political power and specialized production.

Bronze Technology and Elite Power

Bronze technology became increasingly important in the Central Plains during the early second millennium BCE.

Bronze production required several coordinated stages:

  • obtaining copper and other metals;
  • transporting raw materials;
  • preparing molds;
  • controlling high-temperature furnaces;
  • casting objects;
  • finishing and polishing completed products.

These requirements made bronze production an unusually complex craft.

At Erlitou, bronze vessels and weapons became associated with elite political and ritual culture.

Bronze therefore mattered not simply because metal tools were stronger than stone.

Its greater historical importance lay in the ability of political elites to concentrate rare resources, skilled labor, technology, and ritual symbolism within major political centers.

Ritual, Warfare, and Political Authority

Political authority in early Chinese states was closely connected with both ritual and warfare.

Ritual institutions helped elites legitimize their position, organize collective ceremonies, display prestige goods, and reinforce relationships with ancestors or sacred powers.

Military organization allowed political centers to defend territory, control routes, compete with rivals, acquire resources, and impose authority.

Archaeological evidence includes:

  • weapons;
  • fortifications;
  • defensive gates;
  • human sacrifice;
  • elite ritual vessels;
  • ceremonial precincts;
  • high-status burials;
  • symbols of political or military authority.

The combination of ritual legitimacy and organized force became a recurring feature of later Chinese states.

Case Study: Liangzhu

The Liangzhu cultural complex of the lower Yangtze provides some of the earliest strong evidence for state-level political organization in prehistoric China.

The Archaeological Ruins of Liangzhu City date broadly to about 3300–2300 BCE.

The political center contained:

  • large earthen constructions;
  • planned settlement space;
  • major elite cemeteries;
  • extensive jade production;
  • socially differentiated burials;
  • a large regional water-management system;
  • an agricultural economy based heavily upon rice.

Water Management

One of Liangzhu's most remarkable achievements was its extensive system of dams and water-control works.

Constructing and maintaining such infrastructure required large-scale coordination of labor and substantial knowledge of the surrounding landscape.

The water system demonstrates that political organization could develop through the management of agriculture, water, labor, and regional landscapes as well as through warfare.

Early Regional State

UNESCO describes Liangzhu as an early regional state.

This classification reflects the combination of a major political center, regional integration, strong social hierarchy, coordinated public works, specialized ritual traditions, and organized agricultural production.

Liangzhu is therefore important because it demonstrates that state-level political development was underway outside the Central Plains well before Erlitou.

Case Study: Taosi

Taosi, located in southern Shanxi, developed into one of the major political centers of Late Neolithic northern China.

Its large settlement, elite burials, substantial differences in wealth, specialized production, ceremonial areas, and surrounding settlement hierarchy indicate a highly stratified regional society.

Taosi occupies an important position between the chiefdom and state categories because its political organization became substantially more complex than that of ordinary regional centers.

Concentrated Power

Elite cemeteries demonstrate pronounced social differentiation. Monumental construction and settlement organization indicate substantial labor coordination.

Taosi therefore provides evidence for increasingly concentrated political and ritual authority.

Scholars differ over exactly how Taosi should be classified, but the site clearly demonstrates that state-like political systems were developing in northern China before Erlitou emerged as the dominant Central Plains center.

Case Study: Shimao

Shimao, located on the northern Loess Plateau in Shaanxi, represents one of the most dramatic discoveries in the study of early Chinese state formation.

The site flourished approximately 2300–1800 BCE and covered roughly four square kilometers.

Shimao contained:

  • massive stone walls;
  • inner and outer enclosures;
  • monumental gates;
  • a large central elite or ceremonial complex;
  • specialized craft production;
  • strong social hierarchy;
  • elite cemeteries;
  • evidence of human sacrifice.

A Fortified Political Center

Shimao demonstrates an especially strong relationship among fortification, political authority, ritual, and social hierarchy.

The enormous investment required to build its stone defenses indicates the ability of leaders to mobilize substantial labor.

Human Sacrifice

Archaeologists have discovered deposits of human skulls associated with the site's East Gate and other evidence of sacrificial practices.

Such evidence suggests that ritualized violence could form part of the political and ceremonial institutions of early states.

A Northern State-Level Society

Recent archaeological and genetic research describes Shimao as a state-level, socially stratified urban society.

Its existence demonstrates that important political centers developed north of the traditional Central Plains core before the rise of Erlitou.

Case Study: Erlitou

Erlitou, located near modern Yanshi in Henan Province, represents the most important transition between prehistoric state formation and China's dynastic archaeological record.

The Erlitou period is generally placed in the early second millennium BCE, broadly around 1850–1600 BCE, although exact chronological boundaries remain subjects of research.

By approximately 1750 BCE, Erlitou had emerged as a major regional center in western Henan and eventually expanded to roughly 300 hectares.

Palace-Temple District

Archaeologists have uncovered large courtyard compounds commonly interpreted as palace-temple structures.

Their scale, planned arrangement, and location within an elite precinct indicate a powerful political and ceremonial center.

Roads and Planning

Erlitou contained organized roads and distinct functional areas.

Such planning suggests that settlement growth was not entirely accidental and that elite institutions influenced the organization of space.

Specialized Workshops

Craft production near the elite district included:

  • bronze casting;
  • turquoise working;
  • bone working;
  • other specialized crafts.

The concentration of highly skilled production near elite compounds suggests a close relationship between political authority and prestige industries.

Bronze Ritual Culture

Erlitou produced some of the earliest known bronze ritual vessels within the Central Plains tradition.

Bronze vessels, weapons, turquoise objects, jades, and elite burials formed part of a distinctive political culture whose influence extended beyond the immediate settlement.

A Central Plains State

Erlitou stands at the center of a wide distribution of related archaeological sites across western Henan and neighboring regions.

Archaeologists therefore commonly treat Erlitou as an early state and one of the foundational political centers of Bronze Age China.

The Xia Problem

Later Chinese historical traditions describe the Xia dynasty as the first dynasty before the Shang.

Erlitou was discovered in 1959 during archaeological investigations partly motivated by the search for the Xia.

Its chronology, location, political scale, palatial architecture, elite burials, and prestige industries have led many Chinese archaeologists to identify some or all of Erlitou culture with the Xia dynasty.

However, the archaeological evidence has not produced contemporary inscriptions that clearly state:

This polity was called Xia.

The identification therefore remains debated.

Archaeology and Historical Tradition

Archaeology can demonstrate that a powerful early state existed at Erlitou.

Later historical texts can demonstrate that Chinese tradition remembered a dynasty called Xia before the Shang.

What remains difficult is proving that the archaeological society at Erlitou and the Xia of later texts were precisely the same political entity.

The correct historical distinction is therefore:

Erlitou is archaeologically demonstrated. The identification of Erlitou with Xia remains an interpretation.

How Do We Know?

Early states are reconstructed through combinations of archaeological evidence rather than a single defining artifact.

Settlement Hierarchy

Regional surveys identify dominant centers surrounded by smaller settlements and help reconstruct territorial organization.

Urban Planning

Roads, walls, gates, elite compounds, workshops, and residential zones provide evidence for organized settlement space.

Elite Burials

Differences in tomb construction and grave goods reveal social stratification and access to prestige resources.

Craft Workshops

Bronze foundries, jade workshops, turquoise production, pottery kilns, and manufacturing debris reveal specialized industries.

Public Works

Fortifications, dams, platforms, roads, and monumental buildings provide evidence of large-scale labor mobilization.

Human Remains

Burials, trauma, sacrifice, diet, mobility, and ancient DNA can reveal social relationships, inequality, violence, kinship, and population movement.

Material Distribution

The geographic distribution of distinctive ceramics, bronzes, jades, and other objects helps archaeologists reconstruct political influence, exchange networks, and regional interaction.

Radiocarbon Dating

Scientific dating helps establish the chronological relationships among Liangzhu, Taosi, Shimao, Erlitou, and other political centers.

Think Like a Historian

Imagine that archaeologists discover a 300-hectare settlement containing palace-like buildings, planned roads, bronze workshops, elite burials, and smaller related settlements across the surrounding region.

Can archaeologists conclude that they have discovered a state?

The evidence would provide a strong case for state-level political organization because it demonstrates population concentration, settlement hierarchy, specialized production, elite institutions, and regional integration.

Now consider a second question:

Can archaeologists identify the name of that state?

Not necessarily.

Archaeology can reconstruct political complexity without automatically recovering the names that ancient people used for themselves.

This distinction is essential when studying Erlitou.

Archaeologists can demonstrate the existence of a powerful early state. Identifying that state specifically as Xia requires an additional historical argument connecting material evidence with later textual traditions.

Historical Significance

Early states transformed prehistoric Chinese society.

Political authority increasingly operated across large territories and populations rather than within individual villages or loosely connected regional communities.

These societies developed:

  • major political centers;
  • territorial organization;
  • large-scale labor mobilization;
  • elite political and ritual institutions;
  • specialized industries;
  • organized fortification and warfare;
  • increasingly formal social hierarchy;
  • regional systems of exchange and resource control.

State formation also changed the scale of inequality.

Elite groups gained access to prestigious materials, monumental architecture, specialized crafts, ritual authority, and concentrated labor.

At the same time, political centers became capable of coordinating activities that affected thousands of people.

These developments formed the institutional foundation upon which the dynastic political systems of Bronze Age China would develop.

Transition to Dynastic China

The early-state period marks the end of prehistory in this MGU sequence.

By the early second millennium BCE, the Central Plains contained a major political center at Erlitou whose scale, specialized industries, elite institutions, and regional influence differed substantially from earlier Neolithic political systems.

At approximately the same historical threshold, later Chinese tradition places the Xia dynasty.

From this point forward, Chinese history increasingly involves two forms of evidence that must be studied together but not confused:

  1. archaeological evidence from sites, artifacts, burials, architecture, and scientific dating;
  2. historical traditions preserved in texts written and compiled in later periods.

Key Takeaways

  1. An early state exercises sustained political authority across a substantial population and territory through institutions extending beyond individual villages.
  2. Writing is not required for archaeologists to identify state-level political organization.
  3. State formation developed through multiple regional pathways rather than from a single prehistoric Chinese center.
  4. Liangzhu demonstrates an early regional state based upon rice agriculture, major water management, social hierarchy, ritual institutions, and planned settlement.
  5. Taosi demonstrates large-scale regional political organization and substantial social differentiation in northern China.
  6. Shimao was a large fortified state-level center characterized by monumental stone construction, elite institutions, craft production, strong hierarchy, and human sacrifice.
  7. Early states increasingly concentrated political, economic, ritual, and military functions within major centers.
  8. Specialized production allowed elites to control prestige materials and skilled labor.
  9. Bronze production became particularly important within the developing political culture of the Central Plains.
  10. Erlitou developed into a major early state during the early second millennium BCE.
  11. Erlitou provides archaeological evidence for early state civilization, but its identification with the Xia dynasty remains debated.
  12. Early states created the institutional foundations for China's dynastic Bronze Age.

Key Terms

State
A political organization capable of exercising sustained authority over a substantial population and territory through institutions that extend beyond individual households or villages.
State Formation
The historical process through which political authority becomes increasingly centralized, territorial, institutionalized, and capable of governing large populations.
Urbanization
The development of increasingly large and functionally complex settlements containing concentrated populations and specialized political, economic, ritual, or productive activities.
Territorial Authority
Political power exercised across a geographic region containing multiple settlements and communities.
Administration
Systems used to coordinate political decisions, resources, labor, production, construction, defense, and relationships among communities.
Specialized Production
Craft activity performed by workers with concentrated knowledge, tools, and skills, often producing goods for elites, exchange, or political institutions.
Bronze
A metal alloy whose production requires controlled access to raw materials and specialized casting technology. Bronze became an important prestige and ritual material in early Central Plains states.
Liangzhu
A Late Neolithic political and cultural complex of the lower Yangtze associated with rice agriculture, planned settlements, jade ritual objects, social hierarchy, major water-management works, and an early regional state.
Taosi
A major Late Neolithic political center in southern Shanxi associated with settlement hierarchy, elite burials, monumental construction, specialized activity, and increasing political centralization.
Shimao
A large fortified political center on the northern Loess Plateau, approximately 2300–1800 BCE, characterized by monumental stone architecture, social stratification, specialized production, and human sacrifice.
Erlitou
A major early Bronze Age political center in Henan associated with palace-temple compounds, planned roads, elite burials, specialized workshops, bronze production, and broad regional influence.
Palace-Temple
A term used by archaeologists for monumental elite compounds whose architecture may have combined political, residential, and ceremonial functions.
Prestige Industry
Specialized production of rare, technically demanding, or socially valued objects associated particularly with elites.
Xia Dynasty
The dynasty traditionally remembered in later Chinese historical sources as preceding the Shang. Its precise relationship to archaeological cultures, especially Erlitou, remains debated.
