Paleolithic China

Human life in the lands of present-day China before agriculture, villages, dynasties, and written history.

Lesson Orientation

Long before China existed as a state, civilization, or political identity, human communities occupied the lands that are now part of China. For an immense span of time, these communities lived without agriculture, cities, written records, or dynastic government.

The Paleolithic, or Old Stone Age, represents this earliest chapter of human history. People survived by obtaining resources directly from their environments through hunting, gathering, fishing, scavenging, and other forms of foraging. Their technologies were made primarily from stone and other natural materials.

Archaeologists reconstruct this history from physical evidence rather than written documents. Fossils, stone tools, animal remains, sediments, habitation deposits, and traces of human activity provide evidence about people who lived hundreds of thousands—or even millions—of years ago.

Paleolithic China therefore begins the history of China geographically, but not yet politically. It is the history of human societies within the territory of present-day China before the emergence of the institutions that would eventually produce Chinese civilization.

What Is the Paleolithic?

The word Paleolithic comes from terms meaning old and stone. It is commonly translated as Old Stone Age.

The Paleolithic refers to the long period of human development before agriculture and permanently settled farming communities became major features of human society. Stone tools are among the most durable and recognizable artifacts from this period, although Paleolithic people also used materials such as wood, bone, hide, fiber, and plant matter. Many objects made from these materials have not survived.

The beginning and ending dates of the Paleolithic are not identical in every part of the world. Human societies changed at different rates and followed different economic strategies in different environments. Consequently, historians and archaeologists should treat the transition from Paleolithic to Neolithic life as a process rather than a single worldwide event.

China Before China

The term Paleolithic China can be misleading if it is interpreted too literally. There was no unified Chinese kingdom, empire, dynasty, or national identity during most of this period.

In this lesson, the term refers to human societies that lived within the geographic territory that is today part of China.

This distinction is important because modern political boundaries should not automatically be projected backward onto prehistoric societies.

Earliest Human Presence

Archaeological discoveries demonstrate that members of the human evolutionary lineage occupied East Asia extremely early in human prehistory.

At Shangchen, on the Chinese Loess Plateau, researchers have identified stone artifacts in geological layers dating to approximately 2.1 million years ago. No hominin fossils were discovered with the oldest artifacts, so archaeologists cannot say with certainty which hominin species produced them.

The discovery is nevertheless important because intentionally modified stone artifacts provide evidence that tool-making hominins had reached East Asia very early in the history of human dispersal.

Other early sites and fossils demonstrate that different hominin populations occupied parts of China during the long Pleistocene epoch. The archaeological record therefore does not represent a single population continuously occupying an unchanged landscape. It records repeated adaptation, movement, population change, and technological development over immense periods of time.

Life Without Agriculture

Paleolithic communities did not obtain most of their food from domesticated crops and livestock. Instead, they depended upon resources available within natural environments.

Subsistence strategies varied according to region, climate, season, available plants, animal populations, rivers, lakes, forests, grasslands, and other ecological conditions.

Foraging

People gathered edible plants, seeds, roots, nuts, fruits, and other naturally occurring resources. The particular foods available depended upon local ecology and season.

Animal Resources

Animals supplied food and useful raw materials. Archaeological evidence may include animal bones associated with human occupation, stone tools used in processing, and patterns of modification that help researchers understand how humans interacted with animal resources.

Aquatic Resources

Rivers, lakes, wetlands, and coastal environments provided fish, shellfish, plants, and other resources. The importance of aquatic foods varied substantially among regions.

Mobility

Many Paleolithic groups moved through landscapes as resources became available. Mobility allowed communities to respond to changing seasons, animal movements, water availability, and environmental conditions.

Paleolithic life should therefore not be understood simply as wandering without purpose. Successful mobility required detailed knowledge of landscapes, animal behavior, seasonal cycles, water sources, raw materials, and food availability.

Environment and Adaptation

The Paleolithic extended across enormous climatic changes. During the Pleistocene, colder and warmer periods repeatedly altered vegetation, water systems, animal populations, and the landscapes available to human communities.

Different regions of present-day China also presented very different environments. Northern grasslands, river valleys, mountain regions, southern forests, plateaus, and coastal zones created different opportunities and constraints.

Human survival therefore depended upon adaptation. Communities adjusted their movements, technologies, food choices, and patterns of resource use according to local conditions.

This relationship can be represented as:

environment → available resources → human choices → adaptation

Human populations were not simply passive victims of environmental change. They learned about landscapes, developed technologies, shared knowledge, and changed their behavior in response to new conditions.

Stone Tools and Technology

Stone tools provide some of the most important archaeological evidence for Paleolithic life because stone can survive for extraordinarily long periods of time.

Early toolmakers learned that particular stones could be struck in controlled ways to produce sharp edges and useful shapes. Cores, flakes, cutting implements, scraping tools, and other forms of stone technology allowed people to perform tasks more efficiently.

Tools could assist in:

  • cutting materials;
  • processing animal tissues;
  • scraping hides;
  • working wood;
  • processing plants;
  • producing additional tools.

Stone technology demonstrates more than the ability to break rocks. Tool production required the recognition of useful raw materials, knowledge of fracture patterns, learned techniques, and decisions about which tool forms were appropriate for particular tasks.

Archaeologists can therefore use stone artifacts to investigate human behavior, technology, mobility, resource selection, and cultural change.

Case Study: Zhoukoudian and Peking Man

One of the most famous Paleolithic archaeological sites in China is Zhoukoudian, located southwest of Beijing.

Excavations at Zhoukoudian produced human fossils, stone artifacts, animal remains, and evidence of repeated occupation. The site contains deposits representing different periods of prehistoric human activity.

Peking Man

Fossils from Zhoukoudian became popularly known as Peking Man. They are classified as Homo erectus.

The Smithsonian Institution dates the well-known Zhoukoudian Homo erectus material to approximately 780,000 to 400,000 years ago.

The original discoveries became internationally important during the early twentieth century because they contributed to scientific debates concerning human evolution and the antiquity of human occupation in East Asia.

The Lost Fossils

Many of the original Peking Man fossils disappeared during the turmoil of the Second World War while they were being moved for safekeeping. Their disappearance remains one of the best-known episodes in the history of paleoanthropology.

Fortunately, detailed scientific descriptions and high-quality casts had already been produced, and later excavation recovered additional material.

Why Zhoukoudian Matters

Zhoukoudian is significant not simply because a famous fossil was found there. Its importance comes from the combination of:

  • hominin fossils;
  • stone artifacts;
  • animal remains;
  • geological deposits;
  • evidence of repeated human occupation;
  • its role in the development of modern paleoanthropology.

Zhoukoudian therefore demonstrates how a single archaeological site can contribute simultaneously to the study of biological evolution, technology, environment, behavior, and the history of science.

Research Note: Did Peking Man Use Fire?

Zhoukoudian has long been associated with evidence for the human use of fire. Researchers have identified burned materials and other evidence interpreted as signs of burning at the site.

The interpretation has also been the subject of scientific debate. Earlier researchers sometimes treated controlled fire use at Zhoukoudian as firmly established. Later studies questioned whether some deposits demonstrated fires burning directly within the occupation area or could have resulted from other processes.

More recent investigations have presented additional evidence supporting human-controlled fire in portions of the site, while research continues into the chronology and circumstances of fire use.

This debate illustrates an important principle:

Historical and archaeological conclusions can change when researchers obtain new evidence or develop better methods for analyzing existing evidence.

Archaeology is therefore not merely the collection of ancient objects. It is an evidence-based process of proposing, testing, challenging, and refining explanations about the past.

How Do We Know?

Paleolithic societies left no written histories. Knowledge of them comes primarily from archaeology, paleoanthropology, geology, environmental science, and related fields.

Human Fossils

Teeth, skull fragments, bones, and other remains can provide evidence about ancient hominin populations, anatomy, development, and evolution.

Stone Artifacts

Stone tools reveal technological behavior. Their materials, shapes, manufacturing methods, and locations can help researchers reconstruct how people moved through landscapes and performed particular tasks.

Animal Remains

Animal bones can provide information about past environments and human subsistence. Researchers examine species, breakage patterns, cut marks, burning, and archaeological context.

Sediments and Stratigraphy

Archaeological materials occur within layers of sediment. Stratigraphy studies the sequence and relationship of these layers. When deposits remain undisturbed, deeper layers are generally older than layers deposited above them.

Scientific Dating

Archaeologists use different dating methods depending upon the age and type of site. Very old Paleolithic sites may require geological, paleomagnetic, or other scientific dating techniques because they are far beyond the useful range of methods designed for much younger material.

Environmental Evidence

Fossils, sediments, pollen, geological deposits, and other environmental evidence can help reconstruct the climates and ecosystems in which ancient humans lived.

Think Like a Historian

Written documents are only one kind of historical evidence. Paleolithic history demonstrates that historians can investigate the human past even when no written sources exist.

Consider the following problem:

If archaeologists discover stone tools, animal bones, and evidence of repeated occupation at a prehistoric site, what can they reasonably conclude—and what questions would still remain unanswered?

The evidence may demonstrate that humans occupied the location and used technology there. Additional analysis may reveal information about diet, environment, tool production, or occupation patterns.

However, archaeologists should not automatically assume that they know the people's language, political identity, religious beliefs, or social organization unless evidence supports those conclusions.

Responsible historical interpretation therefore requires distinguishing between:

what the evidence demonstrates, what the evidence suggests, and what remains unknown.

The Late Paleolithic

During the later Paleolithic, modern humans occupied regions of China while technologies and subsistence strategies continued to diversify. Different communities developed solutions appropriate to their own environments rather than following a single uniform path.

Late Paleolithic and early post-Pleistocene communities increasingly provide evidence for changes in resource use, technology, settlement, social organization, and relationships with particular landscapes.

These changes are important because the transition toward agriculture did not begin with a single invention or occur simultaneously throughout China.

Transition Toward Agrarian Societies

The Paleolithic did not end because hunter-gatherers suddenly decided to become farmers. The transition toward agriculture was gradual, geographically varied, and involved many intermediate economic strategies.

Some communities increasingly concentrated upon particular plant, animal, and aquatic resources. Patterns of seasonal movement could change. Occupation of favorable locations could become longer or more frequent. Technologies for collecting, storing, processing, and cooking food also developed.

Over time, some populations began deliberately managing and cultivating plants. Domestication and food production eventually transformed the relationship between human communities and their environments.

Agriculture would make possible increasingly permanent settlements, larger populations, greater food storage, and new forms of social organization.

Historical Significance

Paleolithic China represents far more than a preliminary chapter before the appearance of dynasties.

It establishes several fundamental themes that continue throughout the history of China:

  • human adaptation to highly diverse environments;
  • technological responses to changing conditions;
  • regional diversity rather than a single cultural origin;
  • long-distance population movement and interaction;
  • changing relationships between humans and natural resources;
  • the gradual transformation from mobile foraging societies toward increasingly settled communities.

Most importantly, Paleolithic history reminds us that Chinese civilization did not appear suddenly. It emerged after an immense prehistory of human occupation, adaptation, experimentation, and social change.

Key Takeaways

  1. Paleolithic means Old Stone Age and refers to the long period before agriculture became a major basis of settled society.
  2. Paleolithic China is a geographic term. A unified Chinese state did not exist during this period.
  3. Stone artifacts from Shangchen indicate hominin activity in what is now China approximately 2.1 million years ago.
  4. Paleolithic communities depended primarily upon naturally occurring resources and adapted their behavior to local environments.
  5. Stone technology provides archaeologists with durable evidence of ancient human behavior.
  6. Zhoukoudian is one of China's most important Paleolithic sites and is especially associated with Peking Man, classified as Homo erectus.
  7. Archaeologists reconstruct prehistoric history through artifacts, fossils, sediments, environmental evidence, stratigraphy, and scientific dating.
  8. Archaeological interpretations can change when new evidence or better analytical methods become available.
  9. The transition from Paleolithic foraging to agriculture was gradual and varied among regions.
  10. The development of food production leads into the next stage of Chinese prehistory: the First Agrarian Societies.

Key Terms

Paleolithic
The Old Stone Age, the long period of early human history associated with stone-tool technologies and economies based primarily upon naturally available resources.
Prehistory
Human history reconstructed primarily from non-written evidence, particularly for periods before written records.
Hominin
A member of the evolutionary lineage that includes modern humans and extinct species more closely related to humans than to chimpanzees.
Foraging
Obtaining food from naturally occurring resources through activities such as gathering, hunting, fishing, and related strategies.
Homo erectus
An extinct hominin species known from Africa and parts of Eurasia. Fossils commonly called Peking Man from Zhoukoudian are classified as Homo erectus.
Zhoukoudian
A major Paleolithic archaeological locality southwest of Beijing containing hominin fossils, stone tools, animal remains, and evidence of prehistoric occupation.
Peking Man
The popular name for Homo erectus fossils discovered at Zhoukoudian.
Artifact
An object made, modified, or used by humans.
Stratigraphy
The study of archaeological and geological layers and their relationships through time.
Subsistence
The methods through which people obtain the food and resources needed to sustain life.
Adaptation
Behavioral, technological, or biological responses that help populations survive within particular environments.
Neolithization
The long process through which societies developed combinations of food production, sedentism, new technologies, and other characteristics associated with Neolithic life.
