Lesson Orientation

Agriculture changed the relationship between people and the land, but one of the most important transformations of the Neolithic period was the development of increasingly permanent communities.

People had occupied favored locations repeatedly long before farming. Some hunter-gatherer communities could remain in one location for long periods when food, water, shelter, and other resources were sufficiently abundant. The development of agriculture therefore did not invent sedentary life in a single moment.

Food production nevertheless strengthened the advantages of remaining near productive land. Fields required repeated attention. Harvested food needed storage. Houses, pottery, tools, wells, ovens, work areas, and other facilities represented investments that encouraged communities to return to or remain within the same settlement.

Over time, settlements became more than places where people happened to live. They became organized communities composed of households, workplaces, storage facilities, communal spaces, burial areas, and increasingly durable relationships among generations.

The village was therefore both a physical settlement and a new scale of social organization.

What Is a Village?

A village is a relatively permanent settlement in which multiple households live near one another and share a broader community space.

A village is more than a collection of houses. Archaeological villages may contain combinations of:

  • houses and household compounds;
  • storage pits;
  • hearths and ovens;
  • pottery kilns;
  • wells;
  • work areas;
  • refuse pits;
  • ditches or boundaries;
  • communal spaces;
  • cemeteries.

These features demonstrate that inhabitants invested substantial labor in particular places.

Permanent settlement also changed social life. Neighbors encountered one another repeatedly. Families occupied particular houses. Storage facilities contained accumulated resources. Burial grounds connected living communities with earlier generations.

The village therefore created a durable relationship between people, place, resources, and community.

From Mobility to Sedentism

Sedentism refers to living in one location for extended periods or permanently rather than regularly relocating an entire community.

Sedentism should not be treated as the automatic result of agriculture. Archaeological evidence from early Holocene China demonstrates that some communities became increasingly sedentary while hunting, gathering, and processing wild resources remained important.

Several conditions could encourage people to remain in one place:

  • reliable water supplies;
  • abundant plant resources;
  • productive fishing or wetland environments;
  • cultivated fields;
  • stored food;
  • durable houses;
  • specialized facilities;
  • social and family connections.

Agriculture and sedentism could then reinforce one another.

permanent settlement → greater investment in land → increased food production → greater investment in settlement

This feedback relationship helped transform temporary occupations into increasingly permanent communities.

Houses and Households

Houses are among the clearest archaeological signs that people invested labor in a particular location.

Early Neolithic houses varied considerably across China. Communities adapted construction methods to local environments, available materials, climate, and social practices.

Northern settlements commonly included houses partly excavated below ground level. These semi-subterranean structures could provide insulation and stability while reducing the amount of wall construction required.

In wetter southern environments, some communities developed wooden buildings raised above ground. Elevated structures helped inhabitants respond to moisture, flooding, and wetland conditions.

The Household

A house was not simply shelter. It could also serve as a center of cooking, food preparation, craft production, storage, family interaction, and everyday social life.

Archaeologists therefore study houses as evidence for households: groups of people who shared important parts of daily economic and social life.

The number, size, placement, and contents of houses can help researchers investigate how communities were organized.

Storage and Food Security

Permanent settlements made it possible to accumulate resources that would have been difficult to transport continuously.

Villagers could store:

  • grain;
  • nuts;
  • seeds;
  • dried foods;
  • tools;
  • raw materials;
  • pottery and household equipment.

Storage reduced dependence upon food that was immediately available. A successful harvest or season of collecting could help support a community during later periods of scarcity.

Stored crops could also provide seed for future planting.

Surplus

When a household or community produced more food than it immediately consumed, part of that production could become a surplus.

Surplus did not automatically create wealth, social classes, or states. However, the ability to accumulate and control stored resources would eventually become important to the development of increasingly complex societies.

Storage therefore represents an important bridge between subsistence and later forms of economic organization.

Pottery and Household Technology

Pottery became increasingly important within many Neolithic communities.

Ceramic vessels could be used for:

  • cooking;
  • boiling foods;
  • serving meals;
  • storing grain or liquids;
  • transporting materials;
  • fermentation;
  • ritual or burial purposes.

Pottery existed in parts of East Asia before intensive agriculture. Its significance therefore should not be reduced to farming alone.

Permanent settlement nevertheless made heavy and fragile ceramic vessels especially useful because families no longer needed to transport all household equipment across long distances.

Craft Knowledge

Pottery production required knowledge of clay selection, vessel forming, surface treatment, drying, firing, and temperature control.

Villagers also worked stone, bone, wood, fibers, hides, shell, and other materials.

These activities demonstrate that early villages contained substantial technological knowledge even before full-time occupational specialists became common.

Organizing Community Space

One of the major advantages of village archaeology is that researchers can study not only individual artifacts but also the relationships among buildings and activity areas.

Houses may cluster together. Storage pits may occur near residences. Kilns may be placed away from houses because they produce heat, smoke, and fire hazards. Cemeteries may occupy separate areas.

Ditches, open spaces, pathways, and repeated building patterns can indicate that settlement space was organized rather than random.

Spatial organization raises important historical questions:

  • Were households similar in size?
  • Were particular activities communal?
  • Were some areas restricted?
  • Did families control their own storage?
  • Were houses arranged around shared spaces?
  • Were production activities separated from residences?

Archaeologists cannot answer all of these questions from settlement plans alone, but spatial patterns provide important evidence for investigating social organization.

Cemeteries and Community Identity

Permanent settlements also changed relationships between living communities and the dead.

Cemeteries could become enduring places where multiple generations were buried near the communities in which they had lived.

Archaeologists examine:

  • grave location;
  • body position;
  • age and biological sex;
  • grave goods;
  • differences among burials;
  • relationships between cemeteries and settlements.

Burial practices may provide evidence about community membership, household relationships, ritual behavior, status differences, and changing social identities.

However, burial evidence must be interpreted carefully. A rich grave does not automatically reveal a person's occupation or exact political status, and differences in burial treatment can have multiple meanings.

Case Study: Jiahu

Jiahu, located in Henan Province, was occupied during approximately 7000–5700 BCE.

Archaeological remains include houses, storage facilities, burials, pottery, stone tools, bone tools, and evidence for a diverse economy.

Jiahu is especially famous for bone flutes manufactured from the wing bones of cranes. Some surviving examples can still produce multiple musical tones.

These instruments provide remarkable evidence that early village life included activities extending far beyond obtaining food and constructing shelter.

Village Life at Jiahu

Jiahu's inhabitants used both cultivated and wild resources. Rice was present, but hunting, fishing, gathering, and other foods remained important.

The settlement therefore illustrates a major theme of the early Neolithic:

permanent village life could develop while communities still practiced mixed subsistence strategies.

Symbols and Ritual

Some objects from Jiahu contain incised signs. Scholars have debated their meaning and whether they bear any relationship to much later Chinese writing.

They should not simply be called Chinese writing. Their importance lies in demonstrating deliberate symbolic marking within an early Neolithic community.

Jiahu therefore provides evidence for settlement, subsistence, craft, music, ritual, and symbolic behavior within one early village society.

Case Study: Banpo

Banpo, near modern Xi'an in Shaanxi Province, is one of the best-known village sites associated with the early Yangshao cultural tradition.

The main Banpo occupation dates broadly to the fifth millennium BCE. Excavations revealed houses, storage pits, pottery kilns, burial areas, and a large ditch associated with the settlement.

Settlement Organization

Banpo is particularly important because archaeologists can study the spatial relationship among different parts of the community.

Residential structures occupied settlement space distinct from some pottery-production and burial areas.

This demonstrates that village space could be divided according to different activities.

Painted Pottery

Banpo is also associated with distinctive pottery traditions. Vessels could contain geometric decoration as well as representations of animals and human faces.

Pottery therefore became both functional equipment and a medium through which communities expressed visual traditions.

What Banpo Does Not Tell Us Automatically

Earlier interpretations sometimes attempted to reconstruct highly specific political or family systems from Banpo's settlement and burial patterns.

Modern historical interpretation should be more cautious.

Settlement plans can demonstrate spatial organization, but they do not by themselves establish whether a society was matriarchal, patriarchal, democratic, or governed through any other particular political system.

Case Study: Hemudu

Hemudu, located in the lower Yangtze region, represents a very different environmental adaptation from northern villages such as Banpo.

Hemudu communities lived within a wetter environment in which rice, wetlands, aquatic resources, wood, and other southern resources played important roles.

Wooden Architecture

Hemudu is particularly notable for wooden architecture. Buildings were constructed using substantial timber elements, and some structures were raised above wet ground.

These buildings demonstrate how village architecture could respond to local environmental conditions.

Organic Preservation

Wet archaeological conditions can preserve materials that normally decay.

As a result, sites in the lower Yangtze can preserve wooden objects, plant remains, basketry, and other organic materials rarely found at dry archaeological sites.

This is important because prehistoric people used far more than stone and pottery. Much of their technological world was made from materials that usually disappear.

A Different Village Tradition

Comparing Hemudu with northern settlements demonstrates that there was no single Neolithic village model for prehistoric China.

Communities adapted architecture, agriculture, diet, tools, and settlement design to different environments.

How Do We Know?

Villages provide archaeologists with unusually rich information because many activities occurred repeatedly within a limited area.

Postholes and Foundations

Wooden walls and roofs often disappear, but holes dug for structural posts can remain visible as differences in soil. These patterns allow archaeologists to reconstruct building shapes.

Floors and Hearths

Prepared floors, hearths, ash, ovens, and concentrations of artifacts can help identify domestic spaces and activities.

Storage Pits

Underground pits can preserve evidence of food storage, household organization, and changing economic practices.

Pottery

Pottery shapes, manufacturing methods, decoration, and distribution can help archaeologists investigate cooking, storage, exchange, technology, and cultural traditions.

Plant and Animal Remains

Seeds, grains, bones, shells, phytoliths, and other biological remains help reconstruct diet, agriculture, hunting, fishing, and local environments.

Burials

Cemeteries provide evidence concerning population, health, diet, burial customs, social relationships, and differences in treatment among individuals.

Settlement Plans

Mapping where houses, graves, kilns, pits, pathways, and open areas occur allows archaeologists to study the village as an organized whole rather than merely as a collection of artifacts.

Think Like a Historian

Imagine that archaeologists uncover a settlement containing twenty houses, storage pits, a cemetery, several pottery kilns, and an enclosing ditch.

What can the settlement plan demonstrate—and what can it not demonstrate by itself?

Archaeologists can reasonably conclude that the inhabitants invested substantial labor in the settlement and organized different activities across space.

They can examine whether houses differed in size, whether production occurred in specialized areas, whether burials were separated from residences, and whether storage appears household-based or communal.

The evidence does not automatically reveal the names of political leaders, the language spoken by the inhabitants, their exact family structure, or a formal system of government.

Responsible interpretation again requires separating:

what the evidence demonstrates → what it suggests → what remains unknown

Historical Significance

Permanent villages transformed the scale and organization of human society.

People increasingly lived within communities in which the same households, neighbors, storage facilities, work areas, burial grounds, and productive landscapes could remain important across generations.

Villages allowed communities to accumulate:

  • stored food;
  • tools and equipment;
  • architectural investment;
  • craft knowledge;
  • local traditions;
  • burial places;
  • shared memories;
  • territorial attachments.

These developments created conditions in which social organization could become more complex.

Larger communities required cooperation. Resources had to be managed. Conflicts had to be resolved. Work could be coordinated. Traditions were transmitted among generations.

The village therefore became one of the fundamental building blocks from which later towns, ceremonial centers, chiefdoms, and states could develop.

Transition Toward Regional Cultures

Early villages were not isolated experiments.

As settled communities multiplied, villages within particular regions increasingly shared technologies, pottery traditions, crops, burial customs, architectural practices, and forms of material culture.

At the same time, communities exchanged goods and ideas with neighboring populations.

Archaeologists identify recurring combinations of artifacts and settlement practices as archaeological cultures.

These categories do not necessarily represent single ethnic groups, political states, or languages. They are analytical tools used to describe patterns in material evidence.

By the later Neolithic, increasingly distinctive regional traditions had appeared across northern, eastern, northeastern, central, and southern China.

Key Takeaways

  1. A village is a relatively permanent settlement composed of multiple households within a broader organized community.
  2. Sedentism means remaining in one location for extended periods or permanently.
  3. Sedentism could develop before intensive farming and should not be treated simply as an automatic consequence of agriculture.
  4. Agriculture and permanent settlement eventually reinforced one another.
  5. Houses became centers of household production, consumption, storage, and social life.
  6. Storage allowed communities to preserve resources beyond the season in which they were produced.
  7. Pottery became increasingly useful for cooking, storage, serving, processing, and other village activities.
  8. Settlement plans can reveal how communities organized residences, production areas, storage, cemeteries, and communal space.
  9. Jiahu demonstrates that early village life could include agriculture, hunting, fishing, craft production, music, ritual, and symbolic behavior.
  10. Banpo provides important evidence for organized village space within the Yangshao tradition.
  11. Hemudu demonstrates how southern communities developed village architecture and subsistence practices adapted to wet environments.
  12. Permanent villages helped establish the social and economic foundations for larger regional cultures.

Key Terms

Village
A relatively permanent settlement in which multiple households live within a shared community space.
Sedentism
The practice of living in one location for long periods or permanently rather than regularly relocating an entire community.
Household
A group of people who share important aspects of residence, production, consumption, storage, or daily social life.
Surplus
Resources produced or collected beyond immediate consumption that can be stored, exchanged, reserved, or used later.
Storage Pit
A pit dug into the ground for storing food or other materials.
Semi-Subterranean House
A dwelling whose floor is excavated partly below the surrounding ground surface.
Posthole
Archaeological evidence left by a wooden structural post placed in the ground.
Kiln
A structure in which pottery or other materials are heated under controlled conditions.
Cemetery
An area used repeatedly for the burial of members of a community.
Jiahu
An early Neolithic settlement in Henan associated with houses, agriculture, fishing, hunting, pottery, burials, symbolic markings, and famous bone flutes.
Banpo
A major Yangshao-period village near modern Xi'an containing houses, storage pits, kilns, burials, and evidence of organized settlement space.
Hemudu
A Neolithic settlement tradition of the lower Yangtze region known for rice agriculture, wooden architecture, wetland adaptation, and unusually well-preserved organic materials.
Archaeological Culture
A classification used by archaeologists for recurring patterns of material remains found across particular regions and periods.
