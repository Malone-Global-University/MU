Malone Primer

Problem Framework

A disciplined method for converting observed conditions into defined problems, evidence-based analysis, strategic priorities, doctrinal conclusions, and durable institutional responses.

Introduction

The Problem Framework establishes how the Malone Primer identifies, investigates, classifies, and evaluates problems before proposing doctrinal or institutional responses. It is intended to prevent reaction, rhetoric, isolated anecdotes, and preferred solutions from being mistaken for complete analysis.

A valid problem study must explain what is happening, who or what is responsible, how the condition is produced, who is affected, why it is strategically significant, what evidence supports the conclusion, and what form of correction is reasonably available. Uncertainty, competing explanations, and limitations in the available evidence must remain visible throughout the process.

Purpose of the Framework

The framework provides a common analytical discipline for policy briefs, propositions, research papers, case studies, institutional reviews, and future doctrinal development. It allows different subjects to be compared without forcing every problem into the same explanation or remedy.

The objective is not merely to describe harm. The objective is to produce a reliable chain from observation to diagnosis, from diagnosis to strategic judgment, and from strategic judgment to corrective action that can be assigned, measured, reviewed, and improved.

Analytical Workflow

  1. Identify the condition. Describe the observed event, pattern, failure, threat, or unmet need without embedding an unproven explanation in the initial statement.
  2. Define the problem. State the affected population, relevant actors, conduct, jurisdiction, time period, operating environment, and practical consequence.
  3. Establish significance. Determine the scale, severity, persistence, strategic importance, reversibility, and cost of leaving the condition unresolved.
  4. Collect and classify evidence. Separate confirmed facts, documented claims, testimony, circumstantial evidence, indicators, inference, hypotheses, and speculation.
  5. Map the causal system. Identify the rules, incentives, decisions, institutions, customs, resource flows, information systems, and recurring behaviors that produce or preserve the condition.
  6. Distinguish causes from symptoms. Separate visible outcomes from the mechanisms that create them, and identify any reinforcing cycles or dependencies.
  7. Classify the problem. Place the matter within one or more of the Seven Strategic Problem containers while preserving distinctions among overlapping causes.
  8. Evaluate existing responses. Determine what has already been attempted, what produced measurable improvement, what failed, and what merely displaced or concealed the problem.
  9. Design the corrective response. Identify the doctrinal, educational, political, charitable, economic, organizational, administrative, legal, cultural, or defensive intervention appropriate to the verified problem.
  10. Assign responsibility and measurement. Identify the institution or authority responsible for implementation, establish limits on jurisdiction, define success indicators, preserve the record, and schedule reassessment.

Required Analytical Record

A complete problem analysis should preserve enough information for another qualified reader to understand the reasoning, challenge weak conclusions, and continue the investigation. The record should ordinarily contain:

  • A concise problem statement and defined scope.
  • The affected population, institution, territory, or strategic interest.
  • The responsible or contributing actors and their relevant authority.
  • The mechanisms through which the problem is created or maintained.
  • The evidence supporting each material claim.
  • Alternative explanations, counterevidence, uncertainty, and known limitations.
  • The historical timeline and evidence of persistence or recurrence.
  • The relationship to the Seven Strategic Problems and other Malone studies.
  • Existing responses and their documented results.
  • The proposed correction, responsible institution, required resources, risks, and measures of success.

Evidence and Confidence

The framework does not require every operational judgment to meet the standard of proof required in a court proceeding. It does require the analyst to disclose the quality of the evidence, the confidence of the conclusion, and the consequence of being wrong. Stronger claims and more intrusive responses require stronger evidence, greater review, and clearer authority.

Evidence may support awareness, investigation, precaution, internal planning, or formal accusation at different thresholds. These uses must not be conflated. A behavioral indicator may justify further inquiry without proving intent, identity, coordination, guilt, or institutional responsibility.

Strategic Classification

The Seven Strategic Problems provide the principal diagnostic containers for organizing Malone Primer analysis. A study may involve more than one container, but it should identify the primary problem, contributing problems, and the causal relationship among them rather than treating all seven as interchangeable labels.

  • The Seven Strategic Problems

    Reviews the seven principal problem areas and explains their relationship as an interconnected strategic system.

  • The Seven Problems as Doctrinal Containers

    Explains how the seven categories organize research, doctrinal development, institutional planning, and implementation.

  • The Seven Strategic Problems Facing African Americans

    Presents the complete seven-part diagnosis and its application to African American security, development, representation, and organization.

From Diagnosis to Response

Problem analysis within the Malone Primer is explanatory and developmental. It may support recommendations, expose contradictions, test proposed principles, and identify the need for new rules or institutions. It does not independently create binding doctrinal authority.

When the analysis supports a durable governing standard, the proposed conclusion may enter the formal doctrinal development process. When the problem requires implementation, responsibility must be assigned to the Malone institution whose charter, competence, resources, and jurisdiction authorize the response.

Evidence and Verification Studies

  • Confirm and Verify

    Establishes verification as an active discipline rather than passive trust or reflexive disbelief.

  • Evidence in a Survival Doctrine

    Examines the role of evidence in protecting people and institutions under conditions of uncertainty and strategic risk.

  • Evidence Without Evidentiary Dependency

    Distinguishes disciplined evidence use from dependence on external recognition before reasonable internal judgment or preparation may occur.

  • Threat Indicators Versus Proof

    Separates warning signs that justify attention from proof sufficient to establish responsibility or support a formal conclusion.

Review Standard

Problem studies should be revised when material evidence changes, a causal theory fails testing, an intervention produces unintended consequences, jurisdiction is reassigned, or a more precise explanation becomes available. Earlier work should be preserved as part of the development record rather than silently overwritten.

The measure of the framework is not rhetorical force. Its measure is whether it improves diagnosis, supports better decisions, reduces recurring error, creates institutional memory, and contributes to durable problem correction.
