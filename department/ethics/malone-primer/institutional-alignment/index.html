Malone Primer

Institutional Alignment

Standards for keeping Malone institutions, leadership, policy, resources, programs, and cooperative relationships consistent with Malone Doctrine.

Introduction

The Institutional Alignment section explains how the institutions of the Malone Network are evaluated for consistency with Malone Doctrine, their own governing instruments, and the purposes for which they were established. Alignment requires more than verbal support. It must be visible in authority, leadership, policy, resource allocation, programs, conduct, safeguards, and measurable results.

Malone institutions may have different legal forms, governing bodies, missions, constituencies, powers, and operating methods. Alignment does not erase those differences or combine separate organizations into one entity. It establishes a disciplined method for determining whether each institution fulfills its proper role while contributing to the larger Malone mission without exceeding its jurisdiction or compromising another institution's independence.

Purpose and Scope

The purpose of institutional alignment is to prevent mission drift, contradictory policy, misuse of authority, resource diversion, leadership capture, operational duplication, unlawful coordination, and the gradual separation of institutional conduct from the doctrine and purposes that justify the institution's existence.

Alignment review may examine charters, bylaws, policies, leadership decisions, budgets, grantmaking, educational programs, public communications, political activity, partnerships, contracts, operating procedures, performance measures, succession arrangements, and relationships among Malone institutions and outside organizations.

Core Alignment Principle

Every Malone institution should perform the function assigned to it, remain within its lawful and doctrinal jurisdiction, preserve the separations required by its legal form and governing documents, and direct its authority and resources toward the purposes for which it was created.

Alignment is therefore both vertical and horizontal. Vertical alignment tests an institution against Malone Doctrine and its own governing authority. Horizontal alignment tests whether separate institutions cooperate appropriately, avoid conflicting action, respect boundaries, and contribute coherently to the Malone Network without becoming legally, financially, or operationally indistinguishable.

Separation and Coordination

Institutional alignment must not be confused with institutional consolidation. Separate institutions should maintain the records, accounts, assets, liabilities, governing decisions, personnel authority, contracts, compliance systems, and operational responsibility required by their legal and organizational structures.

Coordination should occur through documented and reviewable mechanisms such as grants, contracts, memoranda of understanding, shared standards, referrals, authorized joint initiatives, information-sharing rules, and clearly assigned responsibilities. No cooperative arrangement should obscure which institution made a decision, supplied resources, accepted risk, exercised authority, or remains accountable for the outcome.

Alignment Domains

A complete review should examine the institution across the following domains:

  1. Doctrinal alignment. Whether the institution's conduct is consistent with controlling Malone Doctrine and does not claim authority the Doctrine has not granted.
  2. Mission alignment. Whether the institution remains directed toward its stated purpose rather than convenience, prestige, revenue, factional preference, or unrelated activity.
  3. Legal alignment. Whether governance, programs, finances, political activity, charitable activity, education, employment, contracting, and reporting comply with applicable law and the institution's legal form.
  4. Governance alignment. Whether the proper board, officers, executives, committees, or other authorities make decisions within their delegated powers and preserve required oversight.
  5. Policy alignment. Whether internal policies translate doctrine, law, charter obligations, and institutional purpose into clear operating rules.
  6. Program alignment. Whether programs and services materially advance the institution's mission and address defined needs rather than merely producing activity.
  7. Financial alignment. Whether revenue, grants, expenditures, reserves, investments, assets, and compensation support authorized purposes and remain properly separated and documented.
  8. Leadership alignment. Whether leaders understand their duty, exercise authority competently, resist capture, disclose conflicts, preserve continuity, and remain accountable for results.
  9. Operational alignment. Whether structures, personnel, procedures, technology, facilities, and partnerships are capable of carrying out approved policy reliably and efficiently.
  10. Performance alignment. Whether the institution measures the outcomes that justify its existence and corrects activities that consume resources without producing sufficient benefit.

Institutional Responsibility Test

Before assigning responsibility to an institution, an alignment review should determine whether the institution possessed the relevant authority, knowledge, resources, opportunity, and duty to act. It should distinguish the institution itself from individual leaders, employees, chapters, affiliates, contractors, donors, recipients, members, and independent actors.

An institutional responsibility finding should ordinarily answer:

  • What governing body or office possessed decision-making authority?
  • What duty, policy, charter provision, agreement, or legal requirement applied?
  • What did the responsible authority know or reasonably have the means to know?
  • What resources, options, constraints, and foreseeable consequences existed?
  • Was the conduct authorized, tolerated, negligently ignored, concealed, corrected, or outside institutional control?
  • What records connect the decision or failure to the institution rather than only to an individual actor?
  • What corrective authority now exists, and which institution properly holds it?

Required Alignment Review Record

Each substantial alignment review should preserve a record sufficient for later verification, correction, and institutional action. The record should ordinarily include:

  1. Institution and review period. The exact entity, unit, program, relationship, or decision examined and the time covered.
  2. Governing authorities. The doctrine, charter, bylaws, law, policy, contract, grant terms, or board action controlling the review.
  3. Mission and assigned function. The purpose the institution or program is expected to serve.
  4. Observed conduct. The decisions, activities, expenditures, outputs, communications, or omissions under examination.
  5. Evidence record. The documents, financial records, minutes, testimony, data, communications, and other evidence relied upon.
  6. Alignment findings. The areas of full alignment, partial alignment, uncertainty, conflict, failure, or excess of authority.
  7. Cause analysis. The incentives, leadership decisions, structural defects, resource limitations, external pressures, or control failures that produced the condition.
  8. Risk assessment. The legal, ethical, financial, operational, reputational, strategic, and continuity risks created by misalignment.
  9. Corrective action. The responsible authority, required decision, safeguards, resources, timetable, and measures of correction.
  10. Disposition and follow-up. The final status, review date, responsible office, unresolved questions, and schedule for reassessment.

Alignment Review Workflow

  1. Identify the institution or activity. Define the exact entity, program, decision, agreement, or relationship under review.
  2. Establish controlling authority. Determine which doctrine, law, charter, policy, grant, contract, or governing decision applies.
  3. Define the intended function. State what the institution or activity was created and authorized to accomplish.
  4. Document actual conduct. Establish what was done, by whom, with what authority, resources, and results.
  5. Compare purpose with performance. Determine where conduct supports, neglects, contradicts, or exceeds the assigned mission.
  6. Identify causes and incentives. Separate isolated error from recurring structural, cultural, financial, leadership, or governance problems.
  7. Assess consequences. Measure the effect on beneficiaries, institutional integrity, legal compliance, resources, public trust, and the broader Malone Network.
  8. Assign corrective responsibility. Identify the institution and authority competent to approve, implement, monitor, and enforce correction.
  9. Implement safeguards. Revise policies, authority, reporting, training, controls, agreements, budgets, or leadership arrangements as necessary.
  10. Verify sustained alignment. Review whether correction produced durable results rather than temporary compliance or improved presentation.

Alignment Classifications

Reviews should use defined classifications so institutional status is not reduced to a vague judgment. Recommended classifications include:

  • Aligned. Authority, policy, conduct, resources, and results materially support the institution's assigned purpose.
  • Aligned with Observations. No material conflict exists, but improvements or monitoring are advisable.
  • Partially Aligned. The institution generally supports its mission but contains material gaps, contradictions, or underperformance.
  • At Risk of Misalignment. Current incentives, leadership, controls, resources, or relationships create a substantial likelihood of future conflict.
  • Misaligned. Material conduct, policy, spending, leadership, or structure conflicts with controlling purpose or authority.
  • Outside Jurisdiction. The matter belongs to another institution, authority, or legal process and should be referred rather than absorbed.
  • Indeterminate. Available evidence is insufficient to support a responsible conclusion.
  • Corrective Action in Progress. Misalignment has been identified and an approved remedy is being implemented and monitored.
  • Realigned. Corrective action has produced verified and sustained conformity.
  • Separation Required. The activity, program, relationship, or unit cannot remain within the institution without continuing conflict or unacceptable risk.

Indicators of Misalignment

An indicator is a reason for examination, not automatic proof of misconduct or institutional failure. Material warning signs may include:

  • Programs that cannot be connected to the institution's stated mission or governing authority.
  • Leaders exercising powers not granted by charter, policy, delegation, or law.
  • Funds, personnel, records, contracts, or assets moving among separate institutions without adequate documentation and approval.
  • Public communications that contradict governing policy or create false impressions of authority, endorsement, ownership, or legal relationship.
  • Repeated exceptions benefiting particular leaders, donors, factions, partners, or recipients.
  • Performance reporting based on activity, publicity, or expenditure rather than meaningful outcomes.
  • Resistance to audit, documentation, conflict disclosure, correction, or independent review.
  • Leadership succession controlled by loyalty networks rather than competence, duty, and governing process.
  • Dependence on one person, donor, vendor, political faction, outside institution, or source of legitimacy.
  • Cooperation arrangements that obscure accountability or compromise institutional independence.

Corrective Responses

Corrective action should be proportional to the nature, severity, duration, and consequences of the misalignment. The purpose is to restore lawful and effective performance, preserve institutional integrity, and prevent recurrence rather than merely assign blame.

Available responses may include:

  • Clarifying mission, jurisdiction, delegation, policy, or institutional boundaries.
  • Revising a charter, bylaw, policy, procedure, grant agreement, contract, or memorandum of understanding through the proper authority.
  • Separating accounts, personnel, records, programs, communications, assets, or decision rights.
  • Changing budgets, performance measures, reporting duties, approval thresholds, or audit controls.
  • Providing leadership instruction, technical assistance, compliance review, or operational support.
  • Suspending, restructuring, transferring, or terminating a program or relationship.
  • Removing or replacing responsible leaders through lawful governing procedures.
  • Referring legal, ethical, financial, or disciplinary matters to the competent authority.
  • Publishing a correction, revised finding, or institutional explanation when public understanding has been materially affected.

Alignment Across the Malone Network

Network alignment requires a clear division of labor. The Malone Foundation, Malone Global University, the Malone Political Party, the Church of Malone, and any future Malone institutions should not duplicate one another merely because they share a broad mission. Each institution should possess a defined purpose, constituency, authority, funding model, accountability structure, and method of contribution.

Cooperation should be evaluated according to institutional competence. Education should be conducted through properly authorized educational structures; grantmaking through the competent charitable institution; political activity through the appropriate political organization; religious activity through the separate religious institution; and implementation through the institution legally and operationally capable of accepting responsibility for the work.

Authority Limitation

Material published in this section is explanatory and evaluative. It does not by itself amend Malone Doctrine, alter an institutional charter, direct a board, transfer assets, remove an officer, authorize expenditure, impose discipline, or create a legal relationship among separate institutions.

Binding institutional action must be taken by the competent authority through the governing process applicable to the institution, doctrine, policy, contract, grant, or legal matter concerned.

Publication and Revision Control

Alignment standards and reviews should be revised when doctrine changes, governing documents are amended, institutions are created or dissolved, legal requirements change, material evidence emerges, corrective action alters the condition, or a previously accepted relationship proves ineffective or unsafe.

The success of this section is measured by whether it keeps institutional purpose, authority, resources, conduct, and outcomes connected; preserves lawful separation; improves coordination; exposes drift before it becomes capture or failure; and creates a reliable record for correction, accountability, and succession.
