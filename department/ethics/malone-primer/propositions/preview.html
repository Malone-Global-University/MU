Malone Primer

Propositions

Candidate claims developed through research, argument, criticism, and testing before they may be considered for formal inclusion in Malone Doctrine.

Introduction

The Propositions section is the principal development space for claims that may contribute to Malone Doctrine but have not acquired doctrinal authority. A proposition states a position clearly enough to be examined, challenged, supported, narrowed, revised, rejected, or advanced through formal review.

Propositions allow the Malone Primer to preserve intellectual development without confusing exploratory analysis with adopted doctrine. They may originate in research, case studies, policy briefs, institutional experience, historical analysis, strategic necessity, moral reasoning, or identified failures in an existing rule or practice.

Role of Propositions

A proposition converts a broad concern or preliminary insight into a definite claim that can be evaluated. It should identify what is asserted, why the claim matters, the evidence and reasoning supporting it, the conditions under which it applies, and the consequences that would follow if it were accepted.

Propositions may clarify values, explain causal relationships, define strategic priorities, identify institutional duties, recommend governing standards, or challenge assumptions embedded in current policy. Their purpose is disciplined development rather than rhetorical declaration.

Authority Limitation

A proposition is explanatory and developmental. It is not an axiom, principle, maxim, rule, procedure, authorized interpretation, article, implementation directive, or other binding component of Malone Doctrine merely because it is published in this section.

No proposition may supersede adopted doctrine, amend an institutional charter, create operational authority, impose a duty, or authorize action unless the competent Malone authority formally adopts the relevant conclusion through the prescribed doctrinal or institutional process.

What Qualifies as a Proposition

A qualifying proposition should present one principal claim or a tightly related set of claims that can be evaluated independently. It should be specific enough to permit disagreement and sufficiently bounded to avoid becoming an undefined statement of preference.

A proposition should ordinarily:

  • State the proposed claim in direct and testable language.
  • Define material terms, affected interests, and intended scope.
  • Identify the problem, value, or institutional need that generated the claim.
  • Present supporting evidence and the reasoning connecting that evidence to the conclusion.
  • Address meaningful counterarguments, competing explanations, and adverse evidence.
  • State exceptions, limitations, risks, and foreseeable unintended consequences.
  • Explain its relationship to existing Malone Doctrine and other Primer work.
  • Identify the decision, policy, principle, rule, or institutional response it may support.

Required Proposition Record

Each published proposition should preserve a record that allows later reviewers to reconstruct its origin, reasoning, development, and disposition. The record should ordinarily contain:

  1. Identifier and title. A stable reference number and concise descriptive title.
  2. Proposition statement. The exact claim being advanced for examination.
  3. Purpose. The problem, question, contradiction, or strategic need addressed.
  4. Definitions and scope. The meaning of controlling terms and the boundaries of application.
  5. Supporting case. The evidence, moral reasoning, historical analysis, and causal argument supporting the claim.
  6. Opposing case. The strongest counterarguments, contrary evidence, and alternative explanations.
  7. Conditions and exceptions. Circumstances under which the proposition may not apply or may require qualification.
  8. Institutional implications. The institutions, authorities, resources, duties, or safeguards affected by adoption.
  9. Measures and tests. The evidence or outcomes that would support, weaken, falsify, or require revision of the claim.
  10. Status and history. The current review status, responsible reviewer, revision record, and final disposition.

Development Workflow

  1. Origination. Record the observation, research finding, case, institutional problem, or doctrinal question that produced the proposed claim.
  2. Initial formulation. Convert the underlying concern into a clear proposition with defined terms and an explicit scope.
  3. Evidence review. Determine whether the supporting facts are reliable, representative, current, and sufficient for the strength of the claim.
  4. Adversarial examination. Develop the strongest reasonable objection rather than relying on weak or convenient opposition.
  5. Scope control. Narrow, divide, qualify, or reject claims that extend beyond the available evidence or the jurisdiction of Malone Doctrine.
  6. Compatibility review. Test the proposition against higher doctrinal authority, existing principles, institutional charters, and related propositions.
  7. Practical testing. Examine likely outcomes, implementation burdens, risks, incentives, safeguards, and measures of success.
  8. Disposition. Retain the proposition for further development, revise it, divide it, reject it, archive it, or recommend formal adoption of an appropriate doctrinal or institutional instrument.

Status Classifications

Every proposition should display a status so readers can distinguish preliminary thought from mature recommendations and historical records. Recommended statuses include:

  • Draft. Initial formulation that has not completed internal review.
  • Under Review. Active evidence, logic, scope, and compatibility examination.
  • Contested. Material disagreement or unresolved counterevidence remains.
  • Revised. Substantively changed in response to review or new evidence.
  • Recommended. Review supports advancement to the competent adopting authority.
  • Deferred. Potentially valid but awaiting evidence, institutional capacity, or a related decision.
  • Rejected. Not accepted because of error, insufficient support, conflict, overbreadth, or unacceptable consequences.
  • Superseded. Replaced by a later proposition or more precise formulation.
  • Adopted Elsewhere. The relevant conclusion has entered an authoritative doctrinal or institutional instrument.
  • Archived. Retained for historical and developmental reference without active consideration.

Review Standards

Review should test more than whether a proposition sounds persuasive or agrees with an existing preference. The reviewing authority should examine factual accuracy, logical validity, ethical consistency, scope, jurisdiction, strategic necessity, enforceability, administrative burden, institutional competence, foreseeable abuse, and the consequences of both adoption and rejection.

The required level of support should rise with the breadth of the claim, the severity of the proposed response, the number of people or institutions affected, and the difficulty of reversing an error. Claims that cannot presently be verified may remain identified as hypotheses, questions, or provisional propositions rather than being promoted into apparent certainty.

Relationship to Other Primer Work

  • Problem Framework

    Establishes the analytical process through which observed conditions are defined, investigated, classified, and prepared for corrective action.

  • The Seven Strategic Problems

    Provides the principal diagnostic containers from which many strategic and institutional propositions may arise.

  • Research

    Supplies evidence, historical context, competing explanations, and analytical findings used to support or challenge propositions.

  • Case Studies

    Tests general claims against concrete events, institutions, policies, and outcomes while identifying limits on generalization.

  • Policy Briefs

    Apply developed arguments to defined decisions, policies, institutional questions, and strategic priorities.

Foundational Studies

  • Problem-Driven Doctrinal Engineering

    Explains how concrete problems can generate candidate doctrinal claims while guarding against premature or overbroad universal rules.

  • The Doctrine Development Cycle

    Describes the progression from observation and analysis through proposition, testing, adoption, application, and controlled revision.

  • Confirm and Verify

    Establishes the verification discipline required before a proposition may be treated as a reliable basis for judgment or policy.

  • Threat Indicators Versus Proof

    Demonstrates why preliminary indicators, operational cautions, and proven conclusions must remain analytically distinct.

Published Propositions

No standalone proposition papers are presently listed in this index. Future entries should display their identifier, title, current status, publication date, revision date, and relationship to any adopted doctrinal instrument.

Publication and Revision Control

Material revisions should receive a new version number and a dated change record. Rejected, superseded, or adopted propositions should ordinarily remain available as part of the institutional development history unless confidentiality, legal restrictions, safety, or record-management requirements justify restricted access.

The success of this section is measured by the quality of the claims it clarifies, the errors it exposes before adoption, the institutional memory it preserves, and the reliability of the doctrine and policy eventually produced from its work.
