Harriet Tubman and the $40,000 Bounty Myth

Published: August 9, 2026

Overview

One of the most dramatic stories associated with Harriet Tubman claims that Southern slaveholders placed a $40,000 “dead or alive” bounty on her head because of her repeated Underground Railroad missions. The story reinforces Tubman's image as one of the most feared enemies of American slavery.

The documentary evidence does not support the claim.

Current National Park Service research identifies only one documented reward notice for Tubman: an October 3, 1849 runaway advertisement issued after Tubman, then identified as “Minty,” escaped with her brothers Ben and Harry. Their enslaver, Eliza Brodess, offered $100 for each person if captured outside Maryland and $50 each if captured within the state.

No authenticated notice has been found offering $40,000 for Tubman, and the documented 1849 advertisement did not say “dead or alive.”

The $40,000 figure can instead be traced to an admiring 1867 letter by abolitionist Sallie Holley, written years after slavery had been abolished. Holley was praising Tubman's extraordinary courage while supporters were attempting to obtain compensation for her Civil War service. A rhetorical statement about what slaveholders might have been willing to offer was later transformed through repetition into an actual historical bounty.

The Claim

The familiar version of the story generally states:

Harriet Tubman became so dangerous to Southern slaveholders that they placed a $40,000 dead-or-alive reward on her head.

Variations include:

  • Maryland slaveholders collectively offered $40,000 for Tubman's capture;
  • Southern states offered a combined $40,000 reward;
  • the federal government or slave catchers posted a $40,000 bounty;
  • Tubman became the most wanted woman in America;
  • a wanted poster offered $40,000 “dead or alive” for Harriet Tubman;
  • the reward appeared after Tubman repeatedly returned south to rescue enslaved people;
  • and the size of the bounty proves that slaveholders knew Tubman was the Underground Railroad's principal operative on Maryland's Eastern Shore.

The story contains several separate claims.

Each needs evidence.

Who offered the money?

When was it offered?

Where was the notice published?

What offense or escape produced the reward?

Did the notice identify Harriet Tubman?

Did it actually use the phrase “dead or alive”?

Once those questions are asked, the famous story begins to collapse.

What a Real Runaway Reward Notice Looks Like

Slaveholders routinely advertised for the capture and return of people who escaped slavery.

These advertisements were practical documents.

A useful notice typically provided information that would help strangers, law-enforcement officers, jailers, professional slave catchers, and other members of the public identify the person being sought.

Notices could include:

  • the person's name;
  • age;
  • height;
  • complexion;
  • physical characteristics;
  • scars or other identifying marks;
  • clothing;
  • occupation or skills;
  • suspected destination;
  • circumstances of escape;
  • the amount of the reward;
  • and instructions concerning where the captured person should be delivered.

Thousands of such advertisements survive.

This means that a claim concerning a particularly enormous reward should normally be approached as a documentary question rather than as folklore.

Where is the advertisement?

The Documented 1849 Reward

Harriet Tubman's actual reward notice survives.

On September 17, 1849, Tubman and two of her brothers, Ben and Henry, attempted to escape slavery.

Tubman was then widely known by the nickname “Minty,” derived from her birth name Araminta.

Her brothers were identified in the advertisement as Ben and Harry.

On October 3, 1849, the Cambridge Democrat published an advertisement placed by Eliza Ann Brodess seeking their capture.

Its headline offered:

Three Hundred Dollars Reward.

The $300 figure was not a reward for Harriet Tubman alone.

It represented the maximum combined reward for three people.

The Reward Was $100 for Tubman, Not $40,000

The advertisement specified the payment more precisely.

Brodess offered $100 for each of the three freedom seekers if captured outside Maryland.

She offered $50 for each if captured within Maryland.

Therefore, the documented reward associated with Tubman personally was:

  • $100 if she was captured outside Maryland; or
  • $50 if she was captured within Maryland.

The maximum $300 headline resulted from multiplying the $100 out-of-state reward by three people.

Social-media posts sometimes reproduce the advertisement while calling it a “$300 bounty on Harriet Tubman.”

Even that description is imprecise.

Tubman was one of three people covered by the notice.

The Advertisement Did Not Say “Dead or Alive”

The phrase “dead or alive” is another later addition to the legend.

The 1849 notice offered payment for the return of Tubman and her brothers.

It instructed captors to lodge them in specified Maryland jails.

That requirement presupposed living captives.

The documented advertisement therefore does not support the familiar dramatic image of a poster authorizing Tubman's killing.

No authenticated $40,000 “dead or alive” poster has been produced by the historians and institutions that have investigated the claim.

The First Escape Attempt Failed

Another detail is frequently overlooked.

The October 1849 advertisement followed Tubman's first attempted escape with her brothers.

Ben and Henry became fearful and decided to return.

Tubman returned with them.

A short time later, she escaped again by herself and successfully reached Pennsylvania.

The surviving advertisement therefore relates to an early episode in Tubman's life before she became famous as an Underground Railroad conductor.

It was not issued in response to a decade of rescue missions.

No Documented $40,000 Poster Has Been Found

Current National Park Service research is explicit on this point.

Its Myths & Facts About Harriet Tubman publication identifies the $40,000 “dead or alive” story as a myth and states that the October 1849 advertisement is the only documented reward for Tubman's capture.

This does not mean a historian can prove that nobody ever privately discussed offering money for Tubman.

Historical absence should be described carefully.

The defensible conclusion is narrower:

No authenticated evidence has been found establishing the famous $40,000 bounty.

That is very different from having a surviving $40,000 wanted poster whose authenticity is merely disputed.

There is no such authenticated poster.

Slaveholders Did Not Know Tubman Was Behind the Escapes

The bounty legend contains another historical problem.

It assumes that Maryland slaveholders knew Harriet Tubman was repeatedly entering the Eastern Shore and organizing escapes.

Current National Park Service research concludes that they did not.

Tubman's success depended heavily upon secrecy.

She used:

  • trusted family and community contacts;
  • coded communication;
  • careful timing;
  • disguises;
  • multiple forms of transportation;
  • knowledge of waterways and terrain;
  • and networks of Black and white assistance.

Maryland slaveholders knew that people were escaping.

They did not necessarily know who was coordinating particular escapes.

A secret conductor who had successfully concealed her identity would be a poor candidate for a highly publicized wanted notice identifying her as the mastermind.

“Minty Ross” and “Harriet Tubman” Complicate the Story

The surviving advertisement identifies Tubman as “Minty.”

This was the name by which people in the community had known her before her escape.

Harriet was the name she adopted as an adult, probably around the period of her marriage to John Tubman.

Her growing reputation among Northern abolitionists later centered on the name Harriet Tubman and the nickname “Moses.”

The transformation from locally known enslaved woman to nationally admired abolitionist helps explain why later narratives can mistakenly project her eventual fame backward into the early years of her Underground Railroad activity.

Her enemies did not necessarily possess the same knowledge of “Harriet Tubman” in 1852 or 1855 that later Americans possessed after her story became famous.

Where the $40,000 Figure Came From

Unlike many legends whose origins are impossible to identify, historians can trace the $40,000 figure to a specific postwar source.

In November 1867, abolitionist Sallie Holley wrote a letter praising Tubman.

The letter appeared in the National Anti-Slavery Standard.

Holley described Tubman's repeated returns into slaveholding territory and celebrated her extraordinary courage.

In that context, Holley wrote that:

“Forty thousand dollars was not too great a reward for the Maryland slaveholders to offer for her.”

That sentence became the seed from which the later bounty story grew.

Holley Did Not Reproduce a Wanted Notice

The grammatical distinction is critical.

Holley did not write:

Maryland slaveholders offered $40,000 for Harriet Tubman.

She wrote that $40,000 was not too great a reward for them to offer.

That is rhetoric.

It communicates the magnitude of the danger Tubman represented and the bravery Holley believed Tubman had demonstrated.

It does not provide:

  • the names of slaveholders who offered the money;
  • a date on which the bounty was created;
  • a newspaper carrying the supposed notice;
  • the terms of payment;
  • a government authorization;
  • or a copy of the reward advertisement.

An expression of what someone could reasonably have offered was gradually transformed into an assertion of what someone actually offered.

The Statement Was Written After Slavery Ended

The date of Holley's letter is another important clue.

She wrote in 1867.

By then:

  • the Civil War had ended;
  • the Confederacy had been defeated;
  • the Thirteenth Amendment had abolished slavery;
  • and Tubman's Underground Railroad rescue missions had ended years earlier.

Holley was therefore not reporting the announcement of a new bounty.

She was looking backward on Tubman's antebellum work.

The distinction between contemporary wanted notice and retrospective praise is fundamental.

Why Holley Was Praising Tubman

Tubman's life after the Civil War contained an extraordinary injustice.

She had provided substantial service to the United States during the war as a nurse, scout, intelligence operative, recruiter, and guide.

Yet she struggled for years to obtain adequate federal compensation for that service.

Friends and abolitionist allies publicized her accomplishments and sought financial support for her.

Holley's letter belongs to that advocacy context.

She was presenting Tubman as a woman whose service, courage, and sacrifice deserved national recognition.

Dramatic language served that purpose.

Sarah Bradford Helped Preserve the Statement

Sarah Hopkins Bradford's early biography, Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman, reproduced material from Tubman's friends and supporters, including Holley's praise.

Bradford's book was enormously important in preserving information about Tubman's life and raising money for her.

It also contributed to the formation of Tubman's heroic public legend.

As stories moved from abolitionist testimony to biography and later into commemorative histories, rhetorical descriptions could lose their original context.

Eventually, the claim no longer appeared as:

An abolitionist thought $40,000 would not have been too great a reward.

It appeared as:

There was a $40,000 reward.

Later versions added:

Dead or alive.

The legend became more specific as the evidence behind it became less specific.

This Is a Classic Example of Citation Drift

The development of the bounty story illustrates a common mechanism of historical misinformation.

It can be described as citation drift.

The process works like this:

  1. A source makes a qualified or rhetorical statement.
  2. A later writer summarizes the statement.
  3. The qualification disappears.
  4. The summary is cited instead of the original source.
  5. Later writers copy the simplified version.
  6. Eventually the statement becomes an established “fact.”
  7. Social media reproduces the mature legend without any source at all.

At the end of the process, thousands of people can repeat the same false claim while believing that repetition represents independent confirmation.

The “Dead or Alive” Language Appears to Be an Additional Embellishment

Even if the $40,000 figure were accepted, a second question would remain:

Where did “dead or alive” come from?

The documented 1849 advertisement does not contain that language.

Holley's 1867 statement does not establish it either.

The phrase fits popular imagery associated with wanted posters and fugitives, especially in later Western folklore and entertainment.

Attaching it to Tubman makes an already dramatic story even more dramatic.

But historically effective language is not automatically historically authentic language.

The Size of the Alleged Reward Should Raise Questions

Forty thousand dollars represented an extraordinary sum in the nineteenth century.

That alone does not prove the claim impossible.

It does mean that a responsible historian should expect unusually strong documentation.

A reward of that magnitude would create incentives for:

  • professional slave catchers;
  • law-enforcement officers;
  • private detectives;
  • newspaper publishers;
  • informants;
  • and ordinary citizens seeking the money.

A secret bounty known to nobody would serve little practical purpose.

A public bounty of extraordinary size should leave a substantial documentary trail.

That trail has not been found.

The John Wilkes Booth Comparison

The scale becomes clearer when compared with one of the most famous reward campaigns in nineteenth-century American history.

After President Abraham Lincoln was assassinated in April 1865, the United States War Department issued a nationally distributed reward broadside offering a total of $100,000 for information leading to the capture of the assassination conspirators.

The Library of Congress preserves that broadside.

National Park Service researchers have specifically used the comparison with the Lincoln assassination manhunt to illustrate how extraordinary a genuine $40,000 Tubman bounty would have been.

The point is not that no private slaveholder could theoretically have offered a large sum.

It is that a bounty approaching the scale of a major national manhunt should be expected to produce substantial contemporary documentation.

Tubman Was Certainly in Danger Without a $40,000 Bounty

Rejecting the bounty legend should not create the impression that Tubman travelled safely.

She faced enormous danger every time she returned to Maryland.

Under slavery and federal fugitive-slave law, capture could have resulted in:

  • return to slavery;
  • physical punishment;
  • sale away from her family;
  • imprisonment;
  • violence during capture;
  • or prosecution and retaliation against people assisting her.

The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 made even free states less secure for people who had escaped slavery.

Slave catchers had financial incentives to capture freedom seekers whether or not a spectacular individual bounty existed.

Tubman's courage therefore does not depend upon a $40,000 wanted poster.

Ordinary Rewards Were Dangerous Enough

The documented 1849 notice illustrates how slavery created a market around human capture.

Slaveholders could turn members of the public into potential pursuers by attaching money to the return of a freedom seeker.

A person attempting escape therefore did not need to be nationally famous to face danger.

Innkeepers, jailers, ship captains, railroad workers, police officers, neighbors, strangers, and professional slave catchers could all potentially become threats.

The remarkable feature of Tubman's career is not that a legendary fortune supposedly awaited whoever captured her.

It is that she repeatedly entered a legal environment already designed to capture and return people like her.

Her Success Depended on Not Becoming Publicly Identifiable

The romantic bounty story can actually obscure one of Tubman's greatest strengths.

She survived because she was effective at clandestine operations.

Tubman did not conduct her missions as a publicly identifiable celebrity moving openly through Maryland.

She depended upon:

  • discretion;
  • trusted local knowledge;
  • careful movement;
  • community intelligence;
  • concealment;
  • misdirection;
  • and secrecy about who was responsible for particular escapes.

The image of every slaveholder on the Eastern Shore circulating a famous portrait of “Moses” with $40,000 attached to it makes an exciting movie premise.

It is inconsistent with the clandestine character of her actual work.

There Was No Modern Police Database

Another source of anachronism is the tendency to imagine nineteenth-century manhunts through modern law-enforcement systems.

There was no national digital database containing Tubman's photograph, aliases, fingerprints, travel history, and known associates.

Identification relied upon:

  • written descriptions;
  • printed notices;
  • personal recognition;
  • informants;
  • local knowledge;
  • and physical capture.

Tubman could therefore move through environments where people might know that freedom seekers were escaping without knowing that the woman standing before them was the person helping organize those escapes.

Tubman Used Disguises and Misdirection

Tubman's later accounts describe the use of disguises and other techniques to avoid recognition.

These stories should be evaluated individually like any historical anecdote, but they fit the broader operational necessity of her work.

Someone returning secretly to the same region multiple times needed to prevent casual recognition.

A gigantic public bounty naming Tubman as the principal Underground Railroad operative would have made those operations dramatically more difficult.

The success of her missions is itself consistent with the National Park Service conclusion that local slaveholders did not understand the extent of her role.

The Myth Became Part of Tubman's Heroic Image

By the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Tubman had become a national heroic figure.

Her story naturally accumulated symbols that communicated danger and courage:

  • the nickname “Moses”;
  • the image of secret journeys through the night;
  • armed slave catchers pursuing her;
  • a pistol carried for protection;
  • a huge bounty on her head;
  • and hundreds of people supposedly rescued personally.

Some of those elements are well documented.

Others are exaggerated.

Heroic narratives often become more dramatic because each addition reinforces the central truth that the person was courageous.

But emotional consistency is not documentary proof.

Even Reputable Institutions Have Repeated the Claim

The $40,000 bounty story demonstrates why misinformation cannot be evaluated solely by asking whether it appears on a respectable website.

Older educational and institutional material has sometimes repeated the $40,000 figure.

Legacy pages from reputable historical organizations and government institutions can preserve claims that later research has revised.

This does not mean the institutions are useless.

It means historical knowledge is revised as researchers locate sources, reconstruct chronology, and distinguish contemporary evidence from later tradition.

When two institutional pages conflict, readers should examine which one provides provenance and engages the underlying evidence.

The current National Park Service Tubman myth-and-fact research does exactly that: it identifies the surviving 1849 advertisement and traces the later $40,000 figure to Holley's 1867 statement.

Authority Is Not a Substitute for Evidence

This case provides an important lesson for historical research.

A claim is not automatically true because it appears on:

  • a government website;
  • a museum page;
  • a university website;
  • a textbook;
  • a documentary;
  • or a widely cited biography.

Those sources are generally more promising places to begin research than an anonymous social-media account.

But responsible historical analysis still asks:

What evidence does the source provide?

An institutional source that reproduces a legend without provenance can be weaker on a particular claim than a later source that identifies and analyzes the original documents.

Search for the Original Notice

The simplest way to investigate a viral bounty claim is to request the supposed primary source.

Ask:

  • What newspaper printed the $40,000 notice?
  • What date was it printed?
  • Who signed it?
  • Who supplied the $40,000?
  • Was it one slaveholder or a group?
  • Was it a state government?
  • Did the notice identify Tubman by name?
  • What physical description did it provide?
  • Where was she to be delivered?
  • Did it say “dead or alive”?
  • Which archive holds the original?

The famous story cannot answer those questions because the authenticated document it presupposes has not been found.

Do Not Confuse Repetition With Multiple Sources

Searching the Internet for “Harriet Tubman $40,000 bounty” produces many apparent confirmations.

That can create the impression that numerous historians independently established the claim.

But ten websites can all descend from one earlier statement.

A textbook can cite another textbook.

A museum can reproduce an old biography.

A social-media post can quote a government webpage that itself inherited the claim from a commemorative tradition.

The number of pages repeating a claim therefore tells the historian little about how many independent pieces of evidence support it.

The Known Advertisement Is Stronger Evidence Than a Famous Number

The 1849 notice possesses the characteristics missing from the $40,000 legend.

Historians know:

  • the date;
  • the newspaper;
  • the person who placed it;
  • the names used for the freedom seekers;
  • their physical descriptions;
  • the amount offered;
  • the geographic conditions attached to payment;
  • and where captured individuals were supposed to be jailed.

That is provenance.

The $40,000 claim supplies a memorable number.

The $100 claim supplies a document.

Historical method favors the document.

The Absence of a Huge Bounty Does Not Reduce Tubman's Importance

As with the exaggerated claim that Tubman personally rescued 300 people, correcting the bounty figure can initially sound as though historians are reducing her accomplishments.

They are not.

Tubman's documented life includes:

  • escaping slavery herself;
  • returning repeatedly to Maryland to rescue family and friends;
  • personally leading approximately 70 people to freedom;
  • providing instructions to additional freedom seekers;
  • evading slave catchers and federal fugitive-slave enforcement;
  • working with abolitionist networks;
  • serving the United States during the Civil War;
  • gathering military intelligence;
  • helping guide the Combahee River Raid;
  • advocating for formerly enslaved people;
  • and supporting women's suffrage.

Her courage is established by what she did.

It does not require an invented wanted poster.

The Real Story May Be More Impressive

The bounty legend presents Tubman as courageous because she supposedly kept operating while everyone knew exactly who she was and $40,000 awaited her captor.

The documented history reveals a different skill.

Tubman repeatedly penetrated a hostile slaveholding society without allowing the people searching for freedom seekers to discover the full extent of her role.

That required more than courage.

It required operational discipline.

She understood whom to trust, where to move, how to communicate, when to travel, and how to avoid drawing attention.

The absence of a giant bounty is therefore not evidence that slaveholders considered her insignificant.

It is partly evidence that they failed to understand who was defeating them.

  • Eliza Ann Brodess placed a runaway advertisement for “Minty,” Ben, and Harry in the Cambridge Democrat on October 3, 1849.
  • The advertisement offered a maximum combined reward of $300 for the three freedom seekers.
  • It offered $100 for each individual if captured outside Maryland.
  • It offered $50 for each individual if captured within Maryland.
  • The notice instructed captors to lodge the freedom seekers in specified Maryland jails.
  • The surviving notice did not offer $40,000 for Tubman.
  • It did not describe the reward as “dead or alive.”
  • Tubman's brothers turned back during the first escape attempt, and Tubman later escaped successfully on her own.
  • The documented reward predates Tubman's later fame as an Underground Railroad conductor.
  • Current National Park Service research identifies the 1849 advertisement as the only documented reward notice for Tubman's capture.
  • No authenticated $40,000 wanted poster for Harriet Tubman has been found.
  • National Park Service research states that Maryland slaveholders did not know Tubman was responsible for many of the escapes she helped organize.
  • The $40,000 figure can be traced to abolitionist Sallie Holley's 1867 praise of Tubman.
  • Holley wrote that $40,000 would not have been too great a reward for Maryland slaveholders to offer.
  • Holley did not reproduce an actual $40,000 reward notice.
  • Her statement was written after the Civil War and abolition of slavery.
  • The statement appeared in the context of efforts to recognize and support Tubman after her wartime service.
  • Later biographical and commemorative tradition converted the rhetorical figure into a supposedly literal bounty.
  • Some older institutional and educational sources have repeated the $40,000 claim, demonstrating how historical legends can persist even in generally reputable sources.
  • Tubman nevertheless faced substantial genuine danger from slaveholders, slave catchers, fugitive-slave laws, and the possibility of re-enslavement.

Verdict

Claim: Harriet Tubman had a $40,000 “dead or alive” bounty placed on her head because slaveholders knew she was repeatedly rescuing enslaved people through the Underground Railroad.

Verdict: False.

No authenticated $40,000 reward notice for Harriet Tubman has been found.

The only documented reward identified by current National Park Service research comes from an October 3, 1849 runaway advertisement seeking Tubman—then identified as “Minty”—and her brothers Ben and Harry.

The advertisement offered $100 for each person if captured outside Maryland and $50 each if captured inside the state.

It did not use the phrase “dead or alive.”

The famous $40,000 figure appeared years later in an 1867 letter by abolitionist Sallie Holley. Holley was praising Tubman's courage and stated that such a sum would not have been too great for Maryland slaveholders to offer.

A rhetorical statement about Tubman's value as an enemy of slavery was gradually transformed into a supposed historical wanted poster.

The accurate historical conclusion is:

Harriet Tubman faced genuine and extraordinary danger, but there is no documented $40,000 “dead or alive” bounty on her head. The surviving 1849 reward notice offered at most $100 for Tubman's individual capture, while the famous $40,000 figure originated in later postwar praise rather than an authenticated slaveholder reward.

Media Literacy Principle

A famous number is not evidence of a historical bounty. Ask for the original notice: who offered the money, when it was offered, where it was published, and what its terms actually said. A later statement that a reward would have been justified is not proof that the reward was offered.
