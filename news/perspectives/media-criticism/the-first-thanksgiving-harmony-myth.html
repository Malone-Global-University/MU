The “First Thanksgiving” Harmony Myth

Published: August 9, 2026

Overview

One of the most familiar American historical narratives describes the “First Thanksgiving” as a harmonious founding celebration in which grateful Pilgrims invited friendly Indians to dinner, everyone shared food and friendship, and the two peoples established a peaceful relationship that helped create the United States.

A real three-day harvest gathering involving Plymouth colonists and Wampanoag people occurred in the fall of 1621. Cooperation between the groups was also real. Wampanoag leaders had entered into a political alliance with Plymouth earlier that year, Indigenous knowledge and assistance materially benefited the vulnerable English settlement, and Ousamequin—called Massasoit by the English—attended the harvest gathering with approximately ninety Wampanoag men. They contributed five deer to the feast.

But the later national story removes most of the circumstances that explain why the encounter occurred.

The Wampanoag were not anonymous “Indians” appearing suddenly in American history when the Mayflower arrived. They were a politically organized Native people whose communities had inhabited the region for thousands of years. They had already experienced decades of contact with European fishermen, traders, explorers, and kidnappers. From 1616 to 1619, epidemic disease devastated Wampanoag communities. Patuxet, the Native village where the English established Plymouth, had been largely emptied by that catastrophe.

Ousamequin's decision to develop relations with Plymouth was therefore not simply an expression of spontaneous friendship. It was also diplomacy. Both sides possessed interests, vulnerabilities, and potential enemies. Their 1621 relationship included genuine cooperation within a strategic political alliance.

Nor did the harvest gathering establish permanent harmony. Relations continued for decades and included periods of cooperation, negotiation, dependence, suspicion, land transfer, missionary activity, demographic change, and increasing conflict. By the 1670s, English colonial expansion and deteriorating political relations helped produce the devastating war commonly known as King Philip's War.

The myth is therefore not that Wampanoag people and Plymouth colonists ever ate together peacefully. They did. The myth is that this temporary cooperation represented a simple founding friendship that accurately describes the larger history of colonization that followed.

The Claim

The traditional schoolbook version of the story usually takes a form similar to this:

The Pilgrims came to America seeking freedom. Friendly Indians taught them how to survive. After their first successful harvest, the Pilgrims invited the Indians to a Thanksgiving dinner. Everyone ate together, gave thanks, and became lasting friends.

Variations include:

  • the Pilgrims discovered an essentially empty wilderness;
  • the Indians encountered the Pilgrims for the first time in 1620;
  • Squanto happily volunteered to teach the newcomers how to survive;
  • the Pilgrims formally invited the Wampanoag to a Thanksgiving dinner;
  • the feast closely resembled a modern Thanksgiving meal;
  • everyone gathered primarily to express shared religious gratitude;
  • the meal established a lasting friendship between Indians and colonists;
  • the event represents peaceful cooperation at the founding of America;
  • and serious conflict developed only much later for reasons unrelated to colonization.

Some pieces of this narrative are based upon real events.

Others are later additions.

The problem comes from combining the pieces into a reassuring national origin story and treating that story as a complete description of the historical relationship.

A Real Harvest Celebration Happened

Correcting the mythology does not require denying the central event.

Plymouth colonists held a harvest celebration in the fall of 1621.

Edward Winslow left the only surviving eyewitness account specifically describing the gathering.

Winslow reported that after the harvest was gathered, Governor William Bradford sent four men hunting birds so that the community could celebrate its success.

During the gathering, Ousamequin arrived with approximately ninety men.

Winslow described the visitors remaining:

“for three days”

and reported that the Wampanoag men hunted five deer and contributed them to the celebration.

Thus, the image of Wampanoag and English people participating in the same harvest event has a genuine historical foundation.

There Is Only One Eyewitness Account of the Feast

Many modern depictions present the 1621 gathering in extraordinary detail.

We see:

  • long tables;
  • precise seating arrangements;
  • specific prayers;
  • Native families approaching the settlement;
  • particular foods;
  • formal invitations;
  • and conversations between colonists and Wampanoag guests.

Most of these details cannot be established from the surviving evidence.

Plimoth Patuxet Museums emphasizes that only one English eyewitness account of the 1621 harvest celebration survives.

No contemporary Wampanoag written account of the event survives.

That leaves substantial uncertainty about what many participants thought, said, ate, or expected from the gathering.

Historical illustrations produced centuries later should therefore not be mistaken for photographs of the event.

The Wampanoag Did Not Enter History in 1621

The traditional Thanksgiving narrative often begins with the Mayflower.

Wampanoag history does not.

The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian emphasizes that Wampanoag people had lived in their homelands for thousands of years before the English settlement at Plymouth.

Their communities occupied territory throughout southeastern present-day Massachusetts and portions of what is now Rhode Island, including Cape Cod and the islands.

They possessed:

  • political institutions;
  • local communities;
  • diplomatic relationships;
  • agricultural systems;
  • fishing and hunting practices;
  • trade networks;
  • religious traditions;
  • and long-established knowledge of the land and sea.

Calling them simply “the Indians” turns a specific people into anonymous supporting characters in someone else's history.

The English Were Not the First Europeans the Wampanoag Had Seen

Another common impression is that the Mayflower initiated meaningful contact between Indigenous people and Europeans in the region.

It did not.

European fishermen, explorers, traders, and kidnappers had visited the Northeast coast for decades.

Wampanoag and neighboring Native communities therefore possessed prior experience with Europeans.

Some encounters involved trade.

Others involved violence.

Some Native people travelled across the Atlantic voluntarily or involuntarily.

Others were kidnapped.

European arrival in 1620 was therefore not an encounter between two peoples entirely unknown to one another.

Tisquantum Had Been Kidnapped

Tisquantum—often called Squanto in English-language accounts—is central to the Thanksgiving story.

The traditional version often presents him simply as a friendly Native man who mysteriously spoke English and volunteered to teach the colonists how to plant corn.

The reason he knew English was far darker.

According to the National Museum of the American Indian, English captain Thomas Hunt kidnapped Tisquantum and other Native men from Patuxet in 1614 with the intention of selling captives into slavery.

Tisquantum was taken across the Atlantic.

He spent years in Europe, learned English, and eventually found a route back across the Atlantic.

When he returned to his homeland in 1619, he discovered that his community had been devastated.

Tisquantum Returned to a Community Destroyed by Epidemic

Between approximately 1616 and 1619, epidemic disease devastated Native communities along parts of the New England coast.

The National Museum of the American Indian estimates that nearly three-quarters of the Wampanoag population died during the epidemic period.

Patuxet was particularly devastated.

Tisquantum returned to find that his family and much of his community had not survived.

This demographic catastrophe is essential background to the Plymouth story.

The English did not establish their settlement on untouched land.

They occupied the site of an existing Indigenous community whose population had recently been devastated by disease associated with European contact.

Plymouth Was Built at Patuxet

The place the English called Plymouth was known to the Wampanoag as Patuxet.

The settlement site appeared unusually available to the newcomers precisely because epidemic disease had recently devastated the Native population living there.

The National Museum of the American Indian notes that the English moved across Cape Cod Bay and settled at the largely emptied Wampanoag village.

This fact changes the familiar visual image of Pilgrims entering vacant wilderness.

They were entering an inhabited political landscape altered by catastrophic population loss.

The Colonists Initially Took Native Corn

Relations between the newcomers and Indigenous communities did not begin with the Thanksgiving feast.

During early exploration, Mayflower colonists discovered stored corn and other resources belonging to Native people.

They took some of that corn.

English accounts later acknowledged the act and expressed an intention to repay the owners.

The episode is important because it reveals the desperation of the settlers and the fact that they were already making use of a landscape owned, cultivated, stored, and managed by Indigenous communities.

Colonization did not begin with a blank map.

The English Nearly Failed During Their First Winter

The Plymouth settlers were extremely vulnerable.

Disease, exposure, hunger, poor nutrition, inadequate shelter, and winter conditions killed a large proportion of the English population during their first months in New England.

By the spring of 1621, the colony's survival was far from guaranteed.

That vulnerability is essential to understanding why Wampanoag assistance mattered.

The English did not arrive as an overwhelmingly powerful society from the first day.

In 1621, they were a small and struggling settlement operating within a Native political world they barely understood.

Wampanoag Assistance Was Real

Correcting the national mythology should not erase genuine Wampanoag assistance.

Tisquantum and Hobbamock became important intermediaries between Ousamequin and Plymouth.

Tisquantum's knowledge of English and of the local environment made him especially useful.

Wampanoag assistance helped the English understand:

  • local agriculture;
  • corn cultivation;
  • fishing;
  • regional geography;
  • Native political relationships;
  • and the resources available in the region.

The colonists' improved harvest was connected to Indigenous knowledge and assistance.

The myth is not that Wampanoag people helped the English.

They did.

Why Did Ousamequin Cooperate?

Traditional stories often present Ousamequin's cooperation as uncomplicated generosity.

Generosity and diplomacy are not mutually exclusive.

Wampanoag leaders had political reasons to evaluate the newcomers carefully.

Epidemic disease had severely reduced Wampanoag populations.

Other Native powers, particularly the Narragansett to the west, remained comparatively stronger.

The English possessed weapons, transatlantic trade connections, and potential military value.

Ousamequin therefore faced a strategic decision.

The Smithsonian's Thanksgiving educational materials describe him as choosing to bring the English into a Wampanoag alliance network while also monitoring them carefully.

Friendship may have developed between individuals.

At the political level, however, the relationship was diplomacy.

The 1621 Treaty Came Before the Feast

Months before the harvest gathering, Plymouth and Ousamequin entered into a formal agreement.

The agreement addressed:

  • peace between the parties;
  • the handling of injuries and offenses;
  • the return of weapons;
  • security during meetings;
  • and mutual assistance if either side came under attack.

This agreement helps explain why Wampanoag and Plymouth people could gather together peacefully later that year.

Their relationship did not begin at a dinner table.

The feast occurred after a season of diplomacy.

The Alliance Was Not Simply “Pilgrims and Indians”

The phrase “Pilgrims and Indians became friends” also exaggerates the scope of the arrangement.

Native New England was politically diverse.

Numerous communities and nations possessed their own leaders, interests, alliances, and rivalries.

Plimoth Patuxet Museums notes that the English may have interpreted the agreement as broadly binding, while Wampanoag political organization was more locally complex.

Ousamequin's diplomacy did not mean that every Native community in New England had entered one friendship agreement with every English colonist.

“The Indians” were never one political actor.

Were the Wampanoag Invited to Thanksgiving Dinner?

The familiar story says the Pilgrims formally invited their Native friends to a Thanksgiving dinner.

The surviving eyewitness account does not clearly say that.

Edward Winslow described “many of the Indians coming amongst us” and Ousamequin arriving with approximately ninety men.

He did not preserve an invitation saying:

Please join us for Thanksgiving.

Smithsonian educational materials explicitly encourage students to consider the possibility that the Wampanoag arrived after hearing or observing the colonists' activities, including firearms exercises.

That remains an interpretation rather than a proven fact.

The responsible conclusion is therefore:

Wampanoag people participated in the celebration, but the surviving evidence does not establish the formal Thanksgiving invitation portrayed in later popular culture.

Ninety Wampanoag Men Was a Significant Delegation

The size of Ousamequin's party is important.

Approximately ninety Wampanoag men represented a substantial group compared with the small English colony.

Their presence should not be interpreted as a few Native neighbors arriving to decorate an English celebration.

They represented a powerful Indigenous political community whose alliance was significant to Plymouth's survival and security.

Wampanoag participants were not supporting characters in an exclusively English event.

The Wampanoag Brought Food

The familiar story often portrays the meal as something prepared by the Pilgrims and graciously offered to Indigenous guests.

Winslow's account establishes that Wampanoag participants contributed food themselves.

Ousamequin's men hunted five deer and presented them to colonial leaders.

This was therefore not simply an English meal consumed by invited Native visitors.

Both groups materially contributed to the gathering.

The Menu Is Mostly Unknown

Modern Thanksgiving tables are dominated by foods such as:

  • roast turkey;
  • stuffing;
  • mashed potatoes;
  • cranberry sauce;
  • sweet potatoes;
  • gravy;
  • and pumpkin pie.

The surviving eyewitness account does not document that modern menu.

Plimoth Patuxet Museums notes that only two foods can be identified directly from Winslow's description:

  • wildfowl; and
  • venison.

Other foods available in the seasonal environment were probably consumed, but historians should distinguish probability from direct documentation.

It Was a Three-Day Celebration

Modern Thanksgiving commonly centers on one large meal.

The 1621 gathering lasted approximately three days.

It included food and recreation.

Winslow specifically mentioned military exercises involving English firearms.

Other games or activities may have occurred, but surviving evidence does not permit historians to reconstruct a complete program.

The event therefore should not be imagined simply as a seventeenth-century version of a modern family dinner.

The Colonists Did Not Call It “The First Thanksgiving”

The phrase “First Thanksgiving” is retrospective.

The Plymouth colonists did have religious traditions of giving thanks to God.

They also inherited English harvest-celebration traditions.

Plimoth Patuxet Museums describes the 1621 event as closely related to the English tradition of Harvest Home: a communal celebration following a successful harvest.

A formal Puritan day of thanksgiving could instead take the form of a religious observance focused upon prayer.

Plymouth held such a religious thanksgiving during a drought in 1623.

The fall 1621 celebration later became associated with the national holiday Americans call Thanksgiving.

The participants themselves were not announcing:

Today we establish America's annual Thanksgiving holiday.

Calling It the “First” Thanksgiving Is Also Complicated

Ceremonies of gratitude existed throughout Indigenous North America long before European colonization.

European Christians also observed thanksgiving ceremonies before Plymouth's 1621 gathering.

Spanish colonists and other European settlers held earlier religious observances of thanksgiving in parts of what later became the United States.

The importance of Plymouth's 1621 gathering does not rest upon proving that no human community in North America had ever held a thanksgiving celebration before it.

Its special status comes largely from its later connection to the development of the American national Thanksgiving tradition.

Wampanoag People Already Had Traditions of Giving Thanks

Another implication of the traditional narrative is that Native participants were introduced to the concept of thanksgiving by Christian settlers.

That is incorrect.

Plimoth Patuxet Museums and the National Museum of the American Indian both emphasize that Wampanoag culture possesses longstanding traditions of gratitude connected with seasonal cycles, harvests, animals, plants, family, and community.

Wampanoag ceremonies of thanksgiving did not derive from the Pilgrims.

Both societies had traditions of gratitude before their encounter.

The traditions were culturally distinct.

The Gathering Was About More Than Friendship

The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian describes the 1621 relationship as involving:

  • political alliances;
  • diplomacy;
  • and a pursuit of peace.

Those terms are more historically useful than simply saying everyone became friends.

Friendship describes an emotional relationship between individuals.

Diplomacy describes relations between political communities.

Individuals may genuinely have developed affection or trust.

Governments and leaders could cooperate because cooperation served their interests.

Both things can occur at the same time.

A Political Alliance Can Still Be Genuine

Describing Ousamequin's decisions as strategic should not be interpreted as claiming that the cooperation was fake.

Political alliances throughout history involve strategic interests.

That does not make them meaningless.

Plymouth benefited from Wampanoag knowledge and military cooperation.

Ousamequin had reasons to evaluate English military value and to manage the presence of a new settlement within the region.

Both sides could honor agreements because peace and cooperation were useful.

Reducing the alliance to sentimental friendship understates the political sophistication of the Wampanoag.

The Relationship Did Not Immediately Collapse

Another modern correction sometimes goes too far in the opposite direction and implies that the 1621 meal was immediately followed by war, betrayal, and extermination.

The chronology was more complicated.

Ousamequin and Plymouth maintained a working relationship for decades.

Native and English people:

  • traded;
  • negotiated;
  • formed alliances;
  • transferred land;
  • entered legal disputes;
  • participated in missionary encounters;
  • and sometimes fought alongside one another.

The later history cannot accurately be compressed into:

They ate together in 1621 and immediately became enemies.

The myth of permanent friendship and the myth of immediate inevitable war are both too simple.

But English Settlement Kept Expanding

The balance of power changed dramatically after 1621.

Plymouth was no longer the only English foothold in the region.

Thousands of additional English settlers arrived in New England during the following decades.

New colonies, towns, farms, roads, fences, livestock, courts, and churches transformed Indigenous homelands.

The National Museum of the American Indian notes that Wampanoag communities increasingly experienced pressure as English settlements and agricultural expansion reduced access to traditional hunting, fishing, planting, and gathering areas.

The political circumstances of 1621 therefore did not remain static.

Land Became a Central Source of Conflict

English settlement depended upon land.

As colonial populations expanded, demand for land increased.

English and Indigenous communities did not necessarily share identical understandings of:

  • ownership;
  • use rights;
  • jurisdiction;
  • property transfer;
  • political sovereignty;
  • and the permanence of land agreements.

Native leaders sometimes sold or transferred land through agreements.

English colonial governments increasingly used their own legal institutions to define the consequences of those transactions.

The resulting changes steadily reduced Native political and territorial independence.

Disease Continued to Alter the Balance of Power

Epidemic disease did not end with the catastrophe preceding Plymouth's establishment.

Additional outbreaks continued to strike Native communities.

Population loss weakened communities politically, economically, and militarily while English immigration expanded colonial populations.

This demographic transformation fundamentally changed the relationship between Native nations and the colonies.

A relationship negotiated while Plymouth was a vulnerable settlement could look very different once English communities had become numerically and militarily dominant.

Ousamequin's Death Changed the Political Context

Ousamequin had been the Wampanoag leader most closely associated with the 1621 Plymouth alliance.

After his death, leadership eventually passed to his sons, including Metacom, known to the English as King Philip.

The next generation inherited a political landscape profoundly different from the one Ousamequin had confronted in 1621.

English settlements were larger.

Colonial governments exercised greater power.

Native landholdings had contracted.

Political distrust had increased.

The alliance that had once appeared useful became harder to sustain.

King Philip's War Demonstrates That the Friendship Was Not Permanent

In 1675, war erupted between forces associated with Metacom and English colonies in New England.

The conflict is commonly known as King Philip's War.

The National Park Service identifies English acquisition of Native land and a series of increasingly hostile incidents as major elements in the deterioration of relations between Plymouth and the Wampanoag.

The war expanded to involve multiple Indigenous nations and English colonies.

Communities on all sides suffered devastating casualties and destruction.

Metacom was killed in 1676.

Numerous Native prisoners were executed, displaced, or sold into slavery.

Whatever cooperative relationship existed in 1621 clearly had not created a permanent peace.

The War Was Not Predetermined at the 1621 Feast

The later war should not be projected backward as though everyone eating together in 1621 already knew what would happen fifty years later.

Historical actors make decisions within circumstances that can change.

Ousamequin's alliance could have been rational in 1621 even if the long-term expansion of English colonial power later became catastrophic for Wampanoag autonomy.

Likewise, genuine cooperation in 1621 does not cancel later dispossession.

Both histories belong in the same account.

Do Not Turn the Feast Into a Trap Without Evidence

Some modern retellings attempt to correct the traditional story by presenting the harvest gathering itself as deliberate deception.

In this version, the English supposedly invited the Wampanoag to dinner while secretly planning their destruction.

The surviving evidence does not establish such a conspiracy surrounding the 1621 gathering.

The political relationship was complicated, and colonization ultimately had devastating consequences for Indigenous communities.

That does not mean every peaceful interaction was fraudulent.

Historical criticism should correct mythology with evidence rather than replacing one morality play with another.

The Pequot War Is Sometimes Incorrectly Substituted for the 1621 Story

Another modern claim states that the American Thanksgiving holiday actually originated as a celebration of the 1637 massacre of Pequot people.

Colonial governments did proclaim religious days of thanksgiving in connection with military victories during the Pequot War.

Those events are real and belong in the history of colonial New England.

But Plimoth Patuxet Museums distinguishes those religious observances from the historical development of the modern national Thanksgiving holiday.

The 1621 Plymouth harvest gathering—not the Pequot War celebrations—became the principal historical event later incorporated into the American Thanksgiving origin story.

Correcting romantic history should not require creating an inaccurate massacre-origin story in its place.

The Modern “First Thanksgiving” Story Developed Much Later

The 1621 gathering did not immediately become America's universally recognized founding holiday.

The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian traces much of the familiar “First Thanksgiving” national narrative to the nineteenth century.

Historical accounts of the Plymouth harvest celebration resurfaced and were incorporated into an expanding national story.

Artists created idealized paintings.

Writers transformed the encounter into moral history.

Schoolbooks eventually taught generations of children a simplified version centered on cooperation between Pilgrims and Indians.

Sarah Josepha Hale Helped Nationalize Thanksgiving

Magazine editor Sarah Josepha Hale became one of the most influential advocates for a regular national Thanksgiving observance.

For years she promoted the holiday through editorials, recipes, letters, and appeals to political leaders.

In September 1863, during the Civil War, Hale wrote to President Abraham Lincoln urging him to establish a national and fixed Thanksgiving celebration.

Her advocacy helped connect Thanksgiving with ideals of national unity, family, gratitude, and reconciliation.

This occurred more than two centuries after the Plymouth harvest gathering.

Lincoln's 1863 Thanksgiving Was a Civil War Proclamation

In October 1863, President Abraham Lincoln proclaimed the final Thursday of November as a national day of Thanksgiving and Praise.

The proclamation came during the Civil War and emphasized national gratitude amid extraordinary destruction.

Lincoln did not simply announce:

We are celebrating the anniversary of the Pilgrims' 1621 dinner.

The modern holiday developed from multiple religious, regional, political, domestic, and historical traditions.

The Plymouth story later became one of its most powerful origin narratives.

The National Story Served a Purpose

Nations often construct stories that explain who they believe themselves to be.

The traditional Thanksgiving narrative presented the United States as a nation born through:

  • religious faith;
  • hard work;
  • gratitude;
  • hospitality;
  • cooperation;
  • and peaceful intercultural friendship.

Those values are appealing.

The difficulty arises when the symbolic story replaces historical analysis.

A national story can communicate values without accurately describing every event from which it claims descent.

Generic “Indians” Are Part of the Myth

School presentations historically asked children to divide into two groups:

  • Pilgrims; and
  • Indians.

That terminology creates a false equivalence.

“Pilgrim” refers to a specific colonial population.

“Indian” collapses hundreds of distinct Indigenous peoples into a single racial category.

The people associated with the Plymouth story should be identified as Wampanoag, with further political and community distinctions where the evidence permits.

Tribal specificity changes Indigenous people from scenery into historical actors.

The Word “Pilgrim” Can Also Oversimplify

Even the English side was more complicated than popular memory suggests.

Not every Mayflower passenger belonged to the Separatist religious community commonly associated with the Pilgrims.

The settlement included people with different social backgrounds and motivations.

“Pilgrim” remains useful shorthand in public history.

But it should not encourage the assumption that every Plymouth colonist possessed identical religious motives or experiences.

The Familiar Clothes Are Often Wrong Too

Popular Thanksgiving imagery frequently depicts Pilgrims wearing severe black clothing decorated with large silver buckles.

Seventeenth-century English clothing was more varied and colorful than this stereotype suggests.

Native clothing has likewise often been reduced to generic costumes that do not accurately represent Wampanoag material culture.

These visual inaccuracies matter because repeated imagery can become a form of historical evidence in popular memory.

People eventually remember the painting rather than the documentation.

The Feast Should Not Be Asked to Explain All Native–English Relations

One peaceful gathering cannot serve as a complete interpretation of centuries of colonial history.

Likewise, later warfare cannot prove that no genuine cooperation existed in 1621.

Historical relationships can contain:

  • cooperation and conflict;
  • friendship and exploitation;
  • diplomacy and coercion;
  • trade and dispossession;
  • cultural exchange and cultural destruction;
  • peace treaties and warfare.

The presence of one does not automatically erase the others.

The task of history is to preserve the contradictions rather than force all evidence into one moral theme.

The Wampanoag Still Exist

Another consequence of the traditional Thanksgiving story is that Native people can appear to exist only in the past.

Wampanoag communities survived colonization, war, disease, land loss, cultural pressure, and generations of political change.

Wampanoag people remain part of contemporary New England.

Teaching Thanksgiving exclusively through seventeenth-century costumes can unintentionally present Indigenous people as a vanished population.

A complete account should connect historical Wampanoag people with living Wampanoag communities.

Thanksgiving Has Different Meanings for Native Americans

There is no single Native American opinion about Thanksgiving.

Some Native people celebrate Thanksgiving as a family holiday.

Some emphasize Indigenous traditions of gratitude.

Some use the day to educate others about Native history.

Some reject or protest the national mythology surrounding the holiday.

Native nations and individuals should not be treated as possessing one universal response.

The diversity of modern Native perspectives is itself part of correcting the stereotype.

The National Day of Mourning Presents a Different Memory

Since 1970, Native people and supporters have gathered in Plymouth on Thanksgiving for a National Day of Mourning.

The observance emphasizes Indigenous survival and remembrance while protesting histories of colonization, land dispossession, racism, and cultural destruction.

It demonstrates that the same national holiday can carry radically different meanings depending upon historical perspective.

For one community, Thanksgiving may symbolize family and gratitude.

For another, the mythology surrounding the holiday may conceal the costs of colonization.

These interpretations do not require historians to deny that the 1621 gathering occurred.

They concern what Americans later made the gathering represent.

Do Not Replace Celebration With Mandatory Condemnation

Correcting the Thanksgiving myth does not require telling Americans that gratitude, family gatherings, shared meals, or Thanksgiving celebrations are illegitimate.

Modern holidays can develop meanings substantially different from the historical events later associated with them.

Millions of Americans observe Thanksgiving without consciously celebrating colonial dispossession.

Many Native Americans also participate in Thanksgiving traditions.

Historical literacy requires distinguishing:

  • the documented 1621 event;
  • the later national origin story;
  • the development of the modern holiday;
  • and the meaning individuals assign to Thanksgiving today.

These are related questions.

They are not identical questions.

A Better Version of the Story

The history does not need to lose its human dimension simply because the fairy tale is removed.

A better account might say:

In 1621, a small and vulnerable English colony at Patuxet/Plymouth had survived a disastrous first winter and achieved a successful harvest with important assistance from Wampanoag people. The English had entered into an alliance with Wampanoag leader Ousamequin months earlier. During a three-day harvest celebration, Ousamequin and approximately ninety Wampanoag men participated and contributed five deer. The gathering represented a real period of diplomacy and cooperation, but it did not establish permanent friendship or erase the political consequences of expanding English colonization. Centuries later, Americans transformed the event into the national origin story known as the First Thanksgiving.

That story is less simple.

It is also closer to the evidence.

Verdict

Claim: The “First Thanksgiving” was a harmonious founding celebration in which Pilgrims invited friendly Indians to dinner and established a lasting friendship between Native Americans and English settlers.

Verdict: Misleading and heavily mythologized.

A peaceful harvest gathering involving Plymouth colonists and Wampanoag people genuinely occurred in 1621.

Wampanoag assistance to the struggling English settlement was real.

Ousamequin and Plymouth had established a diplomatic alliance, and he participated in the harvest celebration with approximately ninety Wampanoag men, who contributed five deer.

But the gathering was not the beginning of Native history, was not clearly documented as a formal Thanksgiving invitation, and did not create an uncomplicated permanent friendship.

The Wampanoag had already experienced European trade, kidnapping, slavery, epidemic disease, and political disruption before Plymouth was established. Their alliance with the English was shaped by diplomacy and strategic circumstances as well as cooperation.

Relations continued for decades but changed profoundly as English immigration and colonial expansion increased pressure on Native land and political independence. By 1675, deteriorating relations contributed to King Philip's War.

The familiar sentimental version developed much later as the 1621 gathering was incorporated into a nineteenth-century American national story.

The more accurate conclusion is:

The 1621 harvest gathering was a genuine moment of Wampanoag–English diplomacy, cooperation, and shared celebration. It was not a simple founding dinner that created permanent friendship. The later “First Thanksgiving” story transformed one complex political encounter into a national myth that obscured both the long Indigenous history before Plymouth and the colonial conflicts that followed.

Source Notes

  • Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, Native Knowledge 360°, “Rethinking Thanksgiving Celebrations: Native Perspectives on Thanksgiving.” Explains how the familiar “First Thanksgiving” narrative developed into a nineteenth-century national myth and emphasizes Wampanoag history, diplomacy, and the later consequences of colonization.
  • Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, Native Knowledge 360°, “The ‘First Thanksgiving’: How Can We Tell a Better Story?” Provides Wampanoag perspectives, primary sources, maps, chronology, and historical interpretation concerning the Plymouth colony, Wampanoag diplomacy, and the 1621 harvest gathering.
  • Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, “The First Thanksgiving — Timeline.” Documents European contact, Tisquantum's 1614 kidnapping, the 1616–1619 epidemics, settlement at Patuxet, Ousamequin's alliance with Plymouth, and the three-day 1621 harvest gathering.
  • Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, “Early Encounters.” Provides detailed background on European kidnapping of Indigenous people, Tisquantum's captivity, return to Patuxet, and Ousamequin's strategic response to the English newcomers.
  • Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, “Treaty and Harvest Celebration.” Presents the surviving account of the 1621 gathering along with Wampanoag historical interpretation and discussion of the political relationship between Plymouth and Ousamequin.
  • Plimoth Patuxet Museums, “Eye-Witness Account of the 1621 Harvest Celebration.” Reproduces Edward Winslow's surviving firsthand description of Ousamequin, approximately ninety Wampanoag men, three days of celebration, hunting, firearms exercises, and the contribution of five deer.
  • Plimoth Patuxet Museums, “What Did They Eat at the 1621 Harvest Celebration?” Explains the limited surviving evidence concerning the meal and identifies wildfowl and venison as the foods directly documented in the eyewitness account.
  • Plimoth Patuxet Museums, “1621 Harvest Celebration.” Emphasizes that only one English eyewitness account specifically describes the feast and that many familiar details of modern depictions remain unknown.
  • Plimoth Patuxet Museums, “Wampanoag Traditions of Giving Thanks for the Children of the Earth.” Describes longstanding Wampanoag traditions of gratitude and seasonal thanksgiving that predated the Plymouth colony.
  • Plimoth Patuxet Museums, “Memories of Thanksgiving Across the Centuries.” Examines the distinction between religious thanksgiving observances and the 1621 harvest celebration and traces the later development of the national Thanksgiving tradition.
  • National Park Service, Roger Williams National Memorial, “King Philip's War.” Describes the later deterioration of relations between Plymouth Colony and the Wampanoag, including pressure involving Native land and the war that began in 1675.
  • Library of Congress, “Thanksgiving.” Documents the development of the national holiday and Sarah Josepha Hale's campaign encouraging President Abraham Lincoln to establish a national Thanksgiving observance.
  • National Park Service, Lincoln Home National Historic Site, “Lincoln and Thanksgiving.” Provides a chronology of Thanksgiving observances and Lincoln's 1863 proclamation establishing a national November day of Thanksgiving and Praise.
  • Plimoth Patuxet Museums, “National Day of Mourning Monument.” Documents the Native-led National Day of Mourning observed in Plymouth on Thanksgiving since 1970 and explains its emphasis on Indigenous remembrance, survival, and protest.

Media Literacy Principle

A real peaceful event can still become a historical myth when later storytellers remove its political context and make one moment represent an entire relationship. Ask what happened before the famous scene, what happened afterward, whose perspective is missing, and when the familiar version of the story was actually created.
