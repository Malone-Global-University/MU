The Underground Railroad Quilt Code Myth

Published: August 9, 2026

Overview

A popular story about the Underground Railroad holds that enslaved African Americans secretly communicated escape routes and instructions by hanging quilts containing coded patterns on fences, clotheslines, or windows. According to the story, particular quilt blocks told freedom seekers to prepare supplies, follow animal trails, locate safe houses, disguise themselves, travel in particular directions, or follow the North Star.

It is an appealing story of ingenuity under oppression, but historians have not found evidence establishing that such a standardized Underground Railroad quilt code actually existed.

The National Park Service states that no documented firsthand account from a freedom seeker or Underground Railroad ally has been found describing interaction with a quilt code. It also notes that many patterns included in modern versions of the code did not exist under the names or forms claimed until after the Civil War.

The most influential modern version of the story derives largely from an oral tradition related by South Carolina quilter Ozella McDaniel Williams and published in Jacqueline Tobin and Raymond Dobard's 1999 book Hidden in Plain View: A Secret Story of Quilts and the Underground Railroad. Oral tradition is a legitimate historical source, but this particular account has not been independently corroborated sufficiently to establish a widespread or standardized nineteenth-century communications system.

Quilts were real and important parts of African American material culture. Enslaved people certainly communicated secretly, used coded language, relied on personal networks, followed geographic knowledge, and developed sophisticated strategies for escape. Quilts may also have sheltered, warmed, concealed, or otherwise assisted freedom seekers.

What the evidence does not establish is the familiar system in which recognizable quilt blocks functioned as a shared visual map or sequence of Underground Railroad commands.

The Claim

The modern quilt-code story usually describes a secret communications system hidden within ordinary-looking quilts.

Common versions claim:

  • quilts hanging outside safe houses told freedom seekers where to go;
  • specific quilt blocks represented specific escape instructions;
  • enslaved people memorized a sequence of quilt patterns before escaping;
  • the Monkey Wrench pattern meant to gather tools or prepare for escape;
  • the Wagon Wheel meant to prepare for travel;
  • the Bear's Paw meant to follow animal trails;
  • the Log Cabin identified a safe house;
  • Flying Geese indicated a direction or season of travel;
  • the Drunkard's Path instructed freedom seekers to move unpredictably;
  • a star pattern directed travelers north;
  • and Harriet Tubman used these quilt signals while conducting people to freedom.

Some versions describe only a few symbols.

Others present an elaborate sequence in which ten or more quilt patterns function almost like traffic signs along a secret highway.

The difficulty is not that such a system would have been impossible.

The difficulty is that the historical record has not demonstrated that it existed.

The First Question Should Be: What Is the Evidence?

A covert communication system used by people escaping slavery would not necessarily leave the same documentary record as a public organization.

Secrecy was essential.

Enslaved people could be punished severely for planning escape, and people helping freedom seekers risked prosecution, violence, fines, or other consequences.

Therefore, the absence of an official written manual entitled Underground Railroad Quilt Code would not by itself prove that no textile signal was ever used.

Historians nevertheless have other ways to test the broader claim.

They can examine:

  • accounts written by formerly enslaved people;
  • slave narratives;
  • abolitionist memoirs;
  • Underground Railroad correspondence;
  • vigilance-committee records;
  • court cases;
  • newspaper reports;
  • family oral traditions;
  • Works Progress Administration interviews with formerly enslaved people;
  • surviving quilts;
  • quilt-pattern histories;
  • and accounts left by conductors and station operators after slavery ended.

Those sources provide considerable information about escape from slavery.

They have not produced independent evidence for the standardized quilt code described in popular culture.

The National Park Service Finds No Firsthand Quilt-Code Account

The National Park Service's National Underground Railroad Network to Freedom addresses the question directly.

According to the NPS, quilt codes are no longer widely accepted because they lack historical documentation.

More importantly, the agency states:

To date, there has been no documented first-hand account of a freedom seeker or Underground Railroad ally interacting with the quilt code.

That absence is significant because substantial firsthand Underground Railroad evidence does survive.

Participants eventually described:

  • routes;
  • safe houses;
  • conductors;
  • abolitionist networks;
  • coded expressions;
  • letters;
  • transportation methods;
  • assistance from Black communities;
  • escapes by boat and train;
  • and numerous individual journeys.

Yet a standardized visual quilt vocabulary does not emerge from those accounts.

Secrecy Does Not Explain Everything

Supporters of the quilt-code story sometimes respond:

Of course there are no written records. It was secret.

That argument has some force.

Secret activity naturally produces incomplete documentation.

But it cannot make a historical claim immune from evidence.

Much of the Underground Railroad was also secret, yet historians know a substantial amount about it because participants talked, wrote, preserved correspondence, published memoirs, gave interviews, recorded family histories, and described their experiences after the danger had passed.

William Still, for example, preserved detailed information concerning freedom seekers passing through Philadelphia and later published an extensive documentary history of Underground Railroad activity.

Other formerly enslaved people and abolitionists also left accounts.

Secrecy explains why evidence may be incomplete.

It does not transform an uncorroborated story into a documented national system.

Where the Modern Quilt-Code Story Came From

The most influential version of the modern quilt-code story became widely known through the 1999 book Hidden in Plain View: A Secret Story of Quilts and the Underground Railroad by Jacqueline L. Tobin and Raymond G. Dobard.

Tobin had met African American quilter Ozella McDaniel Williams in Charleston, South Carolina.

Williams related an oral tradition that she said had been transmitted through her family.

According to this tradition, quilt blocks formed part of a coded set of instructions associated with escape from slavery.

Tobin and Dobard attempted to interpret the account by connecting it with African textile traditions, symbolic communication, quilting, slavery, and Underground Railroad history.

The book attracted considerable public attention.

The idea subsequently spread through:

  • schools;
  • museum programs;
  • quilt guilds;
  • children's literature;
  • Black-history presentations;
  • lesson plans;
  • television;
  • websites;
  • social media;
  • and commemorative art.

A family oral tradition gradually became widely presented as a general description of how the Underground Railroad operated.

One Family Tradition Is Not Evidence of a National Code

The distinction between those two propositions is essential.

One proposition is:

A family preserves an oral tradition that ancestors used quilt symbols connected with escape from slavery.

The broader proposition is:

Enslaved people across the Underground Railroad used a standardized system of quilt blocks whose meanings were shared among freedom seekers and abolitionist networks.

Evidence for the first does not automatically establish the second.

The International Quilt Museum's educational project World Quilts: The American Story describes the quilt code as being grounded in an oral tradition passed through at least one family, while noting that historians have not identified additional evidence sufficient to substantiate the proposed code.

Quilt historians therefore generally classify the larger standardized-code narrative as folklore rather than established Underground Railroad history.

Oral History Should Not Be Dismissed

Rejecting an unsupported generalization does not require dismissing oral history.

Oral traditions are particularly important in African American history because slavery frequently restricted literacy, disrupted families, and denied enslaved communities ordinary control over written records.

Family memory can preserve information overlooked by official documents.

Historians therefore should not treat an oral account as worthless simply because it was not written down at the time of the event.

The National Park Service emphasizes that oral traditions can be valuable historical sources.

But it also explains that oral traditions should be analyzed, traced through their transmission, and compared with other evidence whenever possible.

That process is often called corroboration or triangulation.

The appropriate question is not:

Is oral history real history?

It is.

The appropriate question is:

How much can this particular oral tradition establish?

The Pattern-Dating Problem

The quilt-code story encounters another problem when historians examine the history of individual quilt patterns.

The National Park Service notes that many patterns cited in modern quilt-code lists did not exist until after the Civil War.

Quilt patterns are not timeless objects with permanently fixed names.

Geometric arrangements can:

  • appear under multiple names;
  • change over time;
  • be renamed by later publishers;
  • become standardized through commercial pattern distribution;
  • or be retrospectively assigned a name that was not widely used earlier.

That creates a serious chronological test for any claimed code.

If a particular pattern name is supposed to have carried a standardized message in 1845, researchers should ask whether that pattern and that name can actually be documented in the relevant period.

A symbol becoming common decades later cannot reliably explain an antebellum communications system without additional evidence.

A Geometric Design Is Not Automatically a Code

Another methodological problem arises when a historical quilt contains a pattern that resembles one later included in the alleged code.

That resemblance alone does not prove coded use.

Consider a quilt containing a star.

Stars have appeared in decorative traditions for countless reasons.

A star might represent:

  • religious symbolism;
  • aesthetic preference;
  • family tradition;
  • mathematical convenience;
  • a commonly circulating quilt design;
  • or no symbolic meaning at all.

To demonstrate that a particular star quilt communicated “travel north on the Underground Railroad,” additional evidence is required.

Otherwise, the conclusion is being read into the object rather than derived from it.

African Textile Symbolism Is a Separate Historical Question

Discussions of the quilt code sometimes connect the proposed symbols to African systems of visual communication.

African societies possess rich traditions of textile design, symbolic imagery, pattern, color, weaving, embroidery, and visual communication.

African influences also contributed in complex ways to African American artistic and material traditions.

Those facts deserve serious study.

But demonstrating that symbolic textile traditions existed in Africa does not by itself demonstrate that enslaved Americans created a standardized Underground Railroad code using particular American quilt blocks.

The historical chain must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

Researchers would need evidence connecting:

  1. a particular symbol;
  2. to a particular meaning;
  3. among a relevant African American community;
  4. during the antebellum period;
  5. for the purpose of communicating escape instructions.

Cultural resemblance is suggestive.

It is not sufficient proof of operational use.

Quilts Really Were Part of Enslaved African American Life

Rejecting the quilt code should not erase quilts from the history of slavery.

Enslaved women and men produced textiles under many circumstances.

Quilting and sewing could involve:

  • labor performed for enslavers;
  • production of household necessities;
  • clothing manufacture and repair;
  • family and community creativity;
  • reuse of scarce materials;
  • artistic expression;
  • and, after slavery, important traditions of African American material culture.

Quilts therefore belong authentically within African American history.

They do not need an undocumented Underground Railroad code to make them historically important.

Quilts May Have Helped Freedom Seekers in Ordinary Ways

The International Quilt Museum notes that historical accounts do associate quilts with escape from slavery in more practical ways.

Quilts could provide:

  • warmth;
  • bedding;
  • concealment;
  • cover inside wagons;
  • or other ordinary assistance during dangerous travel.

Those uses are entirely different from a standardized symbolic code.

A quilt hiding a freedom seeker under a pile of bedding is documented material assistance.

A quilt block supposedly communicating a multi-step escape instruction requires evidence that the pattern possessed and conveyed that meaning.

Could an Individual Quilt Ever Have Been Used as a Signal?

It is possible.

Historians should distinguish between saying:

No enslaved person anywhere ever used a quilt to signal anything.

and saying:

There is no reliable evidence for the standardized Underground Railroad quilt code commonly presented today.

The second conclusion is much stronger historically.

Human beings improvise.

A family or local network could theoretically agree that hanging a particular blanket meant:

It is safe to approach tonight.

Such an improvised signal would not require a nationally standardized code.

Unless evidence for a particular incident survives, however, historians should describe the possibility as a possibility rather than as established fact.

The Underground Railroad Was Not One Centralized Organization

The standardized quilt-code story can also create a misleading picture of the Underground Railroad itself.

There was no national headquarters distributing an official escape manual to every plantation and abolitionist household.

“Underground Railroad” describes overlapping efforts by enslaved people to escape and by other people to assist them.

Some networks were highly organized.

Others were temporary, local, familial, spontaneous, or improvised.

Participants included:

  • enslaved people;
  • free Black communities;
  • Black churches;
  • Black sailors and workers;
  • vigilance committees;
  • abolitionists;
  • religious communities;
  • individual farmers;
  • ship captains;
  • railroad workers;
  • and sympathetic households.

Many freedom seekers began their journeys without a conductor at all.

A universal symbolic quilt vocabulary would therefore require communication across a remarkably decentralized collection of people and places.

Evidence for that shared system has not emerged.

Many Freedom Seekers Escaped Without the Underground Railroad

Popular storytelling sometimes imagines that a person escaping slavery needed to locate the Underground Railroad and then follow a fixed route from station to station.

The National Park Service emphasizes that many freedom seekers began their journeys without assistance and many completed their self-emancipation independently.

Escape strategies could include:

  • walking at night;
  • following waterways;
  • using roads;
  • boarding ships;
  • using actual railroads;
  • travelling with forged papers;
  • passing as free;
  • hiding in wagons;
  • receiving directions from free Black residents;
  • or moving between relatives and acquaintances.

The quilt-code story can unintentionally replace this variety with a single fictional navigation system.

Enslaved People Really Did Use Coded Communication

Refuting quilt codes does not mean enslaved people lacked covert methods of communication.

The National Park Service notes that firsthand Underground Railroad accounts provide examples of coded language used to discuss escape and other dangerous subjects.

Coded communication could take the form of:

  • metaphorical language;
  • religious terminology;
  • carefully worded letters;
  • verbal messages;
  • signals agreed upon by particular individuals;
  • songs in some documented circumstances;
  • and ordinary words given special meaning within a trusted network.

This is historically different from claiming that a fixed visual vocabulary of quilt blocks existed across the Underground Railroad.

William Still Shows How Much Underground Railroad Evidence Survives

William Still was a free Black abolitionist and secretary of Philadelphia's Vigilance Committee.

He assisted freedom seekers arriving in Philadelphia and carefully recorded information about many of them.

After the Civil War, when publishing such information was safer, he incorporated records, letters, testimonies, and individual narratives into his major work The Underground Rail Road.

Still's records reveal a clandestine world containing:

  • aliases;
  • secret correspondence;
  • transportation arrangements;
  • family connections;
  • financial assistance;
  • routes;
  • safe contacts;
  • and detailed accounts of individual escapes.

This documentary richness is important when evaluating the argument that the quilt code disappeared from history simply because all Underground Railroad methods were secret.

Many secret methods eventually became documented.

Harriet Tubman Did Not Need a Quilt Code

Harriet Tubman is frequently incorporated into quilt-code presentations.

The National Park Service directly rejects the claim that she followed a quilt code during her rescue missions.

Tubman's documented methods were both more practical and more sophisticated.

According to NPS research, she relied upon:

  • trustworthy Black and white contacts;
  • people who told her which routes were safe;
  • direct communication;
  • disguises;
  • walking;
  • horses and wagons;
  • boats;
  • actual trains;
  • carefully composed letters;
  • bribery;
  • rivers;
  • stars and other natural features;
  • and selected songs used as signals.

These methods reveal why Tubman's successful rescue work should not be replaced by a fictional visual navigation system.

Tubman Used a Real Coded Letter

One particularly useful example concerns Tubman's free Black confidant Jacob Jackson.

In December 1854, Tubman had a letter written for her in Philadelphia and sent to Jackson in Maryland.

The letter used religious and transportation imagery to communicate with people she intended to rescue.

According to National Park Service research, Tubman's brothers were to be prepared to:

“step aboard” the “Ol' Ship of Zion.”

This is exactly the kind of coded communication historians can document.

There is:

  • a known sender;
  • a known recipient;
  • a known date;
  • a specific rescue context;
  • and identifiable coded language.

No equivalent documentary chain establishes Tubman's use of coded quilts.

Signals Other Than Quilts Are Better Documented

Historians and quilt scholars do not reject the possibility of covert signaling itself.

The International Quilt Museum notes that other forms of signals and coded communication have stronger support in historical and oral accounts.

Underground Railroad participants could use ordinary objects or actions in ways that appeared harmless to outsiders.

That principle makes intuitive sense.

A signal is useful precisely because the intended recipient understands a meaning that an observer does not.

But the existence of documented signals in one medium does not prove a specific code in another.

Evidence for coded language does not automatically become evidence for coded quilts.

Why Hanging Quilts Seems So Plausible

The quilt-code legend is unusually persuasive because the proposed method appears operationally clever.

A quilt hanging outdoors would not necessarily attract suspicion.

Quilts were ordinary household objects.

A secret message displayed through an ordinary object would fit the logic of clandestine resistance.

That produces a compelling question:

Why couldn't enslaved people have done this?

They could have.

But historical method asks a different question:

What evidence shows that they did?

Plausibility is not proof.

A Standardized Code Would Have Presented Practical Problems

The proposed system also creates logistical questions.

For a standardized quilt code to function across significant distances, freedom seekers would need to know:

  • which patterns belonged to the code;
  • what each pattern meant;
  • whether orientation changed the meaning;
  • which households participated;
  • when a displayed quilt was ordinary laundry and when it was a signal;
  • and whether people hundreds of miles away used the same definitions.

Underground Railroad activity extended through numerous states, communities, religious networks, racial groups, and geographic regions.

Establishing a common visual vocabulary would itself require substantial communication.

That does not make such a system impossible.

It makes documentary corroboration particularly important.

The Geography Was More Complicated Than “Follow North”

Quilt-code stories frequently present Underground Railroad travel as a northward journey culminating in Canada.

Many freedom seekers did travel north.

Canada became especially important after passage of the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 increased the danger of recapture within free states.

But freedom seeking was geographically more complicated.

People escaped:

  • north into free states;
  • into Canada;
  • south toward Spanish Florida during earlier periods;
  • toward Mexico;
  • into maroon communities;
  • onto ships;
  • into cities where they could attempt to disappear;
  • and through numerous local and regional routes.

There was no single road to freedom.

A universal quilt map pointing north oversimplifies that history.

The Quilt Code Makes the Underground Railroad Too Neat

Historical myths often succeed because they impose order on chaotic events.

The actual Underground Railroad could be uncertain and dangerous.

A freedom seeker might not know:

  • where the next safe house was;
  • whether a stranger could be trusted;
  • whether an established contact had been exposed;
  • whether slave catchers were nearby;
  • whether transportation would arrive;
  • or whether weather, illness, hunger, or injury would destroy the plan.

The quilt-code story replaces much of this uncertainty with an elegant sequence of instructions.

Prepare.

Travel.

Follow a path.

Find a safe house.

Disguise yourself.

Follow the star north.

As storytelling, that sequence is powerful.

As documented Underground Railroad history, it is too orderly for the evidence we possess.

The Story Is Especially Attractive in Education

Quilt codes became popular partly because they make difficult history easy to visualize.

Students can:

  • identify patterns;
  • draw quilt blocks;
  • memorize symbolic meanings;
  • construct classroom quilts;
  • and imagine deciphering secret messages.

The activity turns a complicated historical subject into an interactive puzzle.

It also presents enslaved African Americans as intelligent agents using creativity to resist oppression.

Those educational goals are worthwhile.

The problem occurs when an attractive teaching device is presented as established historical fact.

The Real History Already Demonstrates Ingenuity

The quilt-code myth may have gained acceptance partly because it corrects an older problem in American history education.

Enslaved people were once too often portrayed as passive people waiting for white abolitionists to rescue them.

Modern scholarship instead emphasizes African American agency.

Freedom seekers:

  • planned escapes;
  • studied geography;
  • built networks;
  • passed information;
  • saved money;
  • obtained disguises;
  • forged or borrowed papers;
  • used waterways and transportation systems;
  • created alliances with free Black communities;
  • returned for relatives;
  • and sometimes travelled hundreds of miles under extreme danger.

No invented code is required to demonstrate creativity, intelligence, or resistance.

Folklore Can Still Be Historically Important

Calling the quilt code folklore does not mean the story has no historical importance.

Folklore reveals how communities remember the past.

The popularity of Underground Railroad quilts tells us something about:

  • modern African American cultural memory;
  • the recovery of neglected Black history;
  • the importance of quilting traditions;
  • the desire to emphasize enslaved people's agency;
  • the relationship between oral tradition and academic history;
  • and the way museums and schools turn historical narratives into symbols.

A tradition can therefore be culturally meaningful without being accepted as a literal description of antebellum events.

“Myth” Does Not Mean Deliberate Lie

Describing the quilt code as a historical myth should not be interpreted as accusing everyone who has repeated it of deception.

Many teachers, quilters, museums, authors, and families encountered the story through sources they reasonably believed to be trustworthy.

The story was published, promoted, incorporated into educational material, and repeated by respected institutions.

Once a claim reaches that stage, a person can reproduce misinformation entirely in good faith.

Historical correction should therefore focus primarily on the evidence rather than assigning motives to everyone who repeated the story.

Do Not Turn the Correction Into Another Absolute

The most responsible conclusion is not:

Quilts never had anything whatsoever to do with an escape from slavery.

Quilts could provide warmth, concealment, material assistance, and perhaps local improvised signaling in circumstances that were never recorded.

Nor can historians prove what every enslaved person did in every private interaction across centuries of American slavery.

The correction concerns the much more specific claim:

A standardized set of quilt patterns functioned as a known Underground Railroad communications code for routes and escape instructions.

That claim lacks sufficient historical evidence.

What Evidence Would Change the Conclusion?

A historical interpretation should be capable of revision.

Evidence that could significantly strengthen the quilt-code theory might include:

  • an antebellum letter explaining that a named quilt pattern signaled a particular escape instruction;
  • a firsthand narrative by a freedom seeker describing reading quilt blocks as route information;
  • an abolitionist diary describing quilts being displayed according to a known pattern code;
  • several independent family traditions from different regions preserving substantially the same code;
  • a period account identifying a quilt at a specific safe house as a visual signal;
  • or multiple independently documented nineteenth-century sources assigning consistent escape meanings to the same quilt patterns.

Historians would then have something that could be compared, dated, and corroborated.

At present, that evidentiary foundation has not been established.

Absence of Evidence Versus Evidence of Absence

This controversy provides a useful lesson in historical reasoning.

“No evidence has been found” does not logically prove that an event could never have occurred.

That is the distinction between absence of evidence and evidence of absence.

But the significance of missing evidence depends upon what else survives.

If almost nothing were known about Underground Railroad methods, the quilt theory would be difficult either to confirm or reject.

Instead, historians possess a substantial documentary record of escapes, networks, conductors, coded language, transportation, vigilance committees, and firsthand experiences.

The continued absence of quilt-code testimony across those sources therefore matters.

It does not demonstrate mathematical impossibility.

It does prevent the code from being responsibly taught as established fact.

What the Evidence Actually Supports

  • Quilts were important household objects and forms of material culture within African American communities.
  • Enslaved people and freedom seekers used secret and coded communication.
  • Underground Railroad networks could use trusted contacts, verbal signals, letters, aliases, metaphors, and other methods of covert communication.
  • Many freedom seekers escaped without a conductor or formal Underground Railroad network.
  • Quilts could provide warmth, concealment, bedding, or other practical assistance during escape.
  • An isolated person or local network could theoretically have used a particular textile as an improvised signal.
  • No documented firsthand account has been identified in which a freedom seeker or Underground Railroad ally describes using the standardized quilt code popularized today.
  • The National Park Service states that quilt codes lack historical documentation and are no longer widely accepted.
  • The NPS also notes that many patterns included in supposed quilt codes did not exist until after the Civil War.
  • The most influential modern quilt-code account derives substantially from the oral tradition of Ozella McDaniel Williams.
  • Jacqueline Tobin and Raymond Dobard publicized that tradition in their 1999 book Hidden in Plain View.
  • Oral traditions are legitimate historical sources but must still be evaluated and compared with other evidence.
  • Historians have not found independent evidence sufficient to establish the Williams family's code as a widespread Underground Railroad system.
  • The International Quilt Museum treats the standardized quilt-code account as a folk story rather than established historical fact.
  • Harriet Tubman is not documented as having used a quilt code.
  • Tubman's documented methods included trusted contacts, disguises, letters, direct communication, multiple forms of transportation, rivers, stars, bribery, and selected songs.
  • William Still and other Underground Railroad participants preserved substantial documentary evidence about actual escape networks and methods.
  • The absence of evidence for a standardized quilt code does not diminish the sophistication or agency of enslaved people seeking freedom.

Verdict

Claim: Enslaved people communicated Underground Railroad routes and instructions through a standardized system of coded quilt patterns.

Verdict: Unsupported and generally regarded as a historical myth.

Enslaved people unquestionably used secrecy, coded communication, personal networks, geographic knowledge, and numerous ingenious methods to plan and execute escapes from slavery.

Quilts were also real and important parts of African American material culture and may have assisted freedom seekers in ordinary practical ways.

But historians have not found contemporary firsthand documentation establishing the familiar system in which patterns such as Monkey Wrench, Wagon Wheel, Bear's Paw, Log Cabin, Flying Geese, or similar blocks communicated standardized Underground Railroad instructions.

The National Park Service reports that no documented firsthand account from a freedom seeker or Underground Railroad ally describes interaction with the quilt code, and that many patterns included in the alleged code are chronologically problematic.

The best-known modern version of the story derives largely from one family's oral tradition, later popularized through Hidden in Plain View.

That oral tradition is worthy of preservation and study.

It is not sufficient evidence for presenting a standardized national quilt code as established nineteenth-century history.

The more accurate conclusion is:

Freedom seekers used many sophisticated forms of secret communication, but there is no reliable evidence that enslaved people broadly navigated the Underground Railroad through a standardized code of quilt patterns. The familiar quilt-code system is best understood as a modern oral tradition and piece of historical folklore rather than a documented Underground Railroad practice.

Media Literacy Principle

A story can be culturally meaningful, plausible, and empowering without being historically demonstrated. When a secret code is claimed, ask whether independent sources document the code, whether the symbols existed during the period in question, and whether participants themselves ever described using it. Preserve oral tradition—but distinguish tradition from verified historical fact.
