Nat Turner Was Almost Certainly Not “Eaten”: How Social Media Turns Real Atrocity into Bad History

There is no need to invent horrors in order to demonstrate the brutality of American slavery. The documented treatment of Nat Turner after his execution was already grotesque. Turner was hanged by the Commonwealth of Virginia on November 11, 1831, after leading the Southampton County slave revolt. Contemporary reporting stated that his body was handed over to surgeons for dissection. The Library of Virginia, summarizing the historical record, goes somewhat further: Turner was beheaded after his hanging and was “probably skinned and dissected”; the location of his remains is unknown.

That is disturbing history. It is also different from saying white Virginians ate Nat Turner.

The distinction matters because a sensational claim has increasingly circulated on social media that Turner was not merely mutilated, dissected, or made into postmortem trophies, but literally consumed as food. Recent online discussions repeat statements such as “they ate him” and then expand that assertion into a larger story in which the routine consumption of Black bodies supposedly formed a widespread, hidden feature of American slavery and lynching culture.

The available evidence does not support that conclusion.

What Actually Happened to Nat Turner's Body?

The strongest near-contemporary evidence concerns dissection. A report originating with the *Petersburg Intelligencer* shortly after Turner's execution stated that his body was turned over to surgeons. Other newspapers repeated the story. There was also an almost immediate macabre rumor that Turner had somehow sold his own corpse to physicians for dissection and spent the proceeds on ginger cakes. That story was transparently questionable: as an enslaved man condemned by the state, Turner had no meaningful legal authority to sell his own body. The Nat Turner Project, which draws heavily on modern scholarship by historians including David Allmendinger, explicitly warns that “wild rumors circulated” concerning Turner's remains and that their truthfulness is “very much in doubt.”

Much of the grisliest version comes from John W. Cromwell, writing in 1920—nearly ninety years after Turner died. Cromwell reported that Turner's skin was used for souvenirs such as purses, his flesh was rendered into grease, and his bones were distributed as trophies. He also repeated claims that pieces of Turner's skin, an ear, and possibly his skull remained in private possession.

Cromwell's account cannot simply be dismissed. Later historians have taken seriously the possibility that Turner was flayed and that pieces of his corpse circulated as racial trophies. The Library of Virginia itself regards his being skinned as probable.

But Cromwell's account is not contemporary testimony, and even Cromwell does not say that people ate Turner. “His flesh made into grease” is not equivalent to “his flesh was eaten.” Even if the grease story is accurate—and it is less securely documented than the dissection—it does not establish ingestion. Rendering human tissue, collecting bones, tanning skin, anatomical dissection, and eating human flesh are distinct acts.

Converting one into another is precisely how historical misinformation develops.

The evidentiary hierarchy should therefore be stated plainly: Turner's execution is certain; his body being delivered for dissection is very strongly documented; mutilation and flaying are plausible and probably occurred; the trophy stories have historical support but contain uncertain details; literal consumption of Turner as food is not established by credible primary evidence.

Calling the last claim a fact goes beyond the evidence.

The Real Story Is Bad Enough

Correcting the cannibalism claim should not become an exercise in sanitizing what happened.

Nineteenth-century American medical institutions frequently obtained cadavers under circumstances that would be considered profoundly unethical today. Physicians and medical students sometimes relied on grave robbing because legally available cadavers were scarce. Historical medical scholarship describes body theft as a longstanding problem in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century American anatomical education.

Nor is the use of human skin purely mythical. There are authenticated nineteenth-century American examples of anthropodermic bibliopegy—binding books in human skin. The College of Physicians of Philadelphia documents physician John Stockton Hough taking skin from the body of Mary Lynch after her 1869 death and tanning it; he subsequently used human skin in book bindings.

That helps make the reported desecration of Turner's corpse historically conceivable. But again, human-skin artifacts are evidence of corpse desecration, not evidence of cannibalism.

The distinction is not pedantic. If every use of a corpse becomes “cannibalism,” then the term ceases to describe anything precisely. Autopsy becomes cannibalism. Anatomical dissection becomes cannibalism. Collecting bones becomes cannibalism. Making leather from human skin becomes cannibalism. Rendering fat becomes cannibalism. These acts may be desecrating, racist, criminal, or horrifying without being acts of eating another human being.

Historical analysis requires categories even when the categories describe atrocities.

Was Cannibalism Widespread in Early America?

A second social-media claim goes considerably further: that Europeans or white Americans regularly practised cannibalism from the seventeenth through nineteenth centuries and that American history has somehow concealed this fact.

That proposition mixes together several very different phenomena and then calls all of them “cannibalism.”

There are unquestionable cases of cannibalism in the territories that became the United States. The clearest seventeenth-century example is Jamestown.

During the catastrophic “Starving Time” of 1609–1610, the Virginia settlement suffered famine on an extraordinary scale. About 80 percent of the colonists died. Contemporary accounts had long described colonists resorting to cannibalism, but archaeological investigation eventually supplied physical confirmation. Smithsonian researchers examined the remains of a roughly fourteen-year-old English girl, conventionally called “Jane,” and found cuts and chopping marks demonstrating an attempt to remove flesh and brain tissue after her death. The Smithsonian describes this as forensic proof of survival cannibalism at Jamestown.

That case is real.

It does not demonstrate a normal colonial food practice. It demonstrates a starving settlement descending into an extreme survival emergency.

The same distinction applies to perhaps the most famous nineteenth-century American case: the Donner-Reed Party. Snowbound in the Sierra Nevada during the winter of 1846–1847, the emigrants exhausted their livestock and other food. Members of the group eventually consumed the bodies of people who had died. The National Park Service explicitly identifies cannibalism as one of the desperate survival measures taken by the trapped emigrants.

Again: real cannibalism, but survival cannibalism under catastrophic conditions, not evidence of an established American custom.

Jamestown and the Donner Party are famous partly because they were extraordinary.

If cannibalism had been a routine component of seventeenth-, eighteenth-, and nineteenth-century American life, historians would not have to build the case around a small collection of famine, shipwreck, expedition, criminal, or survival incidents. There would instead be extensive evidence of ordinary consumption: recipes, markets, household records, commercial systems, court cases, diaries, medical descriptions, archaeological deposits, and repeated observations across communities.

That evidentiary pattern does not exist.

The More Complicated Case of “Medicinal Cannibalism”

There is one area where the social-media argument sometimes touches something real but then overextends it: corpse medicine.

Early modern Europeans did consume human-derived substances medicinally. From roughly the medieval period through the seventeenth century, and lingering into the eighteenth, European pharmacology sometimes used powdered Egyptian mummy, human skull, blood, fat, and other human material as remedies. Medical historians sometimes call this “medicinal cannibalism.” Scholarly sources describe the use of mummy as particularly widespread in Europe from the twelfth through seventeenth centuries, surviving in declining form afterward.

This produces an important historical irony. European writers routinely represented supposed cannibalism among non-European peoples as proof of savagery while Europeans themselves were consuming substances derived from human corpses as medicine. Historians studying the early modern Atlantic have rightly emphasized that contradiction and shown how accusations of cannibalism could also function politically—helping Europeans depict conquered peoples as barbaric and thereby legitimize conquest, enslavement, and dispossession.

But this fact must also be kept within its evidentiary boundaries.

Evidence that corpse medicine was widespread in early modern Europe is not evidence that ordinary residents of colonial America or the nineteenth-century United States routinely ate human flesh. The practice was already declining substantially by the eighteenth century. The historical literature retrieved for this question supports a major European tradition much more strongly than it supports claims of widespread everyday American consumption.

And medicinal ingestion itself differed from killing people for food or roasting human bodies at communal meals. Powdering supposed mummy material into a medicine certainly meets some broad definitions of cannibalism, but it belongs to a different historical category from famine cannibalism, ritual cannibalism, homicidal cannibalism, and ordinary dietary consumption.

Collapsing those categories creates the illusion of prevalence.

Cannibalism Accusations Have Their Own History

There is another reason to treat sweeping claims cautiously: accusations of cannibalism have historically been unusually powerful propaganda.

Early European colonizers repeatedly circulated stories about cannibalistic peoples. Some reports may have described real practices; others were exaggerated, misunderstood, secondhand, or invented. Historians therefore do not assume either that every early cannibalism report was true or that every report was false. The scholarship instead examines each claim according to the available documentary, archaeological, and anthropological evidence.

This is worth remembering because social media sometimes reproduces the same intellectual error in reverse.

Earlier propagandists said, in effect, “Those people are cannibals; therefore their entire civilization is barbaric.”

Modern viral history sometimes says, “Here is a story involving a mutilated corpse, therefore white Americans were routinely cannibals.”

Neither method is serious history.

One atrocity does not automatically establish a social norm. A documented occurrence does not establish prevalence. A late oral tradition does not equal a contemporary record. And two similar-sounding practices are not automatically the same practice.

The Nat Turner Claim Fails That Test

Applied specifically to Nat Turner, the cannibalism claim requires a piece of evidence that has not been produced.

We have evidence that he was hanged.

We have near-contemporary evidence that surgeons received his body for dissection.

We have later but significant evidence suggesting that his corpse was flayed and that pieces may have circulated as trophies.

We have a later report that some flesh was rendered into “grease.”

What we do not have is credible contemporary testimony that anyone cooked Turner's flesh, ate it, served it, swallowed it medicinally, or otherwise consumed his body internally.

The missing step is not trivial. It is the entire cannibalism claim.

The Nat Turner Project—an academic digital project devoted specifically to reconstructing the revolt and its historical memory—warns readers that stories concerning Turner's corpse quickly became wild rumors and explicitly questions their reliability. That is an especially important caution because extraordinary rumors began circulating almost immediately after the revolt itself. Turner had already become a figure of terror, myth, racial propaganda, and folklore before his execution.

A responsible account should therefore say something like this:

After Nat Turner was executed, his corpse was almost certainly subjected to anatomical dissection and probably further mutilation. Later accounts say his skin was made into souvenirs, his bones distributed as trophies, and some flesh rendered into grease. Those reports deserve serious attention, but there is presently no solid evidence that Turner's body was eaten as food. Calling him a victim of cannibalism converts uncertain postmortem traditions into a claim the surviving record does not establish.

That formulation loses none of the horror.

It merely removes what cannot be demonstrated.

“Widespread Cannibalism” Is an Even Weaker Claim

The broader contention that cannibalism was widespread throughout what became the United States from the seventeenth through nineteenth centuries is even harder to sustain.

There were genuine episodes of survival cannibalism. Jamestown is archaeologically proven. The Donner Party is historically well documented. There were traditions of European corpse medicine whose influence belongs within the Atlantic history of medicine. There were grave robbers, anatomical dissections, trophy-taking, scalp-taking, body mutilations, human-skin artifacts, and grotesque abuses of corpses. There were also accusations of cannibalism—some true, some uncertain, some politically useful inventions.

All of those things belong in American history.

But their existence does not add up to widespread cannibalism.

“Widespread” is a claim about frequency and social distribution. It requires evidence that a behavior occurred routinely or across substantial portions of a society. Isolated famine emergencies separated by more than two centuries do not satisfy that standard. A declining European pharmaceutical tradition does not satisfy it. Medical dissection does not satisfy it. Postmortem racial trophies do not satisfy it. And rumors repeated decades after an event certainly do not satisfy it.

The better historical conclusion is less sensational but more defensible: cannibalism occurred in early American and United States history, sometimes with incontrovertible evidence, but it was exceptional rather than a widespread ordinary practice.

Why the Correction Matters

Some may ask why anyone should object to exaggeration when the underlying history of slavery was already horrific.

Because historical credibility is cumulative.

If a reader discovers that a viral claim about Nat Turner being eaten cannot be substantiated, that reader may begin doubting well-documented atrocities that can be substantiated: family separation, slave sales, whipping, sexual exploitation, kidnapping, forced labor, the suppression of literacy, retaliatory killings after slave revolts, medical exploitation, grave robbing, lynching, and the collection of human remains as trophies.

Bad history therefore does not strengthen the case against historical injustice. It provides an unnecessary vulnerability in a case already supported by overwhelming evidence.

There is also something intellectually important about refusing sensationalism. Nat Turner's body did not need to be eaten for his treatment to demonstrate dehumanization. Giving the corpse of an executed enslaved rebel to physicians, apparently decapitating and dissecting it, probably flaying it, and possibly distributing parts as trophies is sufficiently revealing of the racial order in which he lived and died.

Likewise, American history does not need an invented tradition of mass cannibalism to be disturbing. The actual historical record is more complicated: people under extreme starvation sometimes consumed the dead; European medicine inherited practices involving human remains; physicians violated corpses in the name of anatomy; racialized bodies could become objects of scientific collection and trophies; and accusations of cannibalism themselves became weapons of empire and racial ideology.

That history is stranger, more disturbing, and more instructive than the meme.

The appropriate response to fake history is therefore not to defend the United States from uncomfortable facts. It is to insist upon the uncomfortable facts exactly as the evidence supports them. Nat Turner's corpse was almost certainly desecrated. His body was dissected. It was probably mutilated further. Claims involving trophies deserve serious consideration. But the assertion that “they ate Nat Turner” crosses from documented atrocity into an unsupported conclusion.

There is more than enough real history to condemn without manufacturing another crime.
