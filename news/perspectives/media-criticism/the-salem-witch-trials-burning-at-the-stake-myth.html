The Salem Witch Trials and the Burning-at-the-Stake Myth

Published: August 9, 2026

Overview

Popular depictions of the Salem Witch Trials frequently show accused witches tied to wooden stakes while Puritan authorities set fires beneath them. The image is so closely associated with witch persecution that many people assume burning was the method of execution used at Salem in 1692.

It was not.

No person executed during the Salem Witch Trials was burned at the stake. Nineteen people convicted of witchcraft were executed by hanging: fourteen women and five men. An additional accused man, Giles Corey, was pressed to death beneath heavy stones after refusing to enter a plea and submit to trial. Several other accused people died while imprisoned.

Burning was genuinely used against people accused or convicted of witchcraft in parts of Europe, although even there the familiar image of every victim being burned alive oversimplifies considerable regional variation. In Scotland, for example, many condemned people were strangled before their bodies were burned. In England and its North American colonies, hanging was the characteristic form of execution for witchcraft.

The Salem burning myth therefore arose from the blending of different witch-hunting traditions into one generic image. European burnings, executions for heresy, later literature, illustrations, film, television, Halloween imagery, and the symbolic association between witches and fire all contributed to a popular memory that does not match the Salem court record.

Correcting the method of execution does not make the Salem proceedings less brutal. The victims were imprisoned under harsh conditions, subjected to deeply defective proceedings, convicted using evidence that later authorities rejected, and killed by public hanging or, in Corey's case, crushing.

The Claim

The familiar version is simple:

People convicted of witchcraft during the Salem Witch Trials were burned at the stake.

Variations include:

  • Puritan authorities burned witches alive in Salem;
  • Bridget Bishop was burned as Salem's first convicted witch;
  • Rebecca Nurse was burned at the stake;
  • John Proctor was burned for witchcraft;
  • the Salem executions occurred around large public bonfires;
  • Giles Corey was burned after refusing to confess;
  • American witch trials generally punished witchcraft by burning;
  • and Salem's “witch-burning” history explains the city's modern association with witches.

None of those descriptions accurately identifies the execution method used against the people put to death during the Salem proceedings.

The Salem Victims Were Hanged, Not Burned

The Library of Congress summarizes the execution record clearly: nineteen people were convicted and hanged, while an additional accused man was pressed to death.

Massachusetts legal-history materials give the same basic accounting: nineteen men and women were executed by hanging, Giles Corey died under judicial torture, and others died in prison.

The execution method is therefore not an uncertain detail reconstructed from folklore.

Hanging appears throughout the surviving documentary record of the Salem proceedings.

Four Execution Dates Account for the Nineteen Hangings

The nineteen Salem hangings occurred in four groups between June and September 1692.

June 10, 1692

  • Bridget Bishop

July 19, 1692

  • Sarah Good
  • Elizabeth Howe
  • Susannah Martin
  • Rebecca Nurse
  • Sarah Wildes

August 19, 1692

  • George Burroughs
  • Martha Carrier
  • George Jacobs Sr.
  • John Proctor
  • John Willard

September 22, 1692

  • Martha Corey
  • Mary Eastey
  • Alice Parker
  • Mary Parker
  • Ann Pudeator
  • Wilmot Redd
  • Margaret Scott
  • Samuel Wardwell

None of these nineteen people was burned.

Bridget Bishop Was the First Person Executed

Bridget Bishop became the first person tried by the newly created Court of Oyer and Terminer.

She was convicted on June 2, 1692, and executed on June 10.

The University of Virginia's Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive identifies Bishop as the first person hanged as a result of the 1692 trials.

Later popular culture sometimes depicts Bishop as a woman burned for witchcraft.

The historical record instead places her death on the gallows.

Rebecca Nurse Was Hanged

Rebecca Nurse became one of the most famous victims of the trials.

She was a respected elderly church member whose accusation shocked many members of the community.

A jury initially returned a verdict of not guilty.

After the judges instructed the jurors to reconsider part of the evidence, they returned and convicted her.

Nurse was executed with four other women on July 19, 1692.

She was hanged.

She was not burned.

John Proctor Was Hanged

John Proctor has become especially well known through Arthur Miller's twentieth-century play The Crucible.

The historical Proctor was executed on August 19, 1692, together with George Burroughs, Martha Carrier, George Jacobs Sr., and John Willard.

All five were hanged.

Proctor was not burned at the stake.

Eight People Were Hanged in the Final Salem Execution

September 22, 1692, was the final execution date of the Salem crisis.

Eight people were hanged that day.

It was the largest single execution group of the trials.

Among them was Martha Corey, whose husband Giles had died only days earlier under an entirely different form of judicial coercion.

The September executions again demonstrate that hanging—not burning—was the punishment actually carried out following Salem witchcraft convictions.

Giles Corey Was Not Hanged

One major Salem victim was killed by a method other than hanging.

Giles Corey was approximately eighty years old when he was accused of witchcraft.

Unlike the nineteen people executed following conviction, Corey refused to enter the plea necessary for his trial to proceed.

Under the English legal procedure known as peine forte et dure, a defendant who stood mute could be subjected to extreme pressure intended to force a plea.

Corey continued refusing.

Heavy stones were placed upon him.

He died under the weight on September 19, 1692.

Giles Corey Was Pressed, Not Burned

Corey's death is sometimes folded into a generic account of Salem executions.

It should be distinguished carefully.

He was:

  • accused of witchcraft;
  • imprisoned;
  • brought before the court;
  • and subjected to pressing because he would not enter a plea.

Because he prevented the ordinary trial from proceeding, Corey was not convicted of witchcraft in the same procedural manner as the nineteen people who were later hanged.

His death was nevertheless directly caused by the Salem judicial proceedings.

He was not burned.

What About “More Weight”?

Corey is traditionally remembered for responding to his torturers with the words:

“More weight.”

The phrase has become a powerful symbol of resistance to the Salem court.

As with many famous last words, historians should distinguish later tradition from verbatim stenographic documentation.

Contemporary and near-contemporary accounts confirm the pressing itself. The Massachusetts Historical Society preserves material recording that Corey was pressed to death after standing mute.

The precise dialogue surrounding his death is less securely documented than the method by which he died.

Others Died in Jail

Execution does not account for every death associated with the Salem prosecutions.

Accused people spent extended periods in colonial jails while awaiting examinations or trials.

Surviving records identify prisoners who died in custody, including:

  • Sarah Osborne;
  • Roger Toothaker;
  • Ann Foster;
  • and Lydia Dustin.

Prison conditions were harsh, and prisoners generally had to bear financial costs associated with their confinement.

Thus, even the familiar figure of twenty people killed directly through hanging and pressing does not capture all deaths connected to the crisis.

The Salem Witch Trials Were a Legal Proceeding

The executions were not spontaneous mob killings.

They occurred through colonial legal institutions.

Governor William Phips created a special Court of Oyer and Terminer in 1692 to hear witchcraft cases.

The court examined accusations, received testimony, conducted trials, rendered verdicts, and issued death sentences.

Massachusetts's Supreme Judicial Court history notes that the special court operated from June until late October 1692 and made extensive use of controversial spectral evidence.

The institutional character of the proceedings makes the actual method of punishment recoverable from legal and administrative records.

Witchcraft Was a Capital Crime

Colonial Massachusetts law had long treated witchcraft as a capital offense.

An early Massachusetts legal provision stated:

“If any man or woman be a witch ... they shall be put to death.”

The law reflected a religious and legal worldview in which entering into relations with evil supernatural powers could be understood not merely as religious error but as a threat to the entire community.

The law prescribed death.

The Salem courts carried death sentences out through hanging.

Hanging Fit the English Legal Tradition

Massachusetts developed within an English legal world.

In England, people executed following felony convictions for witchcraft were ordinarily hanged.

Library of Congress legal-history materials document seventeenth-century English witchcraft cases ending in hanging.

Cambridge scholarship likewise distinguishes the English and New England practice of hanging from forms of burning found elsewhere in Europe.

Salem's execution method was therefore not an unusual exception to an English rule of witch burning.

Hanging was consistent with the legal tradition from which Massachusetts authorities drew.

So Where Did the Image of Burning Witches Come From?

The association between witch hunting and burning is not entirely fictional.

Burning occurred during European witch persecutions.

But Europe did not possess one uniform criminal-justice system.

Execution practices varied according to:

  • kingdom;
  • territory;
  • legal tradition;
  • court;
  • period;
  • and the particular offense charged.

The modern image of “the witch burning” combines these different regional traditions into one supposedly universal method of punishment.

Continental Europe Had Different Practices

Major witch hunts occurred across parts of the Holy Roman Empire, Switzerland, France, and other continental European jurisdictions.

Burning was used in a number of these regions.

Some condemned people were burned alive.

In other cases, execution occurred first and the body was burned afterward.

Consequently, even the phrase “burned as a witch” can conceal important procedural differences.

A history of witch persecution in one part of Europe should not automatically be transferred to English Massachusetts.

Scotland Provides an Important Example

Scotland experienced extensive witch prosecution and execution.

Historic Environment Scotland explains that most condemned Scottish witches who were executed were strangled and then had their bodies burned.

Only a relatively small number are known to have been burned alive.

This distinction is useful because even a genuine historical reference to someone being “burned” does not automatically prove that burning itself was the mechanism that caused death.

Popular culture tends to collapse:

  • burned alive;
  • executed and then burned;
  • hanged;
  • and tortured before execution

into one generic picture of a witch tied alive to a flaming stake.

Heresy Burnings Also Contributed to the Image

Early modern Europeans were sometimes burned for religious offenses other than witchcraft.

England itself provides famous examples of Protestant and Catholic martyrs burned during periods of religious persecution.

The Library of Congress, for example, documents Protestant martyr John Rogers being burned at Smithfield in 1555 under Queen Mary I.

Later audiences unfamiliar with the distinctions among:

  • heresy;
  • treason;
  • witchcraft;
  • religious persecution;
  • and different national legal systems

can easily combine them into one generalized history of people being “burned by the Church.”

Salem then becomes inserted into an execution tradition that did not govern its actual proceedings.

“The Church Burned the Salem Witches” Is Wrong in Two Ways

Another common version says:

The Puritan Church burned witches in Salem.

The statement is inaccurate both institutionally and procedurally.

Ministers and religious beliefs strongly influenced the social environment surrounding the crisis.

But the death sentences were imposed through colonial courts and carried out by civil authorities.

And the execution method was hanging.

Religious influence over the proceedings was real.

That does not transform the executions into church-administered burnings.

“Salem” Is Also Geographically More Complicated Than It Sounds

The phrase “Salem Witch Trials” can create the impression that everything happened within the boundaries of today's compact downtown Salem.

The crisis involved:

  • Salem Village, now largely Danvers;
  • Salem Town;
  • Andover;
  • Ipswich;
  • Topsfield;
  • Beverly;
  • and other Massachusetts communities.

Accusations expanded far beyond the original Salem Village conflicts.

The proceedings were therefore a regional witchcraft crisis administered through colonial institutions, not merely a municipal episode involving a few Salem officials.

The Execution Site Has Also Been Misremembered

For generations, accounts loosely placed the Salem hangings on “Gallows Hill.”

Modern historical research has refined the location.

In 2016, researchers confirmed Proctor's Ledge, at the base of Gallows Hill, as the execution site.

The City of Salem subsequently established a memorial there to the nineteen people who were hanged.

This is another example of historical understanding becoming more precise as documentary evidence, landscape analysis, and earlier scholarship are reconsidered together.

There Was No Giant Salem Bonfire

The image of nineteenth or twenty people being gathered around a large burning stake does not correspond to the chronology.

The victims were executed on four separate hanging dates extending across more than three months.

Bridget Bishop was executed alone in June.

Five people were hanged in July.

Five more were hanged in August.

Giles Corey was pressed in September.

Eight people were hanged three days later.

There was no single Salem witch-burning event.

The Accused Were Not All Women

Burning imagery often reinforces another stereotype:

Salem authorities persecuted only women as witches.

Women represented the majority of those accused and executed.

But men were also prosecuted and killed.

Five of the nineteen people hanged were men:

  • George Burroughs;
  • George Jacobs Sr.;
  • John Proctor;
  • John Willard;
  • and Samuel Wardwell.

Giles Corey was a sixth male victim killed through the proceedings.

Gender is essential to the broader history of witch persecution, but Salem cannot accurately be described as exclusively the execution of women.

Fourteen of the Nineteen Hanged Victims Were Women

Correcting the all-women stereotype should not erase the obvious gender imbalance.

Fourteen of Salem's nineteen hanging victims were women.

Early modern beliefs about women's supposed moral, intellectual, and spiritual vulnerability contributed to witch-hunting patterns in many societies.

But gender interacted with numerous other factors, including:

  • family disputes;
  • property;
  • religious conflict;
  • social reputation;
  • community factionalism;
  • prior accusations;
  • and interpersonal grievances.

A serious interpretation therefore preserves the gender pattern without pretending that every accusation arose from one identical cause.

The Victims Were Convicted of Witchcraft, but Historians Should Use “Witch” Carefully

Historical accounts frequently refer to “the Salem witches.”

The phrase can be convenient shorthand.

But it can also unintentionally reproduce the court's accusation as though historians accept it as fact.

The people hanged in 1692 are better described as:

  • people accused of witchcraft;
  • people convicted of witchcraft;
  • Salem Witch Trials victims;
  • or alleged witches.

Saying that Rebecca Nurse was “executed after conviction for witchcraft” accurately describes the legal history.

Saying simply that she “was a witch” adopts the premise used to kill her.

Spectral Evidence Helped Produce Convictions

One of the most notorious features of the Salem proceedings was the use of spectral evidence.

Accusers testified that the specter or supernatural form of an accused person appeared to them and caused harm.

Such testimony posed an obvious problem:

no ordinary observer could independently see or test the alleged apparition.

The Court of Oyer and Terminer nevertheless accepted spectral claims as evidence within the proceedings.

Later criticism of this practice became one of the factors undermining the prosecutions.

The Later Court Was More Skeptical

Governor William Phips dissolved the Court of Oyer and Terminer in October 1692.

A newly established Superior Court of Judicature began hearing remaining witchcraft cases in January 1693.

Massachusetts's Supreme Judicial Court history notes that this court did not rely upon spectral evidence in the same manner.

Of twenty-six accused people whose cases it heard, twenty-three were found not guilty and the remaining three were subsequently pardoned.

The dramatic change in outcomes demonstrates how much the earlier crisis depended upon the evidentiary rules and assumptions of the special court.

Confession Could Produce a Strange Survival Incentive

The Salem system created a grim paradox.

People who denied being witches could be convicted and executed.

People who confessed could become useful to investigators by describing the supposed workings of Satan and identifying additional alleged witches.

Several confessors survived the height of the crisis.

This does not mean confession guaranteed safety.

Samuel Wardwell confessed and later repudiated his confession; he was ultimately hanged.

But the pattern illustrates how a judicial system designed to uncover a presumed conspiracy could reward evidence that expanded the conspiracy while treating denial as suspicious.

Burning Would Not Have Made Salem More “Medieval”

Popular discussion often describes Salem as an example of medieval superstition.

The trials occurred in 1692.

That places them in the early modern period, not the Middle Ages.

Large-scale European witch hunting itself reached important peaks during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Associating witches, stakes, castles, medieval dungeons, and Salem into one visual package compresses several centuries and multiple legal traditions into a fictional setting.

The Burning Image Works Extremely Well in Art

Fire has extraordinary visual power.

A hanging can be depicted through a scaffold or rope.

Burning provides:

  • flames;
  • smoke;
  • crowds illuminated at night;
  • religious symbolism;
  • the stake itself;
  • and an immediately recognizable image of persecution.

As a result, fire became one of the dominant visual symbols of the witch hunt.

Once audiences associate witches generally with burning, the image can be transferred to Salem without anyone deliberately deciding to falsify the execution record.

Historical Fiction Can Merge Different Witch-Hunt Traditions

A novelist, painter, filmmaker, or television producer may borrow visual elements from different countries and centuries to communicate immediately:

A witch hunt is happening.

Audiences then encounter:

  • Salem names;
  • Puritan clothing;
  • European torture devices;
  • continental burnings;
  • later occult symbolism;
  • and modern Halloween imagery

within one fictional presentation.

The resulting story may be dramatically effective while being historically synthetic.

Arthur Miller Did Not Write a Documentary

Arthur Miller's 1953 play The Crucible became one of the most influential cultural representations of Salem.

Miller based the play on real events and people but deliberately altered chronology, ages, relationships, and motivations for dramatic purposes.

His principal objective included using Salem as a lens through which to examine accusation, fear, conformity, and contemporary anticommunist investigations.

Students encountering Salem primarily through historical drama should therefore compare theatrical scenes with the surviving 1692 records.

Historical fiction can illuminate a period without serving as a transcript.

The Real Execution Method Was Already Brutal

Sometimes a myth persists because the false version seems more horrifying than the documented one.

Burning at the stake creates an especially terrible image.

Correcting it should not sanitize the actual executions.

Seventeenth-century hanging generally did not use the later long-drop method designed to break the neck quickly.

Victims could die through strangulation and asphyxiation.

Giles Corey endured crushing beneath heavy stones.

Prisoners experienced confinement, illness, uncertainty, financial burdens, and separation from family.

Historical accuracy does not require exaggeration to establish the cruelty of the proceedings.

Execution Method Matters Historically

It may seem unimportant whether an innocent victim was hanged or burned.

Both are forms of state killing.

But the distinction matters because punishment reveals:

  • which legal system was operating;
  • how an offense was classified;
  • which cultural traditions shaped the court;
  • how different jurisdictions treated similar accusations;
  • and how later memory altered the event.

If the execution method can be checked against surviving records, historians should report the method accurately.

A Small Error Can Reveal a Larger Historical Conflation

The Salem burning myth is useful because the correction initially appears trivial.

Replace:

burned

with:

hanged.

But investigating why the error occurred reveals a larger problem.

Popular memory has merged:

  • English witchcraft law;
  • Massachusetts witch trials;
  • Scottish executions;
  • continental European witch hunts;
  • religious heresy burnings;
  • fiction;
  • and modern supernatural imagery.

The wrong execution method is therefore evidence of a broader collapse of historical context.

Do Not Replace the Myth With “Witches Were Never Burned”

That would be an equally inaccurate overcorrection.

Burning was genuinely connected to witch persecution in parts of Europe.

The correction is geographic and legal:

Salem's convicted witchcraft defendants were not burned.

It is not:

No person anywhere in history was ever burned after a witchcraft conviction.

Historical corrections should become more precise rather than simply reverse the original claim.

Do Not Replace the Myth With “Europe Burned Everyone Alive”

The broader European story also requires qualification.

Jurisdictions differed substantially.

Even where burning was part of the sentence, the condemned person might be strangled, beheaded, or otherwise killed before the body was burned.

Historic Environment Scotland, for example, emphasizes that most Scottish witchcraft victims who were executed were strangled before their bodies were burned and that relatively few are known to have been burned alive.

A phrase such as “burned as a witch” therefore needs to be investigated rather than assumed to mean one universal procedure.

Do Not Replace the Myth With “Nothing Happened in Salem”

Correcting sensational details can occasionally provoke another misunderstanding:

If nobody was burned, perhaps the whole Salem story was exaggerated.

The executions are extensively documented.

Surviving materials include:

  • arrest warrants;
  • examinations;
  • depositions;
  • indictments;
  • trial material;
  • execution warrants;
  • letters;
  • sermons;
  • diaries;
  • and later governmental proceedings responding to the trials.

The University of Virginia's Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive brings large portions of this record together digitally.

The burning is mythical.

The persecution is not.

The Authorities Eventually Recognized Serious Failure

Opposition to the prosecutions increased during and after 1692.

The reliance upon spectral evidence drew growing criticism.

The special court was dissolved.

Later Massachusetts proceedings reversed attainders, compensated some families, and formally condemned the injustice.

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, Massachusetts took additional legislative steps to clear the names of accused people who had remained outside earlier acts of exoneration.

Modern institutions do not preserve Salem as an example of successful witch detection.

They preserve it as a warning about defective evidence, institutional fear, accusation, and wrongful prosecution.

Why the Myth Persists

The burning-at-the-stake story survives because it combines several powerful cultural associations.

Audiences already associate:

  • witches with fire;
  • religious persecution with burning;
  • early modern Europe with witch hunts;
  • Salem with witches;
  • and public execution with dramatic spectacle.

The brain then completes the story:

Salem + witches + executions = witches burned at the stake.

Each individual association appears plausible.

The combined conclusion is wrong.

Historical Images Can Reinforce the Error

Illustrations of witch trials are often reproduced without explaining what country, year, or trial they depict.

An engraving of a European witch burning may appear beside an article about Salem.

A reader then reasonably assumes the image depicts Salem itself.

Over time, visual repetition can become stronger than textual memory.

This is why image provenance matters.

Before treating a historical illustration as evidence, ask:

  • Who created it?
  • When?
  • What event does it actually depict?
  • Was the artist an eyewitness?
  • Was it produced decades or centuries later?
  • And is the caption original or supplied by a later publisher?

A Nineteenth-Century Salem Illustration Is Not a Photograph of 1692

Many familiar Salem images were created in the nineteenth century.

Artists reconstructed events that had occurred nearly two centuries earlier.

Those works are valuable evidence for how later Americans imagined Salem.

They are not eyewitness visual records.

An artist could:

  • choose clothing;
  • invent facial expressions;
  • arrange spectators;
  • dramatize courtroom behavior;
  • and incorporate conventions from other witch-hunt imagery.

The historical event and the history of its representation should be studied separately.

The Real Salem Story Is More Disturbing Than the Myth

Burning creates a spectacular villain:

authorities drag innocent women to a fire and kill them publicly.

The documented Salem history is in some ways more institutionally disturbing.

Ordinary legal processes were used.

Warrants were written.

Magistrates conducted examinations.

witnesses gave sworn testimony.

Juries deliberated.

judges pronounced sentences.

sheriffs carried out executions.

The catastrophe occurred not because all law disappeared but because legal institutions accepted unreliable premises and evidence.

That lesson requires no fictional bonfire.

The Victims Were Real People, Not Halloween Characters

Salem's modern association with witches can make the 1692 defendants seem like characters in supernatural entertainment.

They were members of real communities.

They were:

  • farmers;
  • wives;
  • husbands;
  • mothers;
  • fathers;
  • church members;
  • laborers;
  • ministers;
  • neighbors;
  • and property holders.

Their surviving court records allow historians to recover individual voices, disputes, denials, petitions, and family relationships.

Correcting the execution method helps restore those people from generic “witch-burning” imagery to their actual historical circumstances.

A Better Version of the Story

A historically responsible summary might say:

During the Salem Witch Trials of 1692, Massachusetts authorities executed nineteen people convicted of witchcraft by hanging. Fourteen were women and five were men. Giles Corey, an elderly man accused of witchcraft who refused to enter a plea, was pressed to death beneath heavy stones. Several other accused people died in prison. No Salem victim was burned at the stake. Burning was used in some European witch persecutions, which helped create the later popular association between witches and fire, but Salem followed an English legal tradition in which witchcraft convictions were punished by hanging.

What the Evidence Actually Supports

  • The Salem Witch Trials occurred principally during 1692 in colonial Massachusetts.
  • Witchcraft was treated as a capital crime.
  • Governor William Phips established a special Court of Oyer and Terminer to hear witchcraft cases.
  • The court accepted spectral evidence as part of its proceedings.
  • Nineteen people convicted of witchcraft were executed by hanging.
  • Fourteen of the nineteen hanged victims were women.
  • Five were men.
  • Bridget Bishop was the first Salem defendant executed, on June 10, 1692.
  • Five people were hanged on July 19.
  • Five more were hanged on August 19.
  • Eight were hanged on September 22.
  • No person executed during the Salem Witch Trials was burned at the stake.
  • Giles Corey was accused of witchcraft but refused to enter the plea necessary for trial.
  • Corey was subjected to pressing beneath heavy stones and died on September 19, 1692.
  • Corey was therefore neither one of the nineteen hanged victims nor a victim of burning.
  • Several other accused people died in prison.
  • Hanging was consistent with the English and New England legal tradition for witchcraft convictions.
  • Burning was genuinely used in witchcraft persecutions in parts of continental Europe.
  • European execution practices were not uniform.
  • In Scotland, many condemned witchcraft defendants were strangled first and their bodies burned afterward.
  • Only a relatively small number of Scottish witchcraft victims are known to have been burned alive.
  • Burning was also historically associated with prosecutions for heresy, helping strengthen the larger cultural connection between religious persecution and fire.
  • The phrase “Salem Witch Trials” covers a crisis involving people from numerous Massachusetts communities rather than only today's City of Salem.
  • Modern research identifies Proctor's Ledge, at the base of Gallows Hill, as the site of the nineteen hangings.
  • The Court of Oyer and Terminer was dissolved in October 1692.
  • A subsequent court treated spectral evidence much more skeptically and produced overwhelmingly different outcomes.
  • Later Massachusetts governments took steps to reverse convictions, compensate families, and acknowledge the injustice of the proceedings.
  • The Salem burning-at-the-stake image is primarily a later conflation of different European witch-hunting traditions rather than a description of the executions actually carried out in Massachusetts.

Verdict

Claim: Salem authorities burned people convicted of witchcraft at the stake.

Verdict: False.

No person executed during the Salem Witch Trials was burned at the stake.

Nineteen people convicted of witchcraft were hanged between June and September 1692.

Giles Corey, who refused to enter a plea and therefore did not proceed through an ordinary witchcraft trial, was pressed to death beneath heavy stones.

Other accused people died while imprisoned.

Burning did occur in some European witch persecutions, but execution practices differed considerably by jurisdiction. English and New England witchcraft prosecutions generally used hanging, while in Scotland many condemned people were strangled and their bodies subsequently burned.

The familiar Salem bonfire therefore belongs to later cultural memory rather than the execution record of 1692.

The accurate historical conclusion is:

Salem did not burn witches at the stake. Nineteen people convicted of witchcraft were hanged, Giles Corey was pressed to death after refusing to plead, and several accused people died in custody. The burning image comes from conflating Salem with different European witch-hunting traditions.

Media Literacy Principle

A historically real practice should not be transferred automatically from one place to another. When a familiar image appears in a story—witches burning, gladiators giving thumbs-down, or ships falling from a flat Earth—ask whether contemporary records from that specific event actually document it. Similar events in another country or century are context, not proof.
