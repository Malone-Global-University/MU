Basic Computer Skills / Everyday Productivity

## Lesson 9: Building Good Computer Habits

You sit down to finish a short document. A notification interrupts you; several files have similar names; you remember that a copy should be saved but cannot recall where; and you leave the desk without locking the screen. None of these events requires advanced computer knowledge. Together, however, they reveal the difference between knowing individual commands and developing dependable [[computer-habits|computer habits]]. An effective computer user does not have to remember every shortcut or own expensive software. The goal is to build small, repeatable actions that reduce errors, protect information, and make the next work session easier.

In this final lesson of MGU's Everyday Productivity course, you will connect the eight earlier lessons into one [[personal-workflow|personal workflow]]. You will practice starting work deliberately, saving and verifying changes, managing attention, recognizing security warnings, taking comfortable breaks, ending a session safely, and reviewing your system over time. Windows 11 provides the main desktop example, with comparable guidance for macOS, Chromebooks, phones, and tablets. All practice examples use fictional documents and situations. No exercise requires installing software, signing in to a new account, uploading actual personal records, changing security settings, or deleting files.

This lesson teaches habits rather than perfection. A routine should fit your responsibilities, abilities, devices, and school or workplace rules. Some steps happen every session; others are occasional checks. A learner who can explain *when* to perform an action, *why* it matters, and *how to verify the result* is more capable than one who memorizes a large checklist without understanding it.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 100–130 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Everyday Productivity Lessons 1–8 recommended · **Equipment:** This lesson and paper or a plain text editor; a computer or phone is optional · **Practice condition:** Every required activity can be completed offline with fictional information · **Safety:** Do not expose real credentials, alter managed-device settings, delete records, or change account access as a class demonstration.

> **Essential idea:** **Prepare → work deliberately → save and verify → protect access → record the next action → review regularly.** Good computer habits are small steps performed at the right moment, not an endless list of technical chores.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain how a cue, a small action, and a useful outcome can create a repeatable computer habit.
1. Use a simple start-of-session, during-work, and end-of-session routine.
1. Distinguish saving, synchronization, backup, version history, and restoring a previous copy.
1. Recognize when to update supported software, use unique credentials and [[multi-factor-authentication|multi-factor authentication]], lock a device, and avoid suspicious prompts.
1. Control interruptions, choose the next action, and protect information during sharing or screen presentation.
1. Adapt routines for accessibility, comfortable computer use, different platforms, shared devices, and limited connectivity.
1. Diagnose common breakdowns and select a proportionate, reversible first response.
1. Design, test, and revise a realistic seven-day computer-habit plan without exposing private information.

## 1. What makes a computer habit useful?

A [[habit|habit]] is an action that becomes easier to repeat when a familiar situation reminds you to perform it. A computer habit might be pressing Save after an important edit, checking the recipient before selecting Share, placing a downloaded file in the correct project folder, or locking a computer before walking away. These actions are basic. Their reliability comes from making them part of a predictable sequence instead of depending on memory during a busy day.

### Skills and habits are not the same

A [[skill|skill]] is something you are able to do. A habit is something you actually do when the appropriate situation occurs. Knowing that **Ctrl + S** commonly saves a document in supported Windows applications is a skill; routinely making sure that important work is saved in the intended location is a habit. Knowing how to view a file's sharing settings is a skill; checking those settings before sending a sensitive document is a habit. A student may understand every tool in this course and still benefit from a short, practical routine.

Compare these fictional students. Alex has learned many keyboard shortcuts but begins work by opening random tabs. Alex edits a file without checking its location and later cannot identify the version to submit. Blair remembers fewer shortcuts but first opens the correct class folder, chooses the current file, works on one task, saves, confirms the result, and writes the next step. Blair's workflow is more reliable because it makes the desired result repeatable. Speed matters, but correct work and safe handling matter more.

### A simple structure: cue, action, outcome

A [[cue|cue]] is a situation that reminds you to act. The [[action|action]] should be specific and manageable. The [[outcome|outcome]] is the useful result you can observe. For example, the cue *before leaving a computer* can trigger the action *save the document, verify the saved location, and lock the screen*. The outcome is *work preserved and unauthorized casual access reduced*. A cue does not need an alarm. It might be opening a project folder, finishing a paragraph, receiving a file, beginning a meeting, or changing tasks.

| Everyday cue | Small action | What to verify | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start a work session | Open the intended project folder and task note | Correct project, account, and current file | Prevents editing the wrong copy |
| Finish a meaningful edit | Save in the intended format and location | Saved status and recognizable filename | Reduces accidental loss |
| Download a document | Inspect its name and source before filing it | Correct item and authorized download | Keeps intake understandable |
| Receive an unexpected link | Stop and verify the sender through a trusted route | Source and purpose are legitimate | Reduces phishing risk |
| Share a file | Check recipient, file, role, and link scope | Appropriate access only | Reduces unintended disclosure |
| Step away | Lock the screen when supported | Authentication is required to return | Protects an unattended session |
| End a task | Record one next action | Task note is saved and findable | Makes restarting easier |
| Weekly review | Check important work and any backup evidence | Relevant files are findable and recoverable | Detects problems before an emergency |

The third column is essential. **An action without verification can create a false sense of completion.** For instance, selecting Save may save the wrong file; an online upload may still be pending; and choosing a sharing restriction may not affect other access paths. Verification should be proportional to the task. A brief glance at the filename is enough for an ordinary practice note, while a high-stakes document deserves additional checking.

### Start with a minimum viable routine

A [[minimum-viable-routine|minimum viable routine]] is the smallest process that meaningfully improves the outcome. For many beginners it has four steps: **identify the file, do one task, save and check, write the next step**. You do not need to change all your device settings, buy a special planner, or create twenty reminders. If you already have a dependable routine, keep it; add another step only when it solves a recurring problem.

A common mistake is to start with a long daily checklist and abandon it after a difficult week. If you consistently forget to save, attach a save check to a natural event such as the end of each work segment. If you forget where documents go, choose one approved project folder. If you repeatedly lose your place, keep a single task note containing the next action. **Tie the new habit to an existing action**, and make the first version easy to complete.

### Frequency should match the risk

Different checks belong at different times. Saving a work-in-progress can be frequent; checking for updates is generally periodic or triggered by an official notification; validating a critical backup should occur on a schedule appropriate to how costly lost work would be. There is no universal rule requiring the same number of steps for a grocery list and a confidential work report. School and employer policies may impose additional requirements.

| Frequency | Reasonable beginner examples | Not required every time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Each session | Choose correct working file; save; close out; lock when away | Reinstalling security software |
| Before sharing | Verify file, people, permissions, and necessity | Making every document public for convenience |
| As notified or routinely | Apply legitimate supported updates when appropriate | Clicking urgent pop-up advertisements |
| Weekly or another chosen interval | Review task list, Downloads, available backup status, and unfinished work | Deleting everything in Downloads |
| When a problem appears | Pause, inspect, and use a documented recovery step | Resetting or factory-wiping the device immediately |
| During major changes | Preserve recoverable copies and follow approved migration steps | Moving shared folders without permission |

### Worked example: a routine for a small class project

A student is editing a two-page public handout. The student begins by opening `Documents / Community-Class / Handout` and reading the task note. They confirm the correct file, make one change, save it, review the updated paragraph, and record “add two sources tomorrow.” They then close the document and lock the device if leaving it unattended. On Friday they confirm the document is included in their authorized backup process. The system uses no special application and takes only a few deliberate decisions. A consistent routine is more valuable than a complicated one that is never used.

## 2. How do you start work and finish safely?

Reliable work has boundaries. A [[session-opening|session-opening routine]] establishes the task and file location before editing; a [[session-closeout|session closeout]] preserves the result and makes the next session easy. Without these boundaries, people may switch among unrelated files, confuse tabs with saved work, or assume that leaving a document open is the same as saving it.

### A five-step start-of-session routine

1. **Identify the purpose.** State the one task to move forward, such as “draft the introduction” or “review the completed slides.”
1. **Find the correct project home.** Open the authorized folder or online service. Check which account is signed in when that matters.
1. **Select the working file.** Inspect the name, location, and content before editing; do not choose a version merely because its name contains `final`.
1. **Prepare only necessary windows and tabs.** Keep a reference and the working document visible if useful; close unrelated distractions only after confirming no unsaved work.
1. **Choose the first physical action.** For example, “write the heading” is more usable than “get organized.”

On Windows 11, you can use **File Explorer** to navigate to the intended folder and open the file. In a supported application, **Ctrl + S** commonly requests saving, but its behavior depends on the active program and document type. Some modern apps save changes automatically. A familiar save icon or an “All changes saved” indicator is not proof that a document is in the correct folder; verify the document title and storage location. When using a cloud document, allow synchronization to complete before relying on a second device.

### Use the right action: save, save as, export, or upload

**Save** usually records changes to the working item. **Save As** often creates a separately named or located file. **Export** produces an output in a selected format, such as PDF; the exported file may no longer contain the same editable features as the source. **Upload** transfers a file to a service, but the transfer may remain incomplete until the service confirms it. These actions serve different purposes, and interface labels vary by application.

If a project contains an editable report and a PDF for distribution, treat them as distinct files. Keep the approved editable source in a controlled location, export the PDF, inspect the PDF, and send the intended output using an appropriate sharing method. Do not overwrite the editable source with a PDF unless that change is deliberate and supported. A downloaded copy is not automatically synced back to the online original.

| Action | Immediate purpose | Verification question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Save | Keep changes to the active working file | Did the changes persist in the right file? |
| Save As or Make a Copy | Create another file or version | Is the new name and destination correct? |
| Export | Produce a different output format | Does the exported file open and show the intended content? |
| Upload | Send a copy or file to a service | Does the service show completion and the correct destination? |
| Sync | Coordinate file states between supported locations | Has the service finished and are there conflicts? |
| Backup | Maintain a separate recoverable copy or history | Could the required information be restored? |
| Share | Give another person a route or copy | Are the correct people and rights selected? |

### A five-minute closeout that prevents tomorrow's confusion

1. Save the current work in the agreed location and wait for any necessary confirmation.
1. Check the filename, title, relevant content, and—where applicable—sync status.
1. Record the next specific action in a task note or planner rather than trusting open tabs to remind you.
1. Close no-longer-needed windows and temporary material only after safeguarding unsaved work; leave work open if a legitimate workflow requires it.
1. Lock the device when stepping away, and sign out of accounts when using shared or public computers, following local rules.

It is possible to leave a document open during a break, but an open window is not a backup. Locking is also not the same as signing out: a locked personal session can usually be resumed with authentication, while a public computer may require full sign-out and closing the browser. Learn which action your environment requires.

### Windows 11: a safe practice demonstration

On a personally controlled Windows 11 computer, open a text editor and create a harmless fictional note, such as `Practice session: add a title, save, verify`. Save it in a folder you own and can find. Verify the file by locating it in File Explorer and opening it again if appropriate. Record one next action. When you need to leave the device, **Windows logo key + L** normally locks the Windows session. If the shortcut is unavailable, use the device's supported Lock command. **Do not test a lock screen on a public or managed device if you do not know the correct sign-in procedure.** You can describe the action on paper instead.

On some Windows systems, autosave may depend on the application, account, cloud location, or settings. Never assume it is enabled for every program. If the computer is shutting down or applying an update, follow visible prompts about saving open work. Do not force a shutdown simply to test whether a file can be recovered.

### When you switch devices or pause unexpectedly

Suppose you create a document on a laptop and later open a phone. Before editing, ask whether the copy on the phone is the current item in the same authorized account or an older downloaded file. A screenshot or email attachment may show older content. An offline device may hold unsynced changes. When uncertain, **pause and compare the file names, locations, modification context, and actual content** before making changes. Preserving both versions for review may be safer than overwriting either one.

If work is interrupted by a call, power problem, or travel, a brief note can preserve context: “working file: Community-Handout; section complete: introduction; next: add example; save status: confirmed at closeout.” Do not include passwords or confidential customer data in that note. This method works even without a working computer: a paper record can hold the next action until access is restored.

### Worked example: preparing for tomorrow

A volunteer finishes arranging a public event schedule in a word processor. They save the source document, export a PDF for review, open the PDF and check the dates, then place both in the intended project folder. Their task note says “Tomorrow: obtain approval before publishing the schedule.” They do not share the unapproved file or assume that the PDF proves approval. The session ends with a clear next step, correct storage, and a meaningful distinction between an editable working file and a distribution copy.

## 3. How do you protect files, devices, and accounts?

[[digital-safety|Digital safety]] depends on repeatable judgments, not fear or constant checking. Common protections include supported software updates, unique passwords or passkeys where appropriate, [[multi-factor-authentication|multi-factor authentication]], careful handling of unexpected messages, access controls, and recoverable backups. This is an overview of everyday habits rather than a replacement for an organization's security policy or specialist assistance.

### Keep supported software updated through trusted routes

A [[software-update|software update]] can repair security weaknesses, improve compatibility, and change features. Many devices offer automatic updates; some need a restart or permission before changes take effect. Updates should come through the operating system's official settings, managed-device system, or the application provider's verified update process. Do not assume an alarming webpage saying “your computer is infected” is the operating system's official update screen. If you are using a school or employer computer, the administrator may control update timing, and you should not bypass that arrangement.

For Windows 11, the ordinary route is **Start → Settings → Windows Update**. You may inspect update status and the need for a restart. On a Mac, the standard route is **Apple menu → System Settings → General → Software Update**. On a Chromebook, open **Settings → About ChromeOS** to view version and update status; supported devices commonly check for updates automatically. On iPhone or iPad, the normal route is **Settings → General → Software Update**. Android device paths differ by manufacturer; begin with Settings and the operating-system update area. Labels can change, and managed devices may restrict controls. Observation is sufficient for this lesson; **do not install or defer an update merely to complete a class exercise**.

An older operating system that no longer receives standard security updates creates a different risk from a current supported system that has one pending patch. “It still turns on” is not evidence of ongoing security support. Check the vendor's current support policy and ask an authorized person about migration options when needed. Do not download unofficial operating-system installers from pop-up advertisements.

### Protect accounts without publishing secrets

Use a [[unique-password|unique password]] for each important account when passwords are required; where supported, consider [[passkey|passkeys]] and an approved [[password-manager|password manager]]. A reused password can expose several accounts if one service is compromised. [[multi-factor-authentication|Multi-factor authentication]] adds another verification factor, such as an authenticator app, security key, or another supported method; different methods have different security properties. An unexpected approval request is not something to accept just to make it disappear. Protect recovery methods and follow authorized account-recovery procedures.

You do not need to display a password manager, generate a real password, or turn on authentication for a private account during this lesson. The required exercise is to recognize the principle. For example, if a message asks you to read out a one-time code to a supposed support agent, do not supply it. Confirm the service through a known official app or independently verified contact route. Avoid saving live passwords, recovery codes, or account numbers in ordinary class notes, screenshots, or shared documents.

### Stop at suspicious messages and unexpected prompts

[[phishing|Phishing]] uses deceptive email, text messages, websites, or other communications to obtain information or push an unsafe action. A message may claim an urgent missed delivery, a payment problem, a security breach, or a request from a colleague. Attackers can imitate legitimate logos and names, so polished design is not proof of authenticity. When an unexpected request is consequential, verify it through a known channel rather than relying on the message's link, attachment, or telephone number.

A practical stop-and-check routine is:

1. **Pause.** Do not click, download, approve, or pay in response to pressure.
1. **Identify the request.** What information or action is being demanded, and is it necessary?
1. **Verify independently.** Open the organization's known app or website or contact the person through a trusted channel.
1. **Report or discard.** Use an authorized reporting method for suspicious content, then return to the original task.

Never type an actual password into a website simply to test whether the website is suspicious. Avoid using external scanners for private documents without authorization. The safer habit is verification before disclosure.

### Treat sharing as an access decision

File sharing is a form of permission management. Before sending, check **what** the file contains, **who** can access it, **how** they can access it, and **what** they are allowed to do. Named-recipient access may be more appropriate than an unrestricted link. View access may be sufficient where editing is unnecessary. Removing a particular user's direct access may not eliminate access granted through a folder, group, or other link. A previously downloaded copy may remain outside your control even after a share is revoked.

Review screenshots, exports, and attachments for hidden or unintended information, including names, private tabs, account details, and document history when relevant. For a screen-sharing session, close or conceal unrelated sensitive material and inspect the preview where the platform provides one. This combines Lessons 1, 6, and 7 into one habit: **inspect before disclosing**.

### Sync, backup, and recovery are not interchangeable

A [[sync|synchronization]] service tries to coordinate changes across supported locations. If you delete or overwrite a synced file, that change may spread. A [[backup|backup]] is a separate recoverable copy or retained history that can help after an error, failure, or loss. A [[version-history|version history]] feature may store previous states, but its availability and retention limits vary. For important work, understand which files are protected, how often the system runs, and how an authorized restore would work. **An untested or unverified backup is not a reliable recovery plan.**

For personal Windows devices, Microsoft documents multiple backup approaches, including OneDrive-related and other Windows solutions; the exact protection depends on configuration. On a Mac, Time Machine is a distinct backup option. Cloud synchronization alone should not be treated as unlimited retention or a universal recovery guarantee. Do not create duplicate confidential copies in personal cloud accounts when an organization requires approved storage. Backups should comply with permissions, confidentiality rules, and retention requirements.

| Situation | Immediate safe habit | Why the alternative is risky |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Update required” popup on an unrelated webpage | Check updates through the device's trusted settings | A fake installer may be malicious |
| Unexpected sign-in approval request | Deny or leave unapproved and verify account activity through official routes | Approval might authorize someone else's login |
| Document requested through a broad public link | Reassess the audience and permissions | Anyone with the link may gain access if enabled |
| Important file appears missing after sync | Stop further changes, inspect locations and recovery options | More edits or deletes may compound the problem |
| External drive is the only surviving copy | Make an appropriate authorized additional recovery plan | A single device can fail or be lost |
| Unfamiliar removable storage appears | Do not connect or open it without approval | Unknown media may expose systems to risk |
| Shared computer session is finished | Sign out and follow local session-cleanup rules | The next user may access your active account |

### Worked example: an alarming attachment

A learner receives a message that says “Urgent: your tuition payment failed—open this attached form now.” The learner is not expecting such a message. Rather than opening the attachment, they navigate independently to the institution's known portal or contact the official office through a verified number. They do not share a password, payment card, or code. If the message is fraudulent, they follow institutional reporting guidance. If it is legitimate, they can complete the necessary task through the verified service. **Verification solves the problem without trusting the suspicious route.**

## 4. How do you work efficiently and manage attention?

[[digital-productivity|Digital productivity]] is completing useful tasks with a reasonable amount of effort. It is not having the most tabs, responding to every notification immediately, or sitting at a screen continuously. Focus improves when you know what matters next, keep the relevant tools available, and have a simple way to return after interruption. You can adopt these habits even when family responsibilities, disability-related access needs, or workplace duties make long uninterrupted work sessions impractical.

### Choose an action, not an abstract intention

“Work on report” is a broad intention. “Write the first paragraph of the report using the approved outline” is a [[next-action|next action]]: it identifies a concrete step. Begin by reading the assigned task or relevant note. If the task is large, divide it into stages such as locate the source, draft, review, and submit. Not every stage needs its own application. A basic text note or paper checklist may be sufficient.

One useful method is the [[one-task-focus|one-task focus]] approach: identify the primary action, open the minimum necessary tools, finish a coherent segment, and then reassess. This does not mean ignoring urgent messages or accessibility needs. If your job requires real-time responses, use a plan that recognizes them. The important distinction is between **intentional switching** and **unnoticed drifting**.

### Manage tabs and windows as temporary workspaces

A browser tab is a convenient route to a page, not a substitute for a durable note, downloaded resource, saved file, or backup. When you need to continue later, record the link or next action appropriately. Close unneeded tabs only after checking whether they contain unsaved forms or work. A closed tab may sometimes be recoverable, but browser history or session restore is not a dependable way to preserve critical unsaved text.

A simple workspace might have one active document, one trusted reference page, and a small task note. If two items must be compared, arrange windows side by side using supported controls. If extra windows are making the task harder, close or minimize those not needed. Use a search or bookmark rather than opening ten copies of the same page. The goal is to reduce the number of decisions required to resume work.

### Interruptions: record your place before switching

An [[interruption|interruption]] is anything that diverts attention from the primary task. Some interruptions are legitimate or urgent. The practical skill is to preserve context before switching: save, record the next action, and identify the exact file or tab to return to. If interrupted unexpectedly, a two-line note made afterward can still help. You do not have to finish the original task before responding to an important responsibility.

When supported, a device may offer [[do-not-disturb|Do Not Disturb]] or a [[focus-mode|focus mode]] that reduces selected notifications. In Windows 11, **Settings → System → Notifications** and available Focus controls can help you inspect those options. On Macs, iPhones, Android phones, and Chromebooks, relevant controls differ by version and product. Do not silence safety-critical, caregiver, emergency, school, or workplace communications without understanding the consequences. No notification setting is required for this lesson.

### Verify information before acting on it

An answer from a search engine, an AI-generated summary, or a forwarded message may be inaccurate or outdated. When a task depends on current rules, fees, deadlines, public safety information, or account instructions, check the responsible original source. Preserve the source URL or citation in the appropriate note when the work requires evidence. This is another computer habit: **separate a useful lead from verified information**. A screenshot of a statement captures what was displayed; it does not prove the statement was correct.

### Choose the right amount of automation

Some applications offer scheduled reminders, automatic saving, notification controls, synchronization, or suggested tasks. These can be helpful, but settings should support—not replace—your understanding of the underlying work. Automatic saving does not confirm you selected the intended document. An automated backup does not prove a restore will work. A reminder does not establish that a task was completed. Start with a clear manual process and use automation when you can explain how its result will be checked.

### Worked example: a focused 20-minute work segment

A student needs to prepare a one-page public announcement. They read the task, open the approved template, and write “check event time” in a task note. For the next short segment, they draft the announcement with one reference page available. When interrupted, they save and record “verify venue spelling.” On returning, they read the note, inspect the document title, continue, and finally review the text before exporting. The useful habit is not a particular timer length; it is preserving context and avoiding preventable errors.

## 5. How do you use computers comfortably and across devices?

A sustainable habit works for your body, senses, equipment, and situation. [[accessibility|Accessibility]] is not an exception to productivity. It can make tasks possible or less demanding through text enlargement, captions, screen readers, voice input, keyboard navigation, contrast changes, reminders, or other accommodations. A productive workflow should also recognize that some people use a phone as their main computer and others use shared or managed devices.

### Comfort and changing position

Extended computer use can be uncomfortable, especially with poor workstation fit or prolonged static posture. Consider screen visibility, glare, keyboard and pointer position, chair or other support, and whether you can change position periodically. General occupational guidance favors adjustable workstations and appropriate short breaks or task changes rather than maintaining one rigid posture indefinitely. You do not need a perfect desk or a paid accessory to begin: adjust what is safely within your control, use comfortable text size, avoid glare where practical, and take breaks that fit your circumstances.

A [[microbreak|microbreak]] is a brief pause from repetitive or sustained work. Some people find it useful to look away from the screen, stand or stretch gently if appropriate, or switch briefly to a different activity. Exact schedules are not universal medical prescriptions. If you experience persistent pain, vision problems, numbness, or other concerning symptoms, seek appropriate professional advice rather than relying on a computer lesson as a diagnosis. Do not make painful movements to satisfy a classroom exercise.

### Accessible methods are valid methods

You may use a mouse, keyboard, touch, voice input, assistive technology, or a combination. A [[screen-reader|screen reader]] can present interface content audibly or through supported devices. [[magnification|Magnification]] and larger text can improve readability. Captions can support comprehension of spoken media. A reminder may appear visually, audibly, or through another approved means. A student should be evaluated on understanding and reliable execution, **not on using one prescribed input method**.

A keyboard shortcut is helpful only when the student knows what application is active and can confirm the result. If a shortcut is difficult or unavailable, using the visible command through a menu is equally legitimate. A touchscreen learner may use share sheets, file-management menus, and built-in accessibility controls instead of desktop shortcuts. Tasks can be completed on paper when practice on a real device would expose personal information or require unauthorized changes.

### Cross-platform habits and important differences

Core habits transfer across platforms, but command names and storage arrangements vary. Windows File Explorer, macOS Finder, and Chromebook Files all help locate documents. OneDrive, iCloud Drive, and Google Drive can synchronize or provide online access under particular account settings. Phones have app-specific sharing and document controls. On a personal computer, locking the screen may be enough for a short break; on a public device, signing out and clearing a session through the approved process may be necessary.

| Habit | Windows 11 | Mac | Chromebook | Phone or tablet |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open the right file | File Explorer or approved service | Finder or approved service | Files or approved service | Files app or relevant app |
| Save or confirm changes | App Save or status indicator | App Save or status indicator | App or cloud save indicator | App-specific Save or Done |
| Inspect updates | Settings, Windows Update | System Settings, Software Update | Settings, About ChromeOS | System settings update area |
| Lock when away | Windows logo key + L or Lock | Control + Command + Q or Lock | Search/Launcher + L where supported | Lock using device controls |
| Reduce interruptions | Notification and Focus settings | Focus and Notifications | Supported notification settings | Focus/Do Not Disturb or equivalent |
| Locate backups | Authorized Windows/other solution | Time Machine or approved solution | Approved cloud/local backup method | Device/provider backup settings |

This table provides *starting points*, not a guarantee that every version or managed device supports every feature. On a Chromebook, **Search/Launcher + L** is a common supported lock shortcut; on Mac, **Control + Command + Q** generally locks the screen. When a command or label differs, inspect the official system menu rather than guessing. Always confirm that a secure lock actually requires the relevant authentication method to return to private content.

### Shared and managed devices

An [[account|account]] gives access to particular services, files, and settings. A shared public computer may keep different information in the browser, application, download folder, or operating-system session. Do not assume closing a browser tab signs you out of a website. Use the public facility's approved logout instructions. Avoid downloading confidential documents or storing login details on a public computer. On an employer or school device, use approved storage and follow instructions about permissions, backups, updates, and incident reporting.

If a device blocks a setting, that may be intentional [[device-management|device management]], not a malfunction. Do not circumvent the restriction as an exercise. Ask the authorized support team when a needed feature is inaccessible. On a borrowed device, obtain permission before saving files or changing display, accessibility, or notification settings, even when the change seems harmless.

### Limited internet access and offline planning

An [[offline-file|offline file]] is available without an active network connection when the application and device have stored the required content. A file shown in a cloud folder may be only an online placeholder. Before travel or unreliable connectivity, verify the availability of approved materials in advance, subject to permissions and storage limits. If a file is confidential, do not create an unauthorized offline copy for convenience. When reconnecting, compare any changed versions and allow legitimate sync operations to finish before assuming every device is current.

Paper notes can still support the workflow: write a task title, describe the next safe action, and list public references if needed. Do not create an unprotected paper or digital record of passwords or sensitive account details. A good habit is not tied to one brand or one internet connection.

### Worked example: two students, different interfaces

Two learners must prepare the same public event flyer. One uses Windows with a mouse and File Explorer; the other uses an iPad with touch controls and voice dictation. Both find the correct template, make one edit, verify the content, save the file in the approved location, and explain whom it may be shared with. The actions look different on screen, but the quality standard is the same: **correct file, correct content, appropriate access, recoverable work, and a clear next step**.

## 6. How do you maintain habits over time?

A computer routine is useful only if it survives ordinary disruptions. [[maintenance|Maintenance]] means periodically checking the system that supports your work: files still open, accounts still belong to the right person, backups have usable evidence, pending updates are handled through trusted routes, and tasks can be resumed. Maintenance should be brief enough to repeat and tailored to the consequences of an error. It should not become an excuse for constant checking or unnecessary deletion.

### Daily routine: start, save, end

Use a short [[daily-review|daily review]] based on work you actually do. At the start, choose the main task and current file. During work, save meaningful changes and check before sharing. At the end, confirm the result and record a next step. In a personal environment, lock the device when away. In a shared environment, follow the required sign-out procedure. If a task is truly unfinished, leave a clear note rather than disguising an incomplete state as a completed deliverable.

### Weekly routine: check, decide, and prioritize

A [[weekly-review|weekly review]] can fit into a few minutes. Review open tasks, recent downloads, important project folders, and any notifications requiring legitimate updates. Check that essential files are included in the authorized backup process and that you know where to find recovery instructions. Do not delete apparently duplicated records without checking whether they have a retention or version purpose. Do not change sharing permissions on files you do not control.

A sample review sequence is:

1. Read the task note and close out tasks that really are finished.
1. Check the intended folder for the current working file and any deliverables.
1. Review new or downloaded files, placing approved items where they belong.
1. Inspect a legitimate update notification or backup status without forcing unfamiliar changes.
1. Identify one recurring problem and choose one small improvement.
1. Record the next review date or trigger in whatever planner you already use.

### Monthly or occasional routine: resilience, not spring cleaning

Some checks are infrequent but important: review whether your device still receives supported updates, confirm you have a workable recovery method for important files, check sharing access for projects you own, and archive completed work as permitted. Ask whether a lost or stolen device would mean losing irreplaceable information. If the answer is yes, your recovery plan needs attention. A [[restore-test|restore test]]—performed only on authorized data and through an appropriate procedure—can provide stronger evidence than merely seeing “backup completed.” Students do not need to run real restores for this lesson.

The appropriate schedule depends on how often the files change and what is at risk. Someone editing a valuable report daily may need more frequent protection than someone keeping a rarely changed public reading list. A company or institution may have defined retention, recovery, and security procedures. Follow those policies rather than inventing a personal replacement.

### Diagnose before repairing

When something goes wrong, respond with a [[pause-inspect-verify|pause–inspect–verify]] sequence:

1. Stop the action that could make the problem worse, especially repeated deletions or overwrites.
1. Identify the device, application, file, and account involved.
1. Read visible error messages without clicking suspicious offers to “repair” the computer.
1. Check the intended storage location, current file state, and available approved recovery route.
1. Make one reversible, documented change if you are authorized and understand it; otherwise ask for assistance.
1. Verify the result before repeating the action or declaring the issue solved.

For instance, if a document seems missing, first look in the right folder and account; use the application or file manager's search if appropriate. Do not assume the file was deleted. If the problem concerns syncing, avoid rapidly editing multiple copies. If you notice suspicious account activity, use the service's recognized security and reporting paths. The goal is to avoid making a small uncertainty into a larger failure.

### Make a seven-day plan and improve one habit at a time

A [[habit-plan|habit plan]] needs a cue, a specific action, a method of verification, and a realistic review. For the next seven days, choose two or three habits rather than twenty. Example: “When I finish editing a class note, I will check that it is saved in my course folder; before I leave my personal laptop unattended, I will lock it; on Friday, I will review the next actions for unfinished work.” If a step is not practical, reduce its complexity or attach it to another regular action.

Progress is not measured by a perfect streak. Instead, ask: Did this habit prevent a real problem? Can I perform it without relying on an instructor? Do I understand how to adapt it when software changes? Can I tell what I should do when the result is uncertain? A good routine becomes a trusted part of work, not a punishment for mistakes.

### A practical capstone checklist

| Question | What successful performance looks like | If not yet reliable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can I identify today's current file? | I can point to its correct location and title | Create a short project index |
| Can I preserve an edit? | I can save and verify changes | Practice one save-check step |
| Can I find my next task? | A note or task list states an actionable step | Write one next action before leaving |
| Can I share with the right audience? | I check the item, people, and permissions | Use a pre-share checklist |
| Can I protect an unattended session? | I know the approved lock or sign-out method | Learn and practice safely |
| Can I recognize a suspicious request? | I verify through independent known channels | Rehearse a fictional phishing example |
| Can I distinguish sync from backup? | I know which recovery option actually exists | Review the approved backup arrangement |
| Can I use the device comfortably? | I can adjust access needs and take reasonable pauses | Identify one manageable accommodation |
| Can I explain a recovery first step? | I stop, inspect, preserve, and seek help if needed | Practice with fictional error messages |

### Exit question before practice

If you could teach a new computer user only three habits from this lesson, which would you choose and why? A strong answer names actions that prevent recurring problems: finding the right file, saving and verifying, protecting an unattended account, checking before sharing, keeping a recoverable copy, or recording a next step. Your choices may differ depending on whether you are using a home computer, a public library device, a phone, or a managed workplace laptop. Explain the situation, not just the slogan.

## 7. Guided practice: build a dependable routine

The six required activities use fictional situations and can be completed with paper or a basic text editor. Students must not submit screenshots of personal accounts, private messages, real passwords, medical records, financial documents, or employer files. You can describe menu commands instead of activating them. A teacher may offer optional supervised practice on a designated device with the necessary permission. Each activity includes a model response; alternate answers are acceptable when they are safe, specific, and well explained.

### Activity 1: Identify habits, cues, and outcomes

**Goal:** Convert general advice into observable computer actions.

**Fictional scenario:** A learner named Sam completes short class assignments from a shared family laptop. Sam frequently forgets where documents are saved, often leaves browser accounts signed in, and sometimes begins work without knowing which assignment is due. The family allows Sam to use a separate account but does not permit changing general computer settings.

**Steps:**

1. Identify three repeated problems in Sam's workflow.
1. For each problem, write a *cue* that occurs naturally during the session.
1. Write an action that can be completed without administrator access.
1. Explain how Sam would verify that the action worked.
1. Choose which habit should be practiced first and explain why it offers immediate benefit.
1. Explain one action Sam must *not* take without the family owner's permission.

Use this blank structure if helpful: “When I [cue], I will [action]. I will know it worked when [observable result].” Do not include a real sign-in or screenshot in the response.

**Success standard:** Three realistic cue–action–verification statements that address file location, session security, and the next task while respecting device ownership.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model response
1. **File location:** When starting an assignment, Sam opens the approved class folder and checks the file title. Success means the correct assignment is on screen.
2. **Sign-out:** When leaving the shared laptop after work, Sam signs out of the relevant account as required. Success means the next user cannot access Sam's signed-in private session without authentication.
3. **Task clarity:** When beginning a session, Sam reads the assignment list and writes one next action. Success means the note states a specific task rather than “do schoolwork.”

**First habit to practice:** Find the correct project folder before editing, because it immediately reduces confusion and protects the working copy. **Unauthorized action to avoid:** Changing system-wide settings or removing another person's documents without permission. Other reasoned priorities may also be valid.
:::

### Activity 2: Complete a safe start-and-closeout plan

**Goal:** Demonstrate the difference between saving, verifying, and locking or signing out.

**Fictional scenario:** Dana is preparing `Neighborhood-Event-Agenda.docx` on a personal Windows 11 laptop. A separate PDF version will be sent after a committee member approves the content. The folder also contains an old `Neighborhood-Event-Agenda-Draft.docx` and a photograph unrelated to the agenda. Dana will stop work after twenty minutes and resume tomorrow.

**Steps:**

1. Write a four-action opening routine that selects the correct current document and identifies the editing task.
1. Explain how Dana would avoid accidentally editing the old draft.
1. State the difference between saving the `.docx` file and exporting a `.pdf` file.
1. Write a five-action closeout routine and a short “next action” note.
1. Say when Dana should lock the screen and when full sign-out would be more appropriate.
1. Explain why the unapproved PDF should not be distributed as if it were final.

**Optional practice:** On a permitted device, make a harmless text note named `Habits-Practice.txt`, save it, close it, and reopen it. Do not use a genuine organization document or shared drive.

**Success standard:** A procedure that identifies the correct file, preserves editable work, distinguishes the PDF, records tomorrow's action, and respects account security.

:::answer Show Activity 2 model response
**Opening:** Read the task note, open the approved project folder, select the current editable document by confirming its content and location, and choose the agenda paragraph to edit. Do not rely only on the filename's “Draft” label.

**Closeout:** Save the `.docx`; verify that the edited text persists in the correct file; export a PDF only for review if needed; inspect that output; write “Tomorrow: check venue and request approval”; and lock the personal laptop when away. The extra verification may be combined with save or export steps.

**Sharing:** The PDF is a distribution format, not evidence of approval. Dana should share it only with the intended reviewer through authorized permissions, not publish it before approval. Full sign-out is more appropriate on a public or shared service when the local rules require it.
:::

### Activity 3: Decide what to do with suspicious prompts and missing backups

**Goal:** Apply security and recovery habits without performing risky real-world tests.

**Scenario A:** A webpage suddenly displays “Critical system error. Install our free cleaner immediately.” The learner was reading a public article, not checking operating-system updates.

**Scenario B:** An email that appears to be from a coworker requests an attached client list, and the message is marked urgent. The coworker has never requested this list before.

**Scenario C:** A student's folder is synchronized to a cloud account. The student deletes a file locally and then cannot see it on another device. The student assumes the second device provides an automatic backup.

**Scenario D:** A login approval notification appears on a phone when the user has not attempted to sign in.

**Steps:**

1. Name the warning or misunderstanding in each scenario.
1. Identify the first safe action, before any account or file change.
1. For A and B, state how the request would be verified independently.
1. For C, explain the difference between sync, version history, recycle or trash recovery, and a separate backup.
1. For D, explain why “approve to make the notification disappear” is unsafe.
1. Draft one sentence to record the event without exposing private information.

**Success standard:** Four decisions that stop, verify, preserve available evidence, and use official or authorized recovery and reporting routes as appropriate.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model responses
**A:** Treat the advertisement or webpage prompt as untrusted. Do not install its cleaner. Inspect the actual operating system's update or security area through trusted settings if there is a legitimate reason to check.

**B:** Do not transmit the client list. Confirm the request through the coworker's established contact channel and follow confidentiality and workplace approval policies.

**C:** Sync can propagate deletions. Stop creating further conflicting changes; inspect the service's authorized Trash, Recycle Bin, or version-history options where available, and consult a separate backup or administrator as appropriate. A second synced device is not guaranteed to hold an independent recoverable copy.

**D:** Do not approve an unexpected sign-in. Use official account-security procedures and reporting channels. Never share a one-time verification code with a stranger.

**Safe record:** “Observed an unexpected access request; did not approve; checked through an authorized security channel.” No credentials, account number, or actual client record should appear.
:::

### Activity 4: Plan a focused session with accessibility and interruptions

**Goal:** Apply task selection, workstation comfort, and respectful interruption handling.

**Fictional scenario:** Morgan is using a laptop to write a short book-review paragraph. Morgan benefits from larger text and prefers frequent short pauses. A family member may need to interrupt, and an important appointment reminder must remain visible. Morgan's browser currently has seven unrelated tabs open.

**Steps:**

1. Convert “finish the review” into a smaller first action.
1. Describe the minimum windows, reference materials, and task note needed.
1. Choose a suitable readability or input accommodation without prescribing a specific application.
1. Explain how to make reasonable pauses without treating a fixed timer as mandatory.
1. Decide whether turning on Do Not Disturb is appropriate given the important reminder.
1. Write a two-sentence interruption note that allows Morgan to resume work.

**Success standard:** A plan that protects a needed notification, reduces irrelevant decisions, makes the task accessible, and preserves context during an interruption.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model response
**First action:** Open the review document and write one sentence explaining the book's main topic. Keep the approved reference and working document available; close unrelated tabs only after ensuring they contain no unsaved material.

**Accessibility and comfort:** Increase text size through supported controls or use the preferred assistive technology. Choose short pauses or task changes based on comfort and circumstances, not a rigid classroom mandate.

**Notifications:** Keep the appointment reminder accessible; a focus setting is optional and should not suppress important alerts without consideration.

**Interruption note:** “The paragraph's first two sentences are saved in the Book-Review file. Next: add one example from the assigned reading and check the spelling of the title.”
:::

### Activity 5: Create a realistic weekly and monthly maintenance plan

**Goal:** Distinguish session habits, routine checks, and infrequent resilience tasks.

**Fictional scenario:** A small neighborhood study group uses one authorized shared folder for public handouts and a separate restricted area for attendee contact details. The group prepares a new handout each week. One volunteer owns the shared folder; other members have narrower permissions. The group wants a routine but does not want every volunteer changing settings or deleting files.

**Steps:**

1. Propose three habits that the volunteer preparing a handout should perform each session.
1. Propose three weekly checks for the folder owner or designated coordinator.
1. Propose two less frequent checks involving backups, account or update support, or access review.
1. Assign responsibility to an authorized role for each check.
1. Explain what evidence is enough to mark each step complete.
1. Describe two actions that the group should avoid even if they seem efficient.

**Success standard:** A routine with suitable frequency, ownership, verification, and safeguards for restricted information.

:::answer Show Activity 5 model response
**Each session, preparer:** Open the correct current handout; save and verify edits; record what remains and send it for approval using the authorized procedure.

**Each week, coordinator:** Review which handout is approved; verify that public and restricted files are separated; check for missing content and examine the designated backup or recovery status when responsible.

**Periodically, owner or administrator:** Review whether access roles remain appropriate and confirm the approved restore or retention process. Device or software updates should follow the institution's trusted maintenance policy.

**Evidence:** A named current file, approved release indicator, documented task status, or appropriate backup report—not merely a claim that a button was clicked.

**Avoid:** Moving restricted contact information to the public folder and deleting older approved records without checking retention rules. Volunteers should not change ownership or permissions beyond their authority.
:::

### Activity 6: Capstone — design a seven-day personal computer-habits plan

**Goal:** Integrate the complete course into one practical operating system for everyday work.

**Fictional learner profile:** Riley uses a Windows laptop at home and a phone when traveling. Riley has completed the previous lessons and now manages public class notes, a personal to-do list, simple screenshots, cloud documents, and occasional sharing with an instructor. Riley sometimes edits the wrong file, forgets to record the next task, assumes cloud sync is equivalent to backup, and gets distracted by browser tabs. No real account or personal data may be used in the assignment.

**Steps:**

1. Write one paragraph identifying Riley's three highest-impact workflow risks and why they matter.
1. Select **three habits**: at least one about file quality or preservation, one about attention or task continuity, and one about safety or access.
1. For each habit, record the cue, concrete action, verification method, and appropriate frequency.
1. Write a five-step start-of-session procedure and a five-step closeout procedure; reasonable overlap is acceptable.
1. Describe how the plan changes on a phone, shared computer, or offline device.
1. Draft a short response for two disruptions: unexpected account alert and missing synced file.
1. Choose a measure of success for seven days that does not require perfect compliance or private tracking.
1. End with one improvement Riley should consider after the seven-day review.

**Suggested output:** A one-page routine, a small weekly checklist, and a brief reflection. Paper submission is acceptable. Do not require access logs, actual password settings, or personal screenshots.

| Habit | Cue | Action | Verification | Frequency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Working file | Before an edit | Open the approved project location | Confirm title and correct content | Each session |
| Task continuity | Before leaving work | Write one next action | Note is understandable tomorrow | Each session |
| Secure access | When stepping away | Lock or sign out appropriately | Private account is not exposed | Every relevant departure |
| Backup awareness | Planned review | Inspect authorized backup evidence | Necessary files have a recovery route | According to risk and policy |

**Scoring guidance:** Evaluate whether the student can justify each action, select a safe first response, recognize the difference between syncing and backup, adapt to the device, and verify results. The exercise does not require a flawless routine or any particular commercial app.

:::answer Show Activity 6 model plan and rubric
**Top risks:** Editing the wrong copy can create conflicting versions; failing to record the next step wastes time when work resumes; confusing sync with backup can lead to preventable loss.

**Three habits:** (1) At the start of an edit, verify the approved file and location. (2) When ending a work segment, save and write the next action. (3) Before sharing or stepping away, inspect permissions or secure the session, as appropriate.

**Opening:** Identify today's task; locate the project folder; confirm the working file; arrange the minimum necessary windows; start one defined action.

**Closeout:** Save; verify content and location; check pending sync when relevant; record the next action; lock or sign out according to device ownership.

**Device adaptation:** On a phone, use its file and app controls; on a shared computer, avoid saved credentials and sign out; offline, keep authorized local copies and reconcile differences carefully after reconnecting.

**Disruptions:** Deny and independently investigate an unexpected account approval. For a missing synced file, stop conflicting edits and consult the authorized recovery and backup options before replacing or deleting items.

**Seven-day measure:** Riley can identify the current file and next action on at least several workdays and describe one prevented error. The review asks which step was awkward and simplifies that step rather than assigning blame.

**Improvement:** Attach the file-location check to opening the task list, or create a single simple project index, if that addresses the most frequent problem.
:::

### Optional five-minute supervised exercise

If a learner is using a permitted computer, they may make and save a harmless text note called `Practice-Habits-Plan.txt`. Enter three fictional cue–action statements, save the file in a practice folder, close it, and reopen it to check the content. On a personally owned device, the learner may also identify the normal lock control without changing system settings. **No instructor should demand evidence from the student's private account or family computer.** The offline written version provides equivalent evidence of understanding.

### Practice record and evaluation

For each activity, record the **situation**, **decision**, **reason**, and **verification**. A model answer can use different vocabulary if the same safety and workflow principles are present. Strong work recognizes uncertain situations and escalates to an authorized person when permission or capability is missing. A student who identifies a mistake and proposes a safer next step demonstrates learning; the exercise is not a test of perfect computer ownership.

### Exit practice: explain your routine

Without opening the answers above, describe a typical computer session in six sentences. Include the file you would start with, how you choose the next action, when you save and verify, how you handle an unexpected message, what you do before leaving the computer, and how you know important work could be recovered. If one item is uncertain, say what you would check or whom you would ask. That is a valid and responsible response.

## 8. Common misconceptions and troubleshooting

### “A person with many shortcuts automatically has good computer habits.”

Shortcuts improve efficiency for some users, but a workflow still needs correct file selection, saving, verification, and safe access. Someone using menus carefully may be more reliable than someone using many shortcuts without checking results. Skill and habit are related but not interchangeable.

### “AutoSave means I never need to know where the document is.”

Automatic saving depends on the app, account, storage, and configuration. Even if edits are saved, they may be in the wrong account, wrong folder, or wrong document. The durable habit is to verify the current working location and significant changes.

### “A synced folder is always a complete backup.”

Synchronization may spread deletions, overwrites, and errors. Backups or retained recoverable versions have different purposes and limitations. Understand which recovery mechanism actually protects the important files and what restore procedure is authorized.

### “The first update warning I see must be from the operating system.”

Websites and advertisements can imitate security alerts. Use recognized device settings or verified organizational channels rather than an unexpected installer link. Applying an official update and downloading an unknown ‘system cleaner’ are not the same operation.

### “I should change my password whenever I receive an unexpected verification prompt.”

An unexpected sign-in request requires careful security review, not impulsively following the message. Deny or leave unapproved, use the service's official security route, and follow appropriate reporting and account-recovery guidance. A password change may be warranted after verified compromise or official advice, but the prompt itself must not supply your navigation route.

### “A strong password makes multi-factor authentication unnecessary.”

Strong unique passwords are important, but additional authentication can protect accounts against some credential theft. Where supported and appropriate, enable an approved additional factor and protect its recovery options. Not all authentication methods have identical properties.

### “If I close the browser window, I am signed out everywhere.”

A browser may preserve sessions or account state. Closing a tab is not necessarily logging out. On shared or public devices, use the approved sign-out process and avoid saving credentials or confidential files locally.

### “Locking a device and signing out mean the same thing.”

Locking generally preserves the user session behind authentication. Signing out ends the relevant session or account connection, depending on the system. A personal laptop during a short break and a public library computer at the end of a visit may require different actions.

### “More notifications and reminders always improve productivity.”

Reminders can help, but unnecessary alerts may interrupt work. Keep essential communications accessible while reducing avoidable interruptions when appropriate. Never silence required work, safety, caregiver, or emergency notifications without considering the consequences.

### “I need to buy an app before I can become organized.”

A basic project folder, short task note, and safe closeout are often sufficient. New tools may add complexity or create new accounts and data-exposure risks. Begin with what is already approved and available.

### “Deleting everything in Downloads is always good housekeeping.”

Downloads may contain important documents, installers, or items retained for a reason. Review identity, ownership, and retention needs before deleting. Cleanup is a decision process, not automatic disposal.

### “A ‘Private’ filename guarantees privacy.”

A filename does not impose access permissions. Privacy depends on storage location, account rights, sharing settings, device security, and relevant policies. Renaming a file does not revoke a link or conceal an attachment already sent.

### “A screenshot proves that an online statement is accurate.”

A screenshot captures what a display showed at a moment. It may omit context, date, subsequent changes, or the identity of the original source. For important decisions, verify underlying information through the appropriate official or primary source.

### “Good ergonomics means staying in one perfect position all day.”

Comfort and workstation suitability vary. General guidance favors adjustability, appropriate positioning, and opportunities to vary posture or take breaks. If a method is uncomfortable or inaccessible, seek a suitable adjustment and avoid forcing a prescribed pose.

### “I must do every habit every day, even if it is irrelevant.”

Habits should match risk and context. A pre-share permissions check is important when sharing but unnecessary when no file is shared. A backup review is periodic, not necessarily a step after every sentence. Choose specific actions for specific situations.

### “If an error appears, my next step should be a factory reset.”

Resetting or wiping a device is a high-impact operation that may destroy local data or complicate recovery. Begin by pausing, reading the error, checking location and account context, and seeking authorized assistance. Do not perform destructive troubleshooting as a beginner exercise.

### Troubleshooting decision table

| What you notice | Safest first check | Action to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work appears unsaved | Inspect current file, save status, and app prompts | Closing or forcing shutdown before checking |
| File is missing | Verify account, folder, search scope, and authorized recovery areas | Deleting other copies to ‘tidy up’ |
| Cloud status is uncertain | Inspect visible sync status and service guidance | Editing competing copies without a plan |
| Strange password or code request | Check through a known official route | Sending codes to a message sender |
| Repeated distraction | Narrow the active task and record the next action | Disabling required alerts blindly |
| Display is hard to read | Use permitted text-size, contrast, or assistive options | Continuing through pain or inaccessibility |
| Update seems suspicious | Navigate through trusted system settings | Installing an unknown pop-up tool |
| Shared document might be public | Check file, link scope, inherited access, and ownership | Assuming renaming makes it private |
| Backup is unverified | Read approved backup status and restore procedure | Assuming a sync icon proves recovery |
| Public computer is finished | Follow recognized sign-out and cleanup rules | Leaving active private accounts open |

### Five diagnostic questions before taking action

When a problem persists, ask: **(1)** What exactly is not working? **(2)** Where is the information supposed to be stored? **(3)** Which device, application, and account are involved? **(4)** What is the least risky reversible next step? **(5)** How will I know whether the problem is solved? These questions help separate symptoms from causes and prevent unnecessary changes. If the answers are unclear, ask an instructor, administrator, vendor support channel, or other authorized person before proceeding.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The interactive terms used above appear in the MGU lesson dashboard as selectable definitions. Each interactive term used in the body has an entry in the term registry at the end of this file. The following visible glossary covers the same core ideas so that learners can review without relying on interactive controls.

- **Computer habit:** Repeatable action used to make digital work more dependable, secure, accessible, or efficient.
- **Habit:** Behavior that becomes easier to repeat after a familiar cue or situation.
- **Cue:** Event or context that reminds a person to take a particular action.
- **Action:** Specific step taken in response to a cue or task.
- **Outcome:** Observable result of an action.
- **Verification:** Checking that an action produced the intended result.
- **Skill:** Ability to perform a specific operation.
- **Personal workflow:** Sequence of steps used to complete and manage one's tasks.
- **Minimum viable routine:** Smallest practical set of actions that reliably addresses a recurring problem.
- **Session opening:** Deliberate actions taken before beginning a work session.
- **Session closeout:** Save, verify, and next-step actions at the end of work.
- **Working file:** Identified source being edited for a particular task.
- **Save:** Store changes to the intended file or document.
- **Save As:** Create or designate another file name or location through a supported app.
- **Export:** Produce a file in another output format.
- **Upload:** Transfer a file to a receiving service or location.
- **Sync:** Coordinate supported changes across connected devices or locations.
- **Backup:** Protective copy or recoverable history intended to reduce data loss.
- **Restore:** Recover information from a supported previous copy or state.
- **Version history:** Recorded earlier states of a file retained under service rules.
- **Lock screen:** Restricted state requiring permitted authentication to resume a device session.
- **Sign out:** End an account or user session through the appropriate controls.
- **Software update:** Vendor-provided change that may add features, fix issues, or address security risks.
- **Supported software:** Software still receiving applicable maintenance and updates from its provider.
- **Password manager:** Tool for creating or storing credentials under security controls.
- **Unique password:** Password not reused for other unrelated accounts.
- **Passkey:** Supported account authentication mechanism designed to replace or reduce reliance on traditional passwords.
- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA):** Account sign-in process requiring more than one authentication factor.
- **One-time code:** Temporary code used for a particular authentication or confirmation event.
- **Phishing:** Deceptive communication intended to obtain information or persuade unsafe action.
- **Independent verification:** Confirming a claim through a known trusted route separate from a suspicious request.
- **Sharing permission:** Access right controlling how others may use a file or folder.
- **Least privilege:** Providing only the access necessary for a legitimate task.
- **Public link:** Sharing route that may allow a broad audience access under specific service settings.
- **Sensitive information:** Information that requires restricted handling, such as private account details.
- **Digital safety:** Practices that protect devices, accounts, information, and people using them.
- **Digital productivity:** Completing useful tasks with suitable tools, attention, and quality control.
- **Next action:** Specific step that can be taken to advance a task.
- **Interruption:** Event that diverts attention from the current task.
- **One-task focus:** Deliberately working on one primary action before reassessing priorities.
- **Focus mode:** Device or application control intended to reduce selected distractions.
- **Do Not Disturb:** Setting that reduces certain notifications under configurable rules.
- **Task note:** Brief record of unfinished work and the next action.
- **Accessibility:** Design and supports enabling people with varied abilities to use technology.
- **Screen reader:** Assistive software that presents interface content through speech or other supported output.
- **Magnification:** Enlarged view of on-screen information for readability.
- **Microbreak:** Short pause or task change during sustained computer activity.
- **Ergonomics:** Fitting work, tools, and environment to people's capabilities and comfort.
- **Offline file:** Content made available on a device without an active network connection.
- **Device management:** Administrative control over settings, applications, access, and updates.
- **Account:** Identity and permissions used to access a device or online service.
- **Daily review:** Brief check of work goals, saved work, and next actions.
- **Weekly review:** Periodic check of tasks, files, maintenance, and recovery readiness.
- **Maintenance:** Ongoing checks and care that keep a workflow dependable.
- **Restore test:** Authorized check that a recoverable copy can actually be retrieved.
- **Habit plan:** Defined routine with cues, actions, verification, and review frequency.
- **Pause–inspect–verify:** Safeguard sequence used before making changes during an uncertain problem.
- **Archive:** Deliberately retained material that is no longer active work.
- **Retention policy:** Rule determining which records must be retained and for how long.
- **Authoritative source:** Organization or original record responsible for information being relied on.
- **Project folder:** Approved location where files for one project are organized.
- **Current version:** File state identified as the intended one for ongoing work or use.
- **Cloud storage:** Provider-hosted service for storing and accessing digital files.
- **Local file:** Item stored on a directly accessible device or local storage area.
- **Authentication:** Process used to verify access to an account or device.
- **Security key:** Physical device or mechanism used as an authentication factor where supported.
- **Privacy:** Appropriate control and protection of information about people.
- **Verification checklist:** Short set of checks confirming the intended result and access conditions.
- **Notification:** Alert provided by an operating system or application.
- **Recovery plan:** Documented arrangement for restoring essential information after loss or error.
- **Sync conflict:** Situation in which a system cannot safely combine competing file changes automatically.
- **Independent backup:** Recovery copy or retained history not simply another view of the same live synced state.
- **Progress review:** Periodic evaluation of whether a habit is useful and practical.
- **Managed device:** Device whose configuration and permissions are controlled by an organization.
- **Shared device:** Computer or other device used by more than one person.
- **Screen sharing:** Displaying a device screen or selected window to another person through a service.
- **Risk-based frequency:** Choosing how often to perform a check based on consequences and change rate.
