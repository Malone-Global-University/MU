## Lesson 7: Sharing Files Safely

You finish a short document and need to send it to a classmate. The document is ready, but the next decision matters: should you attach a separate copy to a message, invite the classmate to a live document, or send a link? Should the recipient be allowed to read, comment, or edit? What if you choose the wrong person, share an entire folder by mistake, or later need to withdraw access? These questions arise whenever people share classwork, household records, job documents, photographs, or approved workplace materials.

[[file-sharing|File sharing]] means making a file or its contents available to another person through a chosen method. A file can be shared as an [[attachment|attachment]], a separately transferred copy, or a link granting access to a file held in a cloud service. These methods can look similar to the recipient, but they have important differences. **A separately delivered copy may remain with its recipient, while access to a live shared file can often be adjusted later.** Changing a link or permission is not a way to erase copies that somebody has already downloaded, printed, photographed, or otherwise retained.

This is Lesson 7 in MGU's Everyday Productivity sequence. It builds directly on Lesson 6, *Cloud Storage Basics*, and uses Windows 11 with Microsoft OneDrive as one practical example, while teaching parallel skills for Google Drive, iCloud Drive, macOS, Chromebooks, iPhone, iPad, and Android. You will learn the decision process before the buttons: identify the right document, confirm the authorized recipient, choose the narrowest useful permission, review exactly what will be shared, send through a trusted channel, and periodically review access. The examples are fictional and do not require students to send anything to another person or create an online account.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 90–120 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Everyday Productivity Lessons 1–6 recommended · **Equipment:** A computer or phone and optional access to a file manager or approved cloud account · **Practice condition:** All required activities can be completed offline with fictional files and recipients · **Safety:** Never share authentic personal records, account credentials, student files, workplace confidential material, or family photographs merely to complete practice.

> **Essential idea:** **Check the file → check the recipient → choose the method → select the least access needed → inspect the final settings → share deliberately → verify and review access.** A familiar sharing button is not proof that the recipient is correct, the permissions are private, or the information can later be recalled.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish a separate file copy, an email attachment, a cloud-sharing invitation, and a link to a live file.
1. Explain [[permission|permissions]] such as view, comment, edit, owner, and manager in plain language.
1. Compare private or restricted sharing, organization-wide access, and “anyone with the link” access.
1. Select a file, check the intended recipient, and choose a sharing method that fits the task.
1. Describe safe beginner sharing workflows for Microsoft OneDrive, Google Drive, and Apple iCloud Drive without assuming identical controls.
1. Explain how a shared folder and inherited permissions may expose more than a single intended document.
1. Review, change, or remove sharing access when authorized, and recognize the limits of revocation.
1. Respond to wrong-recipient errors, access requests, unexpected invitations, suspicious links, and other common problems.
1. Apply an accessible, repeatable checklist to fictional school, family, and workplace scenarios.

## 1. What does sharing a file actually mean?

[[file-sharing|Sharing a file]] is an intentional decision to let another person receive, see, or work with information. You may share a document directly from a file manager, attach it to a message, place it in an approved shared location, or use the Share control inside a document or cloud-storage service. Each approach creates a different relationship between the source file and what the other person receives. The first skill is to recognize **what will be transmitted or made accessible**, not merely how to select a Share icon.

### The four most common approaches

A [[sent-copy|sent copy]] is a separate version delivered to a recipient. For example, you might attach a text document to an email or export a PDF and send it through an approved messaging service. After successful delivery, the copy normally exists apart from the original; editing your own original does not necessarily update that delivered copy. Deleting the outgoing message also does not reliably remove the recipient's downloaded or saved copy. An [[email-attachment|email attachment]] is one common way to send a copy, although some email services automatically substitute a cloud link for large files or provide a choice.

A [[shared-link|sharing link]] is a web address or service-generated reference used to reach a file. The link may be [[restricted-link|restricted]] to invited people, usable only inside a particular organization, or usable by anyone who obtains it. The text of the link is not itself an adequate description of its security. The owner must inspect the service's sharing settings and consider other access routes such as folder permissions.

A [[direct-invitation|direct invitation]] grants a named person or account access to a file held in a service. Depending on the product, the recipient may need to sign in or complete identity verification. The invitation can allow viewing, commenting, or editing. A [[collaborative-file|collaborative file]] remains available for the authorized participants to work with, and changes may be reflected in the shared original. Access changes may take effect without sending a new copy, but the service's actual behavior depends on its permissions and organizational policies.

A [[shared-folder|shared folder]] exposes a collection of files rather than just one. In many services, the folder's access settings apply to items inside it. This is convenient for teams but is a much larger decision than sharing a single page. A learner should avoid using a shared folder as a quick way to deliver one harmless document when the folder also contains records not meant for the recipient.

| Sharing method | What the recipient normally receives | Can later edits appear automatically? | Can access usually be withdrawn? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Email attachment or exported copy | Separate delivered file | No; a new copy generally must be sent | Not from a copy already received |
| Device-to-device transfer of a copy | Separate delivered file | No; each copy is independent | Not from the transferred copy |
| Restricted cloud invitation | Access to an item in a service | Often, when the item supports live collaboration | Often, by changing service permissions; copies may remain |
| Anyone-with-link cloud share | Access through a link that may be forwarded | Often, when it points to the same live item | Often by changing link settings; copies may remain |
| Shared folder invitation | Access to a folder and covered contents | Usually, within the shared workspace | Often; examine inherited and alternative permissions |

### A file and a link are not the same object

Imagine a fictional class document called `Garden-Meeting-Notes.txt`. If you attach it to an email, the recipient may receive a file they can save separately. If you copy a cloud link to that same filename, the recipient may instead open an online representation of the document. If you later correct a date in the online original, someone using the link might see the corrected version; the earlier emailed attachment remains the earlier copy. A link can also fail because the viewer lacks permission, uses the wrong account, or is offline.

This distinction is essential when distributing finished work. Someone asked to **review** a draft may need commenting access to a collaborative document. Someone asked to **keep a permanent record** may prefer an authorized, clearly labeled copy or a controlled records system. Someone asked only to **read** a report generally does not need the ability to edit or delete its original. Match the method to the intended outcome.

### Sending a copy versus collaborating

The word *share* appears in many applications, but a Share button may open a system [[share-sheet|share sheet]] with several destinations. In one application, Mail may send a copy, while Collaborate may invite others to the original. In another, copying a link might generate or reuse a cloud-sharing permission. An operating-system menu may also offer Bluetooth, nearby sharing, AirDrop, or a messaging app. **Read the action's final description before confirming.** If the interface offers “Send a Copy” and “Collaborate,” do not assume they are synonyms.

Many file types can be viewed as copies, including `.txt`, `.pdf`, `.jpg`, and `.png` documents. Live collaboration, comments, and editing may require compatible applications or supported document formats. A person who can view a file on a computer may not have the program needed to edit it. Some formats can also preserve tracked changes, hidden sheets, comments, or metadata. Review what you intend to distribute rather than sharing a document merely because its name looks correct.

### What sharing does not guarantee

Sharing does not automatically establish that the file was delivered, the invitation was accepted, the intended person read the material, or the recipient is permitted to reuse it. Sending a link by itself does not make a file private. Setting view-only access generally restricts editing the shared original, not every possible way to capture the content. Placing information in a cloud service does not mean it is appropriate for public distribution. Strong sharing habits therefore include both **technical controls** and **careful decisions about content and recipients**.

### Worked example: choose between three ways to send a class outline

An instructor asks fictional student Maya to send her class partner a two-page outline for comments. Maya can (1) attach a PDF that her partner can read and reply to separately, (2) invite the partner to comment on an approved shared document, or (3) share an editable folder with every assignment from the term. The second method best fits the narrow task **if** collaborative commenting is supported and approved. The first is a workable alternative when collaboration is unavailable. The third exposes unnecessary material and gives broader scope than the assignment requires.

A beginner who can explain **why** one method meets the task with less exposure has learned more than a beginner who only memorizes the position of a Share button.

## 2. How do you choose recipients and permissions?

A safe sharing decision specifies three things: **which item**, **who may access it**, and **what each person may do**. The file's owner or an authorized collaborator usually controls some of these choices. On a school or workplace account, a [[system-administrator|system administrator]] may limit which choices are available. Controls and labels differ between services and account types; if a setting is not available, do not try to bypass the institution's policy with a personal account.

### The meaning of the common roles

[[view-only|View-only]] access usually permits opening and reading a file without altering the shared original. It is often called Viewer, Can view, or Read. [[commenter|Commenter]] access, where supported, permits feedback without ordinary content editing. [[editor|Editor]] or Can edit access permits changes to the shared original and may also permit moving, renaming, copying, deleting, or further sharing, depending on the service and item type. An [[owner|Owner]] generally has broader control over the item, though the exact ownership, transfer, and recovery rules are product-specific.

For collaborative files, granting edit access can lead to accidental changes just as easily as useful teamwork. A recipient who only needs to approve a draft does not necessarily need to modify its contents. In a shared folder, edit access can be particularly broad: it may allow a participant to affect many files. When appropriate, provide a carefully selected file with only the minimum permission necessary.

| Need | Usual permission to consider | Reason | Question before sharing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Read the final instructions | View | No content changes needed | Is the correct finished version selected? |
| Give feedback on a draft | Comment, if supported | Keeps feedback separate from normal editing | Does the file type support comments? |
| Work jointly on a draft | Edit | Allows authorized changes to the original | Are collaborators trusted and is a recovery copy available? |
| Share many approved team resources | Folder view or carefully controlled edit | Keeps related material together | Are all current and future folder contents appropriate to expose? |
| Deliver a fixed snapshot | Send an approved copy | Recipient gets a distinct record | Does the copy reveal comments, metadata, or confidential data? |

The goal is [[least-privilege|least privilege]]: grant only the access needed for the legitimate task and only for as long as needed. Least privilege does not mean refusing every request. It means giving a reader reading access, a reviewer the appropriate feedback function, and a genuine coauthor editing ability when necessary.

### Select the intended audience, not merely the easiest link

A [[specific-people|specific-people]] or invited-people option limits access to the accounts or identities you select, subject to the service's access controls and other preexisting permissions. A [[organization-link|organization-wide link]] can be usable by many people within a school or employer when they obtain the link; it is not the same as a single named colleague. An [[anyone-link|anyone-with-the-link]] setting can make a file available to every person who receives the link, including somebody outside the original conversation. The link need not appear in search results to be broadly usable when forwarded.

Google Drive commonly uses the labels **Restricted** and **Anyone with the link**, along with Viewer, Commenter, and Editor. OneDrive may present people-specific, organization, existing-access, or anyone options depending on account and settings. iCloud Drive uses options such as people you choose or anyone with the link and can view or can edit. **Do not assume the default audience is private, or that an option present in one account exists in every account.**

### Verify recipient identity and context

An [[email-address|email address]] looks precise, but it can still be the wrong address. Autocomplete may suggest someone with a similar name; a contact may have an old mailbox; and a copied recipient list can include a person not involved in the project. Before sending, compare the complete recipient address with a trusted source. If a document is sensitive or business-related, follow your organization's approval process and independently confirm unusual requests through a known channel rather than relying on the requester's message alone.

A [[recipient-verification|recipient verification]] check answers: Did I select the correct full address? Is this the appropriate personal, school, or work account? Does the recipient need this specific file? Would they recognize the invitation and why they are receiving it? Is an external recipient permitted under the organization's rules? Make these checks before clicking Send, not after an access problem appears.

### Understand visible access and hidden access routes

A person may have access through more than one route: a direct invitation, group membership, a broadly usable link, a parent folder, or an organizational library. Some systems grant the higher effective permission when multiple grants apply. **Changing one route may leave another in force.** A view-only direct invitation does not necessarily override an existing edit-capable link or shared-folder permission. For sensitive content, examine the service's complete Manage Access or Share dialog, and ask an authorized administrator when permissions are unclear.

| Audience label or route | Typical scope | Why to be cautious |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Named invitation | Selected accounts or identities | Wrong address, incorrect account, or other existing grants |
| Restricted link | People already granted access | It may not create access for a new recipient; inherited grants still matter |
| Organization link | People in the organization who possess the link | Can reach more employees or students than the intended group |
| Anyone with link | Anyone who obtains the link | May be forwarded or published without your awareness |
| Shared parent folder | People and groups with folder-level rights | Can expose other files and potentially later additions |
| Sent attachment | Copies delivered to message recipients | Once delivered, source permissions cannot recall copies |

### Decide before clicking Share: a brief procedure

1. **Identify the purpose.** Is the recipient supposed to read, comment, edit, or retain a separate copy?
1. **Identify the exact item.** Open the selected document or preview its non-sensitive contents; check its title, folder, and version.
1. **Identify the recipient.** Confirm the full address or approved group through a trusted record.
1. **Choose a method.** Consider an approved attachment for a fixed copy or an invitation for live collaboration.
1. **Choose the audience and role.** Prefer only the people needed, then the least access necessary.
1. **Inspect the scope.** Confirm you are sharing one file rather than a folder, library, or collection.
1. **Review the final confirmation.** Check the item name, audience, permission, and message context before sending.
1. **Plan later review.** Know how to inspect or revoke access if the task ends or a mistake occurs.

### Worked example: an incorrect address and an unnecessary editor role

Imagine a worker preparing `Team-Room-Booking.txt` for a teammate named Avery. The sharing dialog suggests two contacts named Avery, and the proposed role is Editor. The task only asks the teammate to confirm the room number, so **Viewer** is sufficient. The worker checks Avery's approved work address, selects that exact identity, and confirms the file rather than its containing folder. If the address cannot be verified, the appropriate action is **not to share yet**. No urgent deadline makes an unverified external recipient safer.

## 3. How do you share safely with OneDrive on Windows 11?

[[onedrive|Microsoft OneDrive]] supports sharing through its website and, when configured, through Windows 11 File Explorer. The exact interface depends on whether you use a personal Microsoft account or a workplace or school account; settings may also be controlled by an organization. The following procedures describe the common decision points rather than requiring a particular button arrangement. You may complete this section by studying the fictional examples; do not create an account or send a real invitation for this lesson.

### Step 1: confirm the file and account

If you have an approved OneDrive account, use the authorized website or open File Explorer and navigate to the OneDrive location associated with that account. Confirm you are not confusing a local Downloads folder with OneDrive. Find the **single harmless practice file** you would share. Before opening Share, note its filename and location. A folder containing other documents is not a substitute for selecting one file.

In Windows 11 File Explorer, the context menu may contain **Share**, **Copy OneDrive link**, or a provider-specific sharing option. Different commands can open different dialogs or place a link directly on the clipboard. Be especially careful with commands that immediately copy a link: **copying a link does not mean you have inspected its recipients or permission level**. When learning, use the service's sharing dialog or web interface so you can examine the access controls before distributing any link.

### Step 2: inspect the OneDrive sharing dialog

1. Select the intended file, not its parent folder.
1. Open the OneDrive **Share** dialog through an authorized interface.
1. Look for a recipient field, a link or audience setting, and a permission setting such as **Can view** or **Can edit**.
1. Read the active audience, which might be limited to people already granted access, selected people, an organization, or anyone with the link. Options differ by account.
1. For a simple read-only exercise, choose a private audience or named recipient when supported, and choose **Can view** rather than an editing option.
1. Review any other settings, including download restrictions or link expiration if the service and account support them. Do not assume these are available to all users.
1. **Stop before Send or Copy link** if the correct audience or permission cannot be confirmed.

The key lesson is understanding the dialog. Selecting an address without inspecting the current permissions can produce a wider invitation than intended. Some OneDrive interfaces have multiple sharing links with different settings; a link that already exists may also remain usable until its access grant is changed or removed.

### Step 3: choose an invitation or an appropriate link

If you select a named person, carefully enter the complete approved address, choose the intended role, and check the screen's final description. If you choose **Copy link**, determine who can use that link first. A link that works for anyone is not private merely because you paste it into a private chat. Someone who receives it might forward it, and recipients may still make their own copies of accessible content.

For classroom practice, use the following *fictional* review statement rather than actually sending an invitation:

> Item: `MGU-Meeting-Agenda.txt`. Recipient: approved class partner. Audience: selected person only. Role: view. Reason: read the agenda before the meeting. Follow-up: review access after the meeting.

No invented email address is needed for this exercise. On a live institutional system, follow the applicable rules for contact verification and avoid making test invitations to real people.

### Step 4: distinguish edit permissions from file ownership

An editor may be able to revise the file. For shared folders, the ability to edit can include moving, renaming, copying, or deleting covered items. This can be appropriate for trusted collaboration but is too broad for a person who only needs to read a final report. Consider creating a separate, limited-scope file or using a review-only capability when supported. **A person need not become an owner or an editor just to see a document.**

Some OneDrive features offer options to prevent downloading for supported accounts or file types. A control limiting downloads is not a universal promise that visible content cannot be copied or recorded by other means. Apply ordinary confidentiality judgments even when the interface shows more restrictive controls.

### Step 5: review and later remove access

When authorized, locate the file, select Share, and open the service's access-management view. Review direct recipients, group access, folder access, and available sharing links. If a person no longer needs access, use the appropriate Remove access or permission-change option. If the file also has an “anyone” or organization link, adjust or remove that grant as well; otherwise it may still work. The service can also have special administrative or inherited rules. **Confirm the resulting access rather than assuming the most recent click made the file private.**

If an access option appears different from this lesson, consult the service's official documentation linked in Section 11. The current OneDrive help emphasizes that permissions can come from more than one source and must be reviewed together. Do not attempt to erase shared files or change another person's folder access merely to practice.

### Practical Windows 11 example: send a harmless report for reading

Suppose `Public-Event-Outline.txt` contains fictional details about a community open house. A colleague needs to read it before a planning discussion. In an approved OneDrive account, select the **file**, confirm the correct colleague's work account, use a person-specific view permission if available, check whether parent-folder or other links already grant access, and then send through an approved channel. Later, review whether the colleague still needs access. If OneDrive is not available, describe the same decision process on paper or use a fictional screen supplied by an instructor.

### Windows access and accessibility tips

You can navigate a file manager with mouse, touch, or keyboard, depending on your needs. Screen-reader labels and command locations depend on the version of Windows, OneDrive, and browser. Where possible, navigate to the file name first, open the context or Share command, and listen for the full role and recipient description. If the interface is unclear or places critical permissions in unlabeled controls, ask for an accessible route or assisted confirmation before sharing. **Accessibility is not a reason to skip permission review.**

## 4. How do you share through Google Drive and iCloud Drive?

The basic principles are consistent across platforms, but the menus and capabilities are not identical. Many learners encounter [[google-drive|Google Drive]] through a web browser or Chromebook, and [[icloud-drive|Apple iCloud Drive]] through Finder, the Files app, or iCloud.com. The names of controls can change after software updates. Use the current screen and authorized account rather than guessing that a button in one application behaves exactly like a similarly named button in another.

### Google Drive: a beginner's approach

In Google Drive, a file may have a named list of people with access and a separate **General access** setting. The common roles are **Viewer**, **Commenter**, and **Editor**. For typical individual files, **Restricted** means only accounts explicitly granted access can open the item, subject to any other applicable grants such as shared folders. **Anyone with the link** allows link holders to access according to the role selected, which can be much broader than inviting one recipient. School or business administrators may prevent outside sharing or restrict some settings.

A cautious Google Drive workflow is:

1. Go to Google Drive or the approved Google Docs, Sheets, or Slides interface while signed into the correct account.
1. Select the **one** practice file you intend to share; confirm its filename, contents, and parent folder.
1. Open **Share** and inspect the list of existing people or groups with access.
1. Under the appropriate settings, select the named recipient's approved address and a role, or inspect General access if you are using a link.
1. For a routine private reading task, prefer Restricted and Viewer. For feedback, choose Commenter if available and appropriate.
1. Confirm that neither the containing folder nor another grant already supplies broader access. If that cannot be determined, stop and consult the owner or administrator.
1. Review the recipient and role on the final screen before clicking Send or copying and distributing the link.
1. Later, use Share or Manage access to inspect and, when authorized, change or remove the grant.

The exact workflow for sharing a Google document can differ slightly from sharing an ordinary uploaded file. A Google document may have in-app comments and real-time collaboration, whereas an image or PDF may not offer identical editing or commenting features. Do not confuse *having access to a file* with *having compatible software to edit it*. If the user only needs to read a finished document, a viewing role is usually enough.

### Google Drive: understand restricted links and access requests

A [[restricted-access|restricted access]] setting may cause an uninvited recipient to see a request-access page. That is not always an error; it can be evidence that access is appropriately limited. The owner should verify the person's identity and legitimate need before approving a request. Do not change the file to Anyone with the link solely because somebody reports “Access denied.” The safer solution may be a correct named invitation, use of the intended account, or confirmation that the recipient is eligible under organizational policy.

Some files inherit access through the folder containing them. For example, giving Viewer permission to an individual file may not reduce a person's existing Editor permission through its parent folder. When a document is stored in a broadly shared project folder, consider moving **an authorized copy** into an appropriately restricted destination if the institution allows it. Do not move files or alter inherited permissions in a managed workspace without knowing the effect on colleagues.

### Google Drive: view, comment, and edit are different purposes

Consider the fictional document `Draft-Museum-Visit.docx` stored in an approved cloud folder. Student Nia wants a class partner to identify typos, not rewrite the itinerary. If supported for that file type, Commenter is appropriate; otherwise Nia could share a read-only copy and receive feedback separately. Assigning Editor merely because it is the first visible option needlessly exposes the original to changes. A teacher may require a different submission method, so learners should follow their course's actual instructions.

Google Drive also offers download, copy, print, and further-sharing restrictions in some circumstances. **These do not fully prevent another authorized viewer from retaining or communicating the information.** Sharing confidential information still requires policy authorization and a trusted recipient. The account itself might also be subject to school or workplace retention and monitoring rules.

### iCloud Drive: sharing a link versus sending a copy

In Apple's ecosystem, a Share menu may provide **Collaborate** or **Send a Copy**, depending on the application and file. With iCloud Drive collaboration, a file or folder remains available in iCloud while authorized participants view or change it. When you send a separate copy, the recipient receives a distinct item that cannot generally be recalled by changing iCloud permissions. Make the choice based on whether live work or a fixed delivered copy is needed.

On a Mac with an approved iCloud account:

1. Open Finder and locate the correct file inside iCloud Drive; confirm that it is not a different local version.
1. Select the item, open Share, and choose the collaboration or link-sharing workflow if you intend to grant ongoing access.
1. Inspect **Who can access** or its equivalent. Prefer selected or invited people for private collaboration rather than anyone with the link.
1. Inspect the role, commonly **Can view** or **Can edit**, and choose the lowest level sufficient for the task.
1. Confirm the file or folder name and any listed participants before sending the invitation or copying a link.
1. Use the item's sharing-management controls later to change permissions, remove a participant, or stop sharing when authorized.

Apple may present these actions under different phrases as macOS and iCloud interfaces change. If you do not see a management option, verify that you are signed into the correct Apple Account and that you own or are authorized to manage the item. A student should not attempt to alter the permissions of a document owned by someone else.

### iPhone and iPad: Files app and app-specific choices

On iPhone and iPad, the [[files-app|Files app]] lets you browse supported local and cloud locations, including iCloud Drive when it is configured. A typical approach is to locate the file, use its sharing control, decide whether to collaborate or send a copy, inspect the audience and role, and review the destination. Touch-and-hold gestures or button locations vary by device and version. An app may show a system share sheet listing Mail, Messages, AirDrop, or other available destinations; these can produce different kinds of delivery.

When sharing a photograph or document from a phone, inspect the selected item count carefully. Mobile interfaces may select multiple photos or files at once, and location information may be embedded in certain photos. The presence and operation of metadata-removal controls vary by app. For a class exercise, use a fictional note or blank practice document instead of an actual photo containing personal details.

### Android: share from Files, Drive, or another approved app

Android devices may have a Files app or another file manager with a **Share** action, and Google Drive may have its own specific sharing menu. A system share sheet might send a file as an attachment to a messaging app, whereas the Share command inside Drive can grant access to a cloud item. Choose carefully. Verify the receiving app, account, recipient, and whether the action is sending a **copy** or granting access to an **online file**.

A person using a Chromebook can follow the Google Drive web workflow, use the Chromebook Files app to inspect local versus Drive locations, and use a supported browser sharing control where appropriate. Avoid assuming a file in Downloads is already in Google Drive. Similarly, a file in the Android Downloads folder does not automatically become available through a Drive link.

### Cross-device comparison: what to inspect before confirming

| Device or service | Common sharing entry point | Common audience or role choices | Final safety check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Windows 11 with OneDrive | OneDrive web Share or integrated File Explorer action | Named people, existing-access or other link settings; view/edit | Confirm the active account, selected file, and link permissions |
| Google Drive in browser | File Share dialog | Restricted or link access; Viewer, Commenter, Editor | Inspect General access and existing people or groups |
| Chromebook with Google Drive | Drive Share dialog in browser | Similar Drive account options | Distinguish Drive files from local Downloads |
| Mac with iCloud Drive | Finder Share or Manage Shared File | Invited people or link; view/edit | Confirm collaboration versus sending a copy |
| iPhone and iPad | Files app or app-specific Share menu | App-dependent collaboration or copy choices | Check item count and receiving app |
| Android | Files/file-manager Share or Drive Share | Send copy or Drive access options | Check whether the recipient receives a file or a link |

### Worked example: two people, one lesson handout

A student writes `Library-Workshop-Handout.txt` and wants two classmates to read it. The student uses an approved Drive account, selects the file, checks each classmate's identity, chooses Viewer, and keeps General access Restricted. If one classmate cannot open it, the student verifies which account that classmate is using and whether the invitation was sent to the correct address. The student does **not** switch to public link access just to make the warning disappear. That is a platform-specific example of a platform-independent habit.

## 5. How do you review, change, and stop sharing?

Good sharing includes an [[access-review|access review]] after the initial send. The owner should know who can still access the item, why the access exists, whether the permission is still appropriate, and how the service represents that access. A document shared for a one-day review may not need to remain available indefinitely. A team folder may need continuing access but periodic review of membership and contents. **Granting access and maintaining access are separate responsibilities.**

### Find where permissions are actually controlled

In OneDrive, use Share or an access-management view associated with the file. In Google Drive, open Share or Manage access. In iCloud Drive, use Manage Shared File or Manage Shared Folder when available. Interfaces differ, but the questions are the same: Who is listed? Which groups or links apply? Does access come from the parent folder? Which permission is effective? Can someone outside the intended audience still open the file?

If the owner cannot confidently answer these questions, changing one small setting may not solve the problem. Review the full access picture or seek authorized support. For complicated business document libraries, administrators may manage access through groups, shared drives, or project sites that have their own rules.

### Understand direct and inherited permissions

An [[inherited-permission|inherited permission]] is access that arises from a larger folder, group, site, or other container rather than from a grant made directly to the individual file. For example, three people may already be editors of an authorized project folder. If you place a new file into that folder, those people may gain the relevant folder-derived access even though you never typed their names into the file's Share dialog. A file can also have separate link-based grants, so looking only at its direct recipient list may miss part of the effective audience.

**Question:** You change one person's direct invitation from Editor to Viewer, but that person remains an editor because of their project group. Did the change fail? Not necessarily. You changed one grant, while another grant still provides editing. The actual goal—reducing access—requires reviewing the group or shared-folder source as well. On a managed system, the correct response may be to ask the file owner or administrator to restructure access rather than experimenting with group settings.

### Revoke a link without assuming content disappears

[[revocation|Revocation]] is an action that removes or limits an access grant. Depending on the service, an owner may remove a named recipient, change an “anyone” link to a restricted audience, remove an extra link, stop sharing a file, or change the parent folder's membership. The effect can be immediate or depend on service state and caching; verify through the intended service. If the file was sent as an attachment, cloud access revocation cannot retract that delivered copy.

Even for a cloud link, a person might have saved a local copy, made a screenshot, printed the document, or copied text. A link can be disabled while information already received remains outside the owner's control. Therefore, **do not distribute information that would become unsafe merely because someone retains it**, unless the disclosure is required and handled through an approved protected workflow.

### Limits of links with passwords and expiration dates

Some services or plans offer [[link-expiration|expiration dates]] or [[link-password|password-protected links]]. These controls can reduce access after a time or require an additional secret to open a link. They are useful when available, but not universal, and their exact scope varies. A link expiration does not erase copies that were downloaded before expiration. A password sent in the same message as the protected link may provide little protection against that message being forwarded. More importantly, **an expiration setting does not replace choosing an appropriate audience and checking other permissions**.

If the task is important and the platform lacks a suitable control, use an approved alternative rather than improvising around organizational policy. For example, an organization may require a managed file-sharing service for client records instead of a personal cloud account with an expiring link.

### An access-review checklist

| Question | What to look for | Appropriate response if uncertain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which item is shared? | Exact filename and folder location | Preview the correct, nonsensitive item |
| Who has access? | Named recipients, groups, link audience | Review all grants or ask the owner |
| What can they do? | Effective view/comment/edit permission | Prefer minimum authorized access |
| Is the parent folder shared? | Folder membership and inherited access | Inspect folder scope before changing anything |
| Is an old link still active? | Other links, audience settings, link history | Remove or restrict the relevant grant if authorized |
| Is there an independent copy? | Attachments or downloaded copies already delivered | Recognize that revoking the cloud link cannot erase those copies |
| Does the task still require access? | Current project purpose and retention rules | Remove unnecessary grants through approved process |

### Wrong-recipient scenario: first contain, then assess

Suppose a fictional staff member mistakenly invites an external address to a nonpublic draft. The correct response depends on the type and sensitivity of the information, the service's controls, and the organization's rules. For a school or workplace incident, follow the prescribed reporting procedure promptly rather than making silent changes and assuming the problem has vanished.

A general safe response is to stop additional sharing, confirm exactly what file or folder was exposed, remove or restrict the mistaken grant **if authorized**, check other links and inherited permissions, and notify the responsible owner or security contact according to policy. Do not contact an unknown recipient with further private material or send another copy while trying to explain the mistake. Record the necessary non-sensitive facts for an authorized review. **Removing access is a containment measure, not proof the information was unseen.**

### A shared-folder scenario: scope matters more than convenience

A nonprofit creates a folder called `Community-Program`, with subfolders for public flyers, volunteer planning, and private participant records. A worker wants to let a graphic designer read one public flyer. Sharing the entire top-level folder could expose materials the designer does not need. Instead, select the individual approved flyer or an appropriately restricted public-facing subfolder. If the institution manages access centrally, work with the folder owner to create a designated distribution location. The general principle is **share the smallest appropriate unit**, not whichever folder is fastest to click.

### A simple access register for small projects

A class or small team can maintain an ordinary note listing the documents it shares, the purpose, the intended recipients or roles, and the planned review date. Avoid storing confidential link tokens or passwords in that note. Such a record is not a replacement for the actual service's access list. It is a reminder to **check and reconcile the live settings**, especially after a project ends, a member leaves, or a document becomes obsolete.

## 6. How do you avoid common sharing risks?

Most sharing mistakes begin as ordinary productivity actions: choosing a suggested contact too quickly, assuming a link is private, posting a file in the wrong team channel, granting Edit when View is enough, or forgetting that a folder includes more than the item that is visible. Good habits prevent routine errors without requiring advanced technical expertise. This section also explains what to do when another person sends a suspicious invitation.

### Check the content before sharing

A document may contain details beyond the visible paragraph you intended to send. For example, it could include comments, revision history, hidden spreadsheet columns, document properties, or old pages. A screenshot may show open tabs, names, notifications, an address, or account information in the background. A photograph can contain location-related metadata in some formats. The exact possibilities depend on the file type and application. Review the content and relevant properties, and use an authorized **clean export or redacted version** when needed.

[[redaction|Redaction]] means deliberately removing sensitive content from the information you release. Merely drawing an opaque shape over text in an editable document may not permanently remove the underlying text; some PDF redaction tools require an explicit apply step. For beginners, avoid using authentic confidential records in class practice. When a real document needs sanitization, follow an approved redaction procedure and verify the released version before sending it.

### Treat unexpected file-sharing invitations cautiously

An unsolicited email that claims “A document has been shared with you” could be legitimate, but attackers can imitate ordinary collaboration notices. [[phishing|Phishing]] attempts often lead people to pages designed to steal credentials or download harmful software. Do not trust a message just because it displays a familiar service's logo, a plausible sender name, or an urgent request. If an invitation is unexpected, open the service through its known legitimate website or app, check the account's sharing or notification area, and verify with the sender through a contact method you already trust.

Avoid entering passwords on an unfamiliar page reached through an unsolicited link. Do not install a browser extension, remote-support tool, or unknown program simply because a supposed shared file demands it. Files with macros, scripts, or unfamiliar executable content may carry additional risk; use approved security checks, and ask an authorized support person about unexpected file types. **You can decline or pause a suspicious invitation without giving up legitimate access to the underlying service.**

### Privacy levels and authorization

Classify the content before sending it. A public event flyer is different from a student's enrollment record or a family's financial document. Confidential workplace information should use only the authorized systems and recipients for that organization. Information that includes other people's data may require their consent or a legal or institutional basis for disclosure. A learner should not assume that owning a file means having the right to distribute every item inside it.

| Example content | Relative sharing concern | Safer beginner decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fictional classroom practice note | Low, if fully invented | Use it for lesson exercises |
| Publicly released event flyer | Usually lower, subject to source rights | Share only the approved final version |
| Draft team schedule | Moderate; may reveal internal plans | Invite verified team members with needed roles |
| Student contact list | High; contains other people's information | Do not use for practice; follow school policy |
| Client financial records | High; may be regulated or confidential | Use only approved controlled channels and authorization |
| Password list or recovery codes | Extremely sensitive | Do not share through ordinary class exercises or casual links |

### Copies, downloads, and screenshots change the privacy equation

Even if a file is view-only, an authorized recipient can sometimes copy its contents manually or capture what appears on screen. A download restriction may reduce ordinary file copying, but it does not guarantee confidentiality. Think of a shared file as information made available to someone, not as a locked envelope guaranteed to disappear later. For important disclosures, choose the correct channel, review the content, and understand your institution's retention rules.

### Use clear filenames and short context messages

A useful [[filename|filename]] helps the recipient recognize what they have received. A descriptive name such as `Workshop-Agenda-Approved.pdf` is more informative than `final2_new_reallyfinal.pdf`. A short accompanying message can state what the file is for, whether the recipient should read or comment, and when a reply is expected. **Do not put passwords, private account identifiers, or confidential document contents into the message subject merely to make a link easier to recognize.**

For example, an approved message accompanying a class handout could read: “Here is the fictional workshop agenda for review. Please read it before Friday's practice meeting. You have viewing access; send corrections through our agreed class channel.” The message gives purpose without unnecessarily exposing additional information.

### Common collaboration problems and calmer solutions

If two people edit the same document, some applications merge changes automatically while others may create conflicts or duplicate versions. Do not overwrite a colleague's work just because your local copy appears newer. Inspect version information, identify the owner and authoritative document, and use the service's supported version-history or restore controls only when authorized. If a recipient can no longer open a file, diagnose the account and permission issue before changing the link to a broader audience.

If a link was posted to the wrong group chat, consider whether the file is accessible to all group members through the link or through an existing organizational grant. Deleting the chat message may not undo access or remove previously viewed copies. Restrict the relevant sharing grant when authorized and follow any incident-reporting rule. A useful response addresses the **actual access mechanism**, not only the visible message.

### Accessibility and an alternative to complicated menus

People using a screen reader, voice controls, keyboard navigation, magnification, or touch-based accessibility tools should be able to complete the same permission decisions. Work with labeled controls and confirm the accessible name of the selected file, the audience, and the permission before sending. When a service does not expose an essential detail accessibly, use a supported alternate interface or request assistance from an authorized person. A beginner may also describe the workflow from a fictional scenario rather than interacting with a live account.

Accessibility is also a consideration for the recipient. A scanned image of instructions may be difficult to read with assistive software; a structured document or accessible PDF may better support the task. Images need meaningful descriptions when required, and a shared file should use a format the recipient can open. **Successful sharing means the right person can actually use the material in an appropriate form.**

### The eight-point safe-sharing routine

1. **Identify:** Confirm the exact file and its content.
1. **Authorize:** Confirm that the content may be shared for this purpose.
1. **Verify:** Check the complete recipient identity or approved group.
1. **Limit:** Choose the smallest item scope, audience, and permission that work.
1. **Inspect:** Review active links, folder access, and the final confirmation screen.
1. **Deliver:** Send by the approved method and provide a short context message.
1. **Confirm:** Resolve any access issue without automatically making the file public.
1. **Review:** Revisit the grants after the task and remove unnecessary access when authorized.

### Worked example: the wrong file in a polished message

A fictional employee prepares a professional email with a clear subject and the correct recipient. However, the attached file is `Client-Notes-Draft.docx` rather than `Public-Project-Summary.pdf`. The email's polish does not compensate for selecting the wrong content. A safe practice is to inspect the filename **and** the material itself, ideally before beginning the final send. The same precaution applies to cloud links: check the resource the link points to, not just the surrounding message.

### Exit question before the activities

A classmate writes, “I sent the link only to you, so it is definitely private.” What information is missing? A sound answer asks which file the link opens, whether the audience is restricted or anyone-with-link, whether the parent folder grants additional access, what role the link provides, and whether the intended recipient's identity was verified. **Privacy is a property of the effective access settings and the content, not merely of the message used to carry a link.**

## 7. Guided practice: make and review sharing decisions

These six activities develop the skill in stages: first recognize methods, then select permissions, inspect a fictional sharing screen, handle a problem, compare platforms, and complete a safe-sharing plan. **Each required activity may be completed with pen and paper or a local text file.** Do not create real sharing links, send invitations, enter genuine email addresses, change cloud permissions, or upload personal records merely for this lesson. Optional live practice is suitable only with an account and harmless file specifically approved for that purpose.

A strong answer explains the decision. It is not necessary to reproduce the exact placement of a button seen on a particular version of OneDrive, Google Drive, or iCloud Drive. Focus on what the selected action does and how you would verify the outcome. After each activity, use the answer disclosure to compare your reasoning with the model response.

### Activity 1: Match a sharing method to its result

**Objective:** Distinguish delivery of a copy from continuing cloud access.

**Time:** Approximately 10–12 minutes. **Equipment:** This lesson and a local note or paper. **Risk:** None when using fictional examples.

Consider four hypothetical actions:

- **A:** You attach `Public-Library-Flyer.pdf` to a message and send it to a classmate.
- **B:** You invite a verified classmate to view `Study-Outline.txt` in an approved cloud account.
- **C:** You publish an anyone-with-link URL for a harmless public demonstration document.
- **D:** You grant a collaborator Editor access to an approved project folder.

**Steps:**

1. Label each action as **separate copy**, **restricted live access**, **broad link access**, or **folder-level collaborative access**.
1. Explain whether later edits to your source automatically update the material the recipient has.
1. State whether removing a cloud permission would remove a separate copy already received.
1. Identify which action might expose other files in a folder.
1. Choose the safest ordinary method if a partner only needs to read one private practice document.

**Expected result:** You can distinguish both the delivery method and the scope of the recipient's access without opening a sharing dialog.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answers
**A** is a separate copy: editing the original does not automatically revise the attachment, and deleting the sender's message does not reliably remove the recipient's copy. **B** is restricted live access to a selected file; if the provider supports live updates, the authorized viewer may see later changes. **C** is broad link access, which may be forwarded beyond its original audience. **D** is folder-level collaborative access; the recipient may have rights over many contained items, and the exact consequences depend on the service's rules.

For one private document needed only for reading, a verified named recipient with view-only access is normally a good option when an approved service is available. A separate authorized copy may also fit if no ongoing collaboration is required. Neither option allows a sender to erase a copy already retained by the recipient.
:::

### Activity 2: Choose the least necessary role

**Objective:** Apply least privilege to everyday school and workplace scenarios.

**Time:** Approximately 12–15 minutes. **Equipment:** Paper or local note. **Risk:** None.

Use these fictional scenarios:

| Scenario | What the recipient needs | Your proposed role or method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Read a final appointment guide | Read but not revise it | Decide below |
| B. Point out spelling mistakes in a draft | Give feedback without rewriting | Decide below |
| C. Jointly write a club newsletter | Update paragraphs in a shared original | Decide below |
| D. Keep a signed copy of a public event acknowledgment | Retain a record | Decide below |
| E. View one flyer in a folder containing private budgets | Read only the flyer | Decide below |

**Steps:**

1. For each scenario, write **view**, **comment**, **edit**, **send an approved copy**, or a combination when needed.
1. State whether you would share a single item or a folder.
1. Explain why the first role presented by a sharing dialog might not be the right one.
1. Name one condition that would make you stop and seek approval before sharing.
1. Review the model answers, then revise any choice that gave unnecessary rights.

**Success standard:** Your choice fits the recipient's task, and you can identify circumstances where even view access would be inappropriate.

:::answer Show Activity 2 model answers
**A:** Viewer or a read-only authorized copy. **B:** Commenter if the application supports it; otherwise Viewer plus an agreed separate feedback method. **C:** Editor for the specific shared draft, after confirming that collaboration is approved. **D:** A properly authorized separate copy may better fit a fixed record than a live document. **E:** Share the one approved flyer, not the entire budget-containing folder. A recipient should not gain access to unrelated files just because a folder is convenient.

A sharing dialog's default is not proof of appropriateness. Stop if recipients cannot be verified, content is confidential, permission is unclear, or institutional approval is missing.
:::

### Activity 3: Inspect a fictional sharing summary before sending

**Objective:** Identify problems that can remain hidden behind a normal-looking Share button.

**Time:** Approximately 15 minutes. **Equipment:** This page and a note. **Risk:** None.

A fictional sharing screen displays:

Location: Community-Program / Internal-Planning

**Steps:**

1. Identify the item and its containing folder.

1. Determine whether Can view on the direct invitation reliably limits the recipient's ability to edit.
1. Explain the risk of the Anyone with the link setting.
1. Identify any independent permission source that must also be examined.
1. Write the actions you would take **before** distributing a link.
1. Explain what to do if you cannot manage the folder or link yourself.

**Success standard:** You discover the mismatch between the direct permission and the effective access options instead of trusting one role label.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model answers
The item is `Volunteer-Schedule-Draft.docx`, inside an internal planning folder. “Avery P.” might refer to the wrong person; verify the complete approved address. The direct Can view grant does not guarantee the effective permission is limited to viewing, because an anyone-with-link grant is editable and the Project Team folder grant may provide Editor rights. The broad link could be forwarded to someone who was never invited personally.

Do not distribute this link. Confirm whether the document is even approved for external sharing, consult the owner or administrator as needed, reduce unnecessary access through every applicable route, and verify the resulting permission. If you lack authority, stop rather than attempting a workaround.

### Activity 4: Respond to a wrong-recipient or suspicious-invitation problem

**Objective:** Distinguish prevention, containment, and incident follow-up.

**Time:** Approximately 15 minutes. **Equipment:** Local note or paper. **Risk:** None.

**Scenario A:** A student realizes that they selected an unfamiliar address for a draft club document. They have not clicked Send.

**Scenario B:** A worker finds that a restricted file was mistakenly shared to an outside address ten minutes ago.

**Scenario C:** A person receives an unexpected “shared file” message and is asked to enter a password on a page whose address looks unfamiliar.

1. For each scenario, state the first safe action.
1. Explain how you would verify the person or service without trusting the suspicious message itself.

**Success standard:** You can stop preventable mistakes, recognize incident reporting obligations, and avoid providing credentials to suspicious pages.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answers

Removal limits further access but does not prove the file was unseen or not copied. **C:** Do not enter credentials or install anything. Open the legitimate cloud service independently using the known app or address, inspect actual notifications, and verify with the supposed sender through a known contact method. Report suspicious messages through the approved mechanism.

Deleting a chat message cannot reliably recall a delivered attachment or cancel an independent cloud permission. Broadcasting access to anyone with the link enlarges exposure rather than diagnosing the recipient's account or permission issue.

The iCloud Drive user chooses collaboration only if continuing access is intended, restricts the audience to invited people, and selects view rather than edit. If the purpose is simply delivering a fixed copy, Send a Copy may be suitable.

Later, review permissions in OneDrive's sharing/access interface, Google Drive's Share/Manage access controls, or iCloud Drive's Manage Shared File/Folder controls. If an expected feature is unavailable, use the service's actual supported settings or follow the organization's approved alternative; do not bypass restrictions.

### Activity 6: Build an end-to-end safe-sharing plan

**Objective:** Demonstrate the complete decision process from preparation to access review.

**Time:** Approximately 20 minutes. **Equipment:** A local text editor or paper. **Risk:** None with fictional content.

**Capstone scenario:** An MGU student prepares a fictional public workshop agenda, `MGU-Practice-Workshop-Agenda.txt`. Two verified classmates need to read it. ### Practice record and scoring guidance

A useful practice record contains four brief statements: the item and purpose, the approved recipient or audience, the role and method chosen, and the verification or follow-up step. ## 8. Common misconceptions and troubleshooting

### “A link sent in a private message is always private.”

The message and the link have separate properties. A private message can contain an anyone-with-link URL. If the link is forwarded or exposed elsewhere, others may be able to use it. Check the link's active audience setting and any additional grants before assuming the recipient is the only person who can open the file.

### “View-only means the person cannot keep information from the file.”

View-only generally stops editing the shared original. It does not guarantee that a recipient cannot write down, copy, photograph, or otherwise retain what they can see. Some providers offer restrictions on ordinary download and print functions, but those restrictions should not be treated as complete control over information that has been disclosed.

### “Changing a person's direct permission removes all their other access.”

Direct permission may be only one route. A recipient could still have access through a group, link, shared folder, site, or other applicable grant. Review effective access and all applicable routes when the outcome matters. An owner who lacks authority over a group should ask the relevant administrator rather than removing coworkers indiscriminately.

### “Sharing a folder is harmless because I only mentioned one file.”

Folder sharing often applies to its contents and can affect future additions. A top-level folder may contain private subfolders or documents not visible on the initial screen. Share the single approved item or a carefully scoped folder when possible, and review the actual audience and rights.

### “After I revoke a link, everyone loses every copy.”

Revocation can stop access through that link or permission. It cannot retrieve separate copies delivered as attachments, downloads, photographs, screenshots, or printed pages. Choose the content carefully before sharing and use institutionally approved channels for sensitive disclosures.

### “If someone sees Access denied, the safest fix is Anyone with the link.”

Access denied may reflect a wrong account, an incorrect invitation, expired access, group membership, or an organization's restriction. Diagnose the issue and invite the intended person properly if authorized. Broadening the audience can expose material unnecessarily.

### “A contact suggestion proves I selected the right person.”

Autocomplete helps with input but does not establish identity. People can share names; addresses can be outdated or mistyped. Verify the full address from a trusted source, especially for confidential or external sharing.

### “Everyone can edit any file if I select Editor.”

A service may grant edit rights, but actual editing depends on file format, compatible software, account features, and organizational restrictions. Some items are not live-collaborative. Ensure that the recipient can perform the intended task without granting broader rights than necessary.

### “A document is safe to send because the filename looks correct.”

The name may be outdated or ambiguous. Contents can include personal details, tracked changes, hidden sheets, embedded comments, or other information not obvious from the title. Open the selected item, inspect the appropriate preview or exported version, and avoid exposing records unrelated to the recipient's task.

### “A shared invitation from a familiar brand must be legitimate.”

Attackers imitate service notifications and legitimate-looking logos. An unexpected invitation that requests passwords, payment details, or software installation requires independent verification. Use the known app or official website, not the suspicious link as your only route.

### “All file-sharing services have the same options.”

A personal account and a workplace account may show different controls. The available roles, expiry settings, download limitations, and identity-verification behavior also vary by provider. Focus on the common goals—correct item, correct audience, correct role—then consult current vendor documentation for the actual interface.

### “Deleting the sent message undoes a file share.”

Deleting an email or chat message does not necessarily remove a recipient's copy or cancel a cloud permission. If a cloud grant was made, manage that grant in the underlying service. If an attachment was sent to the wrong person, follow any applicable institutional incident-reporting process.

### “If I stop sharing, there is no reason to record what happened.”

For ordinary non-sensitive practice, no detailed record may be needed. For real workplace, school, or other sensitive disclosures, relevant policies may require incident reporting or an access-change record. Revoking a grant does not prove that a file was not downloaded. Report necessary facts through the approved process without making additional copies of confidential material.

### “More security settings always make a document safe to distribute.”

Passwords, expiration dates, and view-only settings are useful controls in some contexts. None replaces the initial question of whether the content should be shared at all. A safer decision may be to send a less sensitive approved summary or to decline the request until proper authorization exists.

### A practical troubleshooting table

| Problem noticed | Check first | Avoid doing immediately |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recipient cannot open the link | Correct account, active audience, recipient grant, service restrictions | Changing it to Anyone with the link |
| Recipient can edit despite View on their name | Other links, groups, or inherited folder permissions | Assuming the direct role overrides everything |
| Wrong person appears in the recipient box | Full address and trusted contact record | Clicking Send because the display name looks familiar |
| Unexpected request for access | Requester's identity and legitimate purpose | Approving without verification |
| File was shared to a wrong group | Actual link scope, existing copies, organization policy | Assuming deleting the chat message reverses access |
| Shared file appears to be the wrong version | Filename, actual contents, location, and version history | Overwriting or deleting versions without review |
| An attachment is too large | Provider limits and approved transfer alternatives | Uploading it to an unauthorized personal account |
| A shared folder contains extra material | Folder tree and inherited permissions | Sharing the entire folder for a one-file task |
| A collaboration invitation asks for a password | Verify directly through the known service and trusted contact | Entering credentials through the unexpected message |
| Revoked user still sees the file | Other permission routes and existing saved copies | Claiming revocation necessarily erased everything |

### Five questions when a file-sharing problem appears

1. **Item:** Is the person looking at the correct file and version?
1. **Identity:** Is the person using the right account or invitation address?
1. **Grant:** Which direct, link, group, or folder permission is supposed to allow access?
1. **Scope:** Does changing that grant affect other people or documents?
1. **Action:** Am I authorized to fix this, or should the owner or administrator handle it?

If the issue cannot be resolved safely, preserve the current information without unnecessary distribution and ask an authorized person. A clear explanation of the problem is more productive than repeated guesses at the Share button.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Terms shown with dotted underlines in the dashboard can be selected to display definitions. The authoritative tooltip registry appears in the single `:::terms` block at the end of this file. The following definitions remain available even when interactive controls cannot be used.

- **File sharing:** Giving another person a way to receive or access a file or its contents.
- **Shared link:** A link that provides access to a cloud-stored item under applicable permissions.
- **Attachment:** A separate file included with a message or email.
- **Sent copy:** A distinct delivered version rather than the original live file.
- **Cloud collaboration:** Multiple authorized people viewing or working with an online item.
- **Permission:** Rule specifying what a person or group is authorized to do.
- **Viewer:** Person permitted to read a shared item, generally without editing the original.
- **Commenter:** Person allowed to give feedback using supported commenting features.
- **Editor:** Person authorized to change shared content, within the service's rules.
- **Owner:** Account or person with primary control over an item and its sharing, subject to service and organizational rules.
- **Least privilege:** Granting only the access needed for a legitimate purpose.
- **Specific people:** Named or invited identities selected to receive access.
- **Restricted access:** A sharing configuration limiting access to authorized identities.
- **Organization link:** Link intended for eligible people in a specified organization, subject to provider controls.
- **Anyone with the link:** Permission that can allow any holder of the link to access an item.
- **Parent folder:** Container in which a file or another folder is stored.
- **Shared folder:** Folder accessible to multiple authorized people under defined permissions.
- **Inherited permission:** Access deriving from a containing folder, group, or other broader permission source.
- **Effective access:** What someone can actually do after considering all applicable grants.
- **Access review:** Checking the current audience, roles, and permission sources for a shared item.
- **Revocation:** Withdrawing or restricting a particular access grant.
- **Link expiration:** Optional setting causing a supported link to stop working after a specified time.
- **Download restriction:** Service-specific control limiting direct downloads, not an absolute safeguard against content capture.
- **Recipient verification:** Confirming that an intended person's full identity or address is correct.
- **Account:** Identity used to reach a provider's online service.
- **Group access:** Permissions assigned to members of a group.
- **Administrator:** Authorized person or system responsible for managing an organization's rules or access.
- **Sharing scope:** The range of items and people covered by a permission decision.
- **Share sheet:** Menu offering ways to send a copy, use an app, or collaborate, depending on the product.
- **Filenames:** Names identifying files in a folder or service.
- **Version history:** Record of earlier document states supported by some applications.
- **Redaction:** Deliberate removal of information before release.
- **Metadata:** Additional information associated with a file, such as certain properties or location data.
- **Phishing:** Deceptive attempt to obtain information or induce unsafe action using misleading communication.
- **Suspicious invitation:** Unexpected or questionable request to open or share content that needs verification.
- **Security incident:** Event involving possible unauthorized access or disclosure that may require reporting.
- **Sensitive information:** Data whose distribution could harm privacy or violate applicable rules.
- **Cloud storage:** Provider-operated storage reachable through online services.
- **OneDrive:** Microsoft's file-storage and sharing service.
- **Google Drive:** Google's file-storage and sharing service.
- **iCloud Drive:** Apple's file-storage and sharing service.
- **Files app:** File-browsing application on supported devices, including Apple mobile devices.
- **File Explorer:** Windows file manager used to locate and work with files.
- **Finder:** macOS file manager used to locate and work with files.
- **Browser:** Application used to view and interact with websites.
- **Download:** Saving data received from an online service to a device or destination.
- **Upload:** Transferring a file from a local source into a receiving online service.
- **Offline copy:** File stored locally so it can be opened without a network connection.
- **Backup:** Independently maintained recovery copy or process intended to restore lost information.
- **Share notification:** Message or alert that may announce a newly granted invitation or access request.
- **Collaboration:** Working with other authorized people on a common task or document.
- **Trusted channel:** Communication route already verified as appropriate for a recipient and purpose.
- **Permission inheritance:** Propagation of access rights from larger containers to covered items.
- **Read-only:** Description of access that does not authorize ordinary changes to the shared original.
- **Access request:** Request from someone who does not currently have the needed permission.
- **Password-protected link:** Sharing link requiring an additional secret where supported.
- **Sharing policy:** Organizational rules about what may be distributed and to whom.
- **Confidential file:** Document requiring restricted handling because of its contents or obligations.
- **Shared workspace:** Collaborative location containing files or other items for an approved group.
- **Public link:** Link whose access audience is intentionally broad.
- **Contact autocomplete:** Interface that suggests stored recipients based on partial input.
- **File format:** Standard form in which a file's data is represented, often indicated by its extension.
- **Document preview:** Display of a file's contents without necessarily downloading or editing it.
- **Comment:** Feedback attached to a supported document without necessarily changing its main content.
- **Protected document:** Document subject to additional restrictions or controls.
- **Audit trail:** Available record of changes or actions, which may be limited by the service.
- **Account verification:** Confirmation that the correct service identity is being used.
- **Accessible document:** File prepared so people using varied assistive technologies can use its information.
- **Information disclosure:** Making information available to another person, intentionally or accidentally.
- **Retention:** How long information or copies are kept under applicable practices or rules.
- **Access denied:** Message indicating an account lacks the required authorization or cannot open an item for another reason.
- **Authoritative copy:** Version designated as the current or official version for a workflow.
- **Share dialog:** User interface used to inspect or set recipients and permissions for a file.
- **Live document:** Shared item on which later approved changes can appear to authorized users.
- **Recipient:** Person or account intended to receive an invitation, link, or copy.
- **Identity verification:** Process of confirming that a person or account is who they are claimed to be.
- **Shared access:** Ability of multiple eligible people to open an item under specified permissions.
- **External recipient:** Person or identity outside the sender's organization or account environment.
- **Copy link:** Interface action that places a link on the clipboard, potentially without sending it.
- **Content review:** Checking the actual information in a file before distributing it.
