## Lesson 5: Notes and To-Do Lists

You are reading instructions for a small project when someone mentions three useful facts and two actions you must complete later. You remember the general conversation, but by the following day you cannot recall which document to read, which task comes first, or whether you have finished the work. The difficulty is not necessarily a poor memory. The information was never captured in a dependable system that separates **what you need to remember** from **what you need to do**.

This lesson teaches two complementary skills. A [[note|note]] preserves information for later reference: an explanation, a decision, an observation, or a useful link. A [[task|task]] describes an action with a recognizable finish: locate a document, revise a paragraph, or send an approved message. A [[to-do-list|to-do list]] collects those actions so that you can choose, perform, and review them. Both can be managed on paper, in a simple text document, or in a dedicated application. **The goal is a reliable habit, not dependence on any one product or account.**

This is Lesson 5 of MGU's Everyday Productivity course. It builds on *Taking Screenshots*, *Copy, Paste, and Clipboard*, *Keyboard Shortcuts*, and *Managing Windows and Tabs*. Windows 11 and its basic text editor are the primary desktop examples. Equivalent practices for Mac, Chromebook, iPhone, iPad, and Android are provided. All required activities can be performed with invented information; they do not require a subscription, account registration, shared document, or working internet connection.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 90–120 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Everyday Productivity Lessons 1–4 recommended · **Materials:** Paper or an available note editor; optional task-list app; fictional practice details only · **Safety:** Do not store passwords, payment numbers, confidential records, or private information in an unprotected practice note

> **Essential idea:** **Capture information → separate facts from actions → give each action a clear next step → save and organize → review and update.** A note helps you find out what was said; a to-do list helps you decide what to do next.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish a note, task, to-do list, checklist, reminder, calendar event, and saved document.
1. Record brief, useful notes with a clear title, context, source, and next action when appropriate.
1. Create and save a simple note or task list in a beginner-accessible text editor, and verify where it was saved.
1. Turn vague intentions into specific, finishable actions, then mark work completed without losing important reference information.
1. Distinguish a due date from a reminder, and prioritize tasks using realistic time and dependency constraints.
1. Recognize common note and task options on Windows, macOS, ChromeOS, iOS/iPadOS, and Android without treating products as interchangeable.
1. Use a brief daily review, a practical weekly cleanup, and straightforward retrieval habits.
1. Protect privacy, understand synchronization and sharing limitations, and troubleshoot common note and task problems.

## 1. What is the difference between notes, tasks, and reminders?

A [[note|note]] is recorded information you want to consult later. It may be a sentence, list of observations, short explanation, set of instructions, or a record of a decision. A note is useful when you can return to it and understand why it mattered. You might write, “The library closes early on the fictional training day,” or “The classroom printer uses recycled paper for drafts.” Neither statement, by itself, tells you that an action is still required. Notes can include action items, but their primary purpose is to **preserve context**.

A [[task|task]] is a specific action that can be completed. “Check library opening hours on the official website” is a task. “Write a three-sentence project summary” is another. A [[to-do-list|to-do list]] brings multiple tasks into one visible place. An item becomes [[completed|completed]] when its defined result has been achieved, not simply because you looked at it or spent time on it. A [[checklist|checklist]] is a list in which items can be checked off; it might describe tasks, recurring safety checks, supplies to pack, or steps in a procedure. The words overlap in everyday use, but their purpose matters more than their label.

A [[reminder|reminder]] prompts you at a selected time or under a supported condition. “Review the practice worksheet on Tuesday at 4 p.m.” could be a task with a reminder. A [[calendar-event|calendar event]] typically reserves or identifies a period of time, such as a training class running from 2 to 3 p.m. Some apps integrate reminders, tasks, and events, but they are not interchangeable. Recording a deadline does not guarantee an alert will appear; an alert usually requires separate setup, permissions, and suitable device or account settings.

| Type | Main question it answers | Beginner example | Completion or storage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Note | What should I remember or refer to? | “The assignment requires three sources” | Retain while useful as a reference |
| Task | What action should I perform? | “Find the three sources” | Mark done after the action is finished |
| To-do list | Which actions remain? | Three practice steps to finish | Update progress and remove clutter intentionally |
| Checklist | Have I verified each item? | Check title, spelling, and save location | Check each item after verification |
| Reminder | When should I be prompted? | Review draft at 4 p.m. | Depends on alert settings and device state |
| Calendar event | When is a scheduled activity? | Workshop at 2 p.m. | Usually occupies a planned time period |
| Reference document | Where are the full details? | Instructions saved as a file | Keep a retrievable copy with an appropriate name |

### The difference between reference and action

Imagine a fictional club meeting. The speaker says, “The presentation must be five minutes, the room supports a projector, and we need one printed handout.” Your note might capture the three facts. Your task list might say, “Draft a five-minute presentation” and “Print one handout.” If you combine everything into the vague item “club meeting,” the list does not show what remains to be done. If you record only the tasks, you may later forget the five-minute limit. **Keep the useful facts and the actions connected, without assuming they are the same kind of information.**

You can connect them by using a common project title or placing a short reference beneath the task. For example, a note called `Club presentation — instructions` may hold requirements. A task called `Draft club presentation` may contain a brief reference to that note. The same principle works on paper: label a notes page and write its page number next to a task.

### Tasks, projects, and substeps

A [[project|project]] is an outcome that typically requires more than one action. “Prepare a class presentation” is a project, because it may involve choosing a subject, collecting sources, writing an outline, practicing, and preparing a handout. A [[subtask|subtask]] is one smaller action inside a larger task or project. Not every project needs sophisticated software: a heading followed by three to six ordinary tasks is sufficient for many beginners.

Start by finding the **next physically or digitally possible action**. “Become organized” is a wish rather than an immediately finishable task. “Create a folder named Practice Notes” is a concrete action. “Complete my education” is a broad goal. “Read pages 1–5 of the assigned chapter” is something you can do now. Broad goals have value, but putting them directly on a daily checklist can make progress hard to recognize.

### A note is not automatically true or up to date

A note records information, but it does not verify that information. A date copied from an outdated flyer could be wrong. A price written in a note can change. A class instruction may be superseded. When details matter, add the [[source|source]], date, or place where you can confirm the information. This is especially important for financial, legal, health, or institutional requirements. The purpose is not to burden every shopping list with citations; it is to know **which information needs verification before it drives a decision**.

### Worked example: sorting five statements

Consider a fictional short workshop. Separate the following into information and actions: “The workshop is 45 minutes”; “Bring a notebook”; “Three copies are needed”; “Read the handout”; “The instructor prefers questions at the end.” The first, third, and fifth statements are reference notes. “Bring a notebook” and “Read the handout” are actions that could become tasks. The fact that three copies are needed may also generate a new task: “Print three copies of the handout.” Turning an important fact into an action is a deliberate choice, not something a note automatically performs for you.

## 2. How do you capture clear, useful notes?

The first purpose of note-taking is to reduce the burden on memory. It is not necessary to transcribe every word. A useful note contains enough information for **future you** to understand what mattered and where to continue. Begin by choosing a short descriptive [[title|title]]. “Workshop instructions — practice example” is much easier to recognize than “New Note,” “Stuff,” or “Untitled 12.” A title may contain a subject and a context; avoid placing sensitive personal identifiers in filenames or titles that might appear in recent-file lists or shared screens.

### A five-part beginner note structure

For many everyday notes, five simple fields are enough: **title, date or context, key information, next action if any, and source or location**. Not all fields are required in every note. A shopping list may need only a title and items. A research note usually benefits from a source. A meeting note may need who made a decision and what follows. This is a flexible template, not a mandatory form.

| Field | Purpose | Fictional example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title | Recognize the subject later | `Community workshop — notes` |
| Context | Know when or why it was recorded | `Practice planning exercise; Week 1` |
| Key information | Preserve facts and decisions | `Session lasts 45 minutes; use a short introduction` |
| Next action | Turn a requirement into work | `Draft a three-point outline` |
| Source | Recheck details if needed | `Fictional class handout, section 2` |

A plain text sample could look like this:

```text
Community workshop — practice notes
Context: Fictional classroom scenario, Week 1

Key information
- The presentation should last five minutes.
- The room has a projector.
- Students should cite their sources.

Next actions
- Draft a three-point outline.
- Find two suitable public educational sources.

Source: Practice assignment sheet, section 2.
```

The sample is a teaching illustration, not a file you must submit or a description of a real event. Notice that the **facts remain in the Key information area** while the **unfinished actions sit in their own area**. An instructor can assess whether a learner has made this distinction without asking for any real-life notes.

### Capture first; tidy second

If you interrupt yourself to find perfect wording every time an idea occurs, you may miss the next piece of information. During an authorized lecture or personal brainstorming session, capture short phrases and important distinctions. Afterward, review the material, correct spelling, expand unclear abbreviations, remove irrelevant fragments, and extract action items. This two-stage process is sometimes called [[capture|capture]] and [[review|review]]. It allows speed during the moment and clarity when you use the note later.

For example, a hurried capture might say `intro 3 pts public sources due fri?`. A thoughtful review might rewrite it as, “Draft the introduction with three main points; use publicly accessible sources; confirm the due date from the class instructions.” The question mark matters: the date was not established. Do not transform uncertainty into a made-up certainty simply to make the note look finished.

### Short summaries and meaningful headings

Use your own concise words to preserve meaning. If an article contains several paragraphs about the benefits of local backups, a note might say, “A local backup gives an additional copy if an online service is unavailable; check the backup by opening a sample file.” Do not copy long passages unnecessarily, and do not treat a summary as a replacement for a source whose exact language is important. For class study, quote only what your assignment permits and give appropriate attribution.

Headings can separate **Background**, **Key points**, **Questions**, **Decisions**, and **Next actions**. A simple note does not require all five. Use two or three when the topic is small. Excessive formatting can make the note harder to maintain. If your app offers bold type, tables, audio, or images, use them when they clarify information rather than because the features exist.

### Distinguish observation, decision, and question

One of the most useful habits is labeling information by its status. **Observation:** “The demo took eight minutes.” **Decision:** “We will shorten it to five minutes.” **Question:** “Who will prepare the handout?” These statements do different jobs. If you turn the question into a task for someone without authorization, you risk implying that a responsibility was assigned when it was not. If someone else must approve a decision, label it as **proposed** until approval is confirmed.

| Statement | Proper label | What to do next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “We observed three spelling errors” | Observation | Record where they were found |
| “We decided to correct them today” | Decision | Create or assign a correction task if authorized |
| “Should we add pictures?” | Open question | Ask the relevant person before deciding |
| “Possible deadline is Friday” | Unconfirmed detail | Check the actual instructions |
| “The document was saved to Practice” | Claimed completion | Verify the file appears in the intended folder |

### A trustworthy source reference

A [[reference|reference]] may be a document title, an official web address, a page number, a course lesson, or a file location. For a public online resource, save enough information to locate it again: title and recognizable source are often sufficient, with the full URL when useful. A copied link is not a backup of the page itself. The page may move, change, require a login, or disappear. Do not paste personal account links into public notes or class submissions.

If an instruction says, “Submit by the date shown in the classroom portal,” record that the portal is the authoritative location. When it is time to submit, open the portal and verify the current date. This is more dependable than leaving an undated guess in a note and assuming it remains correct indefinitely.

### Notes made from conversations or meetings

Personal notes should be accurate and respectful of privacy. If you plan to record audio, photograph another person's material, or create a detailed transcript, obtain the necessary permission and follow applicable rules. A short written summary is usually enough for the basic lesson. For group work, distinguish what participants **suggested**, what they **agreed**, and what someone **committed to doing**. Avoid attributing statements or decisions to people who have not authorized or confirmed them.

### Worked example: from raw information to a useful note

A fictional student hears: “Choose a public science topic, write three questions, use the library's online catalog for at least one source, and bring the outline to class.” A raw note might be a jumble of these phrases. A better note titled `Science practice — assignment details` says: “Topic must be public science; outline includes three questions; at least one source should be identified through the library catalog; bring outline to class.” A linked task list says: “Choose science topic,” “Write three research questions,” “Find a library source,” and “Prepare the outline.” The original instructions remain available for checking whether the student understood them correctly.

## 3. How do you create, save, and find a note on Windows 11?

A beginner needs a simple, reproducible way to make a digital note. Windows 11 includes [[notepad|Notepad]], a basic editor suitable for short notes and simple lists. Depending on your installed version, Notepad may support additional tabs, session restoration, formatting, or other features. Those conveniences do not change the essential requirement: **identify an intentional save location and verify that the file exists**. You do not need to sign in to a cloud service to create a local plain-text note.

### Step-by-step: make a local note

1. On a Windows 11 computer where you have permission to create files, open the Start menu, search for `Notepad`, and open the app.
1. Observe the blank editing area. If several Notepad tabs are open, select an empty one, or create a new document through the available command.
1. Type a practice title such as `Fictional workshop notes`, followed by one or two sentences.
1. On a new line, type `Next action: Draft a three-point outline.`
1. Open the app's **File** menu and choose **Save As** when available, or use **Save** for a new document to open the save dialog.
1. Choose a folder you are allowed to use, such as a personal Documents subfolder named `Practice Notes`, and enter `workshop-practice.txt` as the file name.
1. Select **Save**. If your version offers a format choice, keep a straightforward text format suitable for the exercise.
1. Open **File Explorer**, navigate to the chosen folder, and look for `workshop-practice.txt`. Open it to confirm the actual content.

The exact wording of the dialogs can differ by Windows version and organizational policy. Some computers prevent saving to certain locations. If the folder is not writable, use a permitted personal folder rather than disabling security controls. If the editor automatically retains a working session, still create and verify a persistent file for this assignment.

### Understand file names and destinations

A [[filename|filename]] identifies the saved file. The extension `.txt` generally indicates plain text. A [[folder|folder]] is the container or location in which the file resides. The [[file-path|file path]] identifies its location within the device's file system. Two folders can contain files with the same name; that is why **remembering the destination matters as much as remembering the title**. In File Explorer, you might see `Documents > Practice Notes > workshop-practice.txt`.

Avoid names such as `note`, `final`, or `new` when several files share the same subject. A descriptive pattern such as `science-outline-practice.txt` is easier to locate. You do not need a complicated numbered naming system for every grocery list, but a more formal project may benefit from consistent titles and dates. Remember that a date within the file name is part of the name, not an automatically verified timestamp of what occurred.

### Save, Save As, autosave, and session restoration

**Save** generally writes changes to the existing file once an appropriate destination is set. **Save As** generally creates a separately named or located copy; behavior can vary by app. [[autosave|Autosave]] means an application attempts to store edits automatically, but its exact scope, location, and conditions are product-specific. [[session-restoration|Session restoration]] may reopen an editor window with previous text after a restart. It is not the same assurance as finding a deliberately saved, independently accessible document.

| Action | Common purpose | What still needs checking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Save | Store current changes to an existing file | Correct file and destination |
| Save As | Choose another filename or location | Whether the intended copy was created |
| Autosave | Reduce manual-save steps in some apps | Enabled status, destination, and sync conditions |
| Reopen a recent note | Return to an earlier view | Whether the underlying file still exists |
| Find in File Explorer | Locate stored files in folders | Open the file and inspect its content |
| Copy a note's text | Reuse content elsewhere | Whether copied text is appropriate to share |

If you edit a note and later find an older version in the expected folder, check whether you edited a second copy in another location or whether the application did not write the new changes. Do not delete either copy until you know which one contains the information you need.

### A plain-text checklist for beginners

Even without an app that provides clickable checkboxes, a text file can hold a clear task list:

```text
Practice tasks — fictional assignment
[ ] Choose a public science topic
[ ] Write three research questions
[ ] Find one library source
[ ] Draft a short outline

Completed
[x] Read the assignment instructions
```

Here `[ ]` means not yet completed and `[x]` means completed **because you changed the characters yourself**. These are ordinary text symbols, not interactive checkboxes. A text editor will not necessarily generate a reminder, reorder the tasks, or track progress for you. In the MGU lesson file itself, this illustration appears as code; it is not a new dashboard control.

### Notes apps versus text documents

Other Windows applications can hold notes: a word processor, Microsoft Sticky Notes, a browser-based notes service, or an organization-approved work platform. A [[sticky-note|sticky note]] is useful for a brief visible reminder but may be easy to lose among windows, and its synchronization depends on the product and account. A text document offers a simple file you can locate in a folder, while a dedicated notes app might offer search, tagging, links, checklists, or synchronization. Choose a tool appropriate to the importance and confidentiality of the content.

Do not assume an app is preinstalled just because another learner has it. Avoid installing unknown extensions or purchasing software for this beginner exercise. On a school or company computer, follow the institution's approved software, storage, account, and record-retention policies.

### Retrieving the note without guessing

To [[retrieve|retrieve]] a note, start with the folder where you saved it. If you cannot remember the folder, search within the appropriate authorized area of File Explorer for part of the file name, such as `workshop-practice`, rather than making five new copies. When you open a search result, verify the file path and contents. A search may find a similarly named old file or shortcut. If the note is stored in a cloud application, its search and account rules are different from local File Explorer search.

### Worked example: a correct result and a false sense of completion

A learner types a note in Notepad and sees it on the screen. The note is **visible**, but that alone does not prove it has been intentionally saved to a known folder. The learner uses Save As, names the file, selects the Practice Notes folder, closes the editor only after confirming saving, and reopens the file from File Explorer. Now the learner has stronger evidence that the note exists at the intended location. A learner who merely leaves the editor open has not demonstrated the same outcome.

## 4. How do you turn intentions into a practical to-do list?

A useful list is a set of [[action-item|action items]] that describe real next steps. Start each task with a verb: **open, read, write, compare, verify, call, print, organize, submit**. Avoid vague wording that hides the desired result. “Study better” does not say what to do. “Read the first three pages and write two questions” does. “Fix computer” is very broad; “Write down the error message and check the manufacturer's help page” is a safer, finishable first step.

### Make the next action visible

A clear task normally answers: **What will I do? What result will show that it is finished?** Some tasks also need a time or place. Do not overengineer trivial tasks. “Buy notebook” can be sufficient for a private shopping list. But “Prepare materials” may need steps when several supplies, documents, and permissions are involved. Ask whether you could look at the item tomorrow and know exactly how to begin.

| Vague entry | Better next action | How to verify completion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Get organized | Create a `Practice Notes` folder | Folder appears in chosen location |
| Work on research | Find one reliable public source | Record title and source address |
| Prepare presentation | Draft three outline headings | Three headings exist in the draft |
| Contact instructor | Draft a clear question for instructor approval | Question is written; sending is a separate step |
| Study | Review lesson sections 1–3 | Can summarize each section |
| Finish assignment | Compare completed work with requirements | Every listed requirement has been checked |

### Separate one-time and recurring tasks

A [[one-time-task|one-time task]] happens once, such as “Save the project outline as a file.” A [[recurring-task|recurring task]] happens again on a schedule, such as “Review the task list each weekday.” A dedicated task app may generate repeated occurrences, but a plain-text list requires manual updates. Recurring tasks need a realistic frequency: a daily review might take two minutes; a weekly review might take ten. A task that repeats only when a condition occurs may not fit a calendar-based schedule.

### Due dates, start dates, and reminders

A [[due-date|due date]] is the date by which a task should be finished. A [[start-date|start date]] or planned start is when you intend to begin. A [[reminder|reminder]] is a prompt. These are different. An assignment due on Friday may need a writing session on Wednesday and a review reminder on Thursday. A due date without a time is not necessarily due at midnight, and an app's default notification time is not evidence of the institution's actual deadline. **Verify external deadlines in the authoritative source.**

When using a tool that supports notifications, check that alerts are enabled, the correct account is signed in, the device can receive notifications, and the time zone is appropriate. For consequential deadlines, use a second dependable method rather than assuming a single pop-up will always occur. A reminder also cannot complete the task for you; it only brings it to your attention.

### Priority is not the same as urgency

[[priority|Priority]] expresses relative importance. [[urgency|Urgency]] expresses how soon an action needs attention. A critical action may be important but not due immediately. A less important item can become urgent because a deadline is near. For beginner lists, use only three priority labels when necessary: **Must do**, **Should do**, and **Could do**. Avoid marking every task as the highest priority, because the label then stops helping you choose.

| Situation | Sensible placement | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An assignment is due tomorrow | Must do; verify exact deadline | Near-term obligation |
| A book list is useful next month | Should do or schedule review | Worth doing but not immediate |
| A decorative document cover is optional | Could do | Completion does not depend on it |
| Permission is needed before publishing | Blocked until approved | Acting early would be inappropriate |
| A task was already verified as finished | Completed | No longer belongs among unfinished next actions |

### Dependencies and waiting

A [[dependency|dependency]] is something that must happen before another action can proceed. You cannot accurately “Submit approved flyer” until the flyer has been approved. When a task is blocked, label it [[waiting|waiting]] or write “Waiting for approval” alongside it rather than repeatedly trying the impossible next step. If another person is responsible, do not assume they have accepted responsibility solely because you put their name in a note. Use an authorized channel and an agreed deadline.

For example, a fictional student needs a classroom handout. The steps could be: draft text; request instructor review; wait for approval; print three copies. If approval is pending, the printing task should not be treated as fully ready. A list that records these dependencies is more useful than one that simply announces “Handout — urgent.”

### A realistic daily list

A daily list should fit your actual available time. Beginners often write down every long-term goal and then feel unsuccessful because the list cannot be finished. Choose **two or three important, feasible next actions**, plus smaller items if time permits. Keep future tasks in a broader list or project note. Estimate time only when it genuinely helps; a two-minute action and a two-hour writing task should not occupy identical planning expectations.

A useful check is whether you have space for unexpected delays. If you have thirty minutes before class, do not schedule five independent twenty-minute tasks. If you cannot estimate a task, break it into a first step such as “Read the first page and identify the main question,” then revise the plan after learning how much work remains.

### Mark completed, retain a record, and avoid false completion

When the result meets the task's definition, mark it [[completed|complete]]. Do not check off “Send approved email” after merely drafting the message. Instead, distinguish “Draft email,” “Obtain approval,” and “Send email.” For class exercises, learners must not send any real messages. Many apps allow completed items to remain in a history or completed section; a paper or text list might mark them with `[x]` and move them to a Completed area. A completed marker is a human or app-provided status, not independent evidence that every external system received the intended result.

### Worked example: from one project to a task list

A fictional goal is “Prepare a three-minute demonstration about rainfall.” First, record requirements in a note: three-minute limit, one diagram allowed, and no private data. Then create tasks: “Write three explanation points”; “Locate one public educational diagram and check usage conditions”; “Draft a short opening sentence”; “Rehearse and time the demonstration”; “Check that the final version follows the assignment requirements.” Do not add a “Send to class” task unless the assignment requires it. Each task is specific enough for the learner to begin, and the reference note preserves the original constraints.

## 5. How do you use notes and tasks across devices?

The same basic method—capture facts, identify actions, save clearly, and review—works on computers, tablets, and phones. The applications and controls vary, however. A local note saved on one computer does not automatically appear on another. A [[cloud-notes|cloud notes]] service may synchronize information when the correct account, settings, network, and service conditions are in place. A task application may offer due dates and reminders that a plain text editor does not. Never confuse a familiar app icon with a guarantee that the same data exists on every device.

### Windows: a file-based note and optional apps

On Windows 11, Notepad is an accessible starting point for writing brief notes and simple `[ ]` task lists. It allows you to understand typing, naming, saving, reopening, and editing without introducing an account requirement. For a fuller document, a word processor may offer headings and formatting. Sticky Notes can support short personal reminders; use the available app and confirm whether it saves locally, under an account, or through synchronization. **Do not depend on a desktop note being visible as proof that it is backed up or private.**

Some learners may encounter **Microsoft To Do** in existing Microsoft environments. As of November 2026, Microsoft has announced that its To Do apps will retire on **June 30, 2027**. Microsoft identifies Planner as the direction for eligible work or school accounts but does **not** present Planner as an equivalent replacement for personal Microsoft accounts, and existing To Do lists are not automatically migrated. For a durable beginner course, learn the transferable concepts of lists, actions, dates, and completion rather than treating To Do as a required product. Before depending on it for ongoing work, consult [Microsoft's transition guidance](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/planner/to-do-to-planner). This product-specific notice does not affect the text-file exercises in this lesson.

### Mac: Notes and Reminders

On macOS, Apple's **Notes** app is designed for recording information. A learner can choose an appropriate Notes folder, create a new note, type a descriptive first line, and add text or a checklist where supported. Apple's **Reminders** app is designed for actionable items and provides list and task-management capabilities. You might store an explanation of a reading assignment in Notes and place “Complete the reading questions” in Reminders. Some people prefer a checklist inside Notes for a very small project; that is reasonable when separate timed alerts are unnecessary.

Account choice matters. Depending on settings, Notes can store material in iCloud, a different supported account, or an On My Mac location. A note saved only on one Mac will not necessarily synchronize to an iPhone. A shared or managed Mac may have different capabilities. In Reminders, changing a due date or alert affects a particular reminder entry; it does not automatically change an institution's official deadline. Learn to identify the list name and check the active account before expecting a change to appear elsewhere.

### iPhone and iPad: use Notes for information, Reminders for actions

On an iPhone or iPad, open **Notes** to type a short reference note or create a checklist using the available toolbar. In **Reminders**, create a list or choose an existing list and enter an action, such as “Review the fictional practice outline.” If useful, open the item's details to add a date, time, or priority. When you finish the action, mark it completed. The screen arrangement and labels can differ across iOS and iPadOS versions, so follow the controls visible on your device rather than assuming every phone displays an identical layout.

For accessibility, you may use voice input when available and appropriate, but reread the text for recognition mistakes. Do not speak sensitive or confidential content aloud in a public setting merely to take a note faster. Touchscreens are especially prone to accidental taps on **Delete**, **Share**, or **Done**. Before confirming an action, read the selected item and the resulting screen.

### Chromebook and Android: Google Keep and Google Tasks

On a Chromebook, a simple local text document or an authorized web note application can support the same beginner method. **Google Keep** provides notes and lists; its website is `https://keep.google.com/` when access and account permissions allow. On a computer, a learner can start a note or use the service's **New list** control for checkable items. On Android, Keep has corresponding note and list functions, although the controls and app versions vary. If the device is managed by a school or employer, Keep and other apps may be restricted.

**Google Tasks** is more explicitly task-oriented. A learner can enter a short action, place it in a list, and add a date or time where supported. Keep and Tasks are separate products with different capabilities even though Google may connect reminders or task information with other services. A checklist item in a note is not guaranteed to become a Google Tasks entry, and a dated task is not automatically a calendar appointment with reserved time. Verify the actual destination and notification behavior rather than inferring what was synchronized.

### Cross-platform comparison

| Device or environment | Simple note option | Task or checklist option | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Windows 11 | Notepad text file | Plain text checklist; approved task app | A saved local file does not automatically sync |
| Mac | Apple Notes; text editor | Apple Reminders; Notes checklist | Account and storage location affect availability |
| iPhone or iPad | Apple Notes | Apple Reminders or Notes checklist | Tap labels and account settings vary by version |
| Chromebook | Authorized text editor; Google Keep | Keep list; Google Tasks if available | Browser-based tools may require account and network access |
| Android | Notes app supplied by device maker; Google Keep | Keep checklist; Google Tasks | Apps and notification permissions differ |
| Paper worksheet | Written reference notes | Handwritten checkboxes | No automatic search, sync, or reminders |

### Local storage, cloud sync, and backups

[[local-storage|Local storage]] means the file or record is kept on a particular device or its local storage system. [[synchronization|Synchronization]] coordinates information among devices or services, often through an account. A [[backup|Backup]] is a separate recoverable copy intended to help restore data after loss or damage. Synchronization is valuable, but it is not automatically a backup: deletions and mistakes may synchronize too. A screenshot of a note might record its appearance but is not a complete, editable, reliable backup of every task or link within it.

For this lesson, saving a practice text file to an allowed folder is enough. A student should not need to set up cloud storage, grant sharing permissions, or move personal records to an online service. The next lesson, *Cloud Storage Basics*, teaches storage and synchronization in more detail. When moving between devices, observe whether the same note is available before claiming that the devices are connected.

### What to check before switching apps

If you already have a system that works, consider whether changing tools would genuinely improve retrieval, accessibility, or task completion. Moving information can lose formatting, attachments, task history, reminders, or sharing permissions. Review what is exportable, what is stored locally, whether the new tool is permitted, and how to confirm that the content arrived intact. **Do not delete the original records until you have verified a usable replacement and satisfied any retention obligations.** Beginners gain more value from one consistent workflow than from maintaining duplicate lists in four services.

### Worked example: one task on two devices

A student writes “Read three pages of the public science guide” in a laptop text file. Later they open a phone's task app and see no such item. Nothing is necessarily broken: the laptop's local file and the phone app are different storage systems. The student can complete the reading using the original list, or—if policy permits—manually enter the action into the selected task app. Once the task appears in two places, the student must decide which one is authoritative, or they risk marking it done on one device while forgetting to update the other. The important concept is **source of truth**, not number of applications.

## 6. How do you maintain a safe, accessible productivity system?

Creating a note takes seconds; using it consistently requires a small routine. A good system reduces missed work and unnecessary searching without making the learner constantly manage the system itself. Begin with a [[capture-location|capture location]]—one notebook, one text folder, or one approved note app. Decide where new information goes. Then choose a small place for actionable items, perhaps a single list called `Practice Tasks`. **Do not require a student to maintain complex tags, colors, folders, and priority schemes before they can make a useful note.**

### A simple daily routine

A [[daily-review|daily review]] can take two or three minutes. Look at the current task list, confirm which tasks are still relevant, choose the next two or three finishable actions, and note important deadlines that should be checked in their original sources. At the end of the day, mark verified completions and move genuinely unfinished tasks forward deliberately. If you have an idea or instruction during the day, capture it quickly and process it during review. This keeps the act of collecting information separate from the act of planning work.

1. **Look:** Open the list in its expected location; do not first make a duplicate list.
1. **Check:** Read any important deadline or dependency and confirm changes.
1. **Choose:** Select a realistic number of tasks for the time available.
1. **Act:** Work on one item and mark completion only when the result exists.
1. **Close:** Save changes, record anything unfinished, and leave the list retrievable.

### A weekly cleanup that does not erase history

A [[weekly-review|weekly review]] can help prevent the list from becoming a storage pile. Check older items, remove accidental duplicates, group related actions, clarify vague entries, and confirm whether waiting items still depend on another person or approval. You can archive completed practice items or retain a small completion record when it matters. Do not delete institutional or business records solely because they have been completed: workplace retention requirements and audit trails may apply. For a fictional student worksheet, completed examples can be moved to a simple `Completed` heading.

### Naming and organization: keep it findable

A beginner may need only two text files: `practice-notes.txt` for reference material and `practice-tasks.txt` for actions. Later, separate folders for learning, personal administration, or projects can help. Choose clear names that express the subject. Generic terms such as `miscellaneous`, `stuff`, and `urgent` can become crowded. Avoid creating a new dated file every day if you cannot remember which one contains the original task. The best [[organization|organization]] scheme is one that a learner can actually maintain and explain.

| Need | Simple method | Risk to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capture a new thought | Put it in one known note location | Scattering scraps across many apps |
| Remember an action | Write an action verb and result | Listing only broad goals |
| Find an old note | Use a clear title and consistent folder | Relying only on “recent files” |
| Plan today | Choose a few achievable tasks | Treating an entire project as one daily task |
| Track a blocked item | Label it “waiting” with the reason | Marking it finished before approval |
| Finish a task | Verify the result and mark complete | Assuming a tap proves external completion |
| Reuse a project template | Make a separate, clearly named copy | Accidentally altering the original reference |

### Protect sensitive information

Notes can become repositories for private information because writing them is easy. A password, verification code, private medical detail, financial account number, confidential student record, or customer information may be exposed if stored in an ordinary note or shared list. For a learning exercise, use **invented examples only**. For real sensitive material, follow the organization’s approved information-handling process and use appropriate protected systems. A regular note app is not automatically a password manager, secure vault, confidential case-management system, or authorized institutional record repository.

[[access-control|Access control]] determines who may view or change information. Some note tools allow [[sharing|sharing]] or [[collaboration|collaboration]] through links, invitations, or shared lists. A link may grant view-only or editing access depending on settings; it may also be restricted to an account or organization. Before sharing anything, verify the content, intended recipients, access rights, and whether other people can add, edit, or delete items. A shared checklist can change unexpectedly when multiple people have editing access. Students should **not create or share real notes** as a requirement of this lesson.

### Account boundaries and public computers

On a shared or public computer, do not enter personal secrets into practice material. If you sign in to an authorized service, follow the institution's instructions for signing out and ending the session. Closing a browser tab does not necessarily sign out of the service. Some apps display recent notes, task titles, or notification previews even when the full content is not open. A note title such as “Private financial information” can disclose more than intended in a sidebar or screen share. Choose fictional training topics when demonstrating any interface in front of a group.

### Screen sharing, screenshots, and copying text

Earlier Everyday Productivity lessons showed how to take screenshots and copy text. Those skills connect here: a screenshot may show a task list for a demonstration, and copying may help transfer a permitted instruction into your notes. But screenshots can capture names, accounts, notifications, background tabs, or sensitive document titles. Copying a note may carry more information than the few lines you intended. Use a fictional practice workspace and inspect the exact content before sharing. Never assume a minimized window, collapsed list, or hidden tab is inaccessible to a full-screen capture.

### Accessibility and alternative workflows

[[accessibility|Accessibility]] concerns whether people with different visual, hearing, motor, cognitive, or other access needs can use a process. Notes and tasks should be readable and navigable without reliance on color alone. A note named `Important` in red may be difficult to interpret if the color is unavailable; a written label such as `Priority: must do` is clearer. Keep headings concise and tasks consistent. Many apps support keyboard navigation, screen readers, dictation, or adjustable text sizes, but these features differ. Test any chosen workflow with the actual tools available to the learner.

A [[screen-reader|screen reader]] may announce headings, buttons, list items, and checkbox states in an accessible application. A plain-text `[x]` marker is just characters; a properly implemented interactive checkbox may expose its checked state to assistive technology. For a student who prefers paper or voice notes, the same educational goals can be demonstrated without requiring a particular screen control. Voice capture may help some users, but check transcription accuracy and obtain permission if recording other people.

### Notifications should support work, not control it

A [[notification|notification]] can help recall a scheduled action, but too many alerts become noise. Use reminders for time-sensitive items, not every thought. Consider notification privacy: a reminder preview could appear on a locked screen or shared display. If alerts do not arrive, check device settings, app permissions, focus modes, battery restrictions, and the chosen account before concluding the task was deleted. Always distinguish **a missed notification** from **an absent task**; the record may still exist even when the alert did not appear.

### A five-minute end-of-session check

Before leaving a work session, ask: **Did I record the important facts? Are the next actions clear? Is the note saved in the right place? Are finished tasks accurately marked? Did I avoid including private information? Can I reopen the note later?** If the answers are yes, you have built a small but dependable workflow. Complexity is optional. Consistency and verifiability are the real measures of effectiveness.

### Mini case study: a fictional community workshop

Suppose a small class is preparing a five-minute public workshop. A learner has one reference note containing the session length, approved public sources, and required handout format. A second file holds tasks such as drafting the outline, checking sources, and printing the handout. One item is marked `Waiting for review` because permission to publish has not yet been given. Each morning the learner selects the next available action. After an authorized reviewer approves the materials, the learner records that fact and proceeds. The system is simple enough to use from paper, Notepad, or a suitable app, and it protects against mistakenly treating a proposed publication as approved.

## 7. Guided practice: build notes and a task list

These six exercises progress from recognizing information types to managing a small fictional project. Use paper or a simple text editor; no app installation or account is needed. Students who choose a digital editor should save their files in an authorized practice folder and reopen at least one file to verify storage. **Do not submit screenshots of personal accounts, real reminders, employer records, family information, passwords, or any live private lists.** Each activity includes a solution or model response in an expandable answer disclosure.

### Activity 1: Classify information and actions

**Goal:** Distinguish notes, tasks, reminders, and scheduled events.

**Fictional scenario:** A class planning sheet contains these statements: (A) “The practice lecture is fifteen minutes”; (B) “Write a two-sentence introduction”; (C) “The classroom projector has an HDMI input”; (D) “Check the class portal tomorrow for any schedule change”; (E) “The workshop starts at 2 p.m. on the stated date”; (F) “Set an optional prompt to reread the outline the day before.”

**Steps:**

1. Write each letter under Note, Task, Calendar event, or Reminder.
1. Explain why a scheduled event is not identical to a task with a due date.
1. Choose one reference fact that might later create a task.
1. State how you would verify a fact that might have changed.

**Success standard:** The learner distinguishes information, action, prompt, and scheduled time without assuming that every record is interchangeable.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model classification
**A** and **C** are reference notes: they preserve factual constraints. **B** and **D** are tasks: writing and checking are actions. **E** is a scheduled event or event detail; it specifies when the workshop occurs. **F** is a suggestion to create a reminder, and it may also produce a setup task. A projector's HDMI input could generate the task “Verify an authorized device and cable are compatible.” The portal must be checked for current schedule information rather than treating the practice sheet as permanently correct. A due date describes when an action must be finished; an event identifies a time when something is scheduled to occur.
:::

### Activity 2: Turn rough capture into a clear note

**Goal:** Preserve reference information, uncertainty, and next actions.

**Fictional raw capture:** `climate topic three points, diagram allowed? public sources 2, get outline checked, maybe Fri`.

**Steps:**

1. Write a descriptive title.
1. Rewrite the information as clear, short statements.
1. Identify which details remain uncertain.
1. Create two or more potential action items beneath a separate heading.
1. Add the place where official assignment requirements should be checked.

**Success standard:** A reader who did not hear the original conversation can tell which facts are confirmed, which are uncertain, and what to do next.

:::answer Show Activity 2 example note
**Title:** Climate presentation — practice assignment.

**Information captured:** Prepare three main points about a public climate topic. Use two public sources. The raw note suggests a diagram may be permitted, but that is **not confirmed**. Friday appears to be a **possible**, not verified, deadline.

**Next actions:** Check the official assignment for the deadline and diagram rule; select a climate topic; locate two appropriate public sources; draft three outline points; request authorized review of the outline.

**Source to verify:** Current assignment sheet or classroom portal. The model does not invent permission or a submission date.
:::

### Activity 3: Save and reopen a local note

**Goal:** Demonstrate that visible text is saved in a known location.

**Steps:**

1. Open Notepad on Windows or another authorized text editor on your device. Paper learners may label a page in a practice notebook.
1. Type the heading `Fictional science workshop` and two factual statements about a made-up assignment.
1. Add `Next action: Write a three-point outline.`
1. Save as `science-workshop-practice.txt` in a permitted practice folder, or clearly label the paper page and storage place.
1. Locate the saved file independently through the folder and reopen it.
1. Check whether the words match what you intended to save. If not, correct the correct file and save again.

**Success standard:** The learner can state the filename, storage location, and evidence that the file can be retrieved. The exercise must not rely on an unsaved tab or editor window alone.

:::answer Show Activity 3 verification checklist
A satisfactory result has a descriptive title, at least two fictional facts, a separate next action, and a known saved location. On Windows, the learner can navigate to the Practice Notes folder and reopen `science-workshop-practice.txt` in the text editor. A student may use an equivalent file manager on another system. If the editor restores unsaved text after reopening, that is **not** the same as verifying the named file. For paper, the learner can state where the labeled sheet is stored and retrieve it. No real personal content is required.
:::

### Activity 4: Rewrite vague tasks and identify dependencies

**Goal:** Turn a project into actions with meaningful completion tests.

**Fictional project:** `Make a classroom poster`.

**Initial list:** `Be creative`; `Poster`; `Make it professional`; `Get permission`; `Put it up`.

**Steps:**

1. Rewrite at least four entries so that each begins with a concrete action verb.
1. Identify which action must wait for approval.
1. Explain how you would tell when each task is complete.
1. Put the tasks into a practical order and mark the blocked action appropriately.
1. State why “Get permission” is not finished when a request has merely been drafted.

**Success standard:** The revised list supports execution and does not imply permission exists before it has actually been granted.

:::answer Show Activity 4 example task breakdown
1. **Write** a five-word title for the fictional poster.
2. **Draft** three short educational points.
3. **Choose** an approved public image or prepare an original diagram.
4. **Check** spelling and content against the assignment instructions.
5. **Submit** the draft for authorized review if the class requires it.
6. **Wait** for actual approval before printing or displaying the poster.
7. **Print or display** the approved poster only where permission has been granted.

The printing/display step is blocked until approval. Drafting the request is distinct from sending it; sending it is distinct from receiving approval. Each action has an observable result, such as a written title, checked draft, or confirmed review decision.
:::

### Activity 5: Choose notes, tasks, and reminders across devices

**Goal:** Select an appropriate tool without requiring a particular account.

**Fictional situations:** (A) A Windows learner wants to keep three paragraphs of workshop instructions offline. (B) An iPhone learner wants a personal reminder to review a fictional outline tomorrow. (C) A Chromebook learner needs a short checklist, but their school has not authorized an online account for the exercise. (D) A Mac learner wants to preserve research context and separately track four actions.

**Steps:**

1. Choose one appropriate solution for each situation.
1. State what requires an app feature or permission beyond plain text.
1. Explain why a note saved locally does not necessarily appear on a phone.
1. Describe a fallback if a school-managed device blocks a suggested service.
1. Identify one privacy precaution before sharing or demonstrating the chosen list.

**Success standard:** The learner chooses by task and permission, not by brand loyalty, and recognizes that synchronization and notifications must be verified.

:::answer Show Activity 5 device choices
**A:** Use Notepad and save a `.txt` file to an allowed local folder, then reopen it. **B:** Use an available Reminders-type app or an approved calendar/reminder tool, checking the actual alert configuration; a paper reminder may be used when electronics are not appropriate. **C:** Use a local text editor or paper checklist; do not create an unauthorized account. **D:** Use a Notes-type app or saved text document for the research and a Reminders-type app or plain checklist for actions. None of these choices guarantees cross-device synchronization. For demonstrations, use fictional content and inspect the visible title, notifications, and account before sharing.
:::

### Activity 6: Capstone — capture, plan, update, and review

**Goal:** Complete the full workflow using a fictional small project.

**Fictional brief:** “Prepare a five-minute science mini-lesson. It must have a title, three main points, and two public educational sources. The instructor must approve it before it can be displayed. The practice target is next week, but the exact day is not specified.”

**Steps:**

1. Create a reference note with the known requirements and clearly label the unspecified day as unconfirmed.
1. Write a list of five or more concrete actions, starting with the next useful step.
1. Add a dependency indicating that public display is blocked until approval.
1. Choose two feasible actions for a hypothetical thirty-minute work session.
1. Mark one action done only after describing evidence of its completion; leave an unfinished action unmarked.
1. Save or label the note and list so they can be found again. Describe how you would review them tomorrow.
1. Explain how the process would change if the instructor revised the requirements.

**Success standard:** The learner maintains an understandable reference note, an executable list, an honest completion status, a pending dependency, and a retrieval plan.

:::answer Show Activity 6 capstone model
**Reference note:** `Science mini-lesson — fictional brief`. Requirements: five-minute explanation; title; three main points; two public educational sources; approval before display. Exact due day: **not confirmed**. Source: fictional assignment brief; check current instructor instructions.

**Task list:** (1) Choose a public science topic. (2) Draft a title. (3) Write three main points. (4) Find two relevant public educational sources and record their titles. (5) Prepare a five-minute outline. (6) Check the outline against the requirements. (7) Request authorized instructor approval. (8) Display only after approval.

**Thirty-minute plan:** Choose the topic and draft the title, then start the three main points if time remains. Mark the title task completed only once the title has actually been written. Keep source-finding uncompleted until two sources have been identified and recorded. Display remains **blocked** while approval is pending. Save the note and list to an identifiable practice folder or labeled paper location. Review tomorrow by checking the latest instructions, outstanding actions, and whether any approval or deadline has changed.
:::

### Practice record and instructor guidance

An instructor may assess each activity using a short written response instead of a live application demonstration. The evidence is the student's ability to **classify**, **explain**, **save or organize**, **verify**, and **revise**. A valid substitute for a technical demonstration is a labeled paper note with an explicit storage/retrieval procedure. Never require students to disclose a personal task list, screenshot a signed-in account, turn on cloud synchronization, or invite a real collaborator simply to prove proficiency.

### Exit practice: explain the workflow

Without looking at the examples, explain the following in your own words: What belongs in a reference note? What makes a task actionable? Why should you verify the saved file location? How is a reminder different from a due date? What does it mean for a task to be waiting? What is one way to keep a note private? How would you recover a note from yesterday? Why should you not check off a task before its actual result is confirmed?

## 8. Common mistakes, troubleshooting, and privacy

### “A note and a task are exactly the same thing.”

A note keeps information; a task identifies an action. One written record may contain both, but the distinction matters. If a note says a document needs three references, create a task to locate or verify those references if action is required. Otherwise, the requirement may be remembered without anything being done.

### “If the words are visible on my screen, they are saved.”

An app can show text that exists only in a temporary working view or unsaved session. Use the app's appropriate save process, check the destination, and reopen the file or note. Some apps autosave, but its scope and conditions must be understood rather than assumed.

### “I should record every detail exactly as it was said.”

A complete transcript is not always necessary or permitted. For most ordinary notes, a concise summary, significant requirements, decisions, questions, and sources are more usable. If exact wording is necessary, quote accurately and observe permission, attribution, and confidentiality rules.

### “My list is better when it contains every possible future goal.”

An enormous daily list can hide the next available action. Keep broad projects separately, make actionable tasks, and choose only a manageable number for the available time. Moving something to a future list is not the same as pretending it is complete.

### “Priority means due tomorrow.”

Importance and urgency are different dimensions. A task can be important over the long term but not urgent today. Another task can be approaching a deadline but still less significant. Use realistic labels rather than automatically treating every item as critical.

### “A due date guarantees a notification.”

Many apps allow deadlines without timed alerts or may require separate notification permissions. Check the actual reminder settings. An institutional deadline comes from its governing instructions, not from the app's chosen default alert hour.

### “Checking the box makes the action happen.”

A checkbox stores a status. It cannot itself print a document, obtain approval, or verify external submission. Mark complete only after the intended result exists. A task left as `Waiting for approval` is not a failed task; it describes an unresolved dependency.

### “A sticky note is automatically private because it is small.”

A sticky note can be visible on a desktop, in a task switcher, or during screen sharing. Sensitive titles and contents can be exposed. Follow approved storage and sharing rules; use invented classroom content for practice.

### “A cloud-synced list is a separate backup of everything.”

Synchronization can transmit deletions and mistakes across devices. Reliable backups require their own recoverable-copy and restoration approach. This lesson does not require cloud setup; the following course lesson explores cloud storage more fully.

### “A list shared with one person must be read-only.”

Permissions vary by service and configuration. Sharing may allow editing or further invitations. Verify recipients and effective access rights before sharing. Do not use real group records in a classroom demonstration without permission.

### “All note applications store the same features and data.”

Apps differ in offline access, formatting, attachments, collaboration, reminders, search, and export options. Moving from one app to another can omit important details. Verify an actual destination and backup before deleting the source.

### “A note created on my laptop should be on my phone automatically.”

A local file does not automatically become a synchronized record. It may require a supported cloud service and matching authorized account. Do not troubleshoot a local file as though it were inherently a cloud note.

### “If a reminder does not appear, the task was deleted.”

An alert can fail because of settings, notification permissions, device state, time zone, focus modes, or connectivity. Open the app and check whether the record still exists before recreating it and producing duplicates.

### “If I search a title and find a match, it must be the right note.”

Similar filenames or note titles can appear in several locations. Confirm context, content, path, and date where relevant. Do not overwrite an older version until you have identified what each file contains.

### “I can use my own passwords in a note-taking exercise.”

This lesson never requires real secrets or sensitive records. Use fictional facts. In real use, credentials and highly sensitive institutional data belong in appropriate approved protected systems, not an ordinary class note.

### “I must install the same app as my instructor to learn the skill.”

The objectives are conceptual and practical: capture, distinguish actions, save, retrieve, prioritize, and review. Paper and authorized local editors can demonstrate those skills. App-specific features are optional illustrations, not mandatory prerequisites.

### “I can rely forever on an app without checking whether it is changing.”

Software products can change names, workflows, availability, or migration rules. For example, Microsoft has announced a retirement timetable for the To Do apps. Keep essential work portable where policy permits and verify current vendor guidance before committing important long-term records to a single tool.

### Quick troubleshooting table

| Problem | Likely explanation to check | Safe next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The note cannot be found | Different folder or account | Search known permitted locations; inspect full path |
| The note reopened without recent edits | Unsaved changes or another file copy | Compare locations before saving or deleting |
| A checklist item will not check itself | Plain text is not an interactive control | Type an explicit `[x]` or use a supported checklist |
| Reminder never appeared | Alert settings, time zone, permissions, or device state | Inspect the task and its notification configuration |
| Task appears twice | Entered in two apps or synchronized duplicates | Identify the authoritative copy before removing one |
| The list is too long | Projects and tasks are mixed together | Break one project into next actions; schedule reviews |
| A task cannot proceed | Waiting for permission, materials, or decision | Mark waiting and record the actual blocker |
| The note appears on another person's screen | Shared account, permissions, or visible window | Stop sharing and follow approved privacy procedures |
| Links open but information differs | Source was updated or note is outdated | Consult the current authoritative source |
| A device offers different controls | App version, platform, policy, or account differs | Use the visible help controls and equivalent workflow |

### A dependable final checklist

Ask yourself whether the note contains enough context, the task list contains actions rather than wishes, saved information is in a known place, deadlines are verified, completion marks are honest, privacy has been considered, and the system can be reviewed tomorrow. If you can answer those questions, you have the foundation for dependable personal productivity without requiring elaborate software or complicated planning theories.


## 9. Essential vocabulary

The first time a term appears in key instructional passages, you may see a dotted-underlined phrase. In the MGU lesson dashboard, selecting that phrase displays a short explanation. The term registry at the end of this file supports those popovers. The list here is readable without them, so accessibility does not depend on a popup or a specific pointer device.

- **Note:** Information kept for reference.
- **Task:** An action with a recognizable completion result.
- **To-do list:** A collection of actions to perform.
- **Checklist:** A list with checkable items, often for verification.
- **Reminder:** A configured prompt for an action or event.
- **Calendar event:** An activity associated with a scheduled period.
- **Project:** An outcome requiring more than one action.
- **Subtask:** A smaller action within a larger task.
- **Action item:** A specific next step to be performed.
- **Completion status:** A record of whether an action has been finished.
- **Due date:** The date by which work should be finished.
- **Start date:** A planned date to begin work.
- **Priority:** Relative importance of work.
- **Urgency:** How soon an action needs attention.
- **Dependency:** A condition that must be satisfied before an action can proceed.
- **Waiting:** Status for an item blocked by an unresolved condition.
- **Daily review:** Short check of work and next actions for the day.
- **Weekly review:** Periodic cleanup and replanning of lists and notes.
- **Capture:** Initial recording of useful information.
- **Review:** Revisiting information for correction and next steps.
- **Reference:** Information identifying a source for later checking.
- **Source:** Origin of information that can be consulted or verified.
- **Title:** Descriptive name helping identify a note or file.
- **Filename:** Name identifying a file.
- **Folder:** A file-system location used to organize stored items.
- **File path:** Location of a file within a file system.
- **Notepad:** Windows text editor useful for straightforward notes.
- **Plain text:** Text without document-specific rich formatting.
- **Sticky note:** Short note designed for quick access or display.
- **Autosave:** Application feature intended to store changes automatically.
- **Session restoration:** Reopening past working views, not necessarily proof of a verified file.
- **Retrieve:** Locate and reopen previously stored information.
- **Local storage:** Data kept on a particular device or local storage system.
- **Cloud notes:** Notes held through an online service and account.
- **Synchronization:** Coordinating data among devices or services.
- **Backup:** Separate recoverable copy for restoration after loss.
- **Sharing:** Providing others with some form of access to information.
- **Collaboration:** Multiple authorized people working with shared material.
- **Access control:** Rules and permissions defining who can view or edit information.
- **Notification:** Message or alert shown by a device or app.
- **Accessibility:** Design and use of tools for people with different access needs.
- **Screen reader:** Technology that speaks or otherwise presents interface content.
- **Capture location:** One known place where incoming information is recorded.
- **Organization:** Consistent arrangement of information to support retrieval and use.
- **One-time task:** Task expected to be completed once.
- **Recurring task:** Task repeated at an interval or when a condition arises.
- **Task description:** Wording that identifies an action and its expected outcome.
- **Unconfirmed detail:** Information not yet verified by a responsible source.
- **Decision:** Chosen course of action or established conclusion.
- **Observation:** Recorded fact or experience, without implying approval.
- **Checklist marker:** Symbol indicating task state in text or an application.
- **Task list:** Structured record of outstanding and completed actions.
- **Working copy:** Editable version of a note or document.
- **Save As:** Command used in many apps to choose a new name or location.
- **Paper fallback:** Equivalent non-digital method used if software is unavailable.
- **Account:** Identity or sign-in context that may control access to notes.
- **Export:** Copying information out of a service in a supported format.
- **Migration:** Moving content into a new app or system, possibly with loss of features.
- **Retention:** Rules for preserving records for an appropriate period.
- **Source of truth:** Agreed authoritative place to maintain current information.
- **Permission:** Authorization to perform an action or access data.
- **Privacy:** Appropriate control over personal or restricted information.
- **Screen sharing:** Displaying all or part of a device screen to another person.
- **Alert permission:** Setting controlling whether an app may display notifications.
- **Offline note:** Note accessible when a network connection is unavailable.
- **Verified completion:** Status supported by observing the intended result.
- **Task estimate:** Approximate amount of effort or time needed for a task.
- **Task history:** Record of past completion or changes when supported.
- **Review routine:** Repeated process of checking notes and tasks.

The central vocabulary distinction is **note = what you need to remember**, **task = what you need to do**, **reminder = when you want a prompt**, and **calendar event = when an activity is scheduled**. A single project may use all four.
