Basic Computer Skills / Digital Safety and Scam Awareness

## Lesson 9: What to Do If Something Goes Wrong

A learner receives a frightening email claiming that an account was hacked. A second learner notices a payment they did not authorize. Someone else installed a program after a stranger offered technical support. Another person has simply opened a suspicious webpage and worries that the entire computer is ruined. These situations may feel similar because they are alarming, but **they call for different first steps**. Responding correctly begins by identifying what actually happened, not by assuming the worst or following instructions from the suspicious source.

A [[security-incident|security incident]] is an event that may threaten information, an account, a device, money, or a person's safety. Some incidents are confirmed; others are merely suspected. [[incident-response|Incident response]] is the organized process of identifying the problem, reducing further harm, contacting the proper people, recovering access or information, and checking that the solution worked. Anyone can learn a sensible first response without becoming a cybersecurity specialist.

This is **Lesson 9**, the final lesson in MGU's nine-part **Digital Safety and Scam Awareness** course. Lessons 1–8 taught password safety, two-factor authentication, phishing recognition, suspicious downloads, privacy settings, safe shopping, personal-information protection, and device security. Here you combine those skills into a practical response plan. Windows 11 and common online services provide examples, but the decision principles also apply to Apple, Android, Chromebook, bank accounts, and other devices or services. Real recovery screens, menu names, provider policies, and legal remedies can change.

**Practice safety:** All six guided activities use imaginary people and fictional incidents. Do not intentionally open a malicious message, click a suspicious link, install a risky program, send money, reveal credentials, contact a suspected scammer, reset a real device, or create a false fraud report for class. Do not paste real incident evidence containing personal data into classroom materials. You may describe what *should* happen, consult official help pages, and work on paper. For a real active incident, prioritize the correct provider, employer, financial institution, or emergency service over completing the lesson.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 120–155 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Digital Safety and Scam Awareness Lessons 1–8 recommended · **Practice equipment:** This lesson and paper or a blank document; no live incidents or sensitive data required · **Safety:** Use only imaginary names, accounts, messages, and transactions in exercises.

> **Essential idea:** **STOP → IDENTIFY → CONTAIN → CONTACT → RECOVER → VERIFY.** Stop risky interaction; identify what actually occurred; contain the possible damage without making unsupported changes; contact the appropriate trusted organization; recover through official procedures; and verify that access, payments, devices, and records are stable. Preserve useful evidence safely throughout.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish a warning, suspected incident, and confirmed problem without assuming that every alert proves a compromise.
1. Choose safe first steps after viewing a suspicious page, entering a password, approving a login, downloading a file, or installing software.
1. Describe how to recover an account through official channels and check for persistence such as unknown sessions, recovery details, or email-forwarding rules.
1. Explain how to report an unauthorized charge, an authorized payment induced by fraud, and possible identity theft to the appropriate organizations.
1. Identify when to seek official technical help, preserve evidence, or escalate risks involving threats, children, work systems, or physical safety.
1. Organize follow-up actions, document case details, and recognize fake recovery offers.
1. Work through six fictional incident scenarios and a 30-question self-study assessment without interacting with real scams.

## 1. How do you recognize and classify a problem?

### 1.1 Start with the observed event, not with the message's claim

A [[claim|claim]] is something a message, caller, advertisement, or webpage says is true. An [[observation|observation]] is something you can independently establish. For example, “Your computer is infected with 27 viruses” is a claim if it appears inside an unknown webpage. “I installed an unfamiliar program and gave it permission to control my computer” describes an action that actually happened. The second statement calls for a different response because an untrusted program may have obtained access.

A [[false-alarm|false alarm]] can occur when someone mistakes a routine system alert for an attack or believes a deceptive webpage. A genuine notification can also be missed or misunderstood. Instead of guessing, ask: *What exactly did I receive, view, enter, click, approve, download, install, share, pay, or lose?* A sensible response matches that event.

| Event you can describe | What it establishes | Appropriate initial direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An unexpected message arrived but was not opened or acted on | A suspicious communication exists | Avoid interacting; verify or report through trusted channels |
| A suspicious page appeared in the browser | The browser displayed untrusted content | Leave it safely; do not assume installation or theft without evidence |
| A password was entered into an unverified site | A credential may have been disclosed | Use a trusted device and the official service to secure the account |
| A one-time code or approval was shared | An additional authentication control may have been bypassed | Check the genuine account promptly; secure sessions and recovery details |
| An unknown program was installed or remote control allowed | A device may have been given unwanted access | Stop further access and use trusted security or IT procedures |
| An unfamiliar charge appears on an account | There may be unauthorized financial activity or an error | Contact the real financial institution quickly |
| Identity documents or an SSN may be exposed | Identity misuse may be possible, even if unconfirmed | Assess the exposure and follow IdentityTheft.gov guidance |
| A device is missing | Hardware, data, and account sessions may be at risk | Use official device/account recovery features and report theft as appropriate |

Notice the difference between **exposure**, **suspicion**, and **confirmed harm**. Receiving an email does not prove an account takeover. An account warning does not automatically prove a scam. Likewise, being able to sign in does not by itself prove an account is safe; an intruder might have added another sign-in method or left an unauthorized session active.

### 1.2 Five questions establish the scope

A short [[triage|triage]] is an early assessment that helps determine urgency and the next responsible action. You do not need a detailed technical investigation to begin.

1. **What happened?** State observed actions without adding unverified assumptions.
1. **What is involved?** Identify a device, account, payment method, file, personal record, or someone else's information.
1. **Is the threat still active?** A stranger may still be connected remotely; an unauthorized transaction may still be pending; or a fake alert may already be closed.
1. **Who can stop the harm?** Identify the account provider, bank, device owner, school or workplace IT team, or emergency authority.
1. **What must be preserved?** Note relevant dates, transaction IDs, messages, screenshots, and case numbers while avoiding additional exposure.

A learner who answers only three questions can still make progress. If a situation poses immediate physical danger, direct safety support takes precedence. If a payment or bank transfer is underway, contacting the financial institution quickly may be more important than taking a perfect screenshot.

### 1.3 Risk depends on what was exposed and what was authorized

A [[credential|credential]] such as a password allows someone to attempt account access. A [[session|session]] is a period in which a service remembers a signed-in user. [[sensitive-information|Sensitive information]] may include account numbers, identity documents, medical details, tax records, or other data that can cause harm if misused. An [[authorization|authorization]] is permission that may enable a payment, account change, software installation, or access request.

The response varies with the data and action. If you entered a reused password on a counterfeit page, other accounts sharing that password need attention. If a stranger obtained remote access to a computer, a password change performed *on that same possibly compromised computer* may not be sufficient. If you authorized a payment because a scammer lied about a purchase, that may be treated differently by providers and legal rules than a payment you never authorized. In all cases, describe the sequence accurately to the organization handling the claim.

### 1.4 Use an urgency ladder rather than a single panic response

| Priority | Illustrative conditions | Sensible response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Immediate personal safety | Threat of violence, coercion, or a dangerous physical situation | Move toward safety and contact emergency services or trusted support as appropriate |
| Urgent financial or active access | Ongoing remote access, unknown money movement, or confirmed account hijack | Stop the untrusted interaction and reach the relevant trusted provider immediately |
| Prompt protection | Password disclosed, identity number exposed, stolen phone, suspicious installed app | Secure affected accounts or devices and use the service's recovery and reporting channels |
| Verify before acting | Unexpected security email, dubious pop-up, doubtful delivery message | Do not use supplied links; check the real account or provider independently |
| Routine follow-up | Resolved warning, completed scan, corrected account setting | Document outcomes, review protections, and confirm nothing remains open |

These are teaching categories, not universal legal deadlines. **Financial dispute and notice deadlines differ by payment type and circumstances**, so do not postpone contacting the issuer while attempting to diagnose the incident alone.

### 1.5 Worked example: the same message, three different outcomes

An email says: “Your school account is locked. Click this link now.” In **Outcome A**, a learner deletes the email without clicking. They can use the normal school portal to check whether any account action is actually required. In **Outcome B**, the learner enters a password into the email's webpage; the real account may now be at risk, so the learner should secure it through the known official portal and ask authorized school IT for support. In **Outcome C**, the learner also approves an unexpected sign-in prompt; an attacker may have gained another route past sign-in protections, so the response should include reviewing activity, sessions, and registered authentication methods.

The wording of the email is identical. The recovery plan changes because the **observed actions** change.

## 2. What should you do in the first minutes?

### 2.1 STOP: stop the suspicious interaction

Do not follow more links from an unexpected message, call a telephone number displayed by an alarming pop-up, answer an unknown support caller, continue a requested transfer, or type a password into an unfamiliar page. If the situation involves a scammer speaking with you, end the conversation. If a legitimate service really needs action, you can reach it using a bookmarked address, a known app, a previously verified number, or an official website obtained independently.

A [[trusted-channel|trusted channel]] is a way of contacting an organization that you have established separately from the questionable request. The logo, sender name, or number printed in the suspicious message is not an independent channel. On a work device, the proper route may be the organization's known IT or security contact rather than a public support number.

### 2.2 IDENTIFY: write down what actually occurred

Create a short [[incident-log|incident log]] on paper or an authorized secure device. A useful entry records the date/time, affected service or device, observed action, whether money or information was involved, the support contact used, and any reference number. Prefer summaries to copies of sensitive contents. Never place actual passwords, recovery codes, full payment-card numbers, government-ID numbers, or account secrets in a shared worksheet.

| Log field | Fictional sample | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Date and approximate time | Friday, 10:15 a.m. | Establishes sequence and contact timing |
| Observed action | Entered password on an unexpected sign-in page | Distinguishes exposure from mere receipt |
| Account or device type | Personal email account | Identifies the responsible provider |
| Sensitive activity | No payment; login details possibly exposed | Defines the immediate risk |
| Trusted response | Used saved provider bookmark; changed password | Records the action without storing the secret |
| Provider case number | REF-EXAMPLE-204 | Supports a later follow-up if needed |
| Remaining uncertainty | Unknown whether a second session is active | Directs the next verification step |

The sample number above is imaginary. Do not include a live case number in coursework. If preserving a screenshot, consider whether it contains private addresses, identification numbers, passwords, or another person's information. In a workplace, use the approved incident-reporting system and follow retention policies.

### 2.3 CONTAIN: reduce further harm without erasing the facts

[[containment|Containment]] means limiting the spread or continuation of an incident. Examples include ending a remote-support session, disconnecting a potentially compromised personal computer from the network when safe and appropriate, revoking suspicious account sessions, and asking a card issuer to block further charges. Containment is not the same as randomly deleting files, performing a factory reset, or installing an unfamiliar "repair" application.

When a stranger may still control a personal computer, **stop interacting with the remote session**. If safe and feasible, disconnect the computer from Wi-Fi or network access, then seek help from trusted support. A connected workplace system may be part of a managed network: contact authorized IT/security promptly and follow its instructions rather than independently wiping, rebooting, or investigating it. Disconnecting a device may not solve every problem or be appropriate in every managed environment, but it can limit an active unauthorized connection on a personal device.

### 2.4 CONTACT: use the organization that can actually act

The right first recipient depends on the event. A bank can restrict cards or investigate transfers; an email provider can assist account recovery; employer IT can investigate a managed laptop; a device manufacturer can explain official recovery tools. The FTC and FBI collect reports useful for identifying fraud and cybercrime, but **filing a government report does not replace contacting the bank or service that controls the affected account**.

| Incident | Primary trusted contact | Possible additional help |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Password or account compromised | The account provider's official security/recovery process | Employer or school IT for managed accounts |
| Unauthorized payment or charge | Bank, card issuer, payment service, or transfer provider | FTC and FBI IC3 reporting when appropriate |
| Identity theft or misuse | Relevant affected businesses and IdentityTheft.gov | Credit bureaus and law enforcement as advised |
| Suspected malware on personal device | Built-in security software or trusted manufacturer/technician | Account providers if credentials may be affected |
| Malicious activity on managed device | Employer, school, or institution's official IT/security team | Any other recipients required by policy |
| Threat or immediate danger | Emergency services or appropriate safety authorities | Trusted family member, guardian, or support organization |

### 2.5 RECOVER and VERIFY: restore control, then check it stayed restored

[[recovery|Recovery]] can mean regaining an account, replacing a compromised credential, removing malicious software, recovering files from backup, resolving a payment dispute, or restoring an inaccessible device. [[verification|Verification]] means checking that the intended result actually happened. A password-change confirmation might be followed by reviewing unknown sessions and recovery contacts. A refund request is not a refund until the financial institution confirms it and the account records reflect the outcome. A security scan may find nothing, but absence of a detection is not a complete guarantee of safety.

**Worked example:** A consumer receives a text saying a payment app was compromised. They do not click the link. Instead they open the known payment app and see no unusual transactions or sign-in notices. They may document the suspicious message and report it through the service's official channel. There is no reason to give the text sender a code or install software simply to "check." If the account instead showed an unknown transfer, they would contact the service and associated financial institution promptly.

## 3. How do you respond to a compromised account?

### 3.1 Recognize account compromise and account-recovery scams

An [[account-takeover|account takeover]] occurs when an unauthorized person gains control of an online account. Signs may include password-reset emails you did not request, changed recovery contact information, unfamiliar login locations, unexpected purchases, messages sent without your knowledge, or inability to sign in. These signs merit checking, but some single notices may result from a typo or blocked attempted sign-in rather than a successful takeover.

A [[recovery-scam|recovery scam]] happens when someone pretends they can restore your account or recover lost money, often requesting a fee, code, remote access, or identity documents. Being worried or locked out can make these offers seem attractive. **Do not purchase a stranger's "guaranteed recovery" service or disclose a verification code to someone who contacts you unexpectedly.**

### 3.2 If you can still sign in, secure the real account

Use a trusted device and reach the service through a saved bookmark, official app, or independently found address. Depending on the provider's controls and the facts of the incident:

1. Change the affected account's password to a new, unique, strong one if the password may be disclosed.
1. Change the same compromised password anywhere else it was reused, using the real service for each account.
1. Turn on or strengthen [[multi-factor-authentication|multifactor authentication]]; do not approve unexpected requests.
1. Review recent sign-ins, connected devices, active [[session|sessions]], and account permissions; sign out unknown sessions or use "sign out everywhere" where available.
1. Inspect [[recovery-contact|recovery email and phone details]], registered passkeys, security keys, and authenticator methods; remove unfamiliar additions through official account settings.
1. Review any account-specific persistence, such as email [[forwarding-rule|forwarding rules]], filters, mailbox delegates, connected applications, automatic payments, or social-media posts and messages.
1. Secure the email address used to recover other accounts, especially if it is affected, and notify relevant contacts if fraudulent messages were sent from your account.

Not every provider offers all controls, and removing an unfamiliar entry without understanding its purpose can lock out an authorized user. If a service has a guided "secure account" process, follow it. On a work or school account, alert the administrator and cooperate with its recovery process rather than concealing the event.

### 3.3 If you cannot sign in, do not bypass the official recovery process

An [[account-recovery|account recovery]] procedure verifies your ownership under the provider's rules. Start from the legitimate app or website that you already recognize and find **Forgot password**, **Account recovery**, or the relevant support page. Do not trust a paid recovery advertisement or a social-media stranger asking for your one-time code. You may be asked to use a previously enrolled recovery method, identify past account information, or wait for a security review. The details vary, and recovery is not always guaranteed.

If the attacker changed your recovery address or phone number, explain this to the provider through its available account-restoration channel. Make a list of linked services that use the affected email address and assess whether they need protection. If the account is managed, an authorized administrator may be the only person able to restore it.

### 3.4 Distinguish a compromised password from a compromised device

A [[password-reset|password reset]] can help after a password has been exposed. It does not automatically remove spyware, end every session, undo a malicious email-forwarding rule, or recover a stolen device. If malware or unwanted remote access may still be present, use a **different trusted device** where possible when changing critical account passwords. Seek trusted help to assess the potentially affected computer before using it for banking or other sensitive work.

| Observed account problem | Immediate protection | Important follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Password typed on suspected phishing page | Change affected password through official route | Check reuse, MFA, and account activity |
| Unexpected MFA approval accepted | Review actual sign-in activity and secure account | Revoke unknown sessions or authentication methods |
| Unrecognized recovery email | Use official account-security controls | Confirm contact details and other linked accounts |
| Email sent messages you did not write | Secure access and review session history | Check forwarding/delegation and warn affected contacts |
| Locked out after suspicious change | Start provider's legitimate recovery procedure | Secure linked email, phone, and payments when relevant |
| Device itself may have spyware | Avoid sensitive account work on suspect device | Use trusted security/IT help and known-safe device |

### 3.5 Worked example: securing an email account after phishing

Rosa entered an email password on a convincing imitation of the email provider's sign-in page. She later notices a password-reset request for her shopping account. Rosa should not reply to the phishing site or assume the shopping account was definitely compromised. From a trusted device, she goes to the real email service, secures the email account, reviews sessions and recovery methods, and checks for unknown forwarding rules. She then reviews the shopping account using its legitimate website or app. If she reused the stolen password, she replaces it there and on any other account that shared it.

The key idea is to protect the **account used to recover other accounts**. If an attacker controls email, changing a shopping-site password alone may not be sufficient because password-reset messages might still reach the attacker.

## 4. How do you respond to malware or device trouble?

### 4.1 Differentiate seeing a page, receiving a file, and running software

[[malware|Malware]] is software designed or used to harm a device, steal information, disrupt normal operation, or enable unauthorized access. A [[download|download]] places data on a device; an [[installation|installation]] sets up software to run; [[execution|execution]] means a program or code actually runs. A browser can sometimes face threats even without a normal install, but **merely viewing an alarming webpage is not proof of infection**. A real infection can also be hard to detect. What matters is what happened and what trustworthy evidence is available.

| Event | What not to assume | Sensible first step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fake browser warning displayed | Every warning is a genuine operating-system diagnosis | Close or leave the page safely and inspect known security tools if needed |
| Suspicious attachment received | Receiving a file always means it executed | Do not open it; report or delete as authorized |
| Unknown file downloaded | It is safe because the download finished | Do not run it; review security warnings and source |
| Suspicious installer was run | One quick scan proves nothing happened | Stop sensitive activity and use trusted security help |
| Stranger gained remote control | Closing a support chat ends all possible access | End the session, consider disconnection, seek trusted assessment |
| Files suddenly encrypted with a ransom demand | Paying guarantees files or privacy are restored | Stop interacting with attackers and seek authorized expert help |

### 4.2 Respond proportionately to a possible infection

If you only saw a suspicious pop-up and did not interact with it, leave the page without calling its number or installing software. If you downloaded an unfamiliar file but did not run it, do not open it; consult your device's recognized protection interface, and follow its recommended handling. If you **ran** a suspicious program, installed unexpected software, granted administrator permissions, or permitted remote control, the risk is higher. Stop using the device for sensitive tasks and consult trusted security support. If an attacker still appears connected on a personal device, safely end access and disconnect from the network when appropriate.

For a personal **Windows 11** computer, the built-in **Windows Security** application can display antivirus status, protection history, and scan options. Open it independently through the Start menu rather than through a website warning. On other systems, use the legitimate operating-system security settings, app store, or manufacturer support. A trusted technician may need to determine whether software should be removed, a device restored from a known-good backup, or the operating system reinstalled. **Do not run commands copied from an unknown webpage, disable protections, or use a "cleaner" sold by the suspicious party.**

### 4.3 Consider what was accessible while the device was compromised

If a malicious program ran while passwords, financial websites, files, or email were accessible, assess which accounts and information may be affected. From a trusted device, secure high-priority accounts such as email, banking, and primary sign-in accounts as needed. Review whether payment cards or documents require further action. Avoid assuming that every file was stolen or that nothing was stolen. A security professional or service provider can help evaluate meaningful indicators.

If a computer belongs to an employer, school, public library, or other organization, use the institution's [[incident-reporting|incident-reporting]] process. Do not independently wipe its storage, remove logs, or install outside software; doing so could violate policy or interfere with analysis. In some managed environments, disconnecting without coordination can also disrupt essential services. Obtain directions from authorized staff.

### 4.4 If files were lost, decide whether restoration is possible

A [[backup|backup]] is a separately retained copy of data that may support recovery from loss or corruption. [[synchronization|Synchronization]] usually keeps working files aligned across devices or a cloud service, but unwanted edits or deletions may also synchronize. If files vanish or become unreadable, do not overwrite working storage unnecessarily. Check for a recently changed location, application history, trash/recycle bin, legitimate cloud version history, and a verified independent backup. Consider professional or authorized IT help for physical drive failure or suspected ransomware.

A [[ransomware|ransomware]] attack may encrypt data or involve threats to publish it. Paying a demand does not ensure restoration or deletion of stolen information; contact authorized security support and relevant law enforcement or reporting channels. If the issue is only a mistaken deletion, restoring a version from a legitimate backup may be more appropriate than a security incident response. Diagnose first.

### 4.5 A lost or stolen device creates two separate problems

A missing phone or computer may require replacing hardware **and** protecting accounts or stored data. Use official device-location or account controls through a known trusted login. Options may include marking a device lost, locking it, changing key account credentials, contacting a mobile carrier, or remotely erasing it in serious cases. **Remote erasure is often irreversible and can affect recovery of files**, so check device ownership, backup, and the implications before using it. Do not confront someone based solely on a map location. Report theft through appropriate local channels and the employer or school if the device belongs to them.

**Worked example:** A person misplaces an unlocked phone that contains an active banking application. They should use the phone platform's official lost-device procedure and contact their mobile carrier or financial provider when there is a meaningful account risk. They should not install an unknown location-tracking app on another device or share their account sign-in with a stranger promising to find the phone.

## 5. How do you respond to financial fraud or identity exposure?

### 5.1 Distinguish an unauthorized transaction from a deceptive authorized payment

An [[unauthorized-transaction|unauthorized transaction]] is activity made without the account holder's permission under the relevant provider and legal definitions. A [[scam-induced-payment|scam-induced payment]] occurs when a person authorizes a transfer after being deceived. Both deserve prompt reporting, but protections and reimbursement obligations are **not automatically identical**. The payment rail, account type, facts, provider rules, and applicable law matter.

An [[account-dispute|account dispute]] is a request for a financial institution or merchant to investigate a charge, transfer, or transaction. Contact the issuer, bank, payment service, or transfer provider through the real app, known website, or verified number **as soon as possible**. Explain accurately what happened and ask what steps are available to prevent additional loss or attempt recovery. Do not mischaracterize a payment you authorized as an unauthorized one; accurate reporting helps the institution apply the relevant process.

| Payment or loss | Primary action | What to remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unknown credit-card charge | Contact card issuer and dispute unauthorized activity | Monitor replacement card/account and case updates |
| Unknown debit or bank transfer | Notify bank or credit union immediately | Timing can affect rights; follow bank's fraud instructions |
| Fraudulent payment through an app | Contact the app and funding bank/card promptly | Ask about reversal and available protections |
| Money wired after deception | Contact sending institution or wire company urgently | Ask about recall; success is not guaranteed |
| Gift-card number given to scammer | Contact gift-card issuer promptly | Keep card and receipt; ask about available remedy |
| Cryptocurrency sent to scammer | Contact exchange/operator and report fraud | Recovery is often difficult; avoid paid recovery promises |
| Cash mailed to scammer | Contact shipping or postal provider promptly | Interception may be time-sensitive and not always available |

U.S. consumer-protection rules may give specific dispute rights for qualifying credit-card and electronic-fund-transfer incidents. For some electronic fund transfers, deadlines relate to when the institution sends a periodic statement, and separate rules may apply to a lost access device. These rules are nuanced; **do not use a general lesson as a substitute for the institution's procedures or specific legal guidance**. The safest practical instruction is to notify the provider immediately and retain a clear record of the report.

### 5.2 Do not confuse a merchant problem, a scam, and identity theft

An online purchase that has not arrived might involve ordinary shipping delay, merchant nonperformance, a billing error, or fraud. Check the seller's legitimate order record and the card or payment service. If a seller cannot be reached, use the provider's documented dispute process within its deadlines. By contrast, a payment to a fake landlord, impersonated relative, or false investment opportunity may involve a scam even if the payer intentionally clicked **Send**. Identity theft means someone misuses another person's identifying information; a suspicious data-breach notice alone does not prove that misuse has occurred.

### 5.3 Respond to possible identity theft with a tailored plan

[[identity-theft|Identity theft]] is the misuse of someone's personal information to impersonate them or obtain benefits, services, funds, or accounts. The U.S. FTC's [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) offers case-specific prevention and recovery steps. If identity misuse has occurred, it can guide reports, disputes, account closures, and documentation. If sensitive information may have been exposed but misuse has not been confirmed, follow its relevant exposure/data-breach guidance rather than falsely reporting a transaction.

A [[credit-freeze|credit freeze]] restricts access to certain consumer credit reports, making it harder for someone to open new accounts in your name. In the United States it is free to place and lift, and generally must be placed separately with **Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion**. A [[fraud-alert|fraud alert]] tells creditors to take additional identity-verification steps; a standard one-year alert may be placed with one of the three bureaus, which notifies the others. Neither measure stops every kind of fraud or repairs an existing fraudulent transaction. The appropriate choice depends on the incident and the account holder's needs.

### 5.4 Protect yourself from follow-up fraud

Some scammers return after a loss, pretending to be a bank investigator, government official, technical specialist, or "recovery agent." They may know the victim's name, the amount lost, or the original scam because the information was shared or stolen. This does not establish legitimacy. An unexpected demand for a fee, gift card, code, remote access, or "safe account" transfer is a warning sign. The FTC warns specifically about people who claim they can recover money for a fee. Use the verified original provider and case channel rather than a new caller's instructions.

Never move money to a new account just because an unsolicited caller claims that the original bank is compromised. Do not let someone borrow your identity to "help" with a dispute. If a supposed government representative threatens immediate arrest, secrecy, or payment, independently verify the claim through an official agency channel.

### 5.5 Worked example: one charge, two possible explanations

Daniel sees a $180 transaction in his bank app. **Case A:** Daniel did not make or approve it; he should report an apparently unauthorized transaction immediately through the bank's recognized fraud process. **Case B:** Daniel sent $180 to a fake online seller after being deceived. He should tell the bank or payment provider that he made the payment because of the seller's false statements and ask about the available dispute or recovery options. **Case C:** Daniel recognizes the merchant but not the shortened billing descriptor; he can verify the purchase details through the bank and merchant's legitimate channels before concluding fraud.

The dollar amount is identical. The correct report depends on the actual transaction history and consent.

## 6. How do you report, document, and confirm recovery?

### 6.1 Match the report to the organization and purpose

A [[fraud-report|fraud report]] communicates a suspected scam or deceptive transaction to an appropriate organization. The U.S. **FTC** accepts scam reports at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/). The **FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)** receives reports of internet-related crime at [IC3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/). [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) focuses on identity-theft prevention and recovery. These services serve different purposes from the bank, merchant, payment app, or online provider responsible for an affected account.

| Official route | Best-fit purpose | What it cannot promise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Account provider or official recovery form | Restore or secure access to that service | Cannot necessarily reverse unrelated bank transfers |
| Bank, card issuer, or payment platform | Restrict, investigate, dispute, or attempt to recover payments | Cannot promise that every authorized scam payment is refundable |
| FTC ReportFraud.ftc.gov | Submit scam and fraud information to a federal consumer-protection agency | Does not replace a payment dispute or guarantee personal reimbursement |
| FTC IdentityTheft.gov | Obtain identity-theft reporting and recovery guidance | Does not itself block all new accounts without additional action |
| FBI IC3.gov | Report internet-enabled fraud or cybercrime | Does not guarantee an investigation or immediate recovery |
| Local police or emergency service | Address theft, threats, or safety concerns within jurisdiction | Does not automatically secure an online account |
| Employer/school IT or security | Address incidents on managed systems and organizational data | Cannot replace all personal financial reporting requirements |

If a child, vulnerable adult, coworker, or customer is affected, involve the responsible guardian, authorized administrator, or appropriate support and reporting channel. Do not publicize their private information in an attempt to warn others. Certain professions, employers, and service providers have special legal or contractual reporting obligations. This beginner lesson does not replace them.

### 6.2 Preserve useful evidence carefully

[[evidence|Evidence]] can include the original message, sender information, the genuine provider's account history, payment receipts, case numbers, device warnings, and a simple timeline. Preserve it in a safe place accessible to the right investigator or support team. Do not forward suspicious attachments to friends, upload private documents to a public forum, publish account numbers, or repeatedly reopen harmful files to take screenshots. An organization may ask you to report through a built-in phishing or abuse workflow; follow its directions.

A helpful [[case-number|case number]] is a reference supplied by a legitimate provider after a report. Record which provider issued it, the date, and what follow-up was promised. Be wary of case numbers supplied by unsolicited callers; anyone can invent a reference string.

### 6.3 Use a recovery checklist to track unresolved tasks

A [[recovery-plan|recovery plan]] is a sequence of actions chosen for the particular incident. It is not necessary to do every possible step in every case. Avoid turning a simple suspicious email into an unnecessary device reset or credit freeze, but do not ignore a real unauthorized payment because the computer "looks fine."

| Checkpoint | Confirm the result, not just the attempt | Where to get help |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Suspicious interaction stopped | No further messages, payments, or approvals are being sent to the questionable party | Relevant provider or emergency support |
| Account access controlled | New sign-in is protected and unknown sessions or methods are reviewed | Official account security/recovery |
| Device risk addressed | Trusted security/IT review completed when warranted | Device manufacturer or authorized IT |
| Payments reported | Institution acknowledged the report and gave a next step or case number | Bank, card issuer, or payment service |
| Identity exposure addressed | Appropriate protective steps are documented | IdentityTheft.gov and credit bureaus when relevant |
| People notified responsibly | Contacts or organization receive necessary warning without exposing secrets | Trusted contact or institutional policy |
| Outcome verified | Refund, access restoration, clean recovery, or next review date is confirmed | The responsible provider |

### 6.4 Monitor for repeat attempts without becoming trapped in fear

Following an incident, scammers may try again by citing an earlier loss. Checking official account activity, bills, and notices at reasonable intervals is useful. Repeatedly searching strangers' forums for certainty or paying new "investigators" can produce additional risk. If a problem remains unresolved, follow the legitimate provider's complaint and appeal process, identify any applicable deadlines, and escalate through an authorized regulator or legal-assistance route if appropriate.

If the event causes serious distress, seek a trusted person who can help organize the steps without taking over your accounts or demanding passwords. You can ask for help reading a notice, locating an official website, or making a list of calls while keeping control of your credentials and financial decisions.

### 6.5 A final worked example: the six-stage response

A consumer opens an unfamiliar link and types a banking username and password, but no payment is visible yet. **STOP:** do not interact further with the page. **IDENTIFY:** record the site and the fact that credentials were entered, without writing the password itself. **CONTAIN:** from a trusted device, reach the actual bank to secure sign-in and review suspicious activity. **CONTACT:** use the bank's fraud or security support through a verified number or app. **RECOVER:** follow the bank's account-protection process, including credential and authentication changes as directed. **VERIFY:** confirm the real account's activity and recovery information, retain any case number, and continue monitoring for suspicious changes. If money has already moved, report that immediately; do not wait to finish a password reset first.

> **Course-wide principle:** A good response is not the largest number of security actions. It is the **smallest safe set of actions matched to what actually happened**, taken promptly through trusted channels, with evidence and follow-up appropriate to the consequences.

## 7. Guided practice: Build a safe response plan

These six activities are **fictional and observation-first**. You may work on paper, in a blank document, or by discussing the scenarios with another learner. Do not open dangerous links, try to access an account you do not own, send a test payment, call a stranger, reproduce a malware warning on a live device, or share actual evidence. For each activity, identify what is known, what remains uncertain, the first trusted contact, and how you would confirm resolution. Expand the model answer when ready.

### Activity 1 — Classify the incident before reacting

**Scenario:** Four learners report different events after seeing a notice that says “SECURITY EMERGENCY.”

- **Alex:** Read the message preview, then closed it. No link or attachment was opened.
- **Blair:** Opened a webpage from the message, then closed the page. Nothing was entered or downloaded.
- **Casey:** Entered a reused password on the page but did not approve a second factor.
- **Devin:** Followed the page's instructions and approved an unexpected sign-in prompt.

**Tasks:**

1. Rank the four situations by the degree of action that occurred; explain why this is not an exact technical risk score.
1. Identify a proportionate first action for each person.
1. Explain why Casey's password reuse changes the required account follow-up.
1. Explain what Devin should examine in the real account after securing access.
1. State one fact that remains unknown in every case.

| Person | Known action | High-value question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alex | Message preview only | Is there any genuine account alert in the official service? |
| Blair | Opened page only | Were any additional actions, downloads, or redirects observed? |
| Casey | Entered reused password | Which other accounts share that credential? |
| Devin | Approved an unexpected prompt | Did an unknown session or authentication method become active? |

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answer
Alex can ignore or report the untrusted message and independently review the legitimate service if warranted. Blair should leave the page, avoid further interaction, and check genuine security information if a warning persists; merely seeing a page does not prove infection. Casey should change the compromised password through the legitimate provider, change it anywhere else reused, and review sign-in activity and MFA. Devin should treat the approved unexpected prompt as possible account access: use the real provider, secure credentials, check recent sessions and registered methods, and seek official support when needed.

A sensible progression is Alex, Blair, Casey, then Devin in terms of reported interaction, **not a universal guarantee** about damage. All four lack reliable evidence about whether someone actually accessed an account or installed malware. An independent check, not the message's claim, helps answer that question.
:::

### Activity 2 — Recover a hijacked email account

**Scenario:** Morgan's email account sends messages that Morgan did not write. Morgan can still sign in from a trusted tablet. The account shows an unfamiliar recovery telephone number and a new rule that forwards mail to an unknown address. Morgan also uses that email address for shopping and a bank account.

**Tasks:**

1. Identify three observations that indicate possible account takeover.
1. Explain why securing email should be an early priority.
1. Put these actions into a reasonable plan: check recovery methods, use official account security, notify affected contacts, change compromised credentials, review sessions and forwarding rules.
1. Decide whether Morgan should forward the suspicious messages to everyone in the contact list.
1. Describe how Morgan would verify the account is secure without claiming certainty.

:::answer Show Activity 2 model answer
The messages Morgan did not send, unknown recovery number, and unfamiliar forwarding rule are concrete signs requiring action. Because the email account may receive password resets for shopping and banking, secure it promptly through its genuine security page on the trusted tablet. Follow the provider's process to replace exposed credentials, revoke unknown sessions, correct recovery information, remove unauthorized forwarding or delegates, and strengthen MFA. Review linked services for unexpected recovery attempts or transactions. If contacts received fraudulent messages, warn the relevant recipients without sending the malicious link onward or exposing private details.

Verification means confirming expected sign-in methods and recovery contacts, reviewing recent activity and forwarding rules again, and checking linked accounts. Monitoring is still useful because a single successful password change is not an absolute guarantee.
:::

### Activity 3 — Distinguish a fake warning from possible malware

**Scenario:** Priya sees a browser pop-up: “Computer corrupted! Call 555-0100 and install FastRepair immediately.” In **Case A**, Priya closes it. In **Case B**, Priya downloads an unknown installer but does not open it. In **Case C**, Priya installs the program and lets a stranger control the desktop for several minutes.

**Tasks:**

1. For each case, describe the known action and what you must not assume.
1. Name a safe first response for Cases A, B, and C.
1. Explain why changing passwords directly on a possibly compromised device may be unwise.
1. Identify who should handle the case if the computer belongs to a school.
1. Explain why factory reset is not the default first move in every case.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model answer
**A:** A suspicious webpage appeared. Closing it and using recognized security tools if concerns remain is reasonable; it does not prove malware. **B:** An unknown installer was downloaded, not necessarily run. Avoid opening it and follow legitimate browser/security instructions. **C:** Software execution and remote control create a higher-risk event. Stop the remote session, consider disconnecting a personal device from the network, avoid sensitive tasks on it, and obtain trusted technical help; secure affected accounts from a known-safe device as appropriate.

On school equipment, alert authorized IT rather than independently disabling protection, erasing the device, or installing an unfamiliar "cleaner." A reset can destroy evidence, remove files, or interfere with a managed response, and is not automatically necessary when someone only viewed a page. Follow the actual incident facts and official support guidance.
:::

### Activity 4 — Route a financial problem correctly

**Scenario:** A family notices three payment records: **A:** a $72 card charge that nobody recognizes; **B:** a $250 person-to-person payment someone intentionally sent after a fake relative requested emergency help; **C:** a delayed $43 legitimate online order that has not yet arrived. The family then receives a message from a stranger promising to recover all the money for a $90 upfront fee.

**Tasks:**

1. Describe the difference between Cases A, B, and C without labeling every event identical.
1. Name the first appropriate organization or channel for each case.
1. Explain why the $90 offer is unsafe.
1. Identify what evidence should be retained without sharing secrets publicly.
1. Explain why a government scam report and a bank dispute are different actions.

| Case | Initial classification | Contact route |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: Unknown $72 charge | Potentially unauthorized charge or billing descriptor issue | Real card issuer or financial institution |
| B: $250 sent after fake relative request | Scam-induced payment that was intentionally authorized | Payment app and associated bank/card as applicable |
| C: Delayed $43 order | Potential merchant or shipping issue | Genuine merchant and later payment dispute if appropriate |

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answer
For **A**, contact the actual card issuer immediately and explain the charge was not recognized; verify the merchant information and report unauthorized activity if appropriate. For **B**, accurately explain the deception to the payment provider and funding institution, requesting any available reversal or recovery mechanism. For **C**, verify the order and shipping through the authentic merchant and use the payment provider's dispute process if fulfillment fails or the facts indicate fraud. A missing package is not automatically an unauthorized bank transfer.

Do not pay a new stranger $90 to obtain a "guaranteed" refund. Preserve legitimate receipts, message details, transaction amounts, contact dates, and case numbers in a private place. Reporting to the FTC or FBI IC3 may help authorities identify fraud; it does not replace the action by the bank or payment company that controls the transaction.
:::

### Activity 5 — Respond to exposed identity information

**Scenario:** Taylor learns that a service provider may have exposed names, addresses, and some Social Security numbers. Taylor has not identified any fraudulent accounts or charges. A few weeks later, a mailed statement lists a credit account Taylor did not open. Another letter advertises a paid service that claims a credit freeze costs $40 per month.

**Tasks:**

1. Distinguish the response before and after an unfamiliar account is discovered.
1. Identify the official U.S. recovery resource for suspected identity theft.
1. Describe the general difference between a fraud alert and a credit freeze.
1. Decide whether a genuine credit freeze must require a monthly payment.
1. Explain why Taylor should keep accurate records and avoid placing identity details in a class worksheet.

:::answer Show Activity 5 model answer
An exposure notice warrants reviewing the legitimate provider's notice, checking real financial and credit accounts, and considering protective measures suited to the information involved. Discovery of an unfamiliar credit account raises concern about actual identity misuse. Taylor should use **IdentityTheft.gov**, contact the affected lender or creditor through a trusted route, and follow a tailored recovery and dispute process. A U.S. credit freeze generally restricts access to credit reports and must be placed with each of the three major bureaus; it is **free** to place and lift. A standard fraud alert asks creditors to take extra identity-verification measures, and contacting one bureau starts notification to the others. Both have limitations.

Taylor should reject the claim that a paid monthly subscription is required for a genuine credit freeze. Records of letters, account dates, and official case numbers support disputes. Sensitive identifiers should remain out of fictional exercises and casual shared documents.
:::

### Activity 6 — Complete a cross-incident recovery plan

**Scenario:** During one afternoon, Sam discovers a suspicious login to a personal email account, a $115 unknown debit-card purchase, and a missing school-issued laptop. A caller then says they are from the school and asks Sam to reveal an MFA code to prove identity. Sam has a trusted phone and another internet-connected computer available.

**Tasks:**

1. Identify the three distinct issues and the appropriate contact for each.
1. Prioritize actions that may limit immediate ongoing harm without ignoring any issue.
1. Explain why the caller should not receive the code.
1. Draft a seven-line incident record using imaginary information only.
1. Name three results to verify before declaring the response complete.
1. Identify one situation in which Sam should immediately involve a trusted adult, administrator, or emergency service.

:::answer Show Activity 6 model answer
Sam should contact the real bank immediately about the unknown debit-card purchase and follow its fraud process; use the email provider's real security page from a trusted device to assess and secure the account; and promptly tell the school IT or device administrator that the school-issued laptop is missing. The exact order of the first two actions may depend on whether money is moving or an attacker still controls email. Sam must not share the MFA code, even with a caller who knows the school name. Instead, contact known school staff independently.

A safe fictional record might contain: **Date:** Friday; **Time:** 2:00 p.m.; **Events:** unfamiliar email login, unknown $115 charge, school laptop missing; **First contacts:** bank fraud team, email security portal, school IT; **Known actions:** account reviewed, card reported, device loss reported; **Reference numbers:** fictional placeholders only; **Next checks:** confirm account sessions and recovery details, bank claim status, and school incident receipt. Verification should focus on those actual records, not on an unsolicited caller's assurances.

If Sam believes the laptop theft involves physical danger, coercion, or another person's private records, Sam should seek appropriate immediate safety support and notify the responsible institution without attempting confrontation or independent investigation.
:::

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable mistakes

### “If a message claims I was hacked, the account is definitely compromised.”

A message may be an impostor's attempt to create panic. Independently review the real account or trusted security tools. Conversely, a quiet inbox does not guarantee an account has never been accessed improperly. Base your response on actual activity and meaningful evidence, not on the message's emotional tone.

### “Simply opening a suspicious webpage means my entire computer is infected.”

A browser page can display frightening claims without having diagnosed the device. A harmful page could also exploit a security weakness, so the right conclusion is neither “always infected” nor “always safe.” Identify what happened, avoid further interaction, keep software protected, and use trusted help if there is reason for concern.

### “Changing one password fixes every kind of incident.”

A password change can protect a compromised credential, but other issues may remain: an unrecognized session, a changed recovery phone, an email-forwarding rule, malware, or an unauthorized transfer. A complete account response considers what the attacker could still access. A lost device or stolen identity may require entirely different steps.

### “If security software reports no threat, my device is certainly clean.”

Security products have detection limits. A clean scan is useful evidence, not an absolute guarantee. If remote access was granted, unexpected programs executed, or sensitive data was exposed, broader trusted assessment may be warranted.

### “I should reset or wipe everything immediately so it is over.”

Factory reset and remote erase can permanently remove data, interrupt recovery, or destroy useful evidence. They may be appropriate in some situations, but they should follow a deliberate and authorized process. Managed devices belong under the organization's IT/security response.

### “The same payment protection applies whether I authorized the transfer or not.”

Unauthorized transactions and scam-induced authorized payments can involve different protections and proof requirements. Describe the events accurately to the real bank or payment provider, report promptly, and ask about the specific procedures and deadlines. Do not assume every loss is refundable or that no remedy exists.

### “Reporting a scam to a government site automatically reverses my payment.”

Government reporting helps authorities collect intelligence and sometimes supports investigations. The payment provider or financial institution has a separate role in investigating, freezing, disputing, or attempting recovery of funds. Often both types of report are useful, but neither guarantees reimbursement.

### “To keep evidence, I should share screenshots of everything publicly.”

Screenshots and messages can contain identifiers, private conversations, passwords, account history, or another person's data. Keep useful records secure and provide them only through appropriate authorized channels. Do not redistribute malicious files or send complete credentials to volunteers or strangers.

### “Someone who knows my case number or exact loss amount must be legitimate.”

Case details can be guessed, stolen, circulated, or fabricated. Recovery scammers may cite real losses. Verify independently using the official service or an existing trusted support channel. Never pay an unexpected fee, provide codes, or move money to a supposed safe account just because a caller sounds informed.

### “Once I send the report, my work is finished.”

Reports may need confirmation, documentation, and follow-up. Check that account access is restored, payment claims are acknowledged, unknown sessions are addressed, and important files are recoverable. Calendar reminders for legitimate follow-up can help when a provider gives a date, but do not respond to unsolicited "urgent case updates" without checking them.

### “It is always too late to report after a mistake.”

Prompt action improves the chance of limiting harm and can matter for consumer rights, but an older incident may still warrant reporting or protective steps. Never assume there is no point in contacting the institution. Be accurate about timing and ask which remedies remain available.

### “I should solve an incident involving my school or employer on my own.”

Institutional devices and records are governed by authorization, security policies, and sometimes legal obligations. Report to the designated IT/security contact rather than hiding the event, experimenting with cleaning tools, or deleting logs. Responsible escalation is part of good computer literacy.

### A compact avoidable-mistakes table

| Tempting reaction | Why it can worsen the situation | Safer alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Call the number on a frightening webpage | It may connect directly to an impersonator | Use known genuine support route |
| Reveal MFA code to a support caller | It may approve unauthorized access | Refuse and verify independently |
| Run an internet "cleanup command" | It may install or activate malware | Use legitimate device protection or authorized IT |
| Pay a stranger to recover funds | It may cause a second scam loss | Ask the real payment institution about recovery |
| Erase a managed laptop immediately | It may destroy evidence and violate policy | Report promptly to organizational IT/security |
| Post bank screenshots publicly | It may reveal identifying and financial data | Store evidence privately and use official channels |
| Wait until every fact is known before calling the bank | Delay may increase loss or affect protections | Notify the financial institution promptly |
| Declare success after a password change | Other sessions or recovery changes may persist | Review account activity and recovery methods |

> **Final safety principle:** Stop the questionable interaction, describe what actually happened, and involve the organization that has the authority to help. An informed response is careful and prompt, not reckless or paralyzed.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The visible glossary below groups the main terms by role. The same definitions appear in a single `:::terms` registry at the end of the file so the MGU dashboard can provide contextual popovers for underlined vocabulary. Some terms are used in a different grammatical form inside the reading. The meanings are more important than memorizing any provider's current menu layout.

### Incident recognition and response

- **Security incident:** An event that may compromise an account, device, information, money, or safety.
- **Incident response:** A coordinated process for recognizing, containing, reporting, recovering from, and verifying an incident.
- **Claim:** An assertion made by a message or person that may be true or false.
- **Observation:** An action or condition that a person can directly describe or independently verify.
- **False alarm:** A warning interpreted as a real threat when the alleged threat is not established.
- **Triage:** An early assessment to determine what happened and what needs attention first.
- **Containment:** Steps intended to stop an incident from continuing or spreading.
- **Trusted channel:** A contact route established independently from the suspicious message or caller.
- **Incident log:** A private record of event timing, observations, contacts, and unresolved questions.
- **Recovery:** Restoration of authorized access, money, files, device function, or services after a problem.
- **Verification:** Checking through a reliable method whether a claim or recovery result is correct.
- **Recovery plan:** A prioritized set of actions matched to a particular incident.
- **Evidence:** Information that helps establish what happened and supports support or investigative review.
- **Case number:** A reference identifier supplied by a genuine organization for a report or claim.
- **Risk:** The possibility of harm and its potential consequences.
- **Urgency:** The degree to which an issue requires prompt action.
- **Escalation:** Referral of an issue to a person or organization with greater authority or expertise.
- **Incident timeline:** A sequence of observed events and actions, organized by time.

### Accounts and authentication

- **Credential:** A secret or other item used to establish account access, such as a password.
- **Account takeover:** Unauthorized control of an online account.
- **Password reset:** An authorized procedure for replacing an account password.
- **Multifactor authentication:** Account verification requiring more than one different authentication factor.
- **Session:** A period of authenticated access maintained by a service or application.
- **Recovery contact:** An email address, telephone number, or method used to restore access.
- **Account recovery:** A provider-approved process for proving ownership and restoring access to an account.
- **Forwarding rule:** A mailbox setting that automatically sends selected incoming messages elsewhere.
- **Recovery scam:** Fraudulent promise to restore accounts or funds in exchange for money, codes, or access.
- **Phishing:** Deceptive communication designed to obtain secrets or induce harmful actions.
- **Sign-in activity:** A service record of attempted or successful account access.
- **Security notification:** An alert about account or device activity that must be verified through a trusted channel.
- **Recovery code:** A protected backup code that may enable account access when a usual factor is unavailable.
- **Passkey:** A cryptographic sign-in credential typically tied to a device or account ecosystem.
- **Authenticator app:** An application used to generate or approve certain account authentication challenges.
- **Account permission:** Authorization granted to a person or application to perform actions on an account.
- **Verification code:** A temporary value used for a specific sign-in or validation action.
- **Credential reuse:** Using the same password or other secret across more than one account.

### Devices, software, and files

- **Malware:** Software used or designed for harmful or unauthorized activity.
- **Download:** Transfer of data or a file onto a local device from another source.
- **Installation:** A process that places and configures software on a device.
- **Execution:** Running a program or instruction on a device.
- **Remote access:** A capability to use or control a device across a network.
- **Ransomware:** Malicious activity that blocks data access or threatens exposure to demand payment.
- **Backup:** A separately retained copy of important data intended to support recovery.
- **Synchronization:** Keeping working copies of data aligned across devices or services.
- **Factory reset:** A process returning device software and settings toward a default state, often erasing user data.
- **Remote wipe:** An authorized request to erase data from a device over a network.
- **Security software:** Tools that help detect, block, or respond to digital threats.
- **Device encryption:** Protection that makes stored data harder to read without appropriate authorization.
- **Threat:** A condition or actor capable of causing security, privacy, or availability harm.
- **Data loss:** Loss of access to or integrity of information.
- **Operating system:** Core software managing a device and its programs.
- **Security patch:** A software update intended to address a security weakness.
- **Administrator:** A person or account authorized to manage device or service settings.
- **Incident reporting:** Submitting an event through a designated support, security, or official channel.
- **Device loss:** A device becoming missing, stolen, or unavailable.

### Money, identity, and official help

- **Sensitive information:** Data whose disclosure could create privacy, financial, safety, or identity risks.
- **Authorization:** Permission given for a payment, account change, or access request.
- **Unauthorized transaction:** Account activity initiated without the account holder's authorization under applicable rules.
- **Scam-induced payment:** A payment someone authorizes after being deceived by a fraudster.
- **Account dispute:** A request to investigate a payment or account record considered incorrect or improper.
- **Identity theft:** Misuse of another person's identifying information for impersonation or fraud.
- **Credit freeze:** Restriction on access to certain credit reports to reduce fraudulent account openings.
- **Fraud alert:** A notice on a credit file asking creditors to perform additional identity checks.
- **Payment app:** A digital service that can initiate or facilitate money transfers.
- **Wire transfer:** Movement of funds through a bank or transfer service under a payment instruction.
- **Gift card:** A prepaid store or network value instrument that scammers may request as payment.
- **Chargeback:** A card-network or issuer process that may reverse a disputed card transaction when applicable.
- **Identity document:** A document used to establish personal identity, such as a license or passport.
- **Data breach:** Unauthorized access, disclosure, or acquisition of protected information.
- **Consumer protection:** Legal and institutional safeguards concerning goods, payments, and fair dealings.
- **Fraud report:** A communication to a provider or authority about suspected deceptive or unauthorized activity.
- **Reporting channel:** A designated official place to submit a concern or incident report.
- **Case status:** The recorded progress or disposition of an official complaint or service request.
- **Personal safety:** Protection from physical threats, coercion, or immediate harm.

## 11. MGU Library connections and next steps

### Core lessons learned

1. **Stop** interacting with suspicious links, calls, messages, and requests before you disclose more information or money.
1. **Identify** the observed event precisely: a message received, a page viewed, a secret entered, a code approved, a program run, money transferred, or a device lost.
1. **Contain** ongoing harm without disabling security tools or destroying data unnecessarily.
1. **Contact** the genuine account provider, financial institution, official IT team, reporting organization, or emergency service that matches the incident.
1. **Recover** by replacing compromised credentials, reviewing account access, addressing device risk, disputing eligible transactions, or following tailored identity-theft guidance.
1. **Verify** the result and maintain a short record of unresolved issues, case numbers, and needed follow-up.

### A printable first-response decision sheet

| What happened? | First safe response | Who can help next? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I received a suspicious message only | Do not interact; verify its claims independently | Genuine sender organization or platform abuse report |
| I entered a password or approved an unknown sign-in | Secure the actual account using a trusted device | Account provider and relevant IT |
| I installed software or gave remote access | Stop further interaction and sensitive use; seek trusted assessment | Genuine security support or authorized IT |
| An unknown payment appeared | Contact the real financial provider promptly | Bank, issuer, or payment platform |
| I paid a scammer after deception | Report accurate facts and ask the payment provider about recovery | Payment provider, FTC, or IC3 as appropriate |
| I discovered identity misuse | Secure affected accounts and use a tailored recovery process | IdentityTheft.gov and affected businesses |
| I lost a device | Follow official lost-device procedures and protect related accounts | Device provider, carrier, employer or school |
