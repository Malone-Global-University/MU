## Lesson 7: Protecting Personal Information

A student receives a message that appears to come from a school office. It asks for a photograph of a government identity card, a current home address, and an account verification code so that an enrollment record can be updated. Later, the student wants to send a résumé to a potential employer and finds that an old document includes a full birth date and a copy of a bank statement. The questions are different, but the central challenge is the same: **Which information is necessary, who is entitled to receive it, how will it travel, who can access it afterward, and what should happen if it is exposed?**

[[personal-information|Personal information]] includes details that identify, describe, locate, contact, or relate to a person. Some details, such as a first name or a general interest, may be appropriate to share in many contexts. Others, such as account credentials, government identifiers, financial records, or a photograph of an identity document, can create significant harm if disclosed to the wrong person or combined with other data. Even ordinary facts may become sensitive in combination. Protection therefore means **thoughtful disclosure, appropriate storage, and a practical recovery plan**, not an impossible promise to reveal nothing about yourself.

This is Lesson 7 in MGU's nine-part **Digital Safety and Scam Awareness** course. Lesson 5 addressed privacy settings, and Lesson 6 addressed safer purchases. This lesson focuses on the underlying information itself: classifying it, deciding when disclosure is warranted, safely sharing files, protecting paper and digital records, recognizing identity misuse, and responding to exposure. Windows 11 provides familiar device examples, but the decision principles apply to phones, tablets, Mac computers, shared devices, schools, workplaces, and public services.

**Practice safety:** All people, organizations, documents, and incidents in the six guided activities are fictional. Do not enter a real Social Security number, password, bank number, medical detail, government identifier, verification code, or other private information into a practice sheet or this lesson. Do not upload identity documents, investigate suspicious links, contact strangers, or change an account's security settings as part of the exercises. A printed copy or blank local document is sufficient.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 115–150 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Digital Safety and Scam Awareness Lessons 1–6 recommended · **Practice equipment:** This lesson or a printed worksheet; an optional personal device for observation only · **Safety:** Use invented placeholders and examples, never genuine identifying records.

> **Essential idea:** **PAUSE → VERIFY → MINIMIZE → PROTECT → REVIEW → RESPOND.** Pause before revealing information. Verify the requester's identity and legitimate need independently. Minimize the data you supply. Protect the transfer and the stored copy. Review access and retention. Respond promptly and specifically if exposure or misuse occurs.

### Learning objectives

After completing the lesson, you should be able to:

1. Identify common categories of personal information and explain why context and combinations affect their sensitivity.
1. Distinguish a routine request for contact information from a request for credentials, financial details, or government identifiers.
1. Apply data minimization and independent verification to online forms, messages, phone calls, and document requests.
1. Select appropriate ways to store, send, limit access to, retain, and dispose of personal records.
1. Recognize how public posts, photographs, file metadata, shared links, and physical paperwork can reveal more than intended.
1. Describe additional care needed for family, school, workplace, and shared-device information.
1. Identify typical warning signs of identity misuse and distinguish preventive tools from recovery tools.
1. Match an accidental disclosure, compromised account, lost device, or suspected identity theft with proportionate first actions.
1. Explain six fictional cases and complete a 30-question self-study knowledge check without exposing actual data.

## 1. What counts as personal information, and why does it matter?

### 1.1 Personal information is broader than a secret

Personal information includes a name, contact details, date of birth, financial details, account records, photographs, location history, work or school records, and other information that relates to a person. [[personally-identifiable-information|Personally identifiable information]] or **PII** is a term commonly used for identifying information; exact legal and organizational definitions vary. A detail does not need to be classified as a secret to deserve protection. A public mailing address, for example, can still be misused in a convincing impersonation message.

Information may be [[direct-identifier|directly identifying]], such as a full name paired with an account number, or [[indirect-identifier|indirectly identifying]], such as a rare job title combined with a small neighborhood and an exact event date. What seems harmless in isolation may be revealing when joined with other details, a process sometimes called [[data-aggregation|data aggregation]]. Online posts, a shopping receipt, public records, and a social profile might together give someone enough information to impersonate a person or answer an account-recovery question.

| Information category | Examples | Practical handling principle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Everyday contact details | Name, public work email, general telephone number | Share for a legitimate purpose; consider audience and context |
| Location and routine | Home address, live location, regular commuting time | Limit public and unnecessary disclosure |
| Identity documents and numbers | Social Security number, passport, driver's license, identity-card image | Treat as highly sensitive and verify necessity and recipient |
| Financial information | Card details, account numbers, transaction records, tax documents | Use authorized channels and limit copies |
| Authentication secrets | Passwords, PINs, recovery codes, sign-in approval prompts | Do not disclose to callers, chat contacts, or unexpected messages |
| Health and education records | Medical history, student records, school identifiers | Share only with appropriate, authorized parties and channels |
| Digital records | Photos, device backups, cloud files, location metadata | Check content, access settings, and hidden contextual details |

The table is a guide, not an absolute ranking. A public-facing business telephone number can be ordinary, while a telephone number connected to a private health account can be more consequential. A child, someone facing harassment, or a person in a confidential occupation may reasonably apply stricter controls to information others share openly.

### 1.2 Think about consequence, not just secrecy

[[data-sensitivity|Sensitivity]] concerns the possible consequences of exposure, misuse, or unexpected access. An address can be necessary for a real delivery. A school may need an enrollment record. A legitimate bank may need to verify identity through its official process. The objective is not to reject every request: it is to ensure that the **recipient, reason, quantity, channel, and retention** fit the task.

**Worked example:** A fictional community class asks a learner for a first name and contact email to send schedule updates. This information appears reasonably related to the service. A second version of the form requests the learner's full bank-card number and Social Security number with no explanation. The additional fields create risks and are not visibly necessary to send class reminders. The learner can stop and seek clarification through the organization's known, independent contact channel.

Consider three levels of protection. First, restrict **disclosure** so that unnecessary information does not leave your control. Second, protect **storage and access** after information has been provided. Third, maintain **detection and recovery** procedures in case information is exposed despite precautions. A secure transfer alone cannot erase an unnecessary copy already sitting in a recipient's mailbox.

### 1.3 Know the difference between privacy and security

[[privacy|Privacy]] concerns appropriate collection, use, disclosure, and control of information. [[information-security|Information security]] concerns protecting information against unauthorized access, alteration, loss, or misuse. They overlap but are not interchangeable. A website could store data with strong technical security while collecting more than a person reasonably needs to provide. Conversely, a well-intentioned organization might collect very little data but keep it in an unprotected file.

| Question | Mainly a privacy issue | Mainly a security issue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why was my exact birth date requested? | Whether collection is necessary and justified | How the collected value is guarded |
| Who may see my shared document? | Whether each recipient should have access | Whether permissions prevent unauthorized access |
| How long is a copy retained? | Whether continued retention is necessary | Whether remaining copies are protected |
| Can someone alter my record? | Whether the use is permitted | Whether integrity controls detect unauthorized changes |
| Can the information be deleted? | Whether deletion is allowed and appropriate | Whether backups and duplicates remain controlled |

A [[privacy-notice|privacy notice]] may explain how information is used, but the existence of a notice does not itself prove good judgment, compliance, or trustworthy practices. Look for meaningful answers, not just a long policy page.

### 1.4 Use a simple sensitivity test

Before sharing, ask: **Could this allow a stranger to access an account, open an account, obtain money, locate someone, impersonate someone, or expose something private?** If yes, apply additional care. Avoid placing genuine private details in casual group chats, public comments, ordinary screenshots, or training exercises even when the sender seems familiar.

A [[government-identifier|government identifier]] deserves particular attention. In the United States, a [[social-security-number|Social Security number]] has legitimate uses, but it should not be treated as an ordinary account-login secret or routinely exposed on a photo or form. If it is requested, verify the organization, why it is needed, and the secure process; some legitimate services or legal requirements do require it. An organization asking for an identifier is not automatically fraudulent, and a familiar logo is not sufficient evidence that the request is authentic.

## 2. How do you decide what personal information to share?

### 2.1 Use the VERIFY and MINIMIZE questions

[[data-minimization|Data minimization]] means collecting, using, or sharing only the information reasonably needed for the legitimate purpose. On the learner's side, it means considering whether the task can be completed with less detail. For example, a name and reply email might be sufficient to request an appointment; a copy of a complete bank statement would be unnecessary unless a specific verified process requires it.

Before sending, ask:

1. **Who is requesting it?** Identify the actual organization and specific role.
1. **Why is it needed?** Match each field to the legitimate task.
1. **Is this the genuine channel?** Reach the organization using a known address, app, phone number, or verified portal rather than trusting message-provided details.
1. **Could less information work?** Provide a limited value or redacted copy only when the recipient confirms that it is acceptable.
1. **Who will receive and retain it?** Consider forwarding, other users, and storage duration.
1. **What if I decline or defer?** Seek an alternative official procedure if the request is unclear.

A [[mandatory-field|required field]] in a form means the software expects an entry; it does not independently establish that collection is necessary, lawful, or safe. You may have a legitimate reason to complete a required field, but you can still evaluate whether the organization and transaction deserve your trust.

### 2.2 Verify independently, including when the message looks real

[[impersonation|Impersonation]] occurs when someone falsely presents themselves as another person or organization. A caller may know a real address, an order number, or a colleague's name because the information is public, leaked, or previously shared. [[caller-id-spoofing|Caller ID spoofing]], compromised message threads, and believable email formatting can make deceptive requests look authentic.

**Worked example:** A text says, “This is the benefits office. Reply with a photo of your identity card and the six-digit code that just arrived.” The correct action is not to decide whether the writing looks official. Instead, do not provide the documents or code; use an independently verified office number or sign in through the official service. A surprise [[one-time-code|one-time code]] may be part of someone else's sign-in attempt. Never read it to a caller who requested it. Even when a legitimate service sends a code, enter it only into the genuine sign-in flow you initiated.

| Request or condition | Useful question | Safer first action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An unexpected email requests an identity scan | Did I initiate this verified process? | Contact the organization independently before sending |
| A caller already knows my address | Does knowledge of an address establish identity? | No; verify with an official source |
| A legitimate form requests a tax identifier | Is the identifier necessary for this verified transaction? | Follow the official secure procedure or seek clarification |
| A colleague asks for a customer file by personal email | Is the recipient authorized and channel approved? | Use the organization's authorized transfer method |
| A social-media message requests a verification code | Who initiated the actual sign-in? | Do not send or approve the code; inspect the account |
| A public site offers a free document upload | Where will the document be stored and reused? | Do not upload sensitive files without a verified need and suitable safeguards |

### 2.3 Share the minimum appropriate document

Sometimes an organization needs a document rather than a typed field. A [[redaction|redaction]] removes information from the copy supplied to an authorized recipient. It is appropriate **only if the recipient accepts a limited version**. Do not remove information from an official document in a way that falsely represents its meaning or violates the required submission process. For real records, use a reliable redaction tool that actually removes content; a black rectangle placed over text in an editable document may leave the original text recoverable. A photograph also may include background information or location data.

For a fictional proof-of-address example, the authorized receiver might accept a document that displays a name, address, and recent date but hides unrelated account balances and transaction lines. Confirm the stated requirements before editing. Do not place a public link to a government ID or medical record merely to make sharing more convenient.

### 2.4 Match the transfer method to sensitivity

An [[official-portal|official portal]] may provide a secure place for a specific transaction, but use it only after verifying the exact organization and destination. [[https|HTTPS]] generally secures traffic with the addressed web server; it does not prove the recipient's honesty, the legitimacy of a data request, or the confidentiality of every later copy. Ordinary email is convenient but may be forwarded, stored across systems, or accessed through a compromised account. If a sensitive file must be emailed, follow the institution's approved secure-file or encrypted-message process rather than improvising a method.

| Transfer method | Appropriate considerations | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verified official account portal | Specific recipient, authenticated session, purpose-built upload | Confirm the actual domain and requested fields |
| Approved work or school file service | Access rights, institutional policy, expiration settings | Verify recipient and restrictions before sharing |
| Ordinary email attachment | Useful for non-sensitive routine documents | Can be forwarded and may persist in multiple mailboxes |
| Public or anyone-with-link sharing | Broad distribution of genuinely public material | Avoid for sensitive personal records |
| Printed handoff | Physical delivery to an authorized person | Avoid unattended printers, desks, or envelopes |
| Phone conversation | Necessary routine communication with a verified party | Caller knowledge and caller ID alone do not establish identity |

## 3. How do you protect digital records and file sharing?

### 3.1 Think through the whole life of a file

A document moves through stages: **creation, storage, sharing, use, archiving, and disposal**. A [[digital-record|digital record]] might exist in a Downloads folder, an email attachment, a cloud backup, a chat history, or a recent-files list. Securing only the final saved copy may leave other versions exposed. A photograph of a document can be stored in a photo library even if the photographed paper is later locked away.

A [[file-permission|file permission]] determines who may access or change a file. A [[shared-link|shared link]] may allow specified people, people in an organization, or anyone possessing the link to open it, depending on the service. Treat a link with “anyone can view” permission as broadly shareable, even if you only send it to one person. A file owner can often change permissions or revoke access, but a recipient may already have viewed, downloaded, photographed, or copied the content. Revocation does not necessarily retrieve those copies.

**Worked example:** A fictional learner needs to send one form to an approved instructor. Instead of uploading the entire “Family Records” folder, the learner creates a new copy of the one required form, confirms that it contains no unrelated records, selects the verified instructor's account as the recipient, and uses view-only access when editing is unnecessary. After receiving confirmation, the learner reviews whether ongoing access is still required.

### 3.2 Protect storage, devices, and accounts

[[device-lock|Device locking]] reduces casual access when a device is unattended. [[device-encryption|Device encryption]] can protect stored information if a device is lost or stolen, depending on how it is configured, and does not replace account security. [[backup|Backups]] help recover files after a device failure or ransomware event but create additional copies that also need protection. [[multifactor-authentication|Multifactor authentication]] adds an account-security barrier when available and properly used. The exact menus vary among Windows 11, macOS, Android, iOS, and cloud services.

Use a unique account password or supported passkey, an appropriate second factor, automatic security updates, and an established method to recover account access. Place sensitive records in a controlled folder or service rather than on a desktop visible to everyone who uses the machine. If a device is shared, know which operating-system account and cloud profile are signed in. Private-browsing windows do not protect a saved document from everyone who has access to the device or its storage.

| Where a copy may remain | Typical exposure | Risk-reduction habit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Downloads folder | Forgotten copies after a form is submitted | Review and delete unneeded copies in accordance with retention needs |
| Email attachments | Forwarded messages or old mailbox access | Use official secure transfer for sensitive records |
| Photo library | Camera scans, screenshots, cloud photo backups | Check which photos sync and whom albums are shared with |
| Cloud drive | Misconfigured links or broad folder access | Limit named recipients and review permissions |
| USB drive | Loss, lending, or uncontrolled duplication | Use approved encrypted storage where appropriate |
| Shared computer | Other accounts, active sessions, cached files | Use an authorized individual profile; sign out and remove temporary copies |
| Printed copy | Unattended printer output or discarded pages | Collect promptly, store securely, and dispose appropriately |

### 3.3 Understand photos, screenshots, and metadata

[[metadata|Metadata]] is information about a file or record, such as a creation time, device model, author field, edit history, or location data. [[exif|EXIF]] metadata is one common form in digital photographs. A picture of a boarding pass, office badge, package label, student card, home entryway, or appointment paper can disclose details beyond its main subject. A screenshot might include a visible account name, browser tab, transaction notification, or address bar that the sender did not intend to share.

Before sharing a photograph or screenshot, inspect the full image and any visible background, notification, document name, barcode, or [[qr-code|QR code]]. Be aware that cropping may not remove every recoverable layer in some formats, and the receiving service may preserve or strip some metadata depending on its behavior. For sensitive content, use a purpose-built export or verified redaction workflow when possible, and examine the exported file itself. Do not treat a blur, marker stroke, or screenshot cover-up as guaranteed permanent removal without checking the tool and output.

### 3.4 Use retention and disposal deliberately

[[data-retention|Data retention]] is the continued keeping of information. Some records should be preserved for legitimate tax, legal, medical, educational, employment, or transaction purposes, potentially for prescribed periods. Others are temporary copies that serve no ongoing purpose. A [[retention-schedule|retention schedule]] is a rule or plan specifying what to keep and for how long. In a workplace or school, follow the applicable policies rather than deleting records on your own judgment.

When a file is no longer necessary and can appropriately be discarded, remove working copies, check trash and sharing permissions, and understand backup or synchronization behavior. [[secure-disposal|Secure disposal]] depends on medium and system: paper can be shredded or handled through an approved confidential-destruction service, while devices may require a manufacturer-supported erase or an organizational IT procedure. Moving an item to a recycling bin is not always the same as making it unrecoverable. Do not delete evidence needed to resolve suspected fraud.

## 4. How do you protect information in daily life?

### 4.1 Secure the ordinary paper trail

Identity information also appears on postal mail, receipts, insurance correspondence, school paperwork, printed tax forms, medical bills, shipping labels, and old notebooks. A locked room or file cabinet and an organized folder system may be more effective than leaving sensitive papers in an open stack. Keep high-value original documents in an appropriately secure location, with lawful access available when they are genuinely needed. Do not routinely carry an original Social Security card or other unusual high-value identifier unless a particular verified task requires it.

[[mail-theft|Mail theft]] and misdirected mail can expose details that support identity fraud. Collect mail reasonably promptly, confirm that address changes are legitimate, and use official postal or account channels when you suspect a problem. Before discarding financial or identity-bearing documents, use shredding or an approved confidential disposal process rather than placing fully readable pages in public waste bins. Some plain receipts may not contain enough information to pose a serious risk; evaluate their actual content rather than assuming every scrap needs equal controls.

### 4.2 Limit unnecessary public exposure

A [[public-post|Public post]] can be seen by people beyond the intended audience, and even private groups can contain screenshots or forwarded content. A birthday celebration photo may reveal an exact birth date; a vacation announcement may reveal that a home is empty; a school notice may include a student's ID; a photograph of a delivered parcel may display a full address. [[oversharing|Oversharing]] is relative to audience and purpose, not simply the act of posting online.

| Common public share | Possible unintended disclosure | Practical adjustment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Birthday post with full birth date | Exact age and birth date together | Share a celebration without an unnecessary exact date |
| Photo of an envelope or shipping box | Home address, tracking code, or recipient details | Remove label from the photograph |
| Live travel update | Current location and timing | Delay publication or restrict audience when appropriate |
| Screenshot of an online account | Email, transaction details, recovery prompts | Use invented demonstration data instead |
| Student certificate or badge | Student ID, QR code, affiliation | Remove identifiers before posting |
| Group photograph with a location tag | Other people's whereabouts | Ask permission and consider location disclosure |

A [[data-broker|data broker]] may compile and sell or otherwise provide information from public and commercial sources. Privacy choices on one social network cannot remove information already contained in public records, previously copied posts, or independent databases. Some legitimate [[opt-out|opt-out]] mechanisms can limit certain uses or disclosures, but availability and effect depend on the service and applicable law. Avoid paying an unfamiliar service merely because it claims that a person's name or address is discoverable online.

### 4.3 Share appropriately with real institutions

Hospitals, financial institutions, schools, employers, landlords, public agencies, and other legitimate organizations sometimes must collect sensitive information. The decision rule remains **verify, minimize when permitted, and protect**. Do not assume that refusal is always possible: certain services have legal identification or recordkeeping requirements. Ask how the information will be used and how to send it through an approved process. A trustworthy employee should be able to direct you to established policy or an authorized help channel; a surprising demand to read a verification code aloud to a stranger is different.

For in-person transactions, avoid speaking sensitive numbers within unnecessary hearing distance. For remote service, consider whether a verified portal is safer than attaching an entire document to a message. Beware of any instruction to move a conversation away from an official account into a personal chat, unknown file-sharing site, or direct message solely to “finish verification.”

## 5. How do you handle information about other people?

### 5.1 Responsibility extends beyond your own identity

[[confidentiality|Confidentiality]] means information is made accessible only to the people authorized to receive it. When someone trusts you with a family member's health form, a student's contact list, a customer's invoice, or a colleague's private document, you may hold obligations that go beyond your personal preferences. Sharing something because it is interesting, convenient, or technically accessible does not establish permission to disclose it.

[[consent|Consent]] is a person's voluntary, meaningful agreement to an action under relevant circumstances. Consent may be limited to a purpose and audience; it is not necessarily permanent or transferable. In school and work settings, specific laws, contracts, and institutional rules may apply. Children and vulnerable individuals may need additional protection, and legal guardians or authorized personnel may have defined roles. When uncertain, use the school or employer's documented process rather than improvising your own disclosure rule.

### 5.2 Work and school systems are not personal file drawers

[[least-privilege|Least privilege]] means access should be limited to what someone needs for an authorized role. A volunteer arranging an event may need participant names but not their full medical histories. An employee checking a delivery address may not need a copy of a customer's entire payment history. A teacher's assistant may have permission to see certain records but not to forward them to a personal email account. The relationship between legitimate role, specific task, and approved channel is the governing principle.

| Situation | Legitimate need | Safer practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shared class roster | Instructor or authorized staff need contact details | Use the approved school system; do not publish roster broadly |
| Customer support request | Staff need a limited account reference | Follow employer verification and secure-record procedures |
| Family member asks for another person's report | The requester claims a close relationship | Confirm actual authorization before disclosure |
| Coworker requests records via personal email | Work purpose may be genuine | Use approved work channels and verify authorization |
| Photograph at a community event | Publicity may be intended | Obtain appropriate permission before posting identifiers |
| Shared public computer | Temporary access for a task | Do not view others' data; sign out and remove authorized temporary files |

A [[shared-device|shared device]] may retain browser sessions, downloads, autofill values, or recent file lists. Use only your own account or an authorized guest session, do not inspect another person's data, and sign out when finished. Avoid storing sensitive files on a public device. If another person's account is already signed in, leave it untouched and notify authorized staff.

### 5.3 Handle accidental receipt responsibly

If a file, email, or printout containing someone else's information arrives by mistake, do not spread it further to ask strangers for advice. Avoid opening unnecessary attachments or exploring contents beyond what is needed to recognize the problem. Use the sender's or institution's verified reporting route, explain the mistaken delivery, and follow directions for secure deletion or return consistent with policy. Treat this as a data-handling issue even if no fraud is visible.

## 6. What should you do if personal information is exposed?

### 6.1 Distinguish exposure from proven misuse

A [[data-breach|data breach]] is an incident where information is accessed, disclosed, or otherwise compromised without authorization. A [[data-exposure|data exposure]] may involve an accidentally public file, a lost document, a misdirected message, or a stolen database. A breach notice does not by itself prove that every affected person's identity has been stolen, and a lack of visible fraud does not establish that exposed information is harmless. The first task is to establish **what kind of information was involved, who may have accessed it, and whether active misuse is occurring**.

[[identity-theft|Identity theft]] occurs when someone uses another person's personal information without permission, often to open accounts, obtain funds, impersonate them, or commit other fraud. Warning signs can include unfamiliar accounts or inquiries on a credit report, unexpected bills, missing statements, notifications of unrecognized account changes, or messages about benefits or tax filings you did not submit. Some unusual events have legitimate explanations; verify with the relevant institution rather than automatically treating every alert as proof of a crime.

### 6.2 Match response steps to the exposed information

| Incident | First priorities | Why the distinction matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Password or sign-in code disclosed | Secure the genuine account; change exposed passwords; check sessions and recovery details | Account takeover can happen without identity documents |
| Payment-card details exposed | Contact issuer through verified channels; review and dispute unauthorized activity promptly | Payment controls and deadlines differ from credit-report measures |
| Social Security number or identity document exposed | Assess misuse risk; use IdentityTheft.gov guidance; consider credit freezes and monitoring | New-account identity fraud may require credit-file safeguards |
| Medical or insurance details exposed | Notify the real provider or insurer; monitor statements and explanations of benefits | The primary risk may be misuse of benefits or medical records |
| Lost phone or laptop containing records | Use known device/account recovery controls and authorized IT support; consider remote lock or erase when suitable | Device access, backups, and organizational policy determine next steps |
| Misdirected private document | Notify the sender and intended recipient via trusted channels; request containment | Correcting recipient permissions may limit further access |
| Suspicious account activity | Enter the account using a known official route; inspect history and authentication settings | A deceptive warning may itself be an attempted scam |

**Worked example:** If a person enters a reused password on a fraudulent website, a credit freeze is not the first or only remedy. The immediate account issue calls for changing that password on the real site, securing the email account and other places where the password was reused, reviewing multifactor authentication and recovery methods, and checking recent sessions. By contrast, an exposed Social Security number may call for identity-theft guidance and consideration of a credit freeze even when no password was compromised. Different incidents require different interventions.

### 6.3 Know what credit freezes and fraud alerts actually do

A [[credit-freeze|credit freeze]], sometimes called a security freeze, generally restricts access to a consumer credit report for new credit applications and can make it harder for someone to open new credit accounts in that person's name. In the United States, placing and lifting freezes with the major nationwide credit reporting agencies is free. Freezes must generally be placed **separately with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion** for broad coverage. A freeze does not erase existing credit, fix fraud that already occurred, or prevent every kind of identity crime. It does not itself lower a credit score. Legitimate applications may require temporarily lifting it.

A [[fraud-alert|fraud alert]] asks creditors to take extra steps to verify identity when someone applies for credit. Under U.S. procedures, a person can place an initial fraud alert with one of the three nationwide credit bureaus, which must notify the other two; this differs from placing separate credit freezes. An initial alert generally lasts one year, and other alert types have additional eligibility or durations. See current FTC guidance before taking action. A [[credit-monitoring|credit monitoring]] service may help detect certain changes after they occur; it does not prevent all fraud or guarantee reimbursement.

| Measure | Primary purpose | Important limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Credit freeze | Restrict access to credit files for many new-credit decisions | Separate placement with each bureau; not universal identity-theft protection |
| Fraud alert | Tell creditors to verify identity more carefully before extending credit | Not the same as blocking access to the report |
| Credit monitoring | Notify about selected changes or events | Detection is not prevention, and coverage varies |
| Account transaction alerts | Notify about selected bank or card activity | Does not block every unauthorized transaction |
| Password change and MFA review | Reduce risk of compromised account access | Does not remove an exposed identifier from other databases |
| FTC identity-theft recovery plan | Organize reporting and recovery steps | Does not automatically restore money or complete every dispute |

Do not assume a paid identity-protection subscription is necessary merely because someone contacts you about a supposed breach. Some breach notifications offer free monitoring for a time. Verify the notice independently and determine whether the offered service is appropriate. For credit-report review, use the official [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/) service rather than an unsolicited link claiming to offer a special report.

### 6.4 Use official recovery and reporting channels

For U.S. identity-theft concerns, the Federal Trade Commission's [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) provides a personalized reporting and recovery process. [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) accepts consumer fraud reports. The [FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center](https://www.ic3.gov/) accepts reports of suspected internet crime. For payment-card or bank-account issues, contact the financial institution immediately using an independently verified channel, because time-sensitive rights and actions may depend on the instrument and circumstances. For work or school exposures, notify the authorized privacy, security, or IT office promptly using internal procedures.

Keep an [[incident-log|incident log]] containing only necessary facts: approximate time, what was disclosed, which verified organization was contacted, case numbers, steps taken, and any evidence preserved. Do not publicly post the exposed document or authentication secret as proof. Watch for [[recovery-scam|recovery scams]] in which someone requests advance fees, remote device access, or additional identifying details while promising to retrieve stolen money or “clean” an identity. Reporting is important but does not, by itself, guarantee a refund or credit repair.

### 6.5 A compact response sequence

1. **Stop the exposure:** Do not resend information or continue engaging with a suspicious requester.
1. **Identify what occurred:** Separate an unopened message from a disclosed password, uploaded document, lost device, or unauthorized transaction.
1. **Contain where possible:** Revoke a mistaken sharing link, secure a real account, or contact an issuer or authorized administrator.
1. **Verify and document:** Use independently confirmed contact routes and preserve appropriate evidence without redistributing secrets.
1. **Protect the affected identity or account:** Consider credit freezes, medical-account checks, monitoring, and account recovery when the information involved warrants them.
1. **Report and follow up:** Follow relevant deadlines, official procedures, and recovery plans; review for further misuse.

The goal is a proportionate response. You do not need to investigate a malicious site yourself or prove who committed a crime before protecting your account and seeking help.

## 7. Guided practice: Make safer information decisions

These six exercises use invented data and do not require live internet. Record your decisions in general words, not real identifiers. Use each expandable model answer after attempting the activity. A well-reasoned decision to pause and obtain authorized assistance is a successful outcome.

### Activity 1 — Classify information by consequence

A fictional community-center registration contains the following fields: first name, contact email, favorite music genre, current home address, full Social Security number, emergency contact, and a password for the learner's email account.

1. Identify which fields might reasonably relate to scheduling a class and which would require stronger justification.
1. Explain why a home address may be more sensitive than a favorite genre even if neither is a password.
1. Identify one item that should never be supplied to the center merely to register for class.
1. Write one question to ask before completing an unexpected identity field.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answer
A first name and contact email plausibly support a class schedule. An emergency contact may be appropriate for some programs but should have a clear reason and authorized handling. A home address and Social Security number require more specific justification; a favorite music genre may simply be optional. The email account password should not be provided. A suitable question is: “What verified service or legal requirement makes this field necessary, and is there a less intrusive option?” Sensitivity depends on misuse consequences and context, not whether a field resembles a secret.
:::

### Activity 2 — Decide what to send

A fictional scholarship office has an established secure portal. It requests proof of residence and specifies that a document showing the applicant's name, current address, and date is sufficient. A draft utility statement also shows an unrelated account number, balance, and full payment history.

1. Identify the information the stated task actually needs.
1. Decide what to do about the unrelated fields.
1. Select a reasonable transfer method and explain how you will verify it.
1. State what you should do if the portal rejects a limited document.

:::answer Show Activity 2 model answer
The stated purpose requires the applicant's name, current address, and document date. The account number, financial balance, and payment history are not mentioned as necessary. If the scholarship office permits it, create a properly redacted copy and inspect the exported result. Use the independently verified official portal, not an unsolicited upload link. If the limited document is rejected, seek clarification through the known office channel; do not falsify a record or bypass a legitimate requirement.
:::

### Activity 3 — Choose safe document permissions

A learner needs an instructor to read one signed fictional assignment. In a cloud folder are the assignment, a family tax record, a medical letter, and a photograph. The sharing tool offers “Anyone with the link can edit” or “Selected people can view.”

1. Identify which file should be shared and whether any others are needed.
1. Choose the narrower permission and name its recipient.
1. Explain what to check before and after sending the link.
1. Explain why revoking access later may not erase earlier downloads.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model answer
Share only the assignment through the verified instructor's account, using selected-people view access unless editing is genuinely required. Check the filename, file contents, recipient address, and sharing settings before sending; confirm receipt and review continued access afterward. Do not share the entire folder or family documents. A recipient can make a copy before permission is revoked, so access revocation does not guarantee full retrieval.
:::

### Activity 4 — Inspect a public post

A fictional post includes a travel photograph with a live location tag, a boarding pass in the foreground, a parcel label showing a full home address, and a visible notification with an account email.

1. Identify at least four details that could reveal personal or travel information.
1. Propose a version of the post that preserves the intended message without those disclosures.
1. Explain why restricting the audience alone may not guarantee secrecy.
1. Identify one consideration involving another person photographed in the scene.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answer
The live location, boarding-pass details, home address, and account email are unnecessary exposures; photograph metadata and a travel schedule might add further context. A safer post could use a photograph without documents or notifications, omit precise live location, and share later if appropriate. Restricted audiences can still copy or forward material, and the photographed person's permission and circumstances also matter.
:::

### Activity 5 — Plan a home information routine

A fictional household has three categories of material: important identity originals, tax and financial records with retention requirements, and temporary printed appointment reminders. Family members also use a shared computer and take smartphone photographs of some forms.

1. Propose a safe storage location for original documents.
1. Explain how to determine which records must be retained rather than discarded.
1. Identify two digital locations that may contain extra copies.
1. Describe an appropriate disposal rule for documents that no longer need to be retained.

:::answer Show Activity 5 model answer
Keep originals in a suitable secure physical location with legitimate access when needed. Follow applicable tax, legal, employment, or institutional retention requirements rather than discarding records simply because they are old. Check Downloads, email, phone photo libraries, and cloud backups for unnecessary copies. Dispose of permitted sensitive paper through shredding or an approved confidential service, and use suitable device or organizational procedures for digital disposal. Do not delete records needed as evidence of a dispute.
:::

### Activity 6 — Match an incident to a response

Four fictional events happen on separate days: (A) a learner receives a scam text but does not respond; (B) a password reused on several accounts is entered into a fake page; (C) a letter says that a Social Security number may have been exposed in a verified breach; (D) a payment account shows an unrecognized transfer.

1. Choose the first priority for each case.
1. Identify which case may warrant a credit-freeze evaluation and why.
1. Identify which case requires time-sensitive contact with a financial institution.
1. Explain why doing exactly the same thing after every incident would be ineffective.

:::answer Show Activity 6 model answer
(A) Do not engage; report or block the suspicious text through appropriate channels. (B) Change the exposed password on the real accounts, secure reused-password accounts and email, and review second factors and sign-in sessions. (C) Verify the notice independently, identify exposed data, consult FTC identity-theft guidance, and consider separate freezes with the three nationwide bureaus and other monitoring. (D) Contact the legitimate financial institution promptly and review transaction dispute procedures. Different information creates different risks: an unwanted message is not equivalent to a disclosed credential, government identifier, or stolen funds.
:::

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

### “Only passwords and bank numbers are personal information.”

A location, date of birth, photograph, student record, or combination of public details can also identify or expose someone. Focus on misuse consequences rather than a fixed list of secrets.

### “If a form marks a field required, it must be safe and necessary.”

A software validation rule does not prove that the website is genuine, that the recipient is authorized, or that the data collection is appropriate. Verify important requests and seek an official alternative when warranted.

### “Someone who knows my full name and address must work for my bank.”

Those details may come from public records, commercial databases, earlier transactions, or breaches. Use independent institutional verification; do not trust caller ID alone.

### “HTTPS guarantees that the person receiving my document is trustworthy.”

HTTPS generally protects communications with the addressed server in transit. It does not verify the business purpose, prevent overcollection, or control how the file is stored afterward.

### “An identity document is safe to send because it is only a photograph.”

A readable photograph may expose the same document number and biographical details as the original. The photo may also have metadata and cloud backups.

### “A black rectangle over a digital document always removes hidden text.”

An editable file can retain text or layers beneath a visual cover-up. Use a reputable redaction process that removes data from the final document and verify the resulting file.

### “A private social-media group guarantees nobody else sees a post.”

Audience restrictions reduce exposure but do not prevent screenshots, copying, sharing, account compromise, or later changes in access.

### “Removing access to a shared link takes back every copy.”

Changing permissions may prevent future access through that link; it cannot reliably retrieve copies already downloaded or photographed by recipients.

### “Everything older than a year should be deleted.”

Retention depends on legal, business, medical, educational, personal, and evidentiary needs. Follow valid retention requirements before disposing of records.

### “A credit freeze fixes every kind of identity theft.”

A freeze can limit many new-credit inquiries but does not erase exposed information, protect all bank or medical accounts, or recover stolen money. Match measures to the type of risk.

### “A breach notification means someone has definitely opened an account in my name.”

A notice signals potential exposure, not necessarily completed misuse. Assess the disclosed data, secure relevant accounts, and use appropriate monitoring or recovery steps.

### “Paying a monitoring service is the only way to protect my identity.”

Official free tools, including eligible credit freezes, fraud alerts, account protections, and FTC guidance, may address important risks. Paid services vary and do not guarantee prevention or refunds.

### A practical decision matrix

| When you encounter... | Avoid assuming... | Safer response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An unexpected request for a scanned ID | A familiar logo establishes legitimacy | Verify the requester and exact need independently |
| A form asking for unnecessary personal details | “Required” means automatically appropriate | Evaluate necessity and ask about alternatives |
| A public document-sharing link | Only the intended recipient can open it | Restrict to verified named users |
| A screenshot with cropped account details | The cropped output contains no recoverable information | Examine final output and metadata as relevant |
| Sensitive mail in the trash | Paper identity information is harmless | Follow appropriate confidential disposal |
| Someone else's file in your inbox | You may circulate it to ask others what it means | Notify authorized sender or office and limit further access |
| A verified breach involving an identifier | Every affected person has already suffered identity theft | Determine actual exposure and use proportionate safeguards |
| A suspicious payment notification | Credit monitoring alone will resolve it | Contact the real financial institution promptly |
| A stranger promising to reverse identity theft for a fee | Recovery must require advance payment | Use verified official recovery channels |

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The dotted-underlined vocabulary in this lesson can open short explanations within the MGU dashboard. The definitions below are also visible as ordinary text for learners using printed copies or environments without interactive tooltips. The final `:::terms` block contains machine-readable versions of these definitions.

### Personal data and identity

- **Personal information:** Information that identifies, relates to, describes, locates, or can be linked to a person.
- **Personally identifiable information:** Information that directly or in combination can be used to identify an individual; definitions depend on context.
- **Direct identifier:** A detail that identifies a person directly in an applicable context.
- **Indirect identifier:** A detail that may identify someone when combined with other information.
- **Data aggregation:** Combining information from multiple sources to obtain a fuller picture.
- **Data sensitivity:** The potential harm from disclosure, misuse, alteration, or loss of information.
- **Government identifier:** An official number or credential associated with a person and a public authority.
- **Social Security number:** A U.S. identifier used for specified government, employment, tax, and other lawful purposes.
- **Identity document:** Official or other credential used to support identity verification.
- **Identity verification:** Process for checking a claimed identity in a legitimate transaction.
- **Identity theft:** Unauthorized use of identifying information to impersonate a person or commit fraud.
- **Privacy:** Appropriate collection, use, access, disclosure, and control of information.
- **Information security:** Protection of information against unauthorized access, alteration, misuse, or loss.
- **Privacy notice:** Statement describing an organization's practices for collecting and handling personal data.
- **Data minimization:** Limiting collection and sharing to information reasonably needed for a legitimate purpose.
- **Mandatory field:** Form entry the software requires before submission, independent of whether the request is appropriate.
- **Confidentiality:** Limiting access to information to those appropriately authorized.
- **Consent:** Voluntary agreement to an activity under relevant circumstances.

### Requests, communication, and document handling

- **Impersonation:** Falsely presenting oneself as another person or organization.
- **Caller ID spoofing:** Altering displayed caller information to misrepresent where a call originated.
- **One-time code:** Temporary value used in account sign-in or verification; normally kept private.
- **Redaction:** Removal or obscuring of information in a shared copy through a process that prevents its recovery from that copy.
- **Official portal:** Legitimate web or app channel provided for a verified organization's transaction.
- **HTTPS:** Web connection scheme commonly providing encryption between a browser and addressed web server.
- **Digital record:** Information stored or represented electronically.
- **File permission:** Setting governing access to or modification of a file.
- **Shared link:** Web or app address that grants some level of access to a file or folder.
- **Least privilege:** Providing only the access needed for an authorized task.
- **Shared device:** Computer or mobile device used by more than one person.
- **Metadata:** Descriptive information associated with a file, photograph, or record.
- **EXIF:** Common technical metadata format used by digital photographs.
- **QR code:** Visual code that can carry text, a web address, or other data.
- **Data retention:** Continued storage of information for some period.
- **Retention schedule:** Policy or plan identifying how long records must or should be kept.
- **Secure disposal:** Handling discarded records or devices so sensitive information is appropriately protected.
- **Encryption:** Transformation of data to make it unreadable without the required key or authorization.
- **Device lock:** Mechanism limiting access to an unattended device.
- **Device encryption:** Encryption intended to protect stored device information, especially when a device is lost or stolen.
- **Backup:** Additional copy intended to support recovery after loss or failure.
- **Multifactor authentication:** Sign-in security using more than one distinct authentication factor or supported authentication step.

### Everyday sharing and data exposure

- **Mail theft:** Taking or interfering with mail without authorization.
- **Public post:** Content made available to a wide audience online.
- **Oversharing:** Revealing more information than is appropriate for an audience or purpose.
- **Data broker:** Organization that gathers or provides information about people from multiple sources.
- **Opt-out:** A request or choice not to participate in certain data uses or sharing practices.
- **Location data:** Information indicating where a device or person has been or is currently located.
- **Cloud storage:** Remote file-storage service accessed through an account or network.
- **Account recovery:** Authorized process for regaining access to a legitimate account.
- **Access revocation:** Withdrawal of a previously granted permission or credential.
- **Verification channel:** Independently trusted route used to confirm a request or identity.
- **Audit trail:** Record of selected actions, changes, or accesses within a system.

### Exposure and recovery

- **Data breach:** Incident involving unauthorized access, disclosure, or compromise of information.
- **Data exposure:** Condition where information becomes available to an unintended or unauthorized party.
- **Credit freeze:** Free U.S. credit-file restriction that can make many unauthorized new-credit applications harder.
- **Fraud alert:** Credit-file warning directing creditors to perform additional identity-verification steps.
- **Credit monitoring:** Service that observes specified changes to credit data and sends alerts.
- **Incident log:** Record of relevant incident events, contacts, actions, and reference numbers.
- **Recovery scam:** Fraud promising to recover money, identity, or access while seeking additional money or secrets.
- **Unauthorized transaction:** Financial transaction made without valid account-holder authorization.
- **Identity theft report:** Official report documenting alleged misuse of personal information for designated recovery purposes.
- **Security notification:** Message about a possible or actual security event, requiring verification when unexpected.
- **Independent verification:** Checking a request through a trusted source not supplied solely by that request.
- **Financial institution:** Bank, credit union, card issuer, or other authorized provider handling financial accounts or services.
- **Data lifecycle:** Stages through which information is created, stored, shared, used, retained, and disposed of.

The aim is **operational judgment**: a learner who says, “I will verify the official recipient, provide only the necessary fields, restrict file access, and preserve appropriate records,” has combined several vocabulary concepts into a responsible decision.

- [FTC — Protect Your Personal Information From Hackers and Scammers](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/protect-your-personal-information-hackers-and-scammers): device and account protection, phishing awareness, and handling data exposure.
- [FTC — What To Know About Identity Theft](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-identity-theft): recognition, identity protection, recovery, and monitoring.
- [FTC — Credit Freezes and Fraud Alerts](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/credit-freezes-and-fraud-alerts): differences, application process, and limitations.
- [FTC — Protecting Your Personal Information: Which Documents to Keep and Which to Shred](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/06/protecting-your-personal-information-which-documents-keep-which-shred): paper records and disposal.
- [FTC — Share With Care](https://consumer.ftc.gov/media/79866): information-sharing and privacy choices online.
- [FTC — What To Do if You Were Scammed](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-do-if-you-were-scammed): response steps based on money sent, secrets shared, or accounts compromised.
- [FTC — IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/): identity-theft reporting and personalized recovery plan.
- [FTC — ReportFraud.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/): report fraud to the FTC.
- [Social Security Administration — 10 Ways to Protect Your Personal Information](https://www.ssa.gov/blog/en/posts/2023-02-02.html): everyday identifier and document precautions.
- [CISA — Secure Our World](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world): practical online safety measures.
- [FBI — Internet Crime Complaint Center](https://www.ic3.gov/): report suspected internet crime.
- [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/): U.S. government-authorized free credit reports.

**Publication note:** This November 2026 instructional edition presents general digital-literacy principles, not individual legal, medical, financial, or identity-theft advice. Laws, service processes, breach-notification requirements, and record-retention rules vary and may change. Learners should not provide actual private data or take real-account action merely to complete an exercise.
