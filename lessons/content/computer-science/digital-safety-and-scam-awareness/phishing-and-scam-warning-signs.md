## Lesson 3: Phishing and Scam Warning Signs

A text message says that a package cannot be delivered until you pay a small fee. Another message claims to be from your bank and tells you to call a number immediately. A person using a familiar profile photograph asks you to send money to help with an emergency. Each message might seem different. Yet all three ask you to make a consequential decision using information supplied by the person who contacted you. **The key skill is not guessing which message looks professional. It is knowing when and how to verify a claim independently before you act.**

[[phishing|Phishing]] is a form of deception in which someone tries to persuade you to reveal information, open harmful content, grant access, approve a sign-in, or take another action that benefits the deceiver. A [[scam|scam]] is a deceptive scheme designed to obtain money, property, information, or other advantage. Phishing is one technique among many used in scams. Both can appear in [[email|email]], [[text-message|text messages]], telephone calls, online advertisements, chat applications, social media, search results, and sometimes communications from a compromised real account.

This is Lesson 3 of MGU's nine-part **Digital Safety and Scam Awareness** course. Lesson 1 covered password safety; Lesson 2 introduced two-factor authentication. Here you learn to evaluate the messages, stories, and demands that may attempt to bypass those protections. Lesson 4 will examine suspicious links and downloads more closely. Examples are written for ordinary computer and smartphone users, including students, office workers, older adults, people seeking jobs, small business owners, and families.

**All classroom messages, addresses, organizations, contacts, and scenarios below are fictional.** Use the examples on paper or on your class dashboard. Do not contact a suspicious sender, visit a potentially harmful website, send a verification code, download an attachment, transfer money, or change real account settings to complete an exercise. Never submit a real password, government identification number, bank account number, login code, recovery code, or private correspondence in a course response.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 115–150 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Password Safety and Two-Factor Authentication recommended · **Practice equipment:** Computer, phone, tablet, or printed worksheet; internet access optional · **Safety:** Activities use entirely fictional examples; no real messages, links, payments, accounts, or investigations are required.

> **Essential idea:** **PAUSE → IDENTIFY THE REQUEST → VERIFY INDEPENDENTLY → DECIDE → REPORT WHEN APPROPRIATE.** When an unexpected message asks you to give information, move money, open a file, sign in, install software, or act secretly, do not let the sender supply both the claim and its proof. Use a trusted route you obtained separately. You do not have to prove a message is fraudulent before refusing an unsafe request.

### Learning objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish phishing, impersonation, and broader scams while explaining how they can overlap.
1. Recognize behavioral warning signs such as urgency, secrecy, fear, unfamiliar payment requests, and pressure to ignore established procedures.
1. Evaluate fictional emails, texts, calls, social messages, advertisements, and workplace requests without trusting appearances alone.
1. Distinguish a message's apparent sender, its actual destination, and independently verifiable evidence of authenticity.
1. Select safe responses to common delivery, banking, technical-support, job, family-emergency, investment, and account-alert scams.
1. Explain what changes in your response when you merely receive a suspicious message versus when you disclose information, authorize money, or grant device access.
1. Identify suitable reporting and recovery routes without contacting a questionable person or relying on a link in the suspicious communication.

## 1. What are phishing and scams?

### 1.1 The difference between an ordinary mistake and deliberate deception

A message is not automatically a scam because it contains a typographical error, arrives unexpectedly, or uses an unfamiliar service. Organizations sometimes send genuine alerts, and humans can make mistakes. A scam involves deception intended to produce an improper benefit. A suspicious request deserves careful examination, but a learner should avoid declaring every unusual message criminal without evidence.

The practical distinction is that a warning sign is **a reason to stop and verify**, not always proof of fraud. A real bank might inform a customer about suspicious activity. A real employer might ask an employee to confirm a change in procedure. What matters is whether the request can be authenticated without trusting the same unsolicited message that initiated it, and whether the requested action is appropriate and authorized.

[[social-engineering|Social engineering]] means manipulating human judgment to obtain something that technical protections are intended to guard. An attacker may know the name of your workplace, your supervisor, the delivery company you often use, or a recent purchase. Such details can be taken from public information, leaked records, or a compromised account. Accurate personal details make a message persuasive; they do **not** independently prove that its sender is trustworthy.

### 1.2 What a phishing attempt may try to obtain

| Attacker's objective | Example of requested action | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Account access | Enter your password on an unfamiliar sign-in screen | Credentials may allow unauthorized sign-in |
| Second-factor access | Read out a six-digit code or approve an unexpected alert | Can assist an attacker who already has a password |
| Financial theft | Transfer funds to a supposed safe account | Money may be difficult or impossible to recover |
| Identity information | Upload a government ID or provide personal identifiers | Information may facilitate identity theft or impersonation |
| Device access | Install a remote-support tool on a caller's instruction | An unauthorized person may view or control the device |
| Harmful software | Open an unexpected installer or attachment | A device could be compromised or data exposed |
| Business process change | Update a supplier's bank details from an email | Future legitimate-looking payments could be redirected |
| Reputation or relationship exploitation | Forward a false claim or recruit friends | The scam can spread beyond its first recipient |

A request can involve more than one objective. For instance, a fake delivery notice may start with a small payment demand but then request card details and a verification code. A fake support call may first request device access and later demand payment. Never assume that the first requested action is the full purpose of the interaction.

### 1.3 Common forms of deception

[[impersonation|Impersonation]] is pretending to be a person or organization one is not. [[spoofing|Spoofing]] is making a technical or visible identifier appear to represent another source, such as a misleading caller ID, sender address, or display name. [[credential-harvesting|Credential harvesting]] is an attempt to collect login secrets. A [[pretext|Pretext]] is the story used to justify a request, such as a fictional account freeze or a claimed emergency.

These concepts can combine. A caller might spoof a bank's telephone number, impersonate a fraud investigator, present a pretext about stolen funds, and instruct a person to transfer money. The recipient hears one coherent story; the attacker is using several techniques to make that story believable.

Phishing may also be tailored. [[spear-phishing|Spear phishing]] is directed toward a particular person or group using details that increase plausibility. [[business-email-compromise|Business email compromise]] or **BEC** often involves an impersonated or compromised business email account used to redirect payments or sensitive records. This is especially important for people who manage payroll, purchasing, vendor invoices, or access to company information.

### 1.4 Why ordinary protections are important but insufficient

Spam filters, updated devices, unique passwords, and multifactor authentication reduce risk. Yet an attacker can still ask you to voluntarily transfer money, approve a real authentication prompt you did not initiate, send private records, or provide access to a device. Some scams do not require any software infection or stolen password; the intended action itself is the loss.

A safe routine therefore combines device and account protection with **human verification of the request**. A computer may correctly encrypt the connection to a fraudulent website; a payment application may correctly send money to the recipient you selected; a video call may successfully transmit someone's face. None of these functions alone proves the underlying story is true.

### 1.5 Worked example: a suspicious delivery text

Imagine you are waiting for an actual package. Your phone receives the following fictional text:

> **Parcel desk:** Delivery suspended. Pay a $0.47 handling charge before midnight at `https://parcel-status.example.test/restore` to prevent return of your box.

There is a plausible context: you genuinely expect a parcel. There is also a demand to pay through a new web address and a deadline intended to create pressure. The small amount may make the request seem harmless, although card information could be exposed. The right question is not only *Can I explain why I received this?* but also *Can I confirm the request in the merchant's or carrier's official order-tracking system without using anything in the text?*

A safer sequence is to stop, open the retailer's or carrier's app you already know, inspect the order using a trusted route, and contact official support if the status is still unclear. Do not reply, click, or submit a payment merely to test the message. The special `.test` address above is reserved for examples; it is not a destination students should visit.

### 1.6 The five-step judgment model

1. **PAUSE:** Do not answer immediately, click, download, disclose, or pay because a message creates pressure.
1. **IDENTIFY THE REQUEST:** State in one sentence what the sender wants and what you could lose.
1. **VERIFY INDEPENDENTLY:** Use a saved official app, a website you type from a trusted source, a published contact number, or an established internal company contact.
1. **DECIDE:** Proceed only through a verified, appropriate process. If uncertain, delay or decline rather than obey the questionable instruction.
1. **REPORT WHEN APPROPRIATE:** Use the messaging platform's reporting control, an organization's security channel, or the appropriate fraud-reporting service.

The sequence does not require expert technical knowledge. It requires separating **the claim**, **the action requested**, and **the evidence that would justify the action**.

## 2. What warning signs should you look for?

### 2.1 Pressure designed to bypass judgment

Scams often use an emotional shortcut. A person may be told that money is at risk, an account will be closed, an opportunity will disappear, a loved one is hurt, or a government official will take action unless instructions are followed immediately. These stories target the natural desire to resolve a problem or help someone. The safest response is to allow time for independent verification, even if the message insists that delay will have consequences.

[[urgency|Urgency]] is pressure to act quickly. [[fear-tactic|Fear tactics]] use frightening consequences. [[scarcity|Scarcity]] creates the impression that only a few minutes or places remain. [[reward-bait|Reward bait]] promises a prize, discount, refund, grant, or unusually easy income. [[secrecy-demand|Secrecy demands]] try to prevent a person from checking with relatives, coworkers, banks, or trusted advisers.

The combination matters more than any single word. A legitimate deadline can exist, but a demand to break security procedures, keep the matter secret, and pay by an unusual method deserves substantial skepticism.

### 2.2 A practical warning-sign table

| Warning sign | What it may sound like | Safer question to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Artificial urgency | “You have ten minutes before your account closes.” | Can I verify status in the official account? |
| Fear or punishment | “Police will arrest you unless you pay now.” | Can this claim be independently checked with the claimed agency? |
| Authority pressure | “I'm your bank's security director; obey my instructions.” | Does an independently obtained bank channel confirm this? |
| Secrecy | “Do not tell your manager or family.” | Why would a legitimate process prohibit normal verification? |
| Unusual payment | “Buy gift cards or send crypto to clear the charge.” | Is this a recognized payment method for this purpose? |
| Unexpected code request | “Read the code we just sent to prove you're you.” | Did I initiate this verified sign-in myself? |
| Redirected communication | “Do not use the normal support number.” | Why is the established channel supposedly unavailable? |
| Implausible reward | “Get guaranteed profits with no risk.” | Is the claim supported by credible independent evidence? |
| Unrequested access | “Install this remote desktop app so I can fix a warning.” | Did I knowingly request help from verified support? |
| Changed account details | “Pay this invoice to our new banking destination.” | Can I confirm the change through an existing trusted contact? |

Some scam messages contain none of these obvious phrases. They may be polite, grammatically correct, and specific. Others imitate routine workflows so well that the only reliable way to proceed is to verify the request's authority through a separate channel.

### 2.3 The request matters more than spelling

People are frequently told that phishing always contains poor grammar or strange punctuation. Those can be clues, but many fraudulent messages are professionally written, translated, personalized, or generated with digital writing tools. Conversely, a genuine family member may make several spelling errors. Avoid using writing quality as the main test.

A well-formatted logo, a familiar person's name, a photo, a postal address, or a purported legal notice also does not authenticate a sender. An attacker can copy public images and organization details. The question to return to is: **Who is asking, what do they want, and how can I confirm it without relying on their message?**

### 2.4 Suspicious payment pressure

Gift cards, wire transfers, cash, certain person-to-person transfers, and cryptocurrency can be useful in legitimate contexts, but scammers may prefer methods that are fast, difficult to reverse, or hard to recover. A request to move money to a “safe account” to protect it from fraud is a particularly serious warning. A fraud investigator should not have to invent a new destination for you to transfer all your savings.

Similarly, a “refund” request that requires a person to pay an upfront processing fee is suspect. A “job” that requires a person to deposit personal funds to unlock commissions is suspect. A supposed employer sending a check and asking the worker to return part of it or buy equipment through an unfamiliar intermediary may be running a fake-check scam. The problem is the **payment arrangement and its independently verifiable legitimacy**, not merely the choice of app.

### 2.5 A positive sign is not a guarantee

Suppose an incoming text contains your actual name, correct last four digits of a reference number, and a professional-looking company logo. That information might have been disclosed in a breach, observed from public records, or copied from a previous message. It might also be genuine. These details alone do not authorize payment or account changes.

Consider the difference between two statements:

- **Weak reasoning:** “The sender knew my name, so the message is real.”
- **Stronger reasoning:** “The message knew my name, but I checked the action through my preexisting official account and found no matching request.”

The second statement uses evidence outside the questionable communication. Sometimes the final answer will be that you cannot verify the request. **Not acting until reliable verification is available is an acceptable and responsible result.**

### 2.6 Quick triage: signal, risk, and response

| What you notice | What could be at risk | Appropriate first response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An unexpected payment instruction | Funds and payment credentials | Stop and use established payment verification |
| An unexpected sign-in code | Account access | Do not share or approve; check through the real service |
| A sudden demand for identity documents | Personal information | Do not upload; independently validate identity and necessity |
| A support call requesting remote access | Device and accounts | Decline unsolicited access; contact known support |
| A family emergency request from a new number | Money and trust | Contact the person through a preexisting number or trusted contact |
| A vendor announcing new bank details | Business funds | Call a previously verified vendor contact under company policy |
| An email that only asks for general information | Time or possible follow-up risk | Evaluate normally; avoid unnecessary fear or automatic trust |

## 3. Where do phishing and scams appear?

### 3.1 Email messages and their attachments

Email can carry false invoices, account warnings, shared-document invitations, supposed security alerts, and links to fake sign-in pages. The visible **From** name may be controlled by the sender, and addresses can be confusing or spoofed. A message may appear to be from a real contact whose account was compromised, so even a known email address is not sufficient proof of an unusual request.

Look for the requested action: does an email suddenly direct you to a new login page, ask you to wire funds, present an unrecognized invoice, or demand that you enable an access permission? [[attachment|Attachments]] should not be opened just to discover what a strange message means. The next lesson will explain how to evaluate links and files in more technical detail.

### 3.2 Text messages, messaging apps, and QR codes

[[smishing|Smishing]] refers to phishing carried out through SMS or similar text-message channels. Common stories involve package deliveries, missed toll payments, tax notices, unpaid bills, bank account alerts, and verification codes. A phone may display a message inside an existing conversation thread; that display is not conclusive proof of sender identity. Report unwanted texts using your phone's supported controls and, in the United States where supported, through **7726 (SPAM)**.

Private messaging and collaboration apps can carry similar claims, sometimes using profile pictures copied from real people. A [[qr-code|QR code]] may simply encode an address; scanning it does not establish that the address is authentic. Treat a QR code on an unexpected letter, poster, invoice, or message as another link whose destination and purpose require independent confirmation. Classroom examples do not require scanning anything.

### 3.3 Calls, voicemail, and caller ID

[[vishing|Vishing]] is voice-based phishing. A caller may impersonate a bank, technology company, courier, law-enforcement official, tax agency, utility, or relative. [[caller-id-spoofing|Caller ID spoofing]] can make an incoming number appear to match a real organization. Hearing a familiar organization name or seeing a matching number does not authenticate the caller.

Do not provide private information or codes to an unexpected caller. End the conversation politely if necessary, then call the organization through a number on its official website, your existing payment card or account statement, or another independently verified source. Do not automatically use the callback number given by the caller, even if they claim the main support line is closed.

### 3.4 Social media, job boards, and online communities

A direct message may claim that you won a contest, are being offered easy remote work, or can obtain unusually high investment returns. A fake profile can appear to belong to a friend, recruiter, charity, artist, or public figure. An established profile can also be taken over. Online followers, verified-looking artwork, and shared contacts are not proof that a private request for money is appropriate.

[[romance-scam|Romance scams]] may develop slowly. A person builds a relationship, moves conversation off-platform, then invents emergencies or encourages financial transfers. [[job-scam|Job scams]] may advertise easy pay, ask for fees before employment, or instruct applicants to deposit a check and send part of the funds away. Caution does not require distrusting every new acquaintance or employer; it requires verifying claims, protecting sensitive records, and resisting payment pressure.

### 3.5 Search ads, websites, and fake technical support

A result shown prominently in a search engine may be a paid [[sponsored-result|sponsored result]]. Advertising placement is not proof that the site is official. Fraudulent websites may imitate bank login pages, delivery portals, retailer help desks, government complaint sites, or software-update pages. Even a page using HTTPS may be fraudulent; [[https|HTTPS]] generally helps protect data in transit to the site you reached but does not guarantee that the site owner is honest.

A frightening warning inside a webpage may be [[scareware|scareware]]: content intended to make you believe your device is infected so you will call a fake support line or install something. Do not call a number supplied by an alarming pop-up. A genuine security warning from a trusted operating system or browser is different from untrusted content displayed on a webpage, but you should still avoid hastily bypassing protective alerts.

### 3.6 Synthetic voices, video, and images

[[deepfake|Deepfakes]] and other synthetic media can imitate a familiar voice, face, or manner of speaking. A caller who sounds like your relative or a video apparently depicting a company leader may still be misleading. Poorly formed hands or unnatural speech sometimes reveal manipulation, but sophisticated synthetic media can be difficult to identify; absence of visual mistakes is not proof of authenticity.

For consequential requests, verify the **request and authority** through a prearranged channel, regardless of the audio or video's appearance. A family can agree in advance to call a known number when a request arrives from a new account. A business can require second-person approval through established procedures before changing bank instructions. Neither method requires publicly exposing a private family verification phrase.

### 3.7 Comparison: the channel changes, the principle stays

| Channel | Apparent evidence that may mislead | Safer independent check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Email | Sender name, signature, or real-looking reply chain | Existing website, known contact, approved workplace process |
| Text message | Familiar company name or existing phone thread | Open preinstalled app or official service independently |
| Phone call | Caller ID, confident agent, familiar voice | Hang up and call a previously verified number |
| Social message | Profile photo, shared friends, follower count | Reach the person through a known separate channel |
| Video call | Recognizable face or claimed authority | Verify the decision through an established process |
| Search result | Top ranking, sponsored label, official-looking preview | Use a known bookmark, official document, or verified address |
| QR code | Placement on a printed sign or business document | Verify the organization and destination separately |
| Collaboration platform | Real coworker's name or a copied document title | Confirm unusual requests with the coworker in a normal channel |

## 4. How do you verify a message without following its instructions?

### 4.1 Separate four elements

For any consequential communication, identify these four elements before taking action:

1. **Claimed identity:** Who does the message say is contacting you?
1. **Requested behavior:** Does it ask for a code, payment, new login, file, permission, personal detail, or change to a workflow?
1. **Sender-controlled evidence:** Which details come from the message itself—logos, links, names, phone numbers, screenshots, or photographs?
1. **Independent confirmation:** What trusted evidence could you obtain without depending on those sender-controlled details?

When all of the evidence comes from the person demanding action, you have not independently verified anything. A second email from the same suspicious account, or a callback to the number in the original text, may merely repeat the same false claim.

### 4.2 Compare the actual domain and the familiar name

A [[url|URL]] is a web address. Its [[domain-name|domain name]] is part of the address used to identify a destination. The important task is distinguishing the real address from convincing-looking words elsewhere in the link. For this lesson, addresses ending in `.example` or `.test` are reserved fictional examples and should not be visited.

| Fictional text or address | What it tells you | Safe interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `https://accounts.school.example/login` | An illustrative host under `school.example` | It may be a coherent sample address; a real login still needs verification |
| `https://school.example.login-help.test/verify` | The host is under `login-help.test`, not `school.example` | Familiar words at the beginning do not prove school ownership |
| `https://delivery.example.test/track` | A fictional delivery-style address | Do not assume ownership because the path says “track” |
| `Security Team <alerts@notice.example>` | Display name and sender address are different data | Neither by itself proves a real security team sent it |
| “Open the secure account center” | Visible link text only | The actual destination may differ from the label |

A [[subdomain|subdomain]] can place familiar words before a different base domain. A URL can also contain a path or query text that includes an organization name without belonging to it. Some attackers use look-alike characters or long, confusing names. If you are not confident reading the address, that is a good reason to use the official app or an established bookmark instead of performing a risky experiment.

Do not type a fictional classroom URL into a real browser to see what happens. The purpose is to read the text, not investigate external systems.

### 4.3 Email addresses and displayed names are limited evidence

An email client may show only a display name until the sender details are expanded. A name such as “University Help Desk” does not demonstrate institutional affiliation. Even an address that appears familiar does not guarantee a legitimate message: an account may be compromised, a reply chain may include malicious content, or an address may be visually deceptive.

For an ordinary newsletter, the consequences of misclassification may be minor. For payroll records, authentication requests, invoices, wire instructions, account recovery, and identity documents, the consequences are much larger. Increase verification rigor as the potential impact increases. Use the institution's known procedures for sensitive information, not ad hoc explanations in the new message.

### 4.4 Why HTTPS, logos, and professional design are not enough

[[encryption|Encryption]] can protect data while it travels between your browser and the addressed site. HTTPS commonly indicates such a protected web connection. It does not promise that the website's story is truthful, that a product will be delivered, or that the person receiving a payment is authorized. A scammer can set up a secure connection to a fraudulent page.

The same limitation applies to impressive graphics and plausible documents. A fake invoice can display a correct corporate address; a forged employment contract can contain accurate job descriptions; a copied government seal can look official. Separate document appearance from independently verified authorization.

### 4.5 A verification workflow for individuals

Suppose a text claims your bank account is locked. A sensible workflow is:

1. Read the request without opening its link or calling the supplied number.
1. Open your bank's **existing** app or type an address obtained from a trusted statement or bookmark.
1. Sign in through the official method you normally use, if appropriate, and review genuine alerts or account activity.
1. If uncertainty remains, call the number on the bank card or another verified source.
1. Never share a code with the person who initiated the questionable contact, even if that person claims to be helping with verification.
1. If the bank confirms fraud, follow its official instructions for account protection and transaction review.

If you do not have access to an independent device or reference, it is safer to defer action than to use a questionable contact method merely because it is convenient.

### 4.6 Verification in school and workplace settings

An administrator, instructor, supervisor, or vendor may legitimately request information, but institutional policies often require specific approvals and secure transfer methods. A message that asks you to ignore these controls because it is urgent should be elevated, not automatically obeyed. Contact the purported requester through a directory or communication channel you already use, and notify your organization’s approved security or finance team as relevant.

For a fictional business invoice, your test might be: **Does this payment request match an approved purchase, a verified supplier, the agreed payment details, and the required authorization?** A valid-looking attachment does not replace these checks. This approach is especially important for small businesses, where one person may manage purchasing, payroll, and banking.

### 4.7 When evidence conflicts

If the message says a bank transfer is mandatory but the bank's verified app shows no such notice, do not transfer. If a colleague appears to request a secret code by chat but a trusted phone call reveals no such request, stop and report the impersonation appropriately. If official channels cannot confirm an urgent demand, consider it **unverified**, not “probably safe because time is running out.”

| Situation | Evidence you have | Best immediate conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Incoming SMS says a package is delayed | Only the message's own link | The claim is unverified; use official order tracking |
| Bank's real app shows an account alert | Independent account information | Investigate through the bank's legitimate process |
| Coworker emails new wire instructions | Email alone, regardless of formatting | Do not amend payment instructions without established approval |
| Family member appears in unfamiliar video chat | Image and voice, but no trusted second channel | Independently contact family before sending money |
| Browser shows HTTPS on a new payment site | Protected connection to that site | Connection security does not establish merchant legitimacy |
| You cannot verify an unexpected request | Incomplete information | Delay or decline; get help from a trusted source |

## 5. What do common scam stories look like?

### 5.1 Package, toll, and missed-payment messages

A common [[delivery-scam|delivery scam]] claims that a parcel needs an address correction or small fee. A [[toll-scam|toll scam]] claims you owe a road toll and threatens penalties for not paying by a deadline. These requests can be especially convincing if you recently placed an order or drove on a toll road. Do not assume coincidence proves a connection. Use the retailer's normal order status, the carrier's independently verified tracking page, or the relevant toll agency's official account.

### 5.2 Bank, fraud-department, and “safe account” claims

A person impersonating bank staff may claim to have noticed fraud and ask you to transfer funds to a new “protected” account. Another might ask for a card number, full password, one-time code, or approval prompt to “cancel” a suspicious payment. A real fraud process may involve legitimate verification, but an unsolicited caller is not authorized merely because they know account details. End contact and use your bank's own app or published customer-service line.

### 5.3 Fake invoices, subscription renewals, and refund offers

An unexpected invoice can be designed to make someone call a fake support number. The scammer may then request remote access, card data, or a new payment to reverse a supposedly mistaken charge. An unsolicited [[invoice-scam|invoice scam]] may display a purchase that never occurred; a real transaction must be checked through a trusted account statement or merchant account, not the invoice's callback details.

A refund offer may also be a pretext. A criminal might say they accidentally returned too much money and demand that you repay the difference. Do not send funds or disclose banking information on the basis of a caller's screen-sharing demonstration. Verify transactions in the real institution's account records and communicate through verified support.

### 5.4 Fake government and law-enforcement authority

A scammer may impersonate a tax authority, a benefits office, police, a court, or an internet-crime investigator. The story might promise a grant, demand a fine, threaten detention, or offer help recovering funds stolen in a previous scam. Government logos and references to real laws do not authenticate the person contacting you. Independently contact the agency through its official website and established public channels.

Fraudulent [[recovery-scam|recovery scams]] are particularly harmful because they target someone who has already been deceived. A person claiming they can recover stolen money for an advance fee may be conducting another scam. In 2026, the FBI warned about criminals impersonating the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center and using fabricated videos and websites to reach past victims. The official reporting address is **ic3.gov**; a social-media contact claiming to be IC3 is not an approved recovery process.

### 5.5 Fake technology support and malicious warnings

A call or pop-up may insist that your computer has an urgent infection, your network has been compromised, or a software license has expired. The caller may ask you to install remote-control software, read out codes, or pay for a repair that has not been verified. Do not allow unsolicited remote access. If you need help, contact a verified support service or the authorized IT staff for your device. A device showing a webpage is not the same thing as an operating-system security tool finding a real problem.

### 5.6 Jobs, fake checks, and task-based work

A job advertisement that promises exceptionally high earnings for very little work, asks you to pay upfront to become eligible, or directs you to move money for a recruiter deserves scrutiny. In a [[fake-check-scam|fake-check scam]], a supposed employer sends a check, asks the recipient to deposit it, and directs the recipient to send money elsewhere. Funds appearing temporarily in an account do not necessarily mean the check has finally cleared. If it is later rejected, the recipient may owe the amount sent away.

A [[task-scam|task scam]] offers online “optimization,” ratings, or similar simple activity and later requires workers to deposit their own money to release supposed earnings. Do not make deposits in order to unlock wages. Verify employers using independent company information and a legitimate recruiting process before handing over identity documents or financial details.

### 5.7 Online relationships and investment opportunities

A person met online may build trust and eventually request money for travel, medical emergencies, customs duties, or investments. An investment pitch that promises guaranteed, risk-free high returns is a major warning. It can be a scam even if the website displays professional charts or an account balance. Pictures of successful withdrawals, glowing testimonials, and personal affection are not independent confirmation that funds exist or are recoverable.

Do not transfer money or invest solely because an online acquaintance requests it. Verify financial claims through independently reliable sources and seek appropriate qualified advice for consequential decisions. If you suspect manipulation, speak privately with a trusted person rather than accepting a demand to keep the relationship or transaction secret.

### 5.8 A “relative in trouble” call or message

A caller says they are your child, grandchild, sibling, or friend and urgently need a transfer. They might sound familiar, claim they lost their phone, and forbid you from talking to anyone else. Modern voice imitation can make this story more persuasive. End the call and try the person's established number or contact another trusted family member. If there is an immediate physical safety concern, contact appropriate emergency services or local authorities through established channels rather than an unverified stranger's instructions.

### 5.9 Business email compromise and payment diversion

An email may say a supplier changed banks, an executive needs an emergency transfer, or a payroll deposit must be redirected. The displayed names and signature can match real staff, and the message might appear within an ongoing email conversation. Treat payment-detail changes as high-risk requests. Follow existing vendor-verification and dual-approval procedures. Confirm changes using a previously verified contact method, not the telephone number provided inside the change request.

### 5.10 Recognize the story beneath the surface

| Fictional story | Likely pressure point | Safer response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Your toll fine doubles tonight.” | Fear of penalties and urgent deadline | Check the agency independently |
| “Your package needs $0.47.” | Small-fee compliance and personal data | Use official shipping or order tracking |
| “Move your savings to our safe account.” | Fear of theft and fake authority | Stop; contact the bank's verified fraud channel |
| “Your computer is infected; call this number.” | Technical fear and claimed expertise | Close the suspect page; use trusted support |
| “Pay for training to begin this easy job.” | Hope for quick income | Independently verify employer; do not prepay for work |
| “Deposit funds to release your task earnings.” | Sunk-cost pressure and promised reward | Stop further deposits and document the interaction |
| “Your relative was arrested; keep it secret.” | Family fear and secrecy | Contact family through existing channels |
| “Your supplier has a new bank account.” | Routine appearance and workplace authority | Follow vendor callback and approval procedures |
| “We recovered your stolen money; pay a fee.” | Hope after a previous loss | Use legitimate reporting agencies; avoid recovery impostors |
| “Your sign-in code is needed to stop fraud.” | Account fear and code theft | Never relay unsolicited code; verify service yourself |

## 6. How do you respond safely and report problems?

### 6.1 If you only received the message

If you receive a questionable email, text, call, or direct message and **have not** followed links, entered credentials, granted permissions, paid, or disclosed sensitive information, your next action is usually straightforward: stop interacting, independently verify if the underlying issue matters, and use the platform's report or block tools when appropriate. A suspicious message merely appearing on the screen does not by itself prove your device or accounts were compromised.

Do not forward an active suspicious link to friends to ask whether it works. If you need advice, describe the concern without distributing clickable harmful content or personal details. For work and school messages, follow the organization's instructions for reporting suspected phishing, which may include a dedicated report button or security team.

### 6.2 If you clicked a link but did not disclose anything

Opening a suspicious webpage is not identical to installing a program or giving away a password. Stop interacting with the page. Do not accept unexpected downloads, notification permissions, login requests, or instructions to copy and execute commands. Close the tab or app if possible. Keep your device and browser updated, and follow trusted device-security guidance if you noticed a download, a warning, or unusual behavior. If the device belongs to your workplace or school, notify authorized support when policy requires.

### 6.3 If you entered a password, code, or other secret

Use the **real** service through a trusted route. Change a disclosed password promptly, especially anywhere it was reused, and review available security settings and sign-in sessions. If the account offers a way to sign out other devices, use it when appropriate. Restore or strengthen multifactor authentication and recovery settings. Contact the account provider's official support if unauthorized access appears likely. A code entered on a fake website may already have been used, so simply allowing it to expire may not be sufficient.

If the message involved your workplace account, contact your organization's IT or security team promptly. Do not delay reporting because you are embarrassed. Early notice may help protect coworkers and institutional data.

### 6.4 If money was sent or payment details exposed

Contact the **bank, card issuer, payment app, or transfer service through its official channel immediately**. Explain the transaction, ask about stopping or reversing it, and follow the institution's fraud procedures. Recovery depends on the payment method, timing, and circumstances; do not promise yourself that every transfer can be reversed. Save records such as dates, transaction references, amounts, and contact details while protecting the privacy of financial information.

If a card number was disclosed, ask the issuer about protective measures. If identity information was exposed, the Federal Trade Commission's **IdentityTheft.gov** provides guidance tailored to what was lost. Do not pay strangers who promise guaranteed recovery of stolen funds.

### 6.5 If remote access or software installation occurred

If you allowed an unexpected caller to control the device or installed software at their direction, discontinue the interaction and seek trusted technical help. Depending on the situation, authorized IT or security professionals may recommend disconnecting from networks, scanning or reimaging the system, removing unauthorized access, and changing affected credentials **from a known-clean device**. Avoid an improvised sequence of unverified commands; the correct response depends on what actually happened and whether the device is managed by an organization.

### 6.6 Where to report in the United States

The right reporting channel depends on the incident. Fraud reports can help authorities and platforms identify patterns, but **filing a report does not guarantee personal reimbursement or recovery**. Start by securing accounts and contacting financial providers when there is an immediate loss risk.

| What happened | Appropriate reporting or recovery route | Important note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Suspicious email | Email provider's report-phishing control; organization's security team | Do not forward real secrets or sensitive attachments casually |
| Suspicious SMS | Phone's report-junk option; forward to **7726** where supported | Reporting options vary by carrier and country |
| Suspected consumer scam | [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) | Reporting helps authorities; it is not a refund service |
| Identity details misused or stolen | [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) | Provides tailored recovery information |
| Internet fraud or cyber-enabled crime | [IC3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/) | Use the actual official site, not a recovery message |
| Bank transfer or card fraud | Financial institution's verified fraud department | Contact immediately, then follow other reporting guidance |
| School or workplace phishing | Approved IT/security channel or internal report button | Follow organizational incident-response policy |
| Scam or impersonation on a platform | Platform's official reporting feature | Avoid continuing a conversation with the suspected impersonator |

The FTC also publishes [advice about recognizing and avoiding phishing](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-avoid-phishing-scams) and [what to do after a scam](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-do-if-you-were-scammed). These are educational resources, not instructions to enter private case details on an unfamiliar webpage. Always type or navigate to official addresses with care.

### 6.7 A response matrix based on what actually happened

| Your last confirmed action | Immediate objective | Possible appropriate next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Read message only | Avoid engagement | Report or delete; verify important claim independently |
| Opened page only | Stop unsafe interaction | Close page and watch for further requests or downloads |
| Downloaded but did not run a file | Avoid execution | Do not open it; seek trusted scanning or IT guidance |
| Ran a suspicious file | Contain potential compromise | Contact trusted support or institutional security promptly |
| Entered password or verification code | Protect account access | Use official service to secure account and review activity |
| Approved unauthorized authentication | Investigate account compromise | Contact official support/security and review sessions |
| Granted remote access | Stop unauthorized control | End session and seek trusted device remediation |
| Sent money or card information | Limit financial loss | Contact actual financial provider immediately |
| Sent sensitive identity documents | Limit identity misuse | Use relevant identity-theft guidance and verified providers |

### 6.8 The reporting and recordkeeping principle

When a consequential incident occurs, write down the date, channel, claimed identity, actions taken, affected account or financial provider, and official case reference if one is issued. Keep records in a private and secure location. Do not include full passwords or recovery codes in the record. Preserve relevant messages where permitted and useful, but do not keep interacting with a suspected scammer to gather more evidence.

For a high-risk workplace or school incident, notify the appropriate department early and let trained staff determine preservation and technical-response steps. For personal situations, a trusted person or official consumer-support source can help you follow the appropriate process. Being cautious is not the same as handling everything alone.

### 6.9 The everyday protection routine

You do not need to become an investigator. Use long, unique passwords, protect your accounts with suitable MFA, update supported software, and recognize that a good security setting cannot decide whether a stranger's payment story is legitimate. Keep reliable contact routes for important accounts. For family and professional settings, agree on verification and approval procedures *before* an emergency appears.

> **Operational rule:** A legitimate task can wait long enough for reasonable verification. If a sender insists that you abandon ordinary protections, contact them only through their supplied link, or keep a transfer secret, treat those demands as reasons to stop rather than reasons to hurry.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answers
**Claim and request:** The message claims to be from a campus office and asks the learner to enter a password and one-time code on its chosen site.

### Activity 2: Compare suspicious delivery messages

**Fictional record:** Your phone shows a name resembling your bank. A calm caller knows your first name and states that fraudulent transfers are underway. **Your tasks:**

1. Identify at least three ways the caller attempts to establish authority.
1. Name the requested action that would create immediate financial risk.
1. Write a five-step response that avoids relying on the caller's telephone number.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model answers
**Authority cues:** A plausible bank name on caller ID, knowledge of a first name, a claim of an active fraud investigation, and an invented specialized emergency line.

**Risk:** Moving your own funds to an account the caller controls or selected could result in loss, regardless of the story about protecting the money.

**Safe response:** (1) Refuse the transfer; (2) end the call; (3) open the bank's existing app or use the official number from a card or statement; (4) ask the bank to confirm account status and any real alerts; (5) follow verified fraud procedures and document concerns securely.

**If money was sent:** Contact the bank or transfer provider through its official fraud channel immediately, report what happened, and ask what recovery or recall steps are possible. Time matters and outcomes are not guaranteed. Report the fraud to appropriate authorities such as the FTC or FBI IC3 as relevant.

### Activity 4: Protect a business from an invoice diversion

**Fictional record:** A small organization has worked with Bright Harbor Office Supply for two years. An email appears in a familiar conversation thread: “Our banking details changed. **Your tasks:**

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answers
**Known versus unverified:** The supplier relationship and invoice line items may be real. The requested destination account is a separate, consequential change that still needs validation.

**Evidence limitation:** A compromised account, manipulated reply chain, or stolen invoice could preserve accurate historical details while redirecting payment. The email's claim that a CEO approved the change is not itself a verified approval.

**Safe verification:** Use the vendor's previously documented telephone number, not the one in the new message. Follow the organization's supplier-change, callback, and multi-person approval procedures. Do not modify banking records or release payment while verification is incomplete.

**Control value:** Requiring an independent contact and an appropriate second approver makes it harder for one fraudulent message to authorize a payment change.

**Secrecy risk:** It attempts to prevent the independent checks that might reveal the deception.

**If the person cannot be reached:** Do not send money merely because the caller imposes a countdown. If the situation presents a credible immediate safety concern, seek appropriate assistance through verified emergency or local channels while continuing responsible verification.

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable mistakes

### “Phishing always contains spelling mistakes.”

Poor grammar may be a warning, but polished, personalized messages can be fraudulent. Examine the requested action, the evidence, and the verification channel. A grammatical mistake is not a substitute for analysis.

### “If the sender knows my name, the message is genuine.”

A name can be publicly available, previously leaked, or copied from other communications. Personal details alone do not authenticate the sender.

### “A company logo proves that the company sent the email.”

A logo can be copied. A display name, signature, seal, or profile picture is not a verified authorization to pay, sign in, or disclose information.

### “The caller ID matched my bank, so I should follow the caller's instructions.”

Caller ID can be spoofed. Disconnect and call the bank through a previously verified number.

### “If the message appears inside an old conversation, it is always safe.”

Phone identifiers can be spoofed or misused, and real accounts can be taken over. Verify surprising consequential requests independently.

### “A familiar voice or video always proves identity.”

Synthetic audio and video can be convincing, and genuine accounts can be compromised. Use a trusted separate channel for high-stakes verification.

### “A website with HTTPS cannot be fraudulent.”

HTTPS helps secure the connection to a site. A dishonest operator can also use HTTPS; authenticity and trustworthiness must be evaluated separately.

### “A small fee is harmless, so it is worth paying to test the message.”

A small charge can expose card information or initiate further requests. Do not enter payment details into a questionable site as an experiment.

### “An urgent deadline makes independent verification impossible.”

A deadline is precisely when you should resist instructions that bypass normal protections. Delay or decline until you can reasonably verify the request.

### “If I refuse the request, I have to prove it is a scam first.”

You may refuse or delay an unverified request. Your obligation is to make a responsible decision, not conduct an unsafe investigation.

### “An authenticator code is safe to tell support because it expires soon.”

Short-lived codes can still be used by attackers before expiration. Enter codes only into legitimate processes you initiated, not into an unsolicited caller's conversation.

### “My friend’s social media account sent it, so it is definitely my friend.”

Accounts can be taken over. Contact the person separately when a message asks for money, credentials, private documents, or unusual actions.

### “The first search result must be the official company.”

Ranking and advertising placement do not establish ownership. Verify the organization and address through trusted sources.

### “Scams only happen to careless or technically inexperienced people.”

Fraudsters target many groups and adapt their tactics. Education, established procedures, and timely reporting reduce risk; stereotypes hinder useful responses.

### “If I already paid a scammer, a recovery agent can guarantee my money back.”

Recovery is uncertain and depends on the method and circumstances. Impostors may target previous victims with additional fees. Contact the real financial provider and recognized agencies instead.

### “Receiving a suspicious email proves my computer is infected.”

Receiving or reading a message alone does not establish malware infection. Investigate according to what actually occurred, such as execution, credential disclosure, or unauthorized sign-in.

### “Reporting a scam automatically refunds my money.”

Reporting helps build intelligence and may support investigation. It does not guarantee reimbursement. Contact the actual financial provider promptly when funds are at risk.

### “If the scammer promises secrecy, I must respect it to protect the investigation.”

An unverified person cannot impose a private investigation procedure on you. Seek independent help through trusted institutions, especially for money, identity, and safety requests.

### A concise scam-warning decision guide

| Message feature | Unsafe conclusion | Better decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Familiar name or logo | “They must be official.” | Confirm identity through an independent route |
| Bank caller ID | “The caller has already proved who they are.” | Hang up and call your verified number |
| One-time code request | “Sharing the code is routine support.” | Refuse unsolicited code sharing; check the true account |
| Tiny payment amount | “I have nothing to lose.” | Do not expose payment information to unverified websites |
| Familiar voice in an emergency | “No other check is needed.” | Use an established family or organizational contact |
| Trusted-looking URL with HTTPS | “The site is honest.” | Verify the actual site and the request's legitimacy |
| New supplier payment details | “Accurate invoice means the bank change is genuine.” | Use documented vendor callbacks and approval controls |
| Promise of guaranteed recovery | “An expert has found my money.” | Use real bank and official reporting channels |
| Suspicious link merely received | “My device is already hacked.” | Do not engage; evaluate actual exposure before escalation |

> **End-of-lesson principle:** The most useful question is not “Does this look real?” but “What independent evidence authorizes me to do what this message is asking?” Confidence comes from a repeatable verification process, not from memorizing every scam story.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The dashboard's interactive vocabulary terms provide short explanations at the point of use. These definitions also appear below as ordinary text for learners who are reading offline or without a tooltip-capable interface. Do not confuse vocabulary memorization with trustworthy decision-making: an effective learner can explain what to do and why.

### Threats and deception

- **Phishing:** Deceptive attempts to persuade people into disclosing secrets or taking harmful actions, often through messages or imitation sites.
- **Scam:** A deceptive scheme seeking money, access, information, or another improper advantage.
- **Social engineering:** Manipulating people's decisions to bypass a protective process or obtain unauthorized benefit.
- **Impersonation:** Pretending to represent a person or organization without authorization.
- **Spoofing:** Manipulating an identifier or visible source detail to make a communication appear to come from another origin.
- **Credential harvesting:** Collecting usernames, passwords, or related sign-in secrets through deception.
- **Pretext:** The story or justification supplied to make a requested action sound reasonable.
- **Spear phishing:** Phishing customized for a particular person, team, or organization.
- **Business email compromise:** Payment or data fraud using impersonated or compromised business communications.
- **Smishing:** Phishing through SMS or comparable text-message channels.
- **Vishing:** Phishing through telephone or other voice communications.
- **Romance scam:** Fraud that develops trust or affection to obtain money, accounts, or other benefits.
- **Job scam:** Fraud built around a fictitious, misleading, or unauthorized job opportunity.
- **Fake-check scam:** Deception using a check that appears to provide funds and instructions to send money elsewhere before the check fails.
- **Task scam:** Fake work or commission activity that later demands deposits to release earnings.
- **Delivery scam:** Deception that impersonates a shipping or delivery process to demand action or information.
- **Toll scam:** Fraudulent demand to pay an alleged road toll or fine through an unverified route.
- **Invoice scam:** Fraudulent or manipulated payment request, sometimes used to steal money or provoke contact with fake support.
- **Recovery scam:** Fraud that promises to return previously stolen assets or resolve an earlier scam for additional payment or access.
- **Scareware:** Alarming content that falsely presents a device problem to pressure the user into a risky action.

### Persuasion and psychological pressure

- **Urgency:** Pressure to make a decision before reasonable verification can take place.
- **Fear tactic:** A claim intended to trigger alarm about a threatened loss or consequence.
- **Scarcity:** A suggestion that an opportunity or resource is limited in order to accelerate a decision.
- **Reward bait:** A promised prize, refund, income, or benefit used to attract action.
- **Secrecy demand:** An instruction not to consult ordinary trusted people or channels.
- **Authority pressure:** An attempt to secure obedience by claiming superior status or official power.
- **Independent verification:** Checking a claim using a channel or evidence not controlled solely by the person making the claim.
- **Red flag:** A feature that warrants scrutiny but does not independently prove wrongdoing.
- **Prearranged contact:** A known communication route established before the new, questionable request.

### Messages, accounts, and technical cues

- **Email:** Electronic messaging that can include text, links, and attachments.
- **Text message:** A message sent through mobile-phone or related messaging services.
- **Attachment:** A file included with or linked from a message.
- **QR code:** A machine-readable visual pattern that may contain a web address or other data.
- **Sponsored result:** Promotional or paid placement displayed in or around search results.
- **Caller ID spoofing:** Making a telephone call appear to originate from a different number or caller.
- **Display name:** Human-readable name shown beside an email, profile, or caller identifier.
- **URL:** A web address identifying an online resource or location.
- **Domain name:** A meaningful part of an internet address used to identify a destination.
- **Subdomain:** A named portion preceding another domain in a host address.
- **HTTPS:** An address scheme that normally supports encrypted communication with a web server.
- **Encryption:** Transformation of information to protect it from unauthorized reading under a security design.
- **Authentication:** Checking that a person or device is who or what it claims to be.
- **Multifactor authentication:** Sign-in using more than one independent category of verification evidence.
- **One-time code:** Short-lived value used during certain sign-in or account-verification processes.
- **Account takeover:** Unauthorized control of a person's or organization's online account.
- **Sign-in session:** An ongoing authenticated connection between a user and an online service.
- **Password:** A secret phrase or sequence used to help verify account identity.
- **Remote access:** Ability to control or observe a device from another location.
- **Malware:** Software designed or used for harmful or unauthorized activity.
- **Deepfake:** Synthetic or manipulated audio, video, or images that can misleadingly imitate people or events.

### Protection, recovery, and institutions

- **Report phishing:** Notify a provider, platform, institution, or reporting agency about a suspected deceptive communication.
- **Block sender:** Use a supported message or account control to limit contact from a sender.
- **Fraud department:** A financial institution team responsible for handling suspected fraudulent activity.
- **Incident response:** Steps to understand, limit, report, and recover from a security or fraud event.
- **Identity theft:** Misuse of identifying information to impersonate or defraud another person.
- **Payment reversal:** A potential process to cancel or recover a payment when supported and permitted.
- **Transaction record:** Evidence of a payment or transfer, including date, amount, and identifying reference.
- **Vendor verification:** Confirming a supplier's identity or banking instructions through an approved independent method.
- **Dual approval:** Requirement for more than one authorized person to approve a consequential action.
- **FTC:** United States Federal Trade Commission, a government agency that receives consumer fraud reports and provides guidance.
- **IC3:** FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center, a United States reporting channel for cyber-enabled crime.
- **CISA:** Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, a United States agency that publishes practical security guidance.

### Apply the vocabulary

Try explaining the following with your own words: *“The caller ID might be spoofed; the urgent transfer request is a red flag; and independent verification through the bank's known fraud department should happen before any transaction.”* A sound answer identifies the risk and the appropriate control, rather than relying on whether the caller sounds credible.

- [x] End the call and contact the bank by a verified route
- [ ] Read out your one-time code to prove your identity
! Do not move money at an unsolicited caller’s instruction; verify through the bank’s known channel.

- [ ] Approve because the sender name is familiar
- [x] Require independent vendor verification and approved authorization
! Supplier banking changes need established verification even when the email resembles previous messages.

- [ ] A type of encrypted backup for text messages

! Smishing is a term for text-message phishing.

- [ ] A legitimate application through a verified employer site
- [x] A requirement to pay deposits to release earned wages

## 11. MGU Library connections and next steps

### What you should now be able to do

You should be able to read an unexpected communication and identify its claim, requested action, emotional pressure, and verifiable evidence. You should understand why logos, display names, HTTPS, caller ID, voice recordings, and even accurate personal details can appear in deceptive situations. Most importantly, you should know how to **pause, verify through an established route, refuse unverified requests, and respond proportionately if information or funds were exposed**.

Three practical behaviors deserve repetition: first, do not surrender a password, one-time code, or recovery detail to an unsolicited person; second, independently confirm payment, identity-document, device-access, and account-change requests; third, contact actual providers quickly when accounts, devices, money, or identity information may have been compromised.
