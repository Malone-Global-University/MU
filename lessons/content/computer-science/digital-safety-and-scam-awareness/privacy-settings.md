Basic Computer Skills / Digital Safety and Scam Awareness

## Lesson 5: Privacy Settings

You install a weather application, and it asks to know your precise location all the time. A video-meeting website asks to use your microphone. A shopping website offers to remember your preferences, while a social network makes a photograph visible to people you have never met. Each request involves a different part of your digital life, but the same practical question: **What information or device capability is being shared, with whom, for what purpose, and for how long?**

[[privacy-settings|Privacy settings]] are controls that help you limit or manage access to personal information, device features, account details, online activity, and shared content. They do not make a service completely private. They also cannot undo every disclosure that has already occurred. Learning to find and interpret these controls is a basic computer skill, not a specialized technical task. A learner who knows how to **review, decide, change, and verify** a setting is better positioned to use technology on their own terms.

This is Lesson 5 of MGU's nine-part **Digital Safety and Scam Awareness** course. Earlier lessons addressed password safety, two-factor authentication, phishing, and suspicious links and downloads. Here the emphasis shifts from recognizing malicious activity to controlling *ordinary, legitimate* information sharing as well as unexpected permission requests. Microsoft Windows 11 and Microsoft Edge provide principal desktop examples; Google Chrome, Android, and Apple devices are also covered. Menu wording can change with software versions. The objective is to understand what a control does, not memorize a fixed sequence of clicks.

**Practice safety:** All examples, names, accounts, and permission situations are fictional. The six activities require no actual account, photographs, contact list, location history, medical details, financial information, device access, or changes to real settings. You may use paper or a blank note to complete them. Optional observation on your own device should occur only if you are authorized to use it. Never experiment with someone else's account, a shared or workplace system, or a device involved in a personal-safety concern.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 115–150 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Digital Safety and Scam Awareness Lessons 1–4 recommended · **Practice equipment:** This lesson or a printed copy; optional personal device · **Safety:** Observation-first; no personal disclosures or permission changes required.

> **Essential idea:** **IDENTIFY → QUESTION → LIMIT → VERIFY → REVIEW.** Identify the information or capability involved; question whether it is needed; limit access to a suitable scope; verify what your decision changed; and review later when your needs or the service changes. When uncertain, you may postpone a permission request and ask a trusted, authorized person for help.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Describe how privacy differs from security and why privacy choices involve real-world tradeoffs.
1. Recognize common categories of information and device access that apps and websites may request.
1. Locate and interpret major privacy controls in Windows 11, a browser, and a phone using the device's built-in Settings application.
1. Explain the differences among device-level permission, app-level permission, website permission, and account-sharing settings.
1. Make reasoned choices about location, camera, microphone, contacts, photos, notifications, cookies, advertising preferences, and social-post audiences.
1. Explain why private browsing, blocking trackers, deleting history, and changing a visible audience have limits.
1. Construct a reversible review plan and select an appropriate next step when permissions or information sharing are unexpected.
1. Complete six fictional practice activities and a 30-question self-study knowledge check.

## 1. What do privacy settings control?

### 1.1 Privacy is about information and appropriate access

[[personal-information|Personal information]] is data connected to a person, whether it identifies that person directly or becomes identifying when combined with other information. Examples include a name, email address, contact information, photographs, approximate or precise location, financial records, account identifiers, browsing behavior, and an organization's records about a person. Some pieces seem ordinary in isolation but become sensitive in combination. A picture of a street sign, a public profile name, and a recurring location can reveal more together than each does alone.

[[privacy|Privacy]] concerns the appropriate collection, use, disclosure, retention, and control of that information. [[security|Security]] concerns protecting systems and information from unauthorized access or damage. Strong [[authentication|authentication]] can protect a private account, but it does not decide whether a legitimate service should advertise your activity. Restricting an audience can help privacy, but it does not replace a strong password. Both concerns matter, and they require different controls.

**Worked example:** A fictional student named Maya uses a legitimate calendar app. Her account has a strong password and two-factor authentication, so unauthorized logins are harder. She also checks whether her calendar is shared with other people and whether events are public, because **account security alone does not determine the audience**. The correct privacy decision is about who needs to see which calendar details.

### 1.2 Collection, use, sharing, and retention are separate questions

Privacy is not determined by a single on/off button. A service may collect information when you sign up, receive information from your device, analyze activity to improve a feature, share selected data with partners, or keep records for account security or legal obligations. A setting might affect one of those operations without affecting the others. For example, disabling personalized advertisements may change how advertisements are selected without preventing every form of data collection or making past records disappear.

| Privacy question | Example | Useful control or response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is collected? | A weather app requests location | Review the location permission and its purpose |
| When is it collected? | An app requests access all the time | Prefer a narrower time or usage scope when available |
| How precise is it? | A service wants exact position | Consider approximate location if it meets the need |
| Who can access it? | A photo album is shared by link | Inspect the recipient or link-sharing setting |
| Who can see it publicly? | A profile displays contact details | Review visibility and audience controls |
| How long does it remain? | A service keeps saved activity | Examine retention and deletion options |
| Can it be recovered? | A device syncs settings to an account | Verify recovery and synchronization behavior |
| What happens after a change? | A setting is disabled | Check whether the change applies to new access or existing copies |

### 1.3 Permissions are requests for a defined capability

A [[permission|Permission]] is an authorization that may let an application or website use a capability, such as a camera, microphone, location, photos, contacts, or notifications. The decision should consider **purpose**, **scope**, **timing**, **recipient**, and **alternatives**. A navigation app may reasonably need location while giving directions. A simple flashlight app generally has little reason to read an entire contact list. A video-meeting app may need a microphone during a call, but not necessarily while the app is closed.

Permissions can operate at different levels. The operating system can authorize an application to access a camera; a browser can separately ask whether a specific website may use that camera. Giving permission at one level does not mean every website should automatically have it. A service account may have another independent setting governing who can see a saved recording. The learner should identify *which layer* they are reviewing before assuming a button solves the entire privacy question.

### 1.4 Privacy settings support choices, not perfect secrecy

A change may protect future access without retracting information already shared. A friend may have downloaded a photograph before you changed the audience. A website may hold records under its published retention practices. A platform may disclose information in circumstances described in its policies or required by law. A browser setting cannot force a recipient to erase a copy they already received. These limitations are reasons to make thoughtful decisions **before** sharing, not reasons to abandon privacy controls altogether.

The most useful mindset is [[data-minimization|data minimization]]: provide only the information genuinely needed for an appropriate task. If a school form requires an email address, do not volunteer unrelated financial or identity details. If a map can show local results without exact location, approximate location or manual entry may be sufficient. Minimization should be balanced with the feature's real function and a person's accessibility or emergency needs.

### 1.5 A five-question permission test

When a setting or request appears, ask these questions:

1. **Purpose:** What legitimate task does this information help complete?
1. **Necessity:** Could I complete the task without granting this access?
1. **Scope:** Could I choose selected photos, approximate location, only while using, or a limited audience?
1. **Control:** Can I later inspect and revoke this permission or sharing arrangement?
1. **Consequence:** What will stop working, or what previously shared information may remain, if I change it?

A request is not automatically suspicious merely because it asks for permission. Many services need access to work. Your job is to decide whether the access is appropriate for **that specific feature and that specific moment**.

## 2. How do you review privacy settings in Windows 11?

### 2.1 Start with the operating system's own Settings

On a personally controlled Windows 11 computer, you can usually open **Start → Settings → Privacy & security**. Within that area, the available categories may include Location, Camera, Microphone, Contacts, Calendar, Diagnostics & feedback, and other controls. Exact organization and wording can differ by Windows release, device features, account type, and management policies. Search within Settings for the topic if the interface has changed.

A Windows privacy control can apply to the device, to apps supplied through certain app frameworks, or to desktop applications as a broader category. It is important not to assume that every traditional desktop application appears with an individually adjustable switch. A browser may also have its own permission controls. Some device features, such as cameras, can have physical switches, shutters, or hardware indicators whose availability depends on the model.

**Observation-first procedure:** Open the Settings app only if it is your own device or you have clear permission. Find Privacy & security, identify a category, read the explanatory text, and record which option you would choose for a fictional application. Do not switch off a setting simply because it exists. If the computer is managed by a school or employer, settings may be locked for valid organizational purposes.

### 2.2 Review the meaning of Windows permission categories

| Windows privacy area | Information or capability involved | Example of an appropriate question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Location | Device or app access to location information | Does this app require precise location to work? |
| Camera | Use of camera hardware | Is the app expected to capture video or images? |
| Microphone | Use of microphone hardware | Do I need audio capture during this activity? |
| Contacts | Address-book information available through supported interfaces | Is this service supposed to use my contacts? |
| Calendar | Schedule-related data | Does the feature require access to events? |
| Notifications | Device messages and sometimes sensitive on-screen previews | Who can see notifications on this screen? |
| Diagnostics & feedback | Information Windows may send about device operation | Which options are required or optional? |
| Search or activity preferences | How selected activity and suggestions are handled | What is stored locally or associated with an account? |

These labels describe common functions, not a promise that every device displays the same menu. Review the description *on your actual device* before drawing conclusions. Turning off access for one class of apps may not govern every desktop program or system component. For a critical privacy question, use current vendor documentation and appropriate authorized support.

### 2.3 Camera and microphone: know both permission layers

Suppose Maya joins a legitimate video lesson through Microsoft Edge. First, Windows must permit the browser to use the relevant hardware. Second, the website may ask the browser for its own permission to use the camera or microphone. Third, the conferencing service may offer in-meeting controls such as muting audio or turning video off. The three controls serve different purposes.

If a camera is blocked in Windows, granting it to the website alone may not make it work. If Windows allows the camera, the website still should not be trusted automatically. If a meeting is muted, that is not the same as permanently removing camera or microphone permissions. Recognize the layer you want to change. Follow the software's current indicators and test with a trusted service when appropriate rather than activating the microphone for a random page.

### 2.4 Location: the smallest useful amount

[[location-services|Location services]] may estimate a device's location through several technologies, including network information, sensors, and other signals. A setting may let an app access location only under certain conditions, and some systems permit a distinction between [[precise-location|precise location]] and [[approximate-location|approximate location]]. For emergency calls and essential system functions, devices may use location in ways not fully controlled by an ordinary app toggle. Never disable a safety-critical feature without understanding the consequence.

For a weather forecast, entering a city or permitting approximate location may be enough. For turn-by-turn navigation, precise location while navigating may be justified. An app's request for location in the background should receive closer review. Location can also appear in photographs, check-ins, shared maps, or account activity independently of the app permission you changed.

### 2.5 Diagnostics, personalized experiences, and cloud synchronization

Windows may present settings related to [[diagnostic-data|diagnostic data]], personalized experiences, advertising preferences, activity history, or [[sync|synchronization]]. Some transmission or processing may be necessary to maintain core functionality, while optional controls can affect personalization or additional data use. Review the definitions, since the word “optional” does not always mean the same thing across every screen. Turning off one preference should not be interpreted as deleting all data previously held by a service.

A cloud-connected Windows account can synchronize selected preferences or content across devices. This can make recovery and daily work easier. It can also mean that an action on one device affects others. Ask whether the information is stored only on the current device, in the linked account, or in both places. If you intend to delete data, distinguish disabling future collection from removing existing cloud records.

### 2.6 Managed computers require an authorization check

If a computer says that certain settings are “managed by your organization,” the limitation may reflect a workplace, school, or family-management policy. Do not try to bypass it for an assignment. Likewise, a borrowed device may display private data belonging to its owner. Review privacy concepts through the fictional exercises and consult authorized support. **Being technically able to change a setting is not the same as having permission to change it.**

## 3. How do you manage browser privacy and website permissions?

### 3.1 A browser has its own privacy layer

A [[browser|web browser]] works between you and websites, and can maintain records such as [[browsing-history|browsing history]], saved site data, and individual [[site-permission|site permissions]]. It can also provide [[tracking-prevention|tracking prevention]], private browsing, and controls over [[cookies|cookies]]. These controls are related but not interchangeable. A website's ability to send notifications is different from whether it can store a cookie. A website that is allowed to use your camera is different from an advertisement tracker that measures browsing activity.

In current Microsoft Edge versions, privacy-related settings are generally under **Settings → Privacy, search, and services**, with site-permission controls within the browser's settings. In Google Chrome, they are generally under **Settings → Privacy and security → Site settings**. An option may move or be renamed with browser updates. The goal is to find the relevant category and examine what it grants to a specific site, not memorize one exact click path.

### 3.2 Website permissions are specific choices

| Website request | Why a legitimate site might ask | Appropriate beginner review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Camera | Video meeting or identity verification | Is this the expected service and the right time? |
| Microphone | Voice call or speech input | Can I allow only for this task? |
| Location | Nearby results or local map | Could I enter a city instead? |
| Notifications | Alerts about messages or appointments | Do I want ongoing messages from this site? |
| Clipboard | Copying or pasting information | Is the requested operation expected and safe? |
| Pop-ups or redirects | A workflow needing another window | Is the site legitimate and the behavior necessary? |
| Automatic downloads | Receiving several files in a sequence | Is this an expected and trusted workflow? |
| Saved site data | Session, cart, or preferences | Can I manage what remains after the visit? |

Do not grant a permission merely to remove an annoying message. A genuine site may work perfectly well after you choose **Block** or **Not now**, although the feature requiring that permission will not. Some permissions can be limited to a session; others persist until changed or automatically expire. When a choice is unclear, cancel the request and examine the real task before continuing.

### 3.3 Cookies and site data can be helpful and intrusive

A [[cookie|cookie]] is a small piece of site-associated data that often helps maintain a login, remember a shopping cart, or store preferences. Some cookies are set by the site you visit; some may come from other services incorporated into the page, depending on browser protections and settings. Cookies are not inherently malware. Yet persistent identifiers and other mechanisms may contribute to [[cross-site-tracking|cross-site tracking]] or personalization.

Blocking third-party cookies or limiting trackers can reduce some forms of tracking but does not eliminate them all. Websites can sometimes identify signed-in users directly, observe activity on their own pages, or use other techniques. Deleting cookies may sign you out or reset site preferences; it generally does not close an account or erase information held on the website's servers. Use targeted clearing when you want to fix one site's behavior, and read the implications before removing all site data.

### 3.4 Privacy controls have practical tradeoffs

| Browser action | Typical benefit | Important limitation or consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Block a website's microphone | Prevents that site from recording through the browser permission | A needed voice-call feature may stop working |
| Turn off site notifications | Stops that site's browser notifications | You might miss a wanted message or reminder |
| Limit third-party cookies | Reduces some third-party tracking | Some sign-in or embedded features may change |
| Enable tracking prevention | Limits certain tracking mechanisms | It cannot stop every form of data collection |
| Delete browsing history | Removes selected records stored by the browser | It does not automatically delete server logs or account history |
| Clear saved cookies | Removes certain local site identifiers | You may be logged out and lose preferences |
| Use a private window | Reduces retention of some browsing records on that device | It does not make activity invisible to websites or network operators |
| Reset one site's permissions | Removes or changes that site's browser grants | It does not alter operating-system app permissions |

### 3.5 Private browsing is not anonymity

A [[private-browsing|private browsing]] mode—sometimes called InPrivate or Incognito—generally limits certain information that remains in the local browser after a session ends. It is useful when another person might later use the same browser, but it does **not** make a user invisible to websites, internet service providers, schools, or employers managing a network. Websites still receive requests and may recognize a signed-in account. Downloads and bookmarks can also remain after the private session, depending on the browser and action.

If privacy from someone who shares or controls a device is a serious personal-safety concern, do not assume private windows or clearing history will make activity undiscoverable. Devices, accounts, notifications, synced records, and monitoring systems can retain evidence. Seek appropriate specialized help using a device and communication channel that are safe for your circumstances.

### 3.6 Worked example: a calendar site asks to notify you

Maya visits her legitimate school calendar in Edge. It asks: “May this site send notifications?” She understands that notifications are not required to *read* the calendar. If she wants real-time reminders on her personal device, she may allow them after confirming the actual website. If she shares the computer, does not want sensitive reminders on screen, or prefers email reminders, declining the request may be more appropriate. She records her decision and knows she can revisit the website's permissions later.

Notice the reasoning: the request may be legitimate, yet “legitimate” does not mean “necessary.” Privacy decisions should match what the person actually wants the technology to do.

## 4. How do privacy settings work on phones and tablets?

### 4.1 Phones concentrate information in one device

A phone may contain messages, address books, photos, cameras, microphones, location sensors, payment applications, and accounts. A mobile [[app|app]] therefore can ask for several categories of access. The mere presence of a permission request is not proof of danger, but the range of available information makes careful choices important. Review permissions when installing a new app, after an update changes features, or when an app seems to use information unexpectedly.

On iPhone and iPad, many permission controls appear under **Settings → Privacy & Security**. On Android, controls often appear in **Settings → Security & privacy** or **Privacy**, with **Permission manager** or an equivalent menu. Some Android devices place app-specific settings under **Settings → Apps**. Exact names vary by device manufacturer and software release; use the device's Settings search if needed.

### 4.2 Narrow permission choices often work better than all-or-nothing

| Permission choice | General meaning | Suitable example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deny or do not allow | App cannot use that requested capability through the normal permission | A game does not need contact-list access |
| Ask every time | App requests permission for each applicable use | A rarely used location feature |
| Allow while using the app | Access is tied to active app usage under the system's rules | Map access while checking directions |
| Allow once | A temporary grant for a specific use or session when supported | One-time location lookup |
| Allow all the time | Background access may continue when supported | A legitimate, intentionally enabled background service |
| Approximate location | Less precise location information | General local weather |
| Precise location | More exact location information | Turn-by-turn navigation |
| Selected photos | Access to only the chosen pictures where supported | Uploading two photographs to a project |

Different platforms, permission types, and apps offer different combinations. If you do not see an option in this table, do not assume your device is malfunctioning. Select among the choices actually offered and assess the consequences. The narrowest useful scope is often a sensible starting point, but emergency, accessibility, and other essential functions can justify wider access after informed review.

### 4.3 Camera, microphone, photos, and contacts

A video-chat application may need the camera and microphone. A photo-editing app may need to select photos. An app that offers to “find friends” might request contacts. The fact that a feature is available does not mean everyone should use it. Uploading a photograph also sends that selected image to the service; **restricting photo-library permission does not by itself control the recipient's storage or sharing of an uploaded photo**.

Some devices display camera or microphone indicators while an app is using those features. Such indicators can help someone notice activity, but they do not necessarily explain the full purpose, recipient, or retained content. If unexpected access appears, stop using the app, review permissions, and consult trusted support when appropriate. Do not investigate by sending private files or granting additional access to an unfamiliar party.

### 4.4 Location sharing and tracking controls are not the same

A phone's location-permission setting governs access through that operating system's mechanisms; a map service may also offer a separate account feature that shares your location with chosen people. A messaging app might include a live-location feature. A photograph may contain location metadata. Disabling an advertising tracking request is not the same as ending live location sharing. Identify *all relevant sharing channels*, particularly before making a decision that depends on keeping your whereabouts private.

On supported iPhones, **Settings → Privacy & Security → Tracking** governs certain app tracking permission requests across other companies' apps and websites. On Android, advertising and privacy controls depend on version and device configuration. These settings can reduce specified tracking or advertising uses; they do not erase information already provided or guarantee that no service collects anything about you.

### 4.5 Built-in permission and activity reviews

Supported iPhones provide privacy information and may offer an [[app-privacy-report|App Privacy Report]] showing certain app access or network activity. Supported Android devices may offer a [[privacy-dashboard|Privacy Dashboard]] showing recent use of sensitive permissions. These tools help learners ask why an app accessed a capability, but displayed history, categories, and time periods differ. The absence of a visible entry does not prove that no personal information was collected through other means.

**Optional observation-only walkthrough:** On your own device, locate the privacy section, identify one permission category, and read the available choices. Then exit without making changes. You have completed the exercise if you can explain what the choices mean and where you would return to review them later.

### 4.6 Worked example: a recipe application requests contacts

A fictional recipe app offers to share meal plans with friends and asks for the entire address book. Maya only wants to read recipes. She denies contacts, continues with the reading function, and later considers whether manually entering a friend's address would be sufficient if she chooses to share a plan. If the app refuses to work without unnecessary access, she can seek a different service. This is a measured choice about purpose and proportionality, not a claim that every request for contacts is fraudulent.

## 5. How do you control account sharing and visibility?

### 5.1 Accounts can share information even without device permissions

A [[service-account|service account]] has settings that govern activity and visibility on the provider's system. An app may be denied location access but still show an address you previously entered on a profile. A browser may block microphone use but a saved recording may remain shared under a meeting account. Account-based [[audience-control|audience controls]] need review separately from operating-system and browser permissions.

A useful audit asks what appears publicly, what is visible to selected contacts, what is shared by link, what remains private to the account, and what the provider itself retains. “Only friends” can still be a large or changing audience. Friends may copy or redistribute material. A shared link can sometimes be forwarded beyond the person you originally intended. Check an audience indicator before posting rather than relying on memory of a previous setting.

### 5.2 Common audience and sharing controls

| Content or feature | Potential audience | Review question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Profile name and picture | Public visitors or selected contacts | What can strangers see before connecting? |
| Posts and stories | Public, followers, friends, groups, or selected people | Who will see this particular item? |
| Friend or follower lists | Everyone or a limited audience | Does this expose other people's relationships? |
| Location check-ins | A selected audience or the public | Could this disclose a current or recurring location? |
| Shared album or file | Invited people or anyone with a link | Can recipients download, edit, or re-share? |
| Calendar availability | Colleagues, family, or group members | Do they need event details or only free/busy status? |
| Search discoverability | Search engines or other account holders | Can someone find this profile through contact information? |
| Activity status or receipts | Contacts or conversation participants | Do I want others to see when I am online or have read a message? |

A privacy setting changes the rules a service applies to certain viewers. It does not create an enforceable promise that recipients will never screenshot, forward, record, or copy content. For consequential information, first decide whether it needs to be posted or shared at all.

### 5.3 Location, photos, and information about other people

Photographs can reveal addresses, schools, workplaces, schedules, children, license plates, documents in the background, or location markers. Some images contain [[metadata|metadata]] that identifies the time or place where they were created. Platforms may remove, change, or preserve parts of that metadata depending on their workflows; do not rely on any platform to remove it automatically. Review both what the photo visibly shows and how it will be shared.

Other people have privacy interests too. Ask before posting a photograph of a friend, another person's location, or a document with their information. A convenient “tag everyone” feature is not a substitute for consent. Workplaces and schools may have additional approved practices for photographs, attendee lists, and file sharing.

### 5.4 Connected apps, account activity, and ad preferences

A service may allow other applications to connect to your account using an [[authorized-integration|authorized integration]]. Reviewing connected apps can reveal old services you no longer use. Disconnecting an integration may stop future authorized access, but it may not erase data already copied into that service. Read the service's controls for removal and deletion separately, and use its official account dashboard rather than a link from an unexpected message.

An [[advertising-preference|advertising preference]] may let you reduce personalized ads or manage certain categories. This can be useful, but disabling personalized ads does not necessarily stop ads, remove tracking by every party, or delete records. The FTC advises consumers to understand how websites and apps collect information and to review their privacy controls. Treat marketing settings as one element of privacy management, not a comprehensive protection system.

### 5.5 Public, shared, and private are different contexts

A person may use one computer for personal email, business work, and a community organization. Sensitive documents should be shared only through the authorized channel for the particular purpose. A cloud document set to “anyone with the link” can be more widely accessible than a document shared only with named recipients. An address book synced to a personal account might be inappropriate on a shared workplace device. Clarify which account, recipient, and device you are operating under before changing sharing permissions.

### 5.6 Worked example: a family album link

Maya prepares a fictional family photo album. The service offers three choices: **Only me**, **Invited people**, and **Anyone with the link**. Because she wants two relatives to view it, she chooses invited people if the service supports it, confirms the recipient identities, checks whether downloads or re-sharing are allowed, and previews the resulting access where an appropriate tool is available. She does not assume that forwarding a private invitation can always be prevented, and she asks relatives before sharing photographs of them.

## 6. How do you maintain and verify privacy choices?

### 6.1 Review settings as services and circumstances change

Privacy review is not a one-time setup. An application may gain a new feature, an update may reorganize a menu, a person may stop using a service, or a previously suitable audience may no longer be appropriate. A reasonable routine is to review major categories when you install an app, create an important account, begin a new role, share a device, or notice an unexpected request. A periodic account and permission review can catch unused integrations and public sharing links.

Do not make changes for the sake of changing them. Keep settings aligned with legitimate requirements, accessibility accommodations, and your actual usage. If a feature stops working after you tighten a permission, determine whether its required access can be granted more narrowly rather than disabling unrelated security features.

### 6.2 Make a brief decision record

A [[privacy-review|privacy review]] becomes more effective when the person can explain their choice. A simple record can use these fields: **service or device; data or capability; reason requested; audience or recipient; chosen permission; how to verify; when to reconsider**. Do not write real passwords, recovery codes, account identifiers, or confidential details in a practice worksheet. The purpose is to document a decision process, not create another sensitive database.

| Review stage | Question | Example response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identify | Which device, app, website, or account? | Fictional classroom conferencing site |
| Locate | Which permission or sharing menu? | Browser microphone site permission |
| Assess | Why is the access requested? | Spoken participation in a class |
| Limit | What is the smallest sufficient scope? | Microphone for the meeting only, if supported |
| Verify | What changed in the authorized interface? | Browser indicates the intended site has permission |
| Revisit | When should this be reviewed? | After the meeting or when no longer needed |

### 6.3 Revoking access and deleting data are not equivalent

[[revoke-access|Revoking access]] generally stops or restricts a future permission or sharing route. [[delete-data|Deleting data]] requests the removal of information from a selected location or service. [[sign-out|Signing out]] ends or reduces an authenticated session. [[unsubscribe|Unsubscribing]] from marketing messages changes a communication preference. These actions have different consequences. None guarantees that every previously shared copy or lawful retained record vanishes.

If you want to reduce a service's access, first identify what level currently grants it: device setting, app permission, browser site permission, connected-account authorization, shared link, or public account audience. Then choose the corresponding control. A single universal “privacy off” switch does not exist.

### 6.4 When something unexpected occurs

If an app suddenly asks for contacts, a website unexpectedly requests a camera, or a public document appears to reveal information, pause and determine the actual event. An unexpected permission request does not prove that information was already accessed. A discovered public file may require prompt attention from its owner or an authorized administrator. If you disclosed account credentials, follow the recovery practices in the earlier lessons and change passwords through the real provider. If you suspect identity theft or financial fraud, use official reporting and recovery services.

For managed school or workplace accounts, report through the designated internal process instead of modifying shared access without authorization. For a situation involving stalking, harassment, coercive monitoring, or someone with physical access to the device, changing settings may alert that person or disrupt essential access. A safer response may involve support from a qualified advocate or authorized professional using a separate trusted device.

### 6.5 A privacy-versus-functionality decision example

A navigation app may need exact, current location to guide a driver through an unfamiliar area. Granting that access during navigation could serve a legitimate, immediate purpose. Leaving continuous background access active after navigation ends may offer no comparable benefit for that user. Conversely, a safety-related service designed for opted-in continuous location sharing may require background access to function as intended. The right answer depends on *the purpose, consequences, consent, and alternatives*—not simply choosing the most restrictive switch every time.

### 6.6 The verification loop

After any authorized setting change, verify what you can safely observe: does the permission now display the chosen state; does the account show the intended audience; is the affected feature still usable; and can you find the control again? Some changes may require a restart, a new browser session, or a separate account setting. Avoid making several unrelated changes at once, because you may not know which action caused a result. If a privacy outcome is consequential, consult the provider's current official guidance rather than assuming a label covers every possible data flow.

> **Decision standard:** Privacy management is successful when you can describe **what was shared or blocked, which control you used, what the change can and cannot do, and when the setting should be reviewed again**.

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

### “Privacy settings are only for people who have something to hide.”

Privacy is ordinary information management. Everyone benefits from controlling the audience for photographs, who can see a schedule, and which services can access device features. Wanting limited disclosure is not evidence of wrongdoing.

### “A secure account is automatically private.”

A strong password and two-factor authentication reduce unauthorized account access, but your own public profile or broadly shared files may still be visible as configured. Review account security and sharing separately.

### “A legitimate app should receive every permission it requests.”

An app can be genuine yet request access that is optional, overly broad, or unnecessary for your specific use. Grant only access with a clear purpose and appropriate scope.

### “If I deny one permission, the entire app must stop working.”

Often only a particular feature is affected. Denying a microphone may prevent voice input but allow text chat. Some apps really do require a permission for their core task, so judge the specific dependency rather than assuming either outcome.

### “The privacy menu on my phone controls every website and connected account.”

Device, application, browser, service-account, and sharing controls operate at different levels. Check the settings associated with the actual data or capability you want to control.

### “When a privacy dashboard shows no activity, nothing has been collected.”

A permission dashboard covers selected operating-system events and periods. A service may still receive information you type, upload, or share, and may retain earlier account activity.

### “Disabling personalized ads prevents all advertising and tracking.”

An advertising preference may reduce certain personalization uses. It does not necessarily stop all advertisements, all tracking, or all storage of account information.

### “Cookies are viruses, so every cookie is dangerous.”

Cookies frequently enable basic sessions and preferences. They can also participate in tracking, so review site data and third-party restrictions without confusing ordinary browser storage with malware.

### “Deleting browsing history deletes information from every website.”

Local history and server-side information are different. A website may retain account records even after you erase browser history.

### “Private browsing makes me invisible to websites and the network.”

Private browsing reduces selected local browser traces; it does not make a signed-in user anonymous or guarantee invisibility to website operators, schools, employers, or internet providers.

### “A private post can never be copied by someone else.”

Recipients may screenshot, save, forward, or quote content. Audience limits are helpful, but thoughtful sharing is still necessary.

### “Turning off location permission hides every trace of my location.”

Services may have manually entered addresses, previous records, shared map links, image metadata, or other information. A location toggle affects defined access, not every source of location information.

### “If I change the setting, all previous copies are erased.”

Restricting future access is not the same as deleting stored records or retrieving previously downloaded files. Explore retention and deletion controls separately where appropriate.

### “The most restrictive settings are always best for everyone.”

Strong limits are often useful, but a person may legitimately need precise location for directions, microphone access for an accessible lesson, or background service for an agreed safety feature. Informed, proportionate control matters more than blindly selecting “off.”

### “I should override my employer's restrictions for privacy.”

Workplace and school systems can impose legitimate management controls. Do not bypass them. Ask authorized administrators about relevant policies and permitted settings.

### “If someone may be monitoring my device, I should immediately change every setting.”

In a coercive or unsafe situation, abrupt changes can create additional risk or alert an abusive person. Seek confidential professional assistance using a safer channel appropriate to the circumstances rather than following a generic technical checklist.

### A compact decision guide

| When you notice... | Do not assume... | More reliable first step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A website requests microphone access | The browser or system must grant it | Confirm the site and the task that needs audio |
| A photo app asks for the entire library | The broadest scope is unavoidable | Look for a selected-photo option |

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The dotted-underlined terms in this lesson use the MGU dashboard's contextual definition system. Selecting a supported term displays a brief explanation; some entries also point to an authoritative reference. This visible glossary remains useful even when the interactive controls are unavailable. The words are grouped by the decisions a learner makes rather than alphabetically.

### Privacy principles and information

- **Privacy:** Appropriate control over how information is collected, used, disclosed, and retained.
- **Privacy settings:** Controls for selected information, device capabilities, sharing arrangements, and activity preferences.
- **Personal information:** Data that identifies or relates to a person alone or in combination with other information.
- **Sensitive information:** Information whose exposure could create significant personal, financial, professional, or safety risks.
- **Security:** Measures that protect information and systems from unauthorized use, loss, or harm.
- **Authentication:** A process used to verify someone or something seeking access to an account.
- **Data minimization:** Providing or collecting only what is reasonably necessary for an intended legitimate purpose.
- **Consent:** An informed and voluntary agreement to a specific action or information use.
- **Data collection:** Receiving or gathering information from people, devices, or online activity.
- **Data processing:** Using information for an activity such as storage, analysis, or providing a service.
- **Data retention:** Keeping information for a selected period under specified rules or practices.
- **Data deletion:** Removing data from a specified system or location under an applicable process.
- **Data disclosure:** Making information available to another person, service, or organization.
- **Privacy policy:** A document explaining an organization's stated information-handling practices.
- **Privacy review:** A deliberate examination of permissions, settings, audiences, and consequences.
- **Metadata:** Information describing other information, such as a picture's creation date or location details.
- **Precise location:** More exact location information than a general city or neighborhood area.
- **Approximate location:** Broader location information that may be sufficient for some services.

### Device and application controls

- **Operating system:** Core software that manages a device, its hardware, applications, and permissions.
- **Application or app:** Software used to perform a particular task.
- **Permission:** Authorization for an app, website, or service to use information or a capability.
- **Permission manager:** A settings area that shows or changes permissions for apps or permission types.
- **Location services:** Device functions that estimate or provide location information for authorized purposes.
- **Background access:** Activity or capability use that may occur when an app is not actively displayed.
- **While using:** A permission scope generally connected to active app use under device rules.
- **One-time permission:** A grant that applies to one use or a short session where supported.
- **Selected-photo access:** A permission option limiting an app to photographs the user chooses.
- **Microphone access:** Permission for an app or site to receive input from a microphone.
- **Camera access:** Permission for an app or site to obtain images or video from a camera.
- **Contacts access:** Permission for an app to use selected address-book information through relevant device controls.
- **Diagnostics:** Information about device or application operation used for maintenance or improvement.
- **Synchronization:** Keeping selected data or preferences aligned across devices or accounts.
- **Privacy Dashboard:** A supported device feature summarizing certain recent permission uses.
- **App Privacy Report:** An Apple feature showing certain app data-access and network activity information.
- **Managed device:** A device whose settings may be controlled by an employer, school, or other authorized administrator.
- **Accessibility setting:** A control that helps a person use technology according to their needs or abilities.

### Browser privacy and online tracking

- **Browser:** Software that displays websites and web applications.
- **Browsing history:** A browser's records of webpages or resources visited in a given context.
- **Site permission:** A browser grant for a particular website to use a capability such as camera or location.
- **Notification permission:** Authorization for a site or app to deliver selected alerts.
- **Cookie:** A small piece of website-associated data often used for sessions or preferences.
- **First-party cookie:** A cookie associated with the site the person is visiting.
- **Third-party cookie:** A cookie associated with another service incorporated into the visited site.
- **Site data:** Browser-managed information associated with a website, such as cookies and certain local storage.
- **Cross-site tracking:** Attempts to associate activities across different sites or services.
- **Tracker:** A mechanism used to measure or associate selected activity across contexts.
- **Tracking prevention:** Browser features intended to limit some tracking behavior.
- **Advertising preference:** An account or device choice affecting certain advertising personalization practices.
- **Personalized advertising:** Ad selection influenced by information about interests or activity.
- **Private browsing:** A browser mode designed to limit selected local retention after a browsing session.
- **Autofill:** Browser or app assistance that proposes previously saved entries for fields.
- **Cache:** Stored copies of certain content that help a browser or app work efficiently.
- **Search discoverability:** How readily an account or profile may be found through search features.

### Accounts, audiences, and ongoing decisions

- **Service account:** A user account maintained by an online provider with its own profile and sharing settings.
- **Audience control:** A setting defining who is intended to see selected content.
- **Public profile:** Account information that may be visible to people outside a limited recipient group.
- **Shared link:** A URL that grants or facilitates access to content according to a service's rules.
- **Invited accounts:** Named or approved accounts given access to a specific item or group.
- **Link sharing:** A method of granting access to content through a link and associated permission settings.
- **Connected app:** An application linked to a service account for certain authorized functions.
- **Authorized integration:** A connection that permits one service to access selected functions or information from another.
- **Revoke access:** Withdraw a permission or authorization for later use.
- **Sign out:** End or reduce an authenticated session on a website or application.
- **Unsubscribe:** Stop certain optional messages or marketing communications.
- **Recovery:** Actions intended to regain or secure an account after an access or information problem.
- **Independent verification:** Confirming a request through an established route that the questionable source does not control.
- **Incident response:** Practical steps for limiting harm, documenting, reporting, and recovering from an unexpected event.

### Match the word to the right control

| If your concern is... | Most relevant concept | First place to examine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A website has camera access | Site permission | Browser's permission list for that site |
| A phone app sees all photos | Selected-photo access | Phone's app permission settings |
| A document is available to strangers | Audience control and shared link | Document's service account sharing options |
| A site remembers a login | Cookie or site data | Browser's site data and account settings |
| A browser remembers visited pages | Browsing history | Browser's history controls |
| An advertiser tailors ads | Advertising preference | Applicable account or device privacy options |
| An old app can access another account | Authorized integration | Service account's connected-app dashboard |
| A service keeps old information | Data retention or deletion | Service's account and retention policy controls |

The most important vocabulary skill is to distinguish **setting**, **purpose**, **scope**, and **limitation**. For example: *“The browser's site permission controls this website's microphone access. It does not decide whether the service keeps a recording or which classmates can see it.”* That statement connects a technical control to an everyday choice.

## 11. MGU Library connections and next steps

### Summary: review information, access, and audience together

Privacy settings help you reduce unnecessary information exposure while continuing to use valuable digital services. An operating-system permission, a browser's site permission, and an account's content-sharing setting are not substitutes for one another. A choice may affect future access without deleting earlier copies. A useful privacy habit therefore considers **what is collected, why it is used, who receives it, how long it remains, and how to verify a change**.

| Before you... | Check... | If the purpose or consequence is unclear... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Allow a device permission | Which app, capability, and time scope | Postpone, deny, or seek verified help |
| Allow a website permission | Actual site and requested feature | Decline until the task is understood |
| Change tracking preferences | Which data uses the setting actually covers | Review official documentation and avoid sweeping claims |
| Clear cookies or history | Which local data will be removed or retained | Confirm whether important sessions will be disrupted |
| Publish a photograph | Who and what is visible, including other people | Ask for consent or refrain from sharing |
| Share a cloud document | Audience, link scope, permissions, and recipients | Choose named recipients where appropriate |
| Revoke a connected app | Future authorization and possible existing copies | Check separate deletion or retention procedures |
| Change a managed device | Ownership, policy, and assigned authority | Ask the designated administrator |
| Respond to possible unsafe monitoring | Whether a change could create personal risk | Use a safer support channel and a situation-specific plan |
