Basic Computer Skills / Digital Safety and Scam Awareness

## Lesson 8: Device Security Basics

A laptop displays a notice that a restart is needed to finish an update. A phone is left unlocked on a table. A family member has photographs stored only on one aging computer. Another person sees a frightening pop-up offering to “fix security” if they download a program. These situations involve different devices and threats, but one question connects them: **How can ordinary users keep their devices protected, recognize when protection is missing, and recover if something goes wrong?**

[[device-security|Device security]] is the use of settings, maintenance practices, physical precautions, and recovery procedures to reduce unauthorized access, harmful software, and avoidable data loss. You do not need to be a programmer to protect your computer or phone. You do need to understand several independent layers of protection, recognize genuine system tools, make deliberate choices, and know when to ask for authorized assistance. No security setting can guarantee that a device will never be lost, damaged, infected, or compromised.

This is **Lesson 8** in MGU's nine-part **Digital Safety and Scam Awareness** course. Previous lessons addressed passwords, two-factor authentication, phishing, unsafe links and downloads, privacy, shopping, and protecting personal information. Those skills remain important. This lesson concentrates on the **device itself**: keeping its software supported, controlling local access, using built-in protections, preserving recoverable copies of important files, and responding to warnings or loss. Windows 11 is the main desktop reference; Mac computers, Chromebooks, iPhones, iPads, and Android devices are included where their approaches differ. Menu names may change by version, manufacturer, policy, and accessibility settings.

**Practice safety:** The six guided activities use imaginary people, devices, warnings, and files. They require no live downloads, suspicious websites, account access, security scans, installation, factory resets, remote-wipe actions, command-line instructions, paid products, or changes to security controls. You may *observe* settings on a device you own or are authorized to use, but do not change a managed work, school, or shared device without permission. In particular, **never turn off antivirus, firewalls, updates, device encryption, screen locks, or safety warnings to complete this lesson**.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 115–150 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Digital Safety and Scam Awareness Lessons 1–7 recommended · **Practice equipment:** This lesson, paper, or a blank local note; optional authorized personal device for observation only · **Safety:** Use fictional examples rather than real account credentials, recovery keys, or incident details.

> **Essential idea:** **UPDATE → LOCK → PROTECT → BACK UP → VERIFY → RECOVER.** Keep software supported and updated, prevent casual unauthorized access, retain working protections, preserve recoverable files, verify unexpected messages independently, and prepare a proportionate response to loss or trouble. These actions work together; none replaces the others.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain common device threats and why multiple protective layers are needed.
1. Identify the difference between an operating-system update, an app update, a security patch, and an unsupported device.
1. Describe how screen locks, device sign-in methods, and separate user accounts reduce access risk.
1. Recognize the basic roles of built-in antivirus, firewall, application reputation checks, and device permissions.
1. Compare backups, file synchronization, encryption, and recovery information without confusing their purposes.
1. Explain safer procedures for home Wi-Fi, removable drives, shared devices, and physical access.
1. Distinguish routine security messages from suspicious pop-ups, unauthorized account actions, and possible malware.
1. Select proportionate first steps for a lost device, an interrupted update, a threatening download, or possible unauthorized access.
1. Work through six fictional activities and a 30-question self-study assessment without changing live security settings.

## 1. What is device security, and what can go wrong?

### 1.1 Devices are more than the information displayed on a screen

A [[device|device]] is a computer, phone, tablet, or other piece of equipment that runs software and may store or access information. Its [[operating-system|operating system]] manages hardware, programs, storage, and accounts. [[applications|Applications]] perform particular tasks, such as browsing, calling, creating documents, or checking email. Devices may also store saved sign-ins, photographs, documents, contacts, payment details, and active account sessions. Losing access to a device can therefore interrupt school, work, communication, and personal services even when the hardware itself is inexpensive.

Not every security problem begins with a criminal. A forgotten device in a taxi, a damaged storage drive, a mistaken deletion, and an unsupported application can also interrupt access or expose data. The objective is to reduce both deliberate threats and predictable accidents.

| Risk or event | What could happen? | Relevant protective layer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Device left unlocked | Another person reads files or uses signed-in accounts | Screen lock and separate accounts |
| Software left outdated | A known flaw remains available to attackers | Supported software and updates |
| Malicious program runs | Files, sessions, or device behavior are altered | Safer downloads and security protection |
| Phone or laptop is stolen | Hardware and potentially stored information are lost | Lock, encryption, location/recovery tools |
| Storage fails or files are deleted | Important work or photographs become unavailable | Independent backups and restore tests |
| Fake warning demands a download | Learner may install harmful software | Warning recognition and independent verification |
| Shared account is misused | Activity and access cannot be limited or clearly separated | Individual sign-in accounts and least privilege |

These risks can overlap. For example, a stolen laptop that is locked but has no usable backup may protect confidentiality yet still result in permanent loss of important files. A backed-up device with a weak unlock code may be recoverable but allow unwanted access before it is recovered. Security planning should therefore consider **confidentiality**, **integrity**, and **availability** together: who can see information, whether it remains accurate, and whether authorized people can retrieve it when needed.

### 1.2 Think in layers rather than searching for a magic button

[[defense-in-depth|Defense in depth]] means using multiple safeguards that address different failure modes. A strong screen lock protects against someone casually picking up the device, but it does not patch an old browser. An antivirus product may block a malicious file, but it cannot reconstruct an unbacked-up essay after a drive fails. Encryption can make stored data harder to read when a device is lost, but it does not replace account verification or a backup.

A practical beginner system has six layers: software maintenance, local access controls, protective software and safe installation, recoverable copies, sensible physical/network practices, and an incident response plan. You may already have several of these protections by default; inspect what is present before buying additional tools or changing settings.

### 1.3 Separate a warning from a confirmed compromise

A [[security-warning|security warning]] may come from the operating system, a legitimate application, a browser, or an untrustworthy webpage. They are **not interchangeable**. A webpage that says “Your laptop has 17 viruses—call this number” cannot itself diagnose the entire computer merely by displaying text. A genuine antivirus notification may indicate a detected file, a blocked item, or a problem that needs review; it still deserves verification inside the known security application.

**Worked example:** A learner sees a bright red web page that announces an infection and demands that they install a “repair tool.” The safer course is to avoid the offered download and telephone number, leave the page if possible, and open the operating system's recognized security application independently if a check is needed. By contrast, a genuine Windows Security alert can be investigated through **Start → Windows Security → Protection history**, rather than by trusting an unrelated pop-up. The learner does not need to prove the page malicious by clicking it.

### 1.4 Recognize the limits of general advice

A home Windows 11 laptop, an employer-controlled desktop, an iPad supervised by a school, and a shared library computer may have very different controls and permissions. The device owner or administrator may already manage updates, security products, backups, and network access. A setting that is available on one model may be missing or named differently on another. When instructions conflict with a legitimate workplace or school policy, stop and consult the authorized administrator rather than circumventing the safeguard.

## 2. How do you keep a device updated and supported?

### 2.1 Understand updates, patches, and support

A [[software-update|software update]] is a vendor-issued change to a program or system. Some updates add features or fix ordinary errors; a [[security-patch|security patch]] addresses a weakness that could create a security risk. A [[security-vulnerability|vulnerability]] is a flaw that could allow an unwanted action under certain conditions. A [[restart|restart]] may be required to finish an update, but completing the restart is different from merely downloading a file.

Security maintenance includes more than the operating system. Browsers, office applications, messaging apps, antivirus security intelligence, device drivers, routers, and other connected equipment can require patches. Some updates arrive automatically while others need a person to approve installation or restart. **Turning on automatic updates is helpful, but periodically checking that updates have completed remains valuable.** Devices may be off, low on storage, disconnected, or prevented from restarting when updates are scheduled.

| Update category | Example | What to verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operating system | Windows 11 or iOS version update | Installed or scheduled through genuine device settings |
| Security patch | Fix for a discovered flaw | Applies to the installed and supported software |
| Application update | Browser, document editor, messaging app | Comes through the app, trusted store, or publisher |
| Security intelligence | Malware detection information | Built-in or approved protective tool can update it |
| Device firmware | Router or computer firmware update | Follow verified manufacturer or managed-IT instructions |
| Driver update | Hardware support or security fix | Use supported operating-system or vendor channels |

An [[end-of-support|end-of-support]] product no longer receives ordinary vendor support and relevant patches under the standard arrangement. A computer can still switch on and browse while running an unsupported version; that does **not** make it safe. For example, Windows 10's general support ended on **October 14, 2025**; some devices may receive security updates through an eligible Extended Security Updates program, but that is not a substitute for checking the actual support status and migration plan. Other operating systems and applications also have end-of-support dates.

### 2.2 Find updates without trusting a surprise message

On a personal **Windows 11** device, the common route is **Start → Settings → Windows Update**. The screen can show update status, pending restarts, and available options. You may need to save work and use the operating system's restart process. On **macOS**, the common route is **Apple menu → System Settings → General → Software Update**. On **iPhone or iPad**, it is generally **Settings → General → Software Update**. On **Android**, the route often involves **Settings → System → Software updates** or **Settings → Security & privacy → System & updates**; brands and versions differ. Some Android devices distinguish Android version updates, security updates, and Google Play system updates.

**Worked example:** A text says “Critical Windows patch required” and includes a shortened link to download a program. Even when the device genuinely needs updating, the learner should open **Windows Update independently** and check there. The message itself does not authorize installing unknown software. On work or school equipment, an official IT portal or authorized instruction may be the appropriate channel; verify it through a known contact rather than the link alone.

### 2.3 Apply updates without destroying work

A good update routine begins with reasonable preparation: save open documents, confirm that important files are backed up, allow sufficient power and available storage, and select a suitable time to restart. Do not shut down a device or disconnect power *during a stage that explicitly warns against interruption*. If an update appears stuck, consult official support or an administrator instead of repeatedly force-restarting a device based on an internet comment. Avoid changing advanced settings, deleting system folders, or running copied troubleshooting commands merely because a stranger recommends them.

The user should also look for the **outcome**. An update can be downloaded but not installed, or installed but awaiting restart. A status screen saying “Restart required” indicates that the process may be incomplete. The precise wording varies.

### 2.4 Build a support lifecycle plan

When a device becomes too old for vendor updates, the decision is not always to throw it away immediately; however, continued exposure should be evaluated. Possible authorized options include upgrading to a supported operating system when the hardware qualifies, replacing the device, using an applicable vendor support program, or disconnecting an otherwise functional device from sensitive online activity until a responsible plan exists. Plan for exporting or backing up needed files and securely transferring, recycling, or disposing of equipment later.

## 3. How do you control access to a device?

### 3.1 Use a screen lock and meaningful sign-in method

A [[screen-lock|screen lock]] prevents casual access when a computer or phone is not being actively used. It is different from an app password, and it is different from merely turning the display dark. Modern devices may use a [[passcode|passcode]], password, [[pin|PIN]], fingerprint, face recognition, or combinations of those controls. A device PIN can be appropriate because it is checked locally under the device's security design; it is not automatically equivalent to using a short password on every website.

For **Windows 11**, users can typically review device sign-in options under **Settings → Accounts → Sign-in options** and lock the screen using **Windows key + L**. On a Mac, a person can use the lock-screen option or a supported keyboard shortcut. On phones and tablets, the power/side button usually locks the device, but settings determine how quickly it locks and which unlock methods are permitted. Learn the specific instructions for your model before relying on a particular shortcut or gesture.

A sensible [[auto-lock|automatic lock]] interval helps protect a device left unattended. It should be short enough for the environment while remaining workable for users who need accessibility accommodations. Locking a device does not necessarily sign out of every app. A person with the correct device credentials or an already authorized session may still reach account data after unlocking.

| Control | What it helps with | What it does not guarantee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Screen lock | Reduces casual access to an unattended device | Does not prevent every software attack |
| Device PIN or password | Checks local sign-in authorization | Does not remove the need for account protection |
| Fingerprint or face unlock | Makes supported local sign-in convenient | Does not make lost-device recovery automatic |
| Auto-lock | Reduces the time a device stays open unattended | Cannot protect information exposed before locking |
| Separate user accounts | Limits routine access between users | May still depend on admin settings and file permissions |
| Device encryption | Protects stored data if device is off or appropriately locked | Does not replace secure backups or safe sign-in habits |

### 3.2 Use separate accounts and the least privilege needed

When several people use one computer, separate [[user-account|user accounts]] can provide individual settings, files, and permission boundaries. On a personal computer, a [[standard-account|standard account]] is usually preferable for everyday work when practical, reserving [[administrator-access|administrator access]] for approved system changes. An administrator has broader control and should not approve an unexpected program merely because a dialog appears. On a workplace or school device, account roles and authorization are normally assigned by IT.

**Worked example:** Two family members share a laptop. One person needs to browse the web and complete schoolwork; the other maintains the computer. A single shared administrator account gives both people unnecessary broad access. Individual accounts with appropriate permissions make routine boundaries clearer and can limit accidental configuration changes. However, everyone still needs to sign out of sensitive websites when appropriate and keep their personal credentials private.

### 3.3 Remember that physical access changes the risk

Someone who can freely handle a device may unplug it, take it, look over a shoulder, remove storage, or attempt changes. Lock devices before leaving a desk; keep laptops and phones in a secure place; do not leave sensitive paperwork beside a shared printer; and consider who can see your screen. [[Shoulder-surfing|Shoulder surfing]] is observing a person's screen or input without permission. A [[privacy-screen|privacy screen]] can reduce some side viewing but does not make public use fully confidential.

### 3.4 Know the purpose of encryption

[[Encryption|Encryption]] transforms data so that possession of its raw contents does not automatically provide readable information without the necessary key or authorized process. [[Device-encryption|Device encryption]] or full-disk encryption may protect files on a lost laptop or phone when it is properly locked or powered off, depending on the device and configuration. Windows support may involve **Device encryption** on eligible hardware or **BitLocker** capabilities on applicable editions. Modern phones commonly encrypt stored information under supported configurations. Do not assume encryption is enabled merely because a lock screen exists; availability and status depend on the device.

If a system provides an [[encryption-recovery-key|encryption recovery key]], losing it can prevent recovery from a legitimate lockout. Store it through the platform's approved recovery arrangement or another authorized, secure method. **Do not paste, email, photograph, or publicly disclose a real recovery key for classroom practice.** Turning encryption on or off can affect recovery and data availability; observe settings, and seek official help before changing them.

## 4. How do you use built-in security protections?

### 4.1 Know what protective tools do—and do not do

An [[antivirus|antivirus]] or [[antimalware|antimalware]] tool checks for certain harmful programs and behaviors. [[Malware|Malware]] is software used to harm, misuse, disrupt, or gain unauthorized access. [[Ransomware|Ransomware]] can encrypt or otherwise block files and demand payment. [[Spyware|Spyware]] can collect information without appropriate authorization. Many products provide [[real-time-protection|real-time protection]] and [[security-scan|scans]], but detection is never perfect: a new threat, an allowed app with unsafe behavior, or a social-engineering attack may not be stopped automatically.

Windows 11 includes the **Windows Security** app, which shows protection areas and offers access to Microsoft Defender Antivirus and the Windows Firewall, among other features. On a personal Windows device, a reasonable observation workflow is **Start → Windows Security → Virus & threat protection** and **Protection history**. A different approved antivirus provider may be present, and that can change what is active. Do not assume all status messages are identical or disable a provider to “test” another. A known security application is the reliable place to review its findings—not a webpage's animated warning.

A [[firewall|firewall]] helps control certain network communications, while antivirus checks different types of threats. [[Reputation-protection|Reputation-based protection]] or browser/app warnings may flag suspicious downloads or untrusted applications. [[Application-permission|Application permissions]] control whether an app can access certain device features or data. Each control has a different purpose; no single product replaces them all.

| Security feature | Typical purpose | Better user habit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antivirus or antimalware | Identify and block some harmful software | Keep protection active and review legitimate alerts |
| Firewall | Restrict selected network traffic | Retain recommended protection, especially on unknown networks |
| Browser or app warning | Flag potentially harmful sites or downloads | Stop and verify instead of forcing a bypass |
| Application permissions | Limit access to camera, microphone, location, or files | Grant only needed permissions and review periodically |
| Updates | Repair known vulnerabilities | Install promptly through trusted channels |
| Backups | Restore files after damage or loss | Make recoverable copies and test restoration |
| Device encryption | Reduce exposure of stored data after loss | Verify status and secure recovery information |

### 4.2 Treat downloads and installation prompts as decisions

A [[download|download]] saves content to a device; [[installation|installation]] changes the software available to run. Merely downloading a file is not the same event as opening or executing it, although some systems automatically preview certain files and some vulnerabilities may be triggered by processing content. An [[installer|installer]] can ask for elevated permissions, and an unknown program can be harmful even if its icon resembles a reputable brand.

Prefer software from the recognized store, the genuine publisher, or an approved organizational distribution channel. App stores and publisher signatures can help, but neither is a guarantee of harmlessness. Check the actual publisher, purpose, permissions, and need for the app. Never disable protection, add an antivirus exception, run a terminal command, or install a “support agent” just to satisfy an unexpected webpage, text, or caller. Review [Suspicious Links and Downloads](/department/computer-science/basic-computer-skills/digital-safety-and-scam-awareness/suspicious-links-and-downloads/) for the deeper file-risk procedure.

### 4.3 Understand app permissions and system administrator prompts

An app requesting [[camera-access|camera access]], [[microphone-access|microphone access]], access to contacts or photos, or screen-recording permission should have a reason related to its task. Permissions can often be reviewed in system settings. Removing a permission can affect an app's features, so choose deliberately rather than revoking everything without understanding the result.

An [[elevation-prompt|administrator or elevation prompt]] is a request to approve a change requiring greater authority; on Windows this may appear through **User Account Control (UAC)**. A real UAC dialog does not prove that the program requesting approval is trustworthy. If you did not start the intended installation or setting change, decline and verify. A person should not enter an administrator password supplied by a stranger or copy commands into PowerShell or Terminal because a webpage insists that the machine needs repair.

### 4.4 Windows 11, Mac, and mobile devices use different tool names

On **Windows 11**, Windows Security is the principal built-in interface for checking common protection categories. On a **Mac**, Apple provides system-level protections and update, privacy, and firewall settings; the exact menu depends on macOS version. On **Android**, relevant tools may include **Security & privacy**, **Google Play Protect**, and manufacturer security options. On **iPhone and iPad**, system updates, device sign-in, app permissions, and Apple's platform protections are integrated into settings and the app ecosystem. The absence of a product with the literal label “antivirus” does not itself mean a platform has no security architecture. Conversely, built-in protections do not make every link, account, or application safe.

## 5. How do you protect files and prepare for recovery?

### 5.1 Understand a backup before you need one

A [[backup|backup]] is a separate recoverable copy of information that can be used after the original is lost, damaged, deleted, corrupted, or locked by malware. A reliable backup plan identifies **what matters**, **where the copy exists**, **how often it changes**, **who can access it**, and **how to restore it**. Important items might include coursework, photographs, records, documents, project files, and app-specific exports. Some application content or settings may not be included by default.

[[Cloud-storage|Cloud storage]] can help, but [[file-sync|file synchronization]] is not always the same as a full independent backup. A synced folder may rapidly reproduce a deletion or file change across all connected devices; some providers offer file-version history or restore windows, but those features vary. An [[external-drive|external drive]] can be useful, yet a drive permanently connected to the same computer may also be affected by malware, physical damage, or an error. A separate or offline copy reduces some shared risks.

| Storage or recovery feature | Main strength | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cloud file sync | Makes recent working files available across devices | Mistakes and deletion may sync too |
| Cloud backup service | Preserves defined data according to the service's backup design | Coverage, retention, and access depend on plan and settings |
| External drive backup | Provides another local copy | Connected drives can share physical or malware risk |
| Offline or disconnected copy | Reduces exposure to some online attacks | May be out of date if not refreshed |
| File version history | Helps recover an earlier file state | Versions may expire or not cover all files |
| Recovery drive or system restore tool | Helps recover aspects of the operating system | May not include personal documents or recent work |

A widely taught planning idea is the **3-2-1 backup principle**: aim for multiple copies, different kinds of storage, and at least one separate or off-site copy. This is a useful starting framework, not a substitute for verifying that the actual files can be restored. Consider encryption and access controls for sensitive backup media.

### 5.2 Distinguish backup, sync, reset, and recovery

A [[restore|restore]] places data or settings back from an appropriate stored source. A [[factory-reset|factory reset]] removes or returns device contents and settings according to the platform's process; it is **not** a backup and can cause data loss. An operating-system [[restore-point|restore point]] may help reverse certain system changes but should not be treated as a complete document backup. Likewise, a phone's cloud account might synchronize contacts and photographs without preserving every locally stored item or third-party application record.

**Worked example:** An individual keeps their only family photograph collection in a synchronized folder. After accidentally deleting a folder, the deletion appears on the phone and laptop. This does not necessarily mean every photograph is gone forever—provider trash or version history may help—but it illustrates why synchronization alone is not a guaranteed independent backup. The learner should check backup scope, retention, and a safe restore method before an emergency, preferably using an innocuous test file rather than valuable originals.

### 5.3 Observe backup status and test safely

On Windows 11, **Windows Backup**, **File History**, OneDrive options, and other backup tools serve different purposes and have different eligibility requirements. On Apple devices, iCloud and computer-based backups have distinct coverage and setup choices. Android devices may have Google or manufacturer backup options; backup behavior varies by model and app. Read the actual status and included categories before concluding that “everything” is protected.

A safe [[restore-test|restore test]] uses a harmless sample item and a non-destructive process to confirm that a backed-up file can be retrieved. Do not overwrite, rename, erase, or restore valuable original files during an introductory lesson. If the system requires high-stakes actions, stop and use official instructions or authorized support.

### 5.4 Prepare for losing a phone or laptop

A [[device-finder|device-finding feature]] may help locate a missing device, mark it lost, or remotely lock or erase it, subject to prior setup, account access, connectivity, and device support. Examples include Apple's **Find My** and Google's device-finding features. [[Remote-lock|Remote locking]] can help restrict use; [[remote-wipe|remote wipe]] aims to remove information remotely but can be irreversible, dependent on connectivity, and may affect recovery or tracking. Neither should be used casually or as a classroom exercise.

Preparation includes keeping recovery contact methods current, securing the account associated with the device, knowing whether a finder feature is available, and documenting a lawful way to obtain assistance. If a device appears stolen, do not confront a suspected thief or travel to a displayed location to retrieve it yourself; use the appropriate local authorities and provider procedures. If you cannot access the device, change relevant account credentials from a **known safe device** when warranted and review sessions, connected devices, and available lock/recovery options. Work and school devices should be reported promptly to the responsible administrator.

### 5.5 Securely retire, repair, or transfer equipment

Before donating, recycling, selling, or transferring a personal device, identify needed files, make and verify backups, sign out or unlink accounts as appropriate, and use the manufacturer's documented device-reset or secure-erasure process. A normal file deletion is not automatically a secure wipe. Encryption and supported erase procedures can protect data, but their effectiveness depends on the hardware and method. For managed devices, follow organizational asset-disposal policy. For a repair, back up data, use a reputable authorized channel, and ask what access the technician actually needs; do not provide broad credentials unnecessarily.

## 6. How do you handle networks, physical risks, and warning signs?

### 6.1 Understand the role of home and public networks

A [[router|router]] connects a home network to an internet service and may provide wireless access. It can have its own software, administrator credentials, and settings. A home network should use modern supported Wi-Fi security—often [[wpa2|WPA2]] or [[wpa3|WPA3]] depending on devices and router—along with an appropriate wireless passphrase and a separate strong router-administrator credential. Keep the router updated through its genuine management interface or provider process. For connected smart-home devices, review their support, account access, and whether a [[guest-network|guest network]] is useful for separating access.

On [[public-wifi|public Wi-Fi]], device and account safety still matters. A public network is not automatically compromised, and cellular data does not make phishing impossible. Confirm the legitimate network name, use secure websites and apps, keep protection enabled, and avoid casually granting file or device sharing on untrusted networks. A [[vpn|VPN]] can add privacy and connection protection under its provider's design, but it does not authenticate every website or eliminate scams.

### 6.2 Protect against unfamiliar USB devices and accessories

A [[usb-drive|USB drive]] or other removable medium can contain unfamiliar files or software. Do not plug in an unknown drive found in a public place to “see who owns it.” Avoid running software from unverified media and ask organizational IT how to handle suspicious devices. Use reputable charging accessories and be attentive to unexpected permission prompts when connecting a phone to a computer or accessory. Not every cable or charging station is malicious; the practical principle is to understand what access the connection requests.

### 6.3 Use a proportionate response to problems

Different events call for different first actions. A system pending an ordinary restart is a maintenance task. A suspicious web pop-up does not establish infection. A confirmed malware detection warrants review through recognized security tools. A stolen device creates both physical and account risks. **Do not treat every unusual screen as proof of hacking, and do not ignore verified alerts.**

| Situation | What is known? | Safer first response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Windows Update says restart required | Update process may be unfinished | Save work; restart at an appropriate time through Windows |
| Browser tab displays a fake virus countdown | A webpage made a claim | Do not call or download; leave page and verify independently |
| Antivirus displays a detection in its own history | A protective tool reported an event | Review the result and follow official guidance or authorized IT help |
| Phone is missing | Device location and access are uncertain | Use established finding/account procedures from a safe device |
| Backup status says several folders were excluded | Coverage is incomplete | Review scope before claiming the files are recoverable |
| Unknown program requests administrator permission | A high-privilege change is being requested | Decline unless you deliberately initiated and verified it |
| Shared computer shows another person's files | An account/session boundary may have failed | Do not inspect; sign out of your own session and notify authorized staff |
| Work laptop displays a security-policy notice | Organization controls may be involved | Follow official IT instructions; do not bypass policy |

### 6.4 Keep observations separate from actions

When something seems wrong, record what you actually saw, including the device, approximate time, application, and exact warning wording when safe to do so. Do not enter a password into a pop-up or send a screenshot containing private recovery codes. When a genuine compromise is reasonably suspected, avoid continuing sensitive transactions on the affected device, use a known safe device for account protection if needed, and consult a reputable or authorized support source. In an incident involving a payment, identity data, or access to another person's account, preserve relevant records and follow the appropriate institution's reporting process. The next lesson develops this incident-response workflow in greater depth.

### 6.5 A weekly or monthly security rhythm

A small routine prevents many security decisions from being postponed indefinitely. A weekly check might verify that updates and a pending restart are not accumulating, that the usual security tools show no unresolved alerts, and that important work is included in a backup. A monthly review can cover app permissions, old software, device recovery arrangements, and the practical ability to retrieve a sample file. None of this requires buying a special product or learning advanced administration.

> **Checkpoint:** Before declaring a device “secure,” ask whether software is supported, protective controls are active, local access is restricted, important data can be restored, and a lost-device response is understood. If one item cannot be confirmed, document it as an open question rather than guessing.

## 7. Guided practice: Make device-security decisions

The following activities can be completed with a pencil, a note, or a blank document. All names, devices, settings, and warnings are **fictional**. No activity requires changing the configuration of a real computer or phone. Where a device is mentioned, decide what the user *should inspect or request*, not what you can force the system to do. Use the expandable answers to compare reasoning, not just letters.

### Activity 1 — Build a layered protection plan

**Scenario:** Jordan has a personal Windows 11 laptop. Jordan uses a screen PIN and a browser, but updates are waiting for restart. School reports are saved only on the laptop. A relative sometimes borrows the laptop while Jordan remains signed in.

1. Identify one strength in Jordan's current setup.
1. Identify three different gaps; explain why each matters.
1. Place the gaps into the categories **maintenance**, **access control**, and **recoverability**.
1. Give an appropriate first step for each gap without performing the changes.
1. Explain why installing a second antivirus product would not address all three gaps.

| Observed condition | Category | First question to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A PIN is used to unlock the laptop | Access control | Is the device locked when unattended? |
| An update awaits restart | Maintenance | Can work be saved and the update completed? |
| Reports exist on one drive only | Recoverability | Where is a verified independent backup? |
| Borrower uses the owner's open session | Access control | Could separate accounts be used? |

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answer
The PIN is a positive local-access measure, provided Jordan does not reveal it and locks the device when away. The pending restart is a maintenance gap because a patch may not be complete. The single-copy reports are a recoverability gap because theft, failure, malware, or an accidental deletion could make the files unavailable. Sharing an unlocked session is an access-control gap because another person may open documents or active accounts unintentionally or deliberately.

Safer next steps are to save work and plan the legitimate update restart, establish an appropriate backup and verify it covers schoolwork, and consider separate user accounts or locking before lending the computer. A different antivirus product does not finish an operating-system update, establish a backup, or separate users. The protective layers serve different purposes.
:::

### Activity 2 — Decide whether an update message is trustworthy

**Scenario:** Maya's computer displays three notices on different days:

- **Notice A:** In the Windows 11 Settings application, Windows Update says “Restart required to finish installing updates.”
- **Notice B:** A website opens a flashing window saying “Critical patch: click this link to download `emergency-fix.exe`.”
- **Notice C:** A school-issued laptop displays a maintenance message with an unfamiliar request to disable protection before an update.

For each notice, state whether Maya should **complete a routine authorized step**, **ignore the unverified instruction and check settings independently**, or **seek confirmation through authorized IT**. Explain which details determine your decision.

:::answer Show Activity 2 model answer
**A:** It is a notice inside the expected update interface. Maya should save work and finish the legitimate restart when appropriate, following device instructions. **B:** A webpage cannot authorize a random executable. Do not download or run it; use the operating system's real update tool to check status. **C:** Because the school manages the machine and turning off protection is consequential, Maya should verify through the official school IT channel. Even a familiar logo or realistic-looking message is insufficient reason to bypass protection. The source, requested action, and ownership of the device all matter.
:::

### Activity 3 — Recognize access and encryption limits

**Scenario:** Luis carries a phone to a study center. The phone uses face unlock and a passcode. A friend borrows it to check a bus schedule. Later, Luis reads that the phone has device encryption and assumes nothing else needs attention.

1. What does local face/passcode unlock help protect?
1. What should Luis do before handing over an unlocked device?
1. Why does encryption not replace a backup?
1. Why might a recovery key or sign-in recovery method still matter?
1. If the phone is lost, should Luis confront someone shown by a location-finding map?

:::answer Show Activity 3 model answer
Face unlock and a passcode help control local access, especially when the phone is locked. Luis should avoid leaving sensitive accounts exposed; it may be better to show the requested bus information without handing over an unlocked phone. Encryption can reduce readable exposure of stored data under supported conditions, but it cannot reconstruct a deleted photograph or damaged document. Account recovery and device access may require a trusted recovery process, especially after loss or reset. Location features may be inaccurate or unsafe to act on: Luis should not confront anyone, and should use official device/account and public-safety channels if theft is suspected.
:::

### Activity 4 — Compare a backup with file synchronization

**Scenario:** A learner makes three claims about copies of a course project:

- **Plan A:** “I keep the only copy on my laptop's desktop.”
- **Plan B:** “The desktop folder synchronizes to the cloud and my phone.”
- **Plan C:** “I synchronize working files and make a separate, versioned backup that I periodically verify with a harmless test file.”

Identify the most resilient plan and explain how *each* plan might fail. Do not perform a real deletion, reset, or restore.

| Plan | Advantage | Main unresolved risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: One laptop copy | Simple daily access | Drive loss, theft, or deletion could destroy the only copy |
| B: Synced devices and cloud | Working files are accessible in several places | Unwanted changes may propagate and version history may be limited |
| C: Sync plus separate tested backup | Supports convenience and recovery | Requires maintenance, protected access, and periodic tests |

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answer
**Plan C** is the strongest of the three because it combines working convenience with a separate recovery path and tests whether restoration is possible. **Plan A** has a single point of failure. **Plan B** is better for availability across devices but can reproduce deletion or corruption; cloud retention and file history are service-dependent. Plan C still needs secure account access, enough backup capacity, appropriate retention, and maintenance. A backup is only useful for the files it actually includes, and a successful last backup does not prove that every future change is protected.
:::

### Activity 5 — Read a security alert without rushing

**Scenario:** A person sees a web advertisement promising a free “SuperShield Virus Cleaner.” It says Windows Security is “broken” and directs the person to turn off its protection and run a command copied from the page. The browser then warns about the download.

1. Name at least four separate warning signs.
1. Explain why the browser warning should not simply be overridden.
1. Identify how to inspect genuine Windows protection without using the advertisement's links.
1. If the learner clicked the advertisement but did not download or run anything, what should they avoid concluding?
1. If a program was actually installed, how would the response change?

:::answer Show Activity 5 model answer
Warning signs include a startling claim, unverified software, instructions to disable existing protection, a command copied from a webpage, and an ignored browser warning. Do not download, execute the command, grant administrator access, or call a provided number. On an authorized personal Windows computer, Windows Security can be opened independently from the Start menu; review its recognized status and history. Simply seeing an advertisement does not prove the machine is infected. If harmful software may have been run, stop sensitive account activity on the potentially affected device, avoid further execution, and follow official security or authorized IT procedures; consider using a known safe device to secure relevant accounts if evidence supports account exposure. Do not attempt amateur “cleanup” by running additional unknown commands.
:::

### Activity 6 — Plan for a missing phone and a managed laptop

**Scenario:** A student cannot find a personal phone that contains an active email session. A nearby classmate asks to install an unfamiliar “phone finder” app on the student's school laptop. Separately, the student realizes the school laptop has not restarted after a pending software update.

1. Which device does the student own, and which is managed by an institution?
1. Where should the student check for official lost-phone features?
1. Why should the unfamiliar finder app be rejected?
1. What is the appropriate action regarding the school laptop's update?
1. Which account and recovery arrangements should be reviewed from a known safe device if the phone is still missing?
1. Explain why remote erasure should not be the very first automatic response in every situation.

:::answer Show Activity 6 model answer
The student controls the personal phone, subject to the associated account/provider rules, but the school controls the laptop. Use the phone maker's or associated platform's established official device-finding/account service, accessed independently; do not install a third-party tool suggested by a classmate on school hardware. Notify school IT about the managed device's pending update and follow its approved restart process. From a trusted device, review the phone's linked account, active sessions, recovery methods, available lost mode/lock controls, and any relevant carrier or school notifications. Remote erasure may be irreversible, may depend on connectivity, and may affect the ability to locate or recover data. Its appropriateness depends on circumstances, backups, and the platform's official guidance. If theft is suspected, do not physically confront anyone.
:::

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

### “If I installed antivirus, I no longer need updates.”

Antivirus addresses certain threats, but unpatched operating-system and application vulnerabilities are a separate problem. Keep both the legitimate protection and the software up to date.

### “An update is complete as soon as it downloads.”

Some updates require installation, approval, and a restart. Verify the status rather than assuming a downloaded item has finished protecting the device.

### “Any device that turns on is still supported.”

A computer may operate after ordinary security support ends. Check the actual operating-system and application support lifecycle, including any applicable paid or limited support program.

### “An official-looking pop-up proves my device has been infected.”

Webpages can imitate system alerts. Do not trust a webpage-supplied phone number, payment page, or repair download merely because it looks urgent. Verify through a recognized app or official support channel.

### “A lock-screen PIN and a website password must follow the same rules.”

Their security models may differ. Device PINs can be locally verified and protected by platform controls; website passwords need separate account defenses. Avoid easily guessed device unlock codes and follow the device's supported guidance.

### “Biometrics make a lost phone impossible to access.”

Fingerprint and face recognition can improve local sign-in but are not unlimited guarantees. Recovery methods, passcodes, sessions, software flaws, and device state still matter.

### “Encryption backs up my files.”

Encryption protects the readability of data under its design. It does not create a recoverable second copy. A stolen encrypted laptop can still represent permanent data loss without a tested backup.

### “If a folder appears in the cloud, I have a complete backup.”

Sync may propagate mistakes; the provider's backup scope, deleted-file retention, and version history must be checked. Separate recoverable copies are valuable.

### “An app store guarantees that every application is harmless.”

Store review and platform protections reduce some risks but do not eliminate unsafe permissions, misleading claims, vulnerable apps, or scams. Check the publisher and purpose.

### “A genuine administrator prompt means I should click Yes.”

A legitimate prompt can be generated by a harmful or unwanted action. Authorize only changes you deliberately initiated and understand; managed-device policies may restrict the choice.

### “A VPN makes any public network or website safe.”

A VPN protects defined traffic under its provider's design. It does not authenticate all sellers, eliminate malware, or prevent disclosure of secrets to a fraudulent page.

### “If my device is stolen, I should follow the location dot and get it back myself.”

The location may be approximate, delayed, or dangerous. Use account and device protections, make an appropriate report, and do not confront a suspected thief.

### “Factory resetting a device is harmless and always fixes an alert.”

A reset can delete data or settings, and it may not address an account compromise. Identify the problem, preserve necessary data, and follow appropriate vendor or authorized support procedures before any destructive action.

### A practical decision matrix

| What you notice | Mistaken assumption | Better decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An update is pending restart | Download means completion | Save work and finish the authorized update process |
| A browser warns of a dangerous download | The warning can be ignored if the file looks useful | Stop, verify the publisher, and keep protection active |
| A laptop uses encryption | No backups are needed | Maintain independent recoverable copies |
| A shared device is left unlocked | Anyone may use the existing session | Respect account boundaries and lock or sign out |
| A phone-finder map displays a location | Recovery by confrontation is safe | Use official recovery and appropriate authorities |
| A managed device blocks an install | The user should bypass policy | Seek permission or official IT assistance |
| Cloud sync is enabled | Every file is fully recoverable | Check backup coverage and retention |
| A device shows a suspicious warning | Every message means the device is compromised | Verify the alert source and what actually occurred |

**Final practice principle:** The safest response is rarely “click everything” or “disable everything.” It is to identify the device, observe the trustworthy status, choose the smallest authorized action, and verify the result.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The underlined lesson terms are supported by the MGU dashboard's contextual vocabulary tooltips. This visible list remains useful when tooltips are unavailable. The final `:::terms` block registers the same concepts for interactive display. **Understanding the role of each control matters more than memorizing menu labels**, which can change.

### Device software and upkeep

- **Device:** A computer, phone, tablet, or connected piece of equipment that runs software or processes information.
- **Device security:** Protective settings, maintenance, physical safeguards, and recovery planning for a device.
- **Operating system:** Main software coordinating hardware, files, apps, and device accounts.
- **Application:** Software designed to carry out a particular task.
- **Software update:** A change released for software, sometimes adding features or fixing errors.
- **Security patch:** An update intended to repair a security weakness.
- **Vulnerability:** A weakness that may enable an unwanted action.
- **Restart:** Shut down and start an operating system again to refresh it or finish an update.
- **Firmware:** Low-level software used by hardware devices.
- **Driver:** Software allowing the operating system to interact with certain hardware.
- **End of support:** Status when ordinary vendor maintenance and security updates are no longer provided under standard arrangements.
- **Automatic updates:** Settings that permit supported software to obtain and sometimes install updates with reduced manual intervention.

### Access and local protection

- **Screen lock:** A control restricting direct use of an unattended device.
- **Passcode:** Secret characters or digits used to authorize access.
- **PIN:** Personal identification number, often used for local device access.
- **Biometric authentication:** Supported identity verification using a physical feature, such as fingerprint or face.
- **Automatic lock:** A setting that locks a device after a chosen condition or idle period.
- **User account:** A distinct device identity with associated settings and access.
- **Standard account:** An account intended for ordinary tasks without unrestricted system administration.
- **Administrator access:** Elevated authority to change operating-system settings or install certain software.
- **Least privilege:** Giving a user or app only the access necessary for its legitimate task.
- **Encryption:** A method of transforming information so it is not ordinarily readable without authorized decryption.
- **Device encryption:** Protection for stored data on a computer or phone under supported conditions.
- **Recovery key:** A secret used in a documented process to recover access to encrypted data or an account.
- **Shoulder surfing:** Unauthorized observation of another person's screen or input.
- **Privacy screen:** A display accessory that reduces visibility from some viewing angles.

### Protective tools and threats

- **Defense in depth:** Use of different protections for different ways security can fail.
- **Antivirus:** Software intended to detect or prevent certain harmful programs.
- **Antimalware:** Software intended to address malware and suspicious software behavior.
- **Malware:** Software used to harm, disrupt, spy on, or gain unwanted access to a system.
- **Ransomware:** Malicious activity that blocks access to data or a device and typically demands payment.
- **Spyware:** Software used to gather information without appropriate authorization.
- **Real-time protection:** Monitoring intended to act while suspect activity occurs.
- **Security scan:** Checking files or behavior for indications of threats.
- **Firewall:** A control that filters certain network connections or traffic.
- **Security warning:** A message about a possible risk that requires attention to its actual source.
- **Reputation-based protection:** Checking software or destinations against available risk and trust signals.
- **App permissions:** Settings controlling certain actions or data an application can access.
- **Download:** Receiving a file or other content onto a device.
- **Installer:** Software that places another application on a device.
- **Installation:** Process of making software available for use.
- **Elevation prompt:** Request to authorize a change with greater system privileges.
- **User Account Control:** Windows feature that requests approval for certain elevated actions.

### Copies, recovery, and disposal

- **Backup:** A separate copy used to recover information after loss, damage, or unwanted change.
- **Cloud storage:** Storing information through a remote service accessible over a network.
- **File synchronization:** Keeping selected files or changes aligned between devices or locations.
- **External drive:** Removable storage used for transferring or backing up files.
- **Offline copy:** Copy stored without continuous access from the operating network or device.
- **Version history:** Stored older file states that may support a return to an earlier version.
- **Restore:** Return data or settings from a previous recoverable state.
- **Restore point:** A stored system state that may help reverse some changes, not a full file backup.
- **Restore test:** A safe check that a stored backup can actually be retrieved.
- **Factory reset:** Supported process for returning a device toward a base configuration, often removing data.
- **Device finder:** Feature or service that assists with locating or securing a missing device.
- **Remote lock:** Action that attempts to restrict device access from another location.
- **Remote wipe:** Action that attempts to erase device data remotely, potentially irreversibly.
- **Secure erase:** Supported method intended to remove data before transfer or disposal.

### Networks and daily routines

- **Router:** Equipment directing network traffic between devices and networks.
- **WPA2:** Supported Wi-Fi security protocol often used on home networks.
- **WPA3:** Newer Wi-Fi security protocol supported by compatible equipment.
- **Guest network:** Separate Wi-Fi network commonly used to limit access for visitors or other devices.
- **Public Wi-Fi:** Network access offered in a shared or public setting.
- **VPN:** Virtual private network that carries traffic under the VPN provider's connection design.
- **USB drive:** Portable storage that can be connected to a computer.
- **Incident response:** Steps to contain, investigate, report, and recover from a security problem.
- **Managed device:** Equipment whose configuration and access are administered by a school, employer, or other organization.

### Three questions to connect the vocabulary

1. **What happened?** Is the device merely awaiting an update, or was an untrusted program actually run?
1. **Which protection is relevant?** A screen lock protects local access; backups protect recoverability; updates address vulnerabilities.
1. **What can I verify?** Use the recognized device and account interfaces, not the text of an unexpected pop-up or message.

## 11. MGU Library connections and next steps

### Core lessons learned

1. **Update** operating systems and applications through genuine support channels; distinguish a downloaded update from one that has installed and finished restarting.
1. **Lock** devices against casual unauthorized access and understand the roles of separate accounts, PINs, biometrics, and encryption.
1. **Protect** computers and phones with appropriate built-in security controls, careful permissions, safe software sources, and respectful use of warnings.
1. **Back up** important data in a way that can actually restore files; synchronization, encryption, and resetting are not interchangeable with backups.
1. **Verify** suspicious warnings and unexpected security requests using official applications or known institutional contacts.
1. **Recover** through a prepared plan for missing equipment, deleted files, suspected malware, or account exposure without taking unnecessary destructive actions.

| Important data | Is there a separate recent recoverable copy of essential files? | Check backup scope, retention, and safe restore procedure |
| Device recovery | Is there a known official route for locating or securing a missing device? | Confirm account/recovery arrangements without testing remote erase |
| Physical and network environment | Are shared devices, removable media, and Wi-Fi handled deliberately? | Use approved accounts, router protection, and safe-access habits |

**Publication note:** November 2026 instructional edition. The lesson provides general digital-literacy guidance, not personalized system-administration advice or a guarantee of security. On a device you do not own or manage, request permission before making changes. Instructions for features such as encryption, remote erase, firmware, reset, and account recovery require special care because they can affect access or destroy information. Do not share actual passwords, recovery keys, private messages, or sensitive logs in class exercises.
