## Lesson 6: Safe Online Shopping

A student searches for a replacement laptop charger. The first advertisement promises the exact product for one quarter of the normal price. A second seller has thousands of five-star ratings, but the listing says that returns are not accepted. A third store shows a familiar brand logo and a secure connection, yet asks the buyer to pay by transferring money directly to an unfamiliar individual. The shopper's task is not simply to find the lowest price. It is to determine **who is selling, what is being offered, what the full agreement costs, how payment will be handled, and what evidence or recourse will exist afterward**.

[[safe-online-shopping|Safe online shopping]] means making informed purchases over the internet while reducing the risk of fraud, misleading offers, unauthorized payments, privacy exposure, counterfeit goods, and unresolved delivery problems. It is a practical process, not a guarantee. A legitimate seller can make an honest mistake, a genuine product can arrive damaged, and a fraudulent store can appear highly professional. Good purchasing decisions depend on multiple independent checks rather than a single symbol, advertisement, rating, or promise.

This is Lesson 6 of MGU's nine-part **Digital Safety and Scam Awareness** course. Earlier lessons developed password safety, two-factor authentication, phishing recognition, safe link handling, and privacy choices. Here those skills come together in a familiar transaction: finding an item, comparing offers, deciding whether a merchant is trustworthy, reviewing the checkout, saving records, and resolving a problem. The principles apply to online marketplaces, retailer websites, mobile shopping applications, and many service purchases. Specialized protections and legal rights may differ by payment method, product, location, and transaction type.

**Practice safety:** Every seller, message, order, amount, and address used in the six activities is fictional. There is no need to purchase anything, visit a suspicious site, create an account, enter payment credentials, reveal private information, or contact a seller. The example domain `example.org` is used only for demonstration; any other imaginary seller names are not recommendations. You can complete the entire lesson on paper or in a blank local document.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 115–150 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Digital Safety and Scam Awareness Lessons 1–5 recommended · **Practice equipment:** This lesson or a printed copy; optional personal device for observation only · **Safety:** No real payment, new account, installation, or disclosure required.

> **Essential idea:** **FIND → VERIFY → COMPARE → REVIEW → PAY → RECORD → FOLLOW UP.** Find the item you actually need; verify the seller and destination; compare total costs and policies; review every detail before authorizing payment; use a suitable payment method; retain documentation; and follow up using independently verified channels. If you cannot establish who will receive the money or what you will receive, do not proceed.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish an online marketplace, a platform, an independent seller, a retailer, and a payment processor.
1. Evaluate seller identity and purchase legitimacy using evidence beyond advertisements, positive reviews, or a lock icon.
1. Compare a complete order total, including shipping, taxes, fees, recurring charges, and return conditions.
1. Explain how common payment methods differ in fraud risk and dispute options without assuming any method guarantees reimbursement.
1. Recognize fake stores, counterfeit product listings, off-platform payment requests, delivery-message scams, and unexpected subscriptions.
1. Complete a pre-purchase checklist and keep the essential records of a transaction without unnecessary personal disclosure.
1. Choose an appropriate response to a delayed order, duplicate charge, unauthorized purchase, or suspected fraud.
1. Apply the lesson to six fictional scenarios and complete a 30-question self-study knowledge check.

## 1. What does safe online shopping involve?

### 1.1 An online purchase is a series of decisions

[[online-shopping|Online shopping]] is the purchase of goods or services through a website or application. A shopper may browse a [[product-listing|product listing]], place an item in a [[shopping-cart|shopping cart]], select delivery options, provide required information, and approve a [[checkout|checkout]] transaction. The payment may be completed by the store itself or by a separate [[payment-processor|payment processor]]. The shopping interface may remember an address or card, suggest related products, offer financing, or invite the customer to save an account. Each step can change the commitments a shopper makes.

A transaction has at least three separate questions: **Is the destination authentic? Is the deal acceptable? Is the payment and delivery arrangement appropriate?** A merchant might be real but have poor return terms. A product may be accurately described but overpriced after shipping. A checkout might use encrypted communication but belong to a fraudulent store. Passing one test does not automatically pass the others.

**Worked example:** A fictional retailer offers a desk lamp for $28, charges $15 for delivery, and does not accept returns on clearance items. Another retailer offers the same model for $35 plus $5 shipping with a reasonable return window. The first advertisement has a lower displayed item price, but the second has a lower pre-tax total ($40 compared with $43) and possibly more useful return terms. A careful shopper compares the *entire transaction*, not just the price in a banner.

### 1.2 Who is selling, and who handles the purchase?

A [[retailer|retailer]] sells goods to customers, whether online or in a physical location. An [[online-marketplace|online marketplace]] hosts listings from multiple sellers and may also sell its own products. A [[third-party-seller|third-party seller]] is an independent merchant using that marketplace. A [[platform|platform]] may provide the listing, payment experience, messaging, or dispute tools without itself being the seller of record. The shipping company, warehouse, and merchant can also be different organizations.

Before ordering, identify the seller named on the listing and checkout. Look for wording such as “Sold by,” “Ships from,” “Fulfilled by,” or a separate business name. These labels can describe different roles. The fact that a product appears on a well-known marketplace does **not** mean that the marketplace itself manufactured the product, guaranteed its quality, or will handle every complaint. Review the platform's current rules and the individual seller's terms.

| Participant | Typical role | Question to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retailer | Offers goods or services directly | Which business accepts responsibility for my order? |
| Marketplace | Hosts products from one or more sellers | Which protections apply to this particular listing? |
| Third-party seller | Supplies the product through a marketplace | Who is the actual seller and what are its policies? |
| Payment processor | Handles payment authorization or processing | Who is being paid and what name will appear on my statement? |
| Delivery carrier | Transports the order | What tracking or proof of delivery will I receive? |
| Manufacturer | Makes or brands the item | Is the warranty valid from this seller or region? |

### 1.3 Search results and advertisements are starting points

A [[search-result|search result]] can help locate options, but placement is not an independent safety endorsement. A [[sponsored-listing|sponsored listing]] or advertisement may appear above ordinary results because someone paid for visibility. A convincing logo, brand colors, and polished photographs are easy to imitate. Even a familiar product name does not establish that the seller has the right to use it.

When shopping for a particular store, use an established official address, a trusted bookmark, or another independently verified route. Examine the actual [[domain-name|domain name]] in the browser's address bar rather than relying on the words displayed in the advertisement. A fraudulent site may put the retailer's name at the beginning of a longer address that belongs to someone else. On a phone, expand or examine the address information when the browser hides part of it. Do not assume the first search result must be correct.

A website with [[https|HTTPS]] generally protects communication with the addressed server in transit, but it does **not** prove the merchant is honest. Scam websites can also obtain certificates and use encrypted connections. The presence of a padlock or security indicator is one useful technical check, not a license to ignore the identity and business terms of the seller.

### 1.4 A transaction can expose more than money

Purchases may involve a shipping address, email address, telephone number, payment details, purchase history, and sometimes location or identity information. A store or shopping app may also track behavior for analytics or advertising. Use [[data-minimization|data minimization]]: supply information appropriate to the transaction but avoid unnecessary extras. An ordinary purchase of household supplies generally should not require an unrelated account password, a government identifier, or permission to read a phone's entire contact list.

Use an account protected by a unique password and, where supported, [[multifactor-authentication|multifactor authentication]]. A [[guest-checkout|guest checkout]] may be suitable for a one-time purchase when offered. Creating an account may be helpful for order history or returns, but it also adds a credential and possibly stored information to manage. Neither choice automatically determines whether the merchant is trustworthy. Make a deliberate decision about saving cards or addresses for later use, particularly on shared devices.

## 2. How do you check a seller and a product?

### 2.1 Identify the merchant through independent evidence

A merchant's own “About us” page can contain useful details, but the seller controls that page. For an unfamiliar seller, look beyond the listing. Search for the company name and terms such as “reviews,” “complaints,” “refunds,” or “scam”; check multiple sources with different incentives; review the age and consistency of publicly available information where reliable; and look for a genuine customer-service route. No single signal proves legitimacy. Newly established honest businesses may have limited review histories, while established scammers may have sophisticated websites.

A [[contact-information|contact information]] page should allow reasonable questions about delivery and returns, but an address or phone number typed on a webpage is not proof that it belongs to the merchant. Beware of a seller that refuses all ordinary contact, will discuss an order only through an unrelated messaging app, or suddenly changes the payment destination. When practical, verify the seller using an independently found website or customer-service route—not solely the contact link supplied in a suspicious ad or text.

| Evidence | What it may establish | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Familiar logo or product photography | The listing imitates or presents a brand | Authorization to sell that brand |
| HTTPS connection | Connection security with the visited server | Honesty or reliability of the store |
| Strong star rating | Other users or accounts gave favorable ratings | Whether reviews are genuine or representative |
| Business address and phone number | Published contact information | That the listed business controls those details |
| Marketplace purchase protections | A possible avenue for resolving eligible problems | Automatic refunds for every transaction |
| Clear delivery and return terms | The merchant states its policies | Whether the merchant will honor them without dispute |
| Independent reviews and records | Additional context about seller performance | Guaranteed authenticity or future performance |

### 2.2 Read reviews critically rather than counting stars

A [[customer-review|customer review]] can describe real experiences, but reviews can also be fake, incentivized, selective, or unrelated to the current product. Read a spread of recent, detailed comments and compare independent sources. Look for repeated patterns: wrong item sent, unexplained shipping fees, ignored refund requests, or counterfeit packaging. A few unhappy reviews do not automatically make a seller fraudulent; even reliable businesses sometimes make errors. The important skill is judging the **quality and independence of the evidence**.

Be skeptical of a cluster of nearly identical reviews posted in a short period, reviews containing vague praise without product details, or a product page that appears to have reviews for a different item. A seller may change a listing or reuse a product page over time. Check that the review actually refers to the correct model, size, version, and seller. Never treat a badge reading “verified,” “approved,” or “trusted” as a universal guarantee without understanding who issued it and under what conditions.

### 2.3 Inspect the product, not just the product photograph

Read the [[product-description|product description]] for model number, capacity, size, color, condition, included accessories, compatibility, warranty terms, and any limitations. Pay attention to words like “refurbished,” “open box,” “replacement,” “compatible with,” “used,” “digital download,” “subscription,” or “preorder.” These words can be legitimate and important. A low price for a “compatible” charger may not mean it has the same safety certifications or warranty as the manufacturer's original charger. A listing for a miniature model may use photographs of a full-size product.

For safety-critical goods, such as charging equipment or products intended for children, check the manufacturer's official specifications and any relevant recall or safety information rather than relying solely on marketplace claims. Avoid purchasing suspected [[counterfeit-product|counterfeit products]] or goods whose origin and specifications cannot reasonably be established. Extremely low prices may be a warning, especially for scarce, premium, or commonly counterfeited items, but price alone is not proof of fraud.

### 2.4 Compare seller terms before committing

A [[return-policy|return policy]] explains whether and under what conditions an item can be returned; a [[refund|refund]] is money returned following a qualifying cancellation, return, or dispute. A [[warranty|warranty]] is a separate promise about product defects or performance, often with its own conditions. Check return windows, who pays return shipping, restocking fees, final-sale exclusions, original packaging requirements, and whether refunds go back to the original payment method or become store credit.

For marketplaces, inspect both the platform's rules and the seller's conditions. An item shipped by a familiar warehouse may still be sold by an independent merchant. A seller may present a favorable advertised price but expensive international return shipping. A warranty from an overseas market might not apply where the purchaser lives. Understanding these terms before paying can prevent an ordinary disagreement from becoming an expensive surprise.

### 2.5 Beware of attempts to move a purchase outside the platform

An [[off-platform-payment|off-platform payment]] occurs when a seller tries to move a marketplace transaction to a separate channel—perhaps by requesting a direct transfer, gift card, or payment-app transfer instead of the marketplace's normal checkout. This can bypass transaction records, marketplace dispute procedures, or buyer protections. A seller claiming “You will save the platform fee if you pay me directly” may be asking the customer to give up useful safeguards.

Do not interpret every personal sale or direct-payment request as automatically fraudulent; different legitimate transaction types exist. For a novice buying through a marketplace, however, an unexpected request to leave the approved checkout is a compelling reason to pause. Use the platform's official reporting and support tools if the solicitation conflicts with its rules.

## 3. How do you compare costs and review checkout?

### 3.1 Calculate the complete price

An advertised [[item-price|item price]] may exclude [[shipping-fee|shipping]], handling, taxes, service fees, delivery surcharges, currency-conversion costs, or mandatory add-ons. The final [[order-total|order total]] is what the checkout proposes to charge for that purchase, subject to any clearly stated later charges. Compare the same model, quantity, product condition, delivery speed, and return terms across sellers. A “free shipping” label may be limited to certain order sizes, addresses, or memberships.

| Fictional offer | Item price | Shipping | Other stated fees | Pre-tax total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Store A | $29.00 | $13.00 | $0.00 | $42.00 |
| Store B | $37.00 | $0.00 | $2.00 | $39.00 |
| Store C | $31.00 | $4.00 | $6.00 | $41.00 |

All three offers have different advertised prices. Before applicable sales tax, Store B is the least expensive in this hypothetical comparison. The comparison does not yet establish that Store B has the best product condition, refund terms, delivery promise, or credibility. Those conditions must be evaluated separately. In a real checkout, check whether all relevant taxes and charges are shown and whether they can change by location or selection.

### 3.2 Read the conditions behind deals and discounts

A [[coupon-code|coupon code]], free trial, timed promotion, or advertised markdown may have minimum-purchase rules, exclusions, an expiration date, or a later recurring commitment. A prominent “70% off” message does not necessarily indicate a genuine historical price or an exceptional bargain. A [[countdown-timer|countdown timer]] may be legitimate, but it can also create artificial pressure to act without checking the terms. Ignore a deadline until you understand the actual offer and who is making it.

Be alert to [[subscription|subscriptions]] and [[automatic-renewal|automatic renewal]]. A box may enroll a buyer in recurring shipments, a membership, an extended trial, or a service that renews unless canceled. Examine whether add-ons are preselected, whether a trial becomes paid, how often charges occur, the amount of recurring fees, and how to cancel. Retain the relevant terms and any cancellation confirmation. A one-time purchase should not become a recurring obligation merely because the shopper rushed through checkout.

### 3.3 Review a checkout before the final authorization

The word [[checkout|checkout]] covers the final steps that turn a cart into an order. Some checkouts charge immediately when a button is pressed; others reserve an item, request an authorization, or redirect to another payment service. Before selecting “Place order,” “Pay,” or an equivalent action, inspect the recipient, product, quantity, address, shipping method, delivery estimate, taxes, fees, and payment method. Pay close attention if the browser suddenly changes to an unfamiliar domain or the payment recipient differs from the seller you thought you were using.

A practical pre-payment procedure is to stop at the final review screen and answer six questions: **Which seller? Which item? Which quantity? Which complete price? Which delivery or recurring obligations? Which payment method?** If any answer is missing or materially different from the expected terms, back out and verify through a trusted route. Do not enter financial information simply to explore whether a suspicious checkout might be legitimate.

| Checkpoint | Example question | Safer response if unclear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seller | Does the merchant name match the listing and payment request? | Verify the actual seller independently |
| Product | Is this the correct model, condition, and quantity? | Review the listing and cart |
| Cost | Are taxes, delivery, service fees, and add-ons included? | Find the full order total before confirming |
| Delivery | Is the shipping address and promise accurate? | Correct details before payment |
| Recurring obligation | Will I be charged again or enrolled in a plan? | Inspect terms and decline unwanted extras |
| Payment | Is this the approved checkout and payment recipient? | Stop if unexpectedly redirected or asked to transfer funds |
| Documentation | Will I receive a receipt and order identifier? | Save available terms and order confirmation |

### 3.4 Separate genuine account verification from impersonation

A legitimate payment service may require additional verification, such as a one-time code or approval in a banking app. That does not make every code request safe. Verify that the request occurs within a transaction you intentionally initiated through an authentic channel. A scammer may send a text claiming a delivery failed and ask for a “small re-delivery fee,” or may phone asking the buyer to recite a [[verification-code|verification code]] to “approve a refund.” A code used for authentication is not something to disclose to an unexpected caller or message sender.

If a payment or verification prompt appears when you were not completing a purchase, **do not approve it**. Use your normal banking or account app independently to review activity. A real merchant's customer-service agent generally does not need your account password or a security code for a separate sign-in to issue ordinary order assistance.

### 3.5 Observe account and device boundaries

Avoid completing financial transactions on a shared computer when you cannot control who can see the screen, what credentials may be stored, or whether the device is authorized for that use. If a trusted shared device must be used, do not save a password or payment card; sign out afterward and follow the owner's device rules. [[private-browsing|Private browsing]] can reduce selected local history retention but does not make an untrusted device safe, hide the transaction from the merchant, or ensure that saved files and receipts disappear.

Public [[wi-fi|Wi-Fi]] is not automatically compromised and mobile data is not automatically scam-proof. Use the official website or app over an appropriately secured connection, keep the device and browser updated, heed connection warnings, and avoid transactions on devices or networks whose reliability you cannot establish. The central risks include **the wrong site, the wrong recipient, and an insecure or uncontrolled device**, not merely the label of the network.

## 4. How do you protect payment and account information?

### 4.1 Payment choices affect remedies

A [[credit-card|credit card]] generally offers strong U.S. consumer protections for qualifying billing errors and unauthorized transactions. The Federal Trade Commission recommends paying by credit card when possible for online shopping. This does not mean a credit card makes every purchase wise, that all losses are automatically reimbursed, or that a shopper should borrow money they cannot repay. Interest, fees, issuer terms, and a person's ability to pay still matter. The important distinction here is how a payment is authorized and what process may be available if something goes wrong.

A [[debit-card|debit card]] normally draws from funds in a linked bank account. It can have protections and dispute pathways too, but applicable rules and response times may differ from those for credit cards. A [[digital-wallet|digital wallet]] or [[payment-app|payment app]] may help route a payment; the exact legal and platform protections depend on how the money is sent and what funding source or transaction type is used. Do not assume the presence of a well-known payment-app logo means that a personal transfer is protected as a merchant purchase.

| Payment method or request | Practical consideration | Warning or action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Credit card through a verified checkout | Often strong protections for qualifying billing errors | Review balance and terms; keep statements and receipts |
| Debit card through a verified checkout | Connected to bank funds, with different dispute rules | Monitor transactions and report problems promptly |
| Supported digital wallet | May reduce direct exposure of card details to a merchant | Verify actual merchant and transaction terms |
| Payment app merchant checkout | Protections vary by product and transaction classification | Read current eligible-purchase rules |
| Personal payment-app transfer | May be difficult to reverse when voluntarily sent | Do not treat as equivalent to protected merchant checkout |
| Gift card code requested by a seller | High-risk payment demand for online purchases | Do not send numbers or PINs as shopping payment |
| Wire transfer requested by an unknown store | Often difficult to recover after transfer | Pause and verify independently; do not comply with pressure |
| Cryptocurrency requested as the only payment | Different protections and frequently difficult reversals | Avoid paying an unverified merchant this way |

### 4.2 Understand what buyer protection can and cannot do

[[buyer-protection|Buyer protection]] is a term for possible remedies under laws, card-network rules, platform policies, or service agreements. Some apply only if a transaction is made through an approved checkout and meets specified conditions. [[purchase-dispute|Purchase disputes]] can involve goods not delivered, wrong items, damaged products, duplicate charges, or charges not authorized by the account holder. These situations may have different rules and evidence requirements; they are not interchangeable.

In the United States, there are specific legal procedures for many credit-card [[billing-error|billing errors]]. To preserve applicable federal rights, a consumer may need to submit a written notice to the issuer's designated billing-dispute address **within 60 days after the first statement containing the error was sent**. Notify the issuer promptly through its official channel, follow its current instructions, and do not assume that an online chat or ordinary telephone call alone always meets the formal notice requirement. Other types of disputes, debit-card transactions, and payment-platform claims can follow different processes. A shopper should consult the issuer's documentation or competent consumer help for the actual circumstances.

### 4.3 Protect credentials and payment details

Treat card numbers, security codes, login passwords, verification codes, and recovery information as sensitive. Enter them only in an appropriate verified process. A store may need payment data to complete a purchase, but a supposed support representative asking you to photograph your entire card, send a one-time code, or disclose your bank login is a warning sign. Some merchants can handle a refund using existing transaction records; a new payment or unrelated transfer should not be accepted on the unsupported claim that it is necessary to “unlock” a refund.

Use a [[password-manager|password manager]] to help create and retrieve unique account passwords where suitable; enable MFA where available and practical. Review stored cards and addresses, particularly after using a shared device or if an account is no longer used. If a merchant asks for excessive personal data, examine why it claims to need it. Do not complete a purchase when the request is unnecessary, unclear, or inconsistent with the product and delivery purpose.

### 4.4 Beware of financing and deferred-payment surprises

A [[buy-now-pay-later|buy now, pay later]] arrangement splits payment into installments or otherwise defers amounts due. It is not automatically free, riskless, or covered by the same dispute and billing protections as an ordinary credit-card purchase. Review the repayment schedule, late fees, potential interest, returns process, and how any merchant refund affects outstanding installments. A shopper may owe payments even while seeking a refund, depending on the provider and agreement. This lesson does not recommend a particular financing product; it teaches that deferred payment is still a financial obligation requiring careful review.

### 4.5 Keep notifications meaningful

Some banks and card providers offer [[transaction-alert|transaction alerts]] for purchases or account activity. These can help identify unexpected charges, but they do not prevent every type of fraud and may arrive after a transaction. Review statements independently. Never interpret a payment-related text with a link as proof that the transaction is real. If an alert seems unfamiliar, open the legitimate financial provider's app or type its known address rather than using the embedded message link.

## 5. How do you handle orders, shipping, and delivery?

### 5.1 Save the promise and the proof

After paying, retain a [[receipt|receipt]] or [[order-confirmation|order confirmation]] showing the merchant, date, product, quantity, charges, promised delivery terms, order number, and payment method without unnecessarily retaining complete card numbers. Screenshots or saved copies of important listing and return terms can be helpful when terms later change; store them securely. A receipt email can serve as evidence, but receiving it does not prove the merchant is legitimate or that delivery will occur.

A [[tracking-number|tracking number]] can help follow an order, but it may reflect only a created shipping label rather than a package in motion. Tracking data may be delayed or inaccurate. Compare the original delivery promise to updates received through the merchant or carrier's independent official site. A “Delivered” status does not, by itself, settle whether an item was correctly delivered or received by the authorized person. Keep facts distinct when reporting the problem.

### 5.2 Verify messages about delivery and fees

A [[delivery-scam|delivery scam]] may use a text or email claiming a package cannot be delivered until the recipient pays a small fee or updates an address through a supplied link. Scammers often exploit the fact that many people have a genuine package on the way. Do not click an unexpected message link or enter payment details to resolve a claim you have not verified. Open the actual merchant or carrier's established website or application and check the order using information you already have.

If a notification asks for an unfamiliar card, identity document, password, or one-time code, compare it with the merchant's original order terms. A legitimate carrier may require information in certain circumstances, but you should verify the requirement independently before taking action. Avoid calling a number supplied only in the suspicious notice.

### 5.3 Understand delivery expectations and basic U.S. protections

Under the FTC's U.S. Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule, covered sellers generally must have a reasonable basis for the advertised shipment time. If no shipping time is promised, the rule generally requires shipment within 30 days after receiving the properly completed order. If the seller cannot ship as promised, notice, consent, cancellation, or refund obligations may apply under the rule. The exact application can depend on the transaction type and circumstances; **shipping** is not identical to the date a package arrives at the buyer's door.

A merchant's estimate such as “delivers in two days” should be evaluated in context, and not every order is governed by exactly the same rules. Digital goods, subscriptions, custom services, and international orders can differ. If your shipment appears late, first document the seller's actual commitment and the current evidence. Do not assume that a tracking delay automatically means fraud or that silence from a seller makes every payment instantly reversible.

### 5.4 Inspect a delivered item without losing records

When a purchase arrives, check that it matches the model, count, condition, and specifications in the order. Save packaging and documentation if the return terms require them, and avoid discarding essential items until the condition is clear. For electronics or products involving safety risks, do not use a visibly damaged or suspect item in a way that could cause harm. If there is a defect or mismatch, record objective details and use the merchant's legitimate return process.

| Issue | Evidence to preserve | Appropriate initial next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Order confirmation missing | Order history, account statement, checkout details | Check official account before attempting another charge |
| Tracking not updated | Order date, shipment promise, tracking status | Check merchant and carrier through trusted channels |
| Unexpected delivery-fee text | Message details without opening links | Verify in official carrier or merchant account |
| Item never received | Receipt, promised dates, carrier status, correspondence | Contact seller and protect dispute deadlines |
| Wrong or damaged item | Photos of received item, packaging, order terms | Request remedy and retain documentation |
| Duplicate charge | Statement showing transaction dates and amounts | Verify whether one entry is temporary; contact issuer promptly if unexplained |
| Repeated subscription charge | Trial terms, cancellation steps and confirmations | Follow authorized cancellation and issuer procedures |

## 6. How do you respond when something goes wrong?

### 6.1 Identify the problem before choosing a remedy

A delayed package, an ordinary return, a [[billing-error|billing error]], an [[unauthorized-charge|unauthorized charge]], and a suspected [[fraud|fraud]] incident are different situations. First identify what happened and what you can confirm: Was an order actually placed? Was the account charged or merely shown a pending authorization? Did the seller promise a shipment date? Did the package arrive at the wrong location? Did you give payment credentials to an impersonator? Effective [[incident-response|incident response]] begins with an accurate description of the event rather than a single generic reaction.

If you only viewed a suspicious listing and did not disclose information, install software, or send payment, there may be no account or payment incident to remediate. Close the page and use a safer shopping route. If you disclosed a password or one-time code, account security becomes urgent. If money changed hands, notify the relevant financial institution or payment service immediately. If a suspicious application was installed, device security also requires attention.

### 6.2 Use an independent, timely resolution process

For ordinary order problems, contact the seller using an established channel, specify the order number and the requested remedy, and preserve copies of correspondence. On a marketplace, use the platform's authorized order and dispute interfaces where applicable. If a seller is unresponsive or the event appears fraudulent, contact the issuer or payment service through a known official route. Do **not** wait indefinitely for the merchant while an issuer, platform, or legal filing deadline approaches.

**Important distinction:** A merchant return policy, a platform buyer-protection deadline, and a federal billing-error deadline are separate. You may need to follow more than one process. No instructor or lesson can promise a refund merely because a scam was reported. The available remedies depend on what happened, the payment method, applicable law, evidence, and timing.

### 6.3 What to do after a suspected shopping scam

Use a proportional, ordered response. Stop additional communication with the suspicious seller if continued contact is unsafe or appears designed to extract more money. Notify the payment provider or bank promptly, explain whether the charge was authorized or you were deceived, and ask about the applicable dispute or reversal process. If card data may be exposed, ask the issuer whether replacement, monitoring, or other safeguards are appropriate. Change compromised account credentials through the real service and review sign-in activity; turn on MFA if available. If someone gained device access or installed software, use trusted security support and avoid typing new sensitive information into a possibly compromised device until it is safe.

Report suspected fraud to the FTC at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/); in circumstances involving identity theft, use [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/); for suspected internet crime, [FBI IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/) may also be appropriate. A report can help authorities identify patterns, but it does not itself reverse a charge. Save transaction details, dates, communications, and case or claim numbers in a safe place. Do not send additional funds to a person promising to recover a previous loss; [[recovery-scam|recovery scams]] can target victims after the initial incident.

### 6.4 Decision table: choose the next authorized step

| What happened? | First response | What not to assume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You saw a suspicious ad but did not interact | Leave the page and verify the seller elsewhere | Merely seeing it means payment data was stolen |
| You placed a legitimate order but no confirmation appeared | Check official account and card activity before resubmitting | The payment definitely failed |
| A seller reports a shipment delay | Compare promised terms and request an update or applicable remedy | Every delay is fraud or always entitles instant refund |
| A seller insists on a second payment to process a refund | Pause; verify independently and contact payment provider | Paying another fee will recover the first payment |
| You notice an unauthorized charge | Contact issuer or bank immediately using its verified contact channel | The merchant is the only party you can contact |
| You supplied a password to a fake checkout | Secure the real account and reused credentials | Canceling the order alone fixes credential exposure |
| You installed suspicious software | Stop sensitive tasks and seek trusted security assistance | Changing a password on the affected device is always sufficient |
| You sent money by a hard-to-reverse method | Contact the transfer provider immediately and report | Every transfer can be reversed or funds are certainly lost |

### 6.5 A repeatable shopping checklist

Before any real purchase, use the same checklist. First, identify the exact product and who is selling it. Second, inspect independent evidence about the merchant and the real website address. Third, compare the final price and any additional obligations. Fourth, check delivery, returns, warranty, and the payment method's safeguards. Fifth, confirm that the checkout has not changed the recipient or inserted unwanted subscriptions. Sixth, save the receipt and promised terms. Finally, monitor the delivery and financial account and resolve problems promptly using official channels.

> **Final operating rule:** A convincing website is not the same as a verified merchant, a low displayed price is not the same as a low total cost, and a completed payment is not the same as a completed order. Make each decision separately and preserve the evidence needed to explain it.

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

### “A padlock proves the seller is legitimate.”

HTTPS helps secure the connection with the website reached by the browser. It does not verify that the business behind the page is honest or authorized to sell the advertised product. Check the real destination, seller information, external evidence, and purchasing terms. Encrypted scam websites exist.

### “A famous marketplace guarantees every individual seller.”

Marketplaces can host many different merchants under different fulfillment, return, and protection arrangements. Identify the seller of record and read the relevant platform and seller policies. Marketplace visibility alone is not a guarantee of product authenticity or refund eligibility.

### “Thousands of five-star reviews must be authentic.”

Ratings can be manipulated or mismatched to the item. Compare multiple sources and evaluate review substance, dates, specificity, and relevance. A credible-sounding positive review does not erase warning signs about payment methods or the real seller.

### “The lowest advertised price means the least expensive purchase.”

An item price can exclude taxes, delivery, fees, needed accessories, and return costs. A discounted item with a nonreturnable condition or recurring charge can be more expensive overall. Compare the full agreement, not just a marketing banner.

### “It is harmless to send a small test payment.”

A small fee can still disclose payment information, create an opportunity for further fraud, or represent money that cannot be recovered. Do not make test purchases at suspicious stores as a way to check their legitimacy. Legitimate verification should not require financial experimentation.

### “Any secure payment logo means a refund is guaranteed.”

Legal and platform protections vary by funding source and type of transfer. Personal payments, merchant checkouts, credit-card charges, and digital-wallet transactions may have different rights. Understand the actual payment method, recipient, eligibility conditions, and deadlines.

### “If a support person knows my order number, I should trust them.”

Order information may be shared, stolen, guessed, or obtained through impersonation. Verify identity through the merchant's real account or independently sourced customer-service route. Never disclose passwords or authentication codes to a caller simply because they know an order detail.

### “If a page freezes, I should click Pay several more times.”

Repeated clicks or resubmissions can create multiple orders or authorizations. Check the official order history and financial account before trying again. Investigate uncertain payment status rather than assuming every unsuccessful-looking screen means no transaction occurred.

### “Every delivery delay means the seller is a scam.”

Legitimate delays happen. Review the actual shipment promise, evidence of carrier acceptance, seller communications, and governing terms. Escalate when warranted, but base the claim on the facts and protect the relevant deadlines.

### “I can take as much time as I want to dispute a charge.”

Consumer rights and card, marketplace, and payment-service deadlines are not unlimited. U.S. federal credit-card billing-error procedures may require a timely written notice, typically within 60 days after the first statement containing the error is sent. Other dispute categories can follow other rules. Contact the issuer promptly through official channels when a problem arises.

### “An app or payment method makes an unknown seller safe.”

A reputable device, browser, shopping app, or wallet does not evaluate every transaction for you. A scammer can appear inside a familiar communication tool or persuade a buyer to move a transaction to a poorly protected channel. Seller verification remains necessary.

### “A report to the police or FTC automatically reverses the charge.”

Reporting helps document and investigate fraud but generally does not itself initiate a card or bank reversal. The shopper must promptly use the appropriate issuer, bank, platform, or transfer-provider process. Keep reports and claim identifiers as evidence, but do not substitute them for financial action.

### Compact reminder: stop, verify, or proceed

| Situation | Unsafe assumption | Safer decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unexpected “official store” ad | Brand graphics prove legitimacy | Open the verified seller independently |
| Attractive discount with a countdown | Urgency proves the offer is rare | Compare terms before deciding |
| Checkout adds a membership | The extra fee cannot be changed | Review and remove unwanted commitments before paying |
| Seller requests payment-app friends transfer | A familiar app guarantees buyer protection | Use approved merchant checkout or do not proceed |
| Product listing says “refurbished” | It must be brand new | Confirm condition and included warranty |
| Text demands a carrier re-delivery fee | Every parcel notice is from the carrier | Check the official order and carrier separately |
| Payment screen stalls | The payment definitely failed | Check official order and account activity before retrying |
| Order arrives damaged | The problem can only be solved through a public review | Document condition and use return, support, or dispute channels |
| Unrecognized card charge appears | It is safe to wait until the next month | Notify the issuer promptly through an established contact route |
| Scammer promises refund for another fee | More payment will recover the loss | Stop and report; contact the original payment provider |

> **Safety principle:** When you cannot independently establish the seller, full terms, recipient, or remedy pathway, stopping is not indecision. It is an informed transaction-control decision.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The underlined expressions in the lesson are [[vocabulary-tooltip|interactive vocabulary terms]]. Selecting one in the MGU dashboard shows a brief definition; some terms may also link to a relevant official or MGU reference. This visible glossary works even if tooltips are unavailable. The final glossary registry supplies machine-readable versions of these definitions for the dashboard.

### Sellers, platforms, and trust

- **Safe online shopping:** Using repeatable checks to reduce fraud, payment, privacy, and delivery risks during internet purchases.
- **Online shopping:** Buying goods or services through a website or app.
- **Retailer:** Business that sells products or services to end customers.
- **Online marketplace:** Service hosting listings from multiple sellers, sometimes including itself.
- **Third-party seller:** Independent merchant using a larger marketplace.
- **Platform:** Digital service providing a marketplace, account, communications, or transaction tools.
- **Product listing:** A webpage or app record advertising a particular item or offer.
- **Product description:** Details about an item's model, specifications, condition, and included parts.
- **Customer review:** Published opinion or account of a product or shopping experience.
- **Sponsored listing:** Advertisement or paid placement in shopping or search results.
- **Search result:** Listing returned by a search service, not a guarantee of trustworthiness.
- **Domain name:** Named website destination used in its internet address.
- **HTTPS:** A protocol commonly used to encrypt communication between the browser and addressed server.
- **Counterfeit product:** Item presented as another brand's authentic product without authorization.
- **Contact information:** Published means for communicating with a merchant, which must still be evaluated.
- **Independent verification:** Checking a claim through a trusted route not supplied solely by a questionable source.

### Prices, conditions, and orders

- **Shopping cart:** Temporary collection of chosen items before ordering.
- **Checkout:** Final steps for reviewing an order and authorizing purchase or payment.
- **Item price:** Price of the product before separate charges.
- **Shipping fee:** Charge related to transporting a purchase.
- **Order total:** Complete stated amount due for a purchase, with relevant included charges.
- **Service fee:** Separate charge associated with transaction or service processing.
- **Coupon code:** Code used to request an offer or discount subject to its terms.
- **Countdown timer:** Display showing limited time, which may be legitimate or manipulative.
- **Subscription:** Repeated delivery or service arrangement with specified charges or terms.
- **Automatic renewal:** Arrangement renewing an agreement or billing period unless canceled under its rules.
- **Return policy:** Conditions for sending an item back to a seller.
- **Refund:** Money returned after a qualifying reversal, cancellation, return, or dispute.
- **Warranty:** Promise concerning product quality, defects, or performance under specified terms.
- **Guest checkout:** Completing a purchase without registering a persistent shopping account.
- **Receipt:** Record of an order or payment.
- **Order confirmation:** Record that a merchant or system acknowledges an order.
- **Tracking number:** Identifier assigned to follow a shipment through a delivery process.

### Payments and financial safeguards

- **Payment processor:** Service or system that handles payment authorization or processing for a merchant.
- **Credit card:** Payment card that generally uses an extension of credit subject to terms and applicable protections.
- **Debit card:** Payment card usually connected to funds in a bank or credit-union account.
- **Digital wallet:** Application or system used to store or present payment credentials or tokens.
- **Payment app:** Application allowing money transfers or merchant payments under particular rules.
- **Off-platform payment:** Sending money outside the marketplace or service's approved checkout route.
- **Buyer protection:** Possible remedies provided by applicable law, card rules, or platform policies.
- **Billing error:** Qualifying issue with a statement or credit account charge under applicable rules.
- **Purchase dispute:** Request for resolution of a transaction problem through an issuer or service.
- **Unauthorized charge:** Charge made without valid authorization from the account holder.
- **Transaction alert:** Account notification about selected transaction activity.
- **Buy now, pay later:** Deferred or installment-payment arrangement with its own terms.
- **Verification code:** One-time value used to complete a sign-in or other authentication process.
- **Pending authorization:** Temporary transaction approval that may not be a settled charge.

### Safety, personal information, and response

- **Phishing:** Deceptive messages or pages intended to extract secrets or induce harmful action.
- **Fraud:** Intentional deception for improper benefit.
- **Delivery scam:** Fraudulent notice or request exploiting an expected package or shipment.
- **Recovery scam:** Additional fraud promising to recover lost money or property for a fee or secret.
- **Personal information:** Information identifying or relating to a person.
- **Data minimization:** Limiting disclosure to information needed for a legitimate purpose.
- **Multifactor authentication:** Account verification using an additional factor or supported step.
- **Password manager:** Tool that securely stores and helps use account credentials.
- **Private browsing:** Mode limiting some local browsing records, not guaranteeing anonymity or a safe device.
- **Wi-Fi:** Wireless networking technology used to connect devices and local networks.
- **Incident response:** Practical steps to contain, document, report, and recover from a problem.
- **Consumer protection:** Laws, policies, and practices intended to address unfair or harmful business conduct.
- **Authentication:** Process of confirming a person's or system's claimed identity.
- **Secure connection:** Connection providing specified protection for data in transit.
- **Personal transfer:** Money sent to another person outside a conventional merchant purchase arrangement.
- **Privacy policy:** Statement describing how an organization handles personal data.
- **Identity theft:** Misuse of someone's identifying information to impersonate them or commit fraud.
- **Chargeback:** Reversal or dispute mechanism involving a payment-card transaction under applicable rules.

Knowing vocabulary is useful only if it improves a decision. For example: *“The marketplace is familiar, but this listing names a third-party seller; the seller's request for an off-platform payment may bypass buyer protection, so I will not pay until I can use a verified checkout.”* That single explanation connects the correct participants, the proposed action, and the relevant risk.

## 11. MGU Library connections and next steps

### Summary: buy deliberately and preserve your options

Safe online shopping involves verifying the real seller, checking the actual item and complete transaction, selecting an appropriate payment method, retaining evidence, and following up through legitimate channels. Protecting an account with strong authentication is important, but it does not validate the seller or product. HTTPS protects a connection, not a merchant’s honesty. A familiar marketplace, a five-star review, and a polished advertisement are useful clues only when weighed with independent evidence. The buyer’s best protection is a purchasing process they can explain and document.

| Before you... | Confirm... | If uncertain... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Follow a shopping advertisement | Actual destination and seller identity | Visit the official merchant through an independently verified route |
| Trust a product listing | Correct item, condition, reviews, and warranty | Compare manufacturer and independent information |
| Accept a discount | Final costs and any recurring obligations | Postpone checkout and inspect the terms |
| Pay on a marketplace | Seller of record and approved checkout | Do not move funds to an unrelated transfer channel |
| Authorize a charge | Recipient, item, quantity, full amount, and payment method | Correct or abandon the transaction |
| Respond to a carrier text | Order history and authentic tracking status | Use the legitimate retailer and carrier independently |
| Repeat a stalled payment | Whether an order or authorization already exists | Check account and order status before trying again |
| Seek a refund or dispute | Applicable seller, platform, and issuer requirements | Act promptly and preserve deadlines |
| Report suspected fraud | Evidence and relevant payment or account exposure | Contact financial provider immediately as well as reporting |
