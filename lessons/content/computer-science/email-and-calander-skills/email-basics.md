## Lesson 1: Email Basics

Email lets you send written messages and other information to people using an internet-connected device. It is used for school, work, job applications, appointments, online accounts, shopping receipts, and everyday communication. In this lesson, you will learn how to recognize an email address, find your inbox, read a message, prepare and send a simple email, and check whether your work was saved or sent. You will also develop habits that protect your information and help prevent avoidable mistakes.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 70–100 minutes (including practice) · **Prerequisites:** Basic familiarity with a device, keyboard or touchscreen, and internet access · **Practice equipment:** Computer, tablet, or smartphone; an email account is optional for observation and written simulations

> **Essential idea:** Email is a way to exchange messages. You use an **email address** to identify the intended recipient, an **inbox** to read received messages, **Compose** to create a new message, and **Send** to submit it for delivery. Always check the recipient, message, and safety of your actions before sending or opening unfamiliar content.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Describe email in everyday language and distinguish it from texting, live chat, and ordinary postal mail.
1. Recognize the basic parts of an email address and tell an address apart from a website address.
1. Identify the principal parts of an email message: From, To, subject, body, and date or time.
1. Navigate a typical email service to locate Inbox, Compose/New message, Drafts, Sent, and Search.
1. Create a short, courteous email, check its recipient and content, and send it only to an authorized address.
1. Explain the difference between a saved draft, a sent message, and a successfully delivered message.
1. Apply basic privacy, password, sign-in, and suspicious-message precautions.
1. Complete simple email tasks or equivalent no-account simulations and evaluate common mistakes.

## 1. What is email and what is it used for?

[[email|Email]] means **electronic mail**. It is a system for exchanging messages through connected computer services. You usually access it by visiting an email website in a [[web-browser|web browser]] or opening an [[email-app|email application]] on a computer or mobile device. If you have access to your account, you can ordinarily read messages and send new ones from more than one compatible device.

Think of email as a digital version of sending a letter. You prepare a message, specify who should receive it, and ask the email service to deliver it. The recipient may see it quickly, but they do not have to be online at the same moment. They can read it later, save it, or respond when convenient.

Email supports everyday situations such as:

- A student asking when a class begins or receiving a school announcement.
- A worker receiving a shift schedule or sharing a short project update.
- A patient receiving a notification that a secure medical portal has a new message; sensitive medical details are often handled inside the portal rather than in ordinary email.
- A shopper receiving an order confirmation and reviewing the purchase details.
- An applicant exchanging messages with an employer or checking an interview invitation.
- A family member sharing a nonurgent update with another family member.
- An organization distributing a newsletter to people who have subscribed.

Email is useful because it creates a written record you can revisit. That does **not** mean it is automatically private, permanent, or proof that the information is truthful. Messages can be deleted, altered when quoted, forwarded, sent to the wrong person, or impersonated by an attacker. Developing reliable habits matters as much as learning which button to press.

### Email is not the same as a website, an account, or the internet

The [[internet|internet]] is the wider network that helps connected services communicate. A [[website|website]] is information or an application you access through a browser. An [[email-service|email service]] provides the infrastructure for receiving and sending mail. Your [[email-account|email account]] is your individual access to that service. A particular message is a piece of content exchanged through that system.

For example, you might visit a provider's website in Chrome or Edge and sign in with your email account. The browser is the program; the website is the interface; the account is yours; and the inbox contains messages. These terms describe different things.

### Email, texting, chat, and postal mail compared

| Method | Typical use | Does the recipient need to be online at the same time? | What to remember |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Email | Written notices, questions, records, professional communication | No | Messages can be saved, forwarded, and reviewed later |
| Text message (SMS or messaging app) | Short, often immediate exchanges | No for basic SMS; app behavior varies | Phone numbers or app accounts usually identify contacts |
| Live chat or video call | Real-time discussion | Usually yes for a live exchange | Conversation is intended to happen together |
| Postal mail | Physical letters and documents | No | Delivery occurs through a physical mailing system |

Some email services display messages as a **conversation** or [[thread|thread]] when they have the same subject and are related by replies. This makes multiple messages appear together. It does not turn email into a live call, and it does not mean every participant will automatically read each update.

### What happens after you select Send?

A simplified description is sufficient for a beginner:

1. You create a message and enter an intended recipient.
1. You press **Send**, requesting that your email service process the message.
1. The service attempts to route it to the receiving service or mailbox.
1. The receiving system may accept it, filter it, delay it, or reject it.
1. The recipient may find it in an inbox, in spam/junk, or somewhere else, depending on the system.

A message appearing in **Sent** is evidence that your service handled it as a sent message. It is **not a guarantee** the recipient received, opened, or understood it. The next section explains the information needed to direct the message.

> **Remember:** Email is usually an *asynchronous* communication method: people write and read on their own schedules. For urgent emergencies, do not rely on email alone; use the appropriate emergency or immediate-contact channel.

## 2. How do email addresses and messages work?

An [[email-address|email address]] identifies an account or mailbox that can receive messages. It is not necessarily a person's legal name. Organizations may use shared addresses for a department, and one person can have more than one address.

### The three visible parts of an address

Consider the **fictional example** `alex.student@example.com`:

- `alex.student` is the **local part** or mailbox name chosen or assigned within the service.
- `@` (pronounced “at”) separates the local part from the domain.
- `example.com` is the [[domain-name|domain name]] associated with the receiving email service.

An address must be entered accurately. A missing character, extra period, incorrect domain, or unexpected space can cause a message to go to the wrong address or fail. Email addresses are not generally interchangeable with people's displayed names. A contact may appear as “Alex Student” even though the actual address is something else.

> **Practice-only addresses:** Addresses ending in `example.com`, such as `alex.student@example.com` and `sam.helper@example.com`, are fictional examples for reading and writing exercises. **Do not send practice mail to these addresses.** If you send a real test message, use only your own address or a willing participant's confirmed address.

### An address is different from a website URL

| Example | What it is | How it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `alex.student@example.com` | An email address | Enter it in the To field of a message |
| `https://www.example.com` | A website address or URL | Enter it in a browser's address bar |
| `Alex Student` | A display name | May be shown beside an email address; does not prove identity |
| `example.com` | A domain name | Can form part of an email address or website address |

The **@ symbol** is the simplest visual clue that a string may be an email address. A valid-looking address is not proof that the person or organization using it is legitimate. An attacker might create a similar-looking address or forge the visible sender information.

### The main parts of a message

An email has several commonly visible fields. The exact order and design differ among services, but their functions are similar.

| Field | Plain-language meaning | Example in a safe practice message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| From | The sender address shown by the service | `alex.student@example.com` |
| To | The person or mailbox the message is addressed to | `sam.helper@example.com` |
| Subject | A short description of the message | `Practice: Thursday library visit` |
| Message body | The main text you want the reader to see | `Hello Sam, I plan to visit the library on Thursday.` |
| Date/time | When the message was sent or received, as displayed | `Thursday, 10:30 AM` (illustrative) |
| Attachment | A file included with a message, when present | A permitted document or picture; not needed here |

The **subject** should communicate the topic, not carry the entire conversation. The **body** contains the information. The **To** field deserves special attention because sending to an unintended address could reveal something private or confusing. A visible **From** address is useful but is not sufficient by itself to authenticate the sender.

### A model email, shown without sending anything

**From:** `alex.student@example.com`  
**To:** `sam.helper@example.com`  
**Subject:** `Practice: Thursday library visit`

Hello Sam,

I am planning to visit the library on Thursday afternoon. Is the study room available after 2:00 PM?

Thank you,  
Alex

This example has a **greeting**, a clear request, and a **sign-off**. The full lesson on sentence-level clarity, tone, and professional email writing appears next in *Writing Clear Emails*. For now, your goal is to understand how the parts fit together.

### What are Cc and Bcc?

Some message forms include **[[cc|Cc]]** and **[[bcc|Bcc]]** fields. They are ways to address additional recipients. Cc recipients are normally visible to the other recipients. Bcc recipients are generally hidden from the other recipients' lists, but Bcc is not a security or confidentiality guarantee. Use **To** for this lesson. You will practice Cc, Bcc, Reply All, and forwarding in a later topic.

> **Before sending:** Read the entire recipient address, not just its first few letters or its display name. Contact suggestions and automatic completion are convenient, but they can insert the wrong person.

## 3. How do you access an email account?

To receive and send mail, you normally need an [[email-account|email account]] with an email provider or an account supplied by your school or organization. Common consumer services include Gmail and Outlook.com; employers and schools may use managed accounts. There is no requirement to choose a particular provider for this course.

### Browser-based email and email applications

You may access your account in two common ways:

1. **Webmail:** Open a browser, go to your provider's verified website using a bookmark or a correctly typed address, then sign in as needed. Examples include Gmail in a browser and Outlook on the web.
1. **Email app:** Use a trusted mail application installed on your phone or computer. The application may request you to add or connect an email account. Choose a legitimate application and follow your provider's sign-in process.

A phone may show the word **Mail** or an envelope icon. On a desktop, the link may say **Mail**, **Gmail**, **Outlook**, or a provider name. The label and button placement can change after updates. Focus on their *purposes*, not only the appearance of one screenshot.

### Signing in safely

Your [[username|username]] may be your email address or another account identifier. Your [[password|password]] is a secret credential. Many accounts use [[multi-factor-authentication|multi-factor authentication (MFA)]], which adds a separate verification step such as an authenticator prompt or a security key.

A cautious sign-in process is:

1. Start from your own saved bookmark, your organization's trusted instructions, or a web address you have verified independently.
1. Check that the page or app belongs to the provider you intended to use. An HTTPS padlock alone does **not** prove that a page is trustworthy.
1. Enter your own credentials only into the legitimate sign-in experience. Never use credentials supplied by an untrusted stranger.
1. Complete the provider's approved additional verification step when prompted.
1. Confirm that the signed-in account is the one you intended to use, especially if you have personal, school, and work accounts.
1. On a shared or public computer, avoid saving your sign-in information; sign out when finished and follow the location's security policy.

Never share your password, recovery codes, one-time sign-in codes, or account recovery answers with an instructor, classmate, or someone claiming to be technical support. A genuine class exercise does not require those secrets.

### If you do not have an email account

You can complete every *observation, vocabulary, worksheet, and knowledge-check task* in this lesson without creating an account. A real send-and-receive test is optional. If you later decide to register:

1. Choose an established provider or follow your school's or employer's instructions.
1. Navigate to the provider independently instead of following an unsolicited link.
1. Review the provider's requirements, terms, privacy notices, and any age or administrator restrictions.
1. Create a unique password or passphrase; consider a reputable password manager.
1. Set up a suitable recovery method and MFA if available.
1. Keep recovery information accurate and secure; do not publish it in a practice worksheet.

Many providers offer free account options, but services can also have paid tiers, limits, or school-managed policies. Registration interfaces change over time. This lesson does not require a purchase or the submission of personal information to MGU.

### Accounts, devices, and syncing

An email account can often be used through both a computer and a phone. When both applications connect to the same account, changes may be [[synchronization|synchronized]]: a sent message or a read/unread change on one device may appear on another. Timing and behavior differ by provider, account settings, app, and connectivity. A message that appears in a local app while offline may not have been sent yet.

If your inbox looks different on two devices, first confirm you are signed into the **same account**. Also check internet connectivity, whether the message is in a different folder or label, and whether the application has refreshed. Do not repeatedly press Send while the connection is uncertain; that can create duplicates.

### Accessible ways to work

Email does not require a mouse. Most services support keyboard navigation and are designed to work with accessibility features, though keyboard shortcuts differ. You may use screen magnification, browser zoom, voice dictation, screen readers, touch input, or a physical keyboard. If the screen is hard to read, increase text size or zoom without changing account security settings. Do not feel required to memorize one provider's exact button locations.

> **Remember:** **Signing in** opens access to your account; **creating an account** establishes a new one. They are different actions. For this lesson, it is acceptable to practice without either.

## 4. How do you navigate an email inbox?

The [[inbox|Inbox]] is the usual starting location for received email that the service has placed there. It may show a list of messages, each with a sender, subject, short preview, and date or time. Some services also group messages into categories or display conversation threads.

When you first open email, locate the main areas without clicking on unknown messages or links. You are conducting a **screen survey**, not a cleanup or security inspection.

### Common locations and commands

| Location or command | Primary purpose | Beginner habit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inbox | Displays ordinary received messages | Look for sender, subject, and time |
| Compose or New message | Opens a form for writing a message | Use it when creating something new |
| Drafts | Holds messages saved but not sent | Recheck a draft before sending |
| Sent or Sent Items | Stores copies of messages treated as sent | Confirm the message appears there |
| Spam or Junk | Holds messages identified as suspicious or unwanted | Do not assume a message is safe because you find it elsewhere |
| Trash or Deleted Items | Holds messages marked for deletion | Deleting may be reversible only for a limited period |
| Archive | Removes a message from the normal inbox view without necessarily deleting it | Do not confuse Archive with Delete |
| Search | Finds messages by words or other criteria | Search by a known subject or sender |
| Unread indicator | Shows a message you have not marked as read | Do not equate unread status with importance |

Services sometimes use **folders** and sometimes **labels**. These can serve related organizational purposes but are not identical in every product. You will practice classifying and organizing mail in the *Inbox Organization* topic. For now, know how to find and recognize the key locations.

### What does an inbox row tell you?

Suppose a practice inbox line displays:

`Sam Helper  |  Library visit  |  The study room is open...  |  10:42 AM`

The **display name** tells you what sender name the interface presents. The **subject** gives the topic. The **preview** shows the opening text, and the **time** reports when the service associates the message with the event shown. The precise order depends on the application.

Before responding, open the actual message and inspect the sender's **full address** where the interface allows it. A displayed name alone can be misleading. Do not click a link merely because a sender name looks familiar.

### Read, unread, selected, starred, and important

A [[read-status|read status]] often changes when you open a message; the item may become lighter or lose bold formatting. This visual change means the app marked it as read. It does not necessarily mean you understood it, agreed with it, or acted on its request.

- **Selected** often means you have checked a box or highlighted a message so that commands can be applied.
- **Starred/flagged** usually marks a message for attention.
- **Important/priority** may reflect a label, personal selection, or a provider's automated judgment.
- **Unread** usually means the service has not marked that message as opened or read.

If you are unsure what a symbol means, look for a visible label, tooltip, accessibility name, or provider help page. Avoid clicking irreversible controls simply to discover their function.

### Looking at a message without acting on it

1. Choose a message you recognize as yours or a trusted class example.
1. Locate the **sender address**, **subject**, and **main text**.
1. Observe the date or time and any Reply or Forward controls, without using them.
1. If there is an attachment icon or hyperlink, recognize its presence but do not open or download it for this exercise.
1. Return to the inbox with the application's Back or Inbox control.

> **Practical caution:** **Opening a message** and **opening an attachment or link inside it** are separate actions. The latter can carry additional risk. This course covers attachments and suspicious email in dedicated lessons.

## 5. How do you compose and send an email?

[[compose|Compose]] means creating a new outgoing message. The goal is to produce one complete, accurate email, not to experiment with unknown accounts or send repeated test messages.

### A six-step sending workflow

1. **Choose New message or Compose.** Look for a button labeled *Compose*, *New*, or *New mail*. On a phone, it may be a pencil or plus symbol. If you cannot identify it, use the app's help instructions.
1. **Check the account shown in From.** Especially if you use multiple accounts, make sure you are sending from the intended one.
1. **Enter the recipient in To.** Type the address carefully or select a verified contact. Confirm the complete result, including everything after `@`.
1. **Write a subject.** Use a brief description such as `Practice message` or `Question about library hours` rather than an empty subject or an entire paragraph.
1. **Write the body.** Use a greeting, one or two clear sentences, and a simple ending. Check spelling and whether the message tells the recipient what you need.
1. **Review and Send.** Read To, From, subject, body, and any unintended added recipients or files. Select Send only when the message is ready and authorized.

You can **stop before step 6** during a simulated lesson. Nothing requires sending a message to a stranger, organization, or instructor. A [[draft|draft]] may be saved so you can return to it; depending on the application, it may save automatically or you may need to save it explicitly.

### A realistic beginner message

**To:** `your-own-confirmed-address` (for a real self-test)  
**Subject:** `My first practice email`

Hello,

This is a short practice message. I am learning how to write an email, check the recipient, and use the Send button.

Thank you,  
A student

The `your-own-confirmed-address` text is a **placeholder**, not a working email address. Replace it only with **your own actual email address** if you choose to perform an optional real test. Do not copy it literally into the To field.

### The final five checks before Send

| Check | Question | If the answer is no |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recipient | Is this the correct, fully verified address? | Correct the address before continuing |
| Sender | Am I sending from the account I intended? | Switch or check account selection |
| Subject | Does it briefly tell the reader why I am writing? | Add or revise the subject |
| Body | Is the request complete, courteous, and appropriate for this person? | Edit and reread it |
| Privacy | Have I avoided unnecessary secrets, private details, or unintended recipients? | Remove sensitive content or choose a safer channel |

The checks take very little time once they become a habit. They are especially valuable when a contact list offers two people with similar names or you are writing from a shared work account.

### Is every email private?

No. Standard email is not a guaranteed confidential communication channel. Depending on the service, account configuration, sender and recipient systems, and organizational controls, messages may be retained, processed, forwarded, or accessed by other authorized parties. Avoid sending passwords, one-time security codes, full financial account numbers, or highly sensitive documents by ordinary email unless an authorized secure process specifically requires and protects them.

### What happens when you press Send?

Many email services display a **Message sent** notification and place a copy in **Sent**. Some provide an **Undo Send** option for a short cancellation period. This is not a universal ability to retrieve a delivered message. For example, Gmail offers a configurable short cancellation window; once that window expires, you should not assume the message can be taken back.

If you change your mind **before** sending, return to the draft and edit it. If you already sent the message to an incorrect person, follow your organization's policies if applicable and promptly determine the appropriate corrective action. Do not assume deleting your local Sent copy deletes the recipient's copy.

### A draft is useful, not a failure

A [[draft|draft]] is an unfinished or intentionally unsent message. You may save a draft because you need to confirm a date, check a name, or think through a response. When you reopen it, check the entire message again. Some applications remember the earlier recipient or attachments, which may no longer be appropriate.

> **Remember:** **Compose → To → Subject → Body → Review → Send** is the routine to master. Accuracy comes before speed.

## 6. How do you read, check, and handle email?

A useful email routine has two sides: sending messages correctly and understanding incoming messages before acting. This section brings together the introductory concepts and identifies what belongs in later lessons.

### Reading a new message in the right order

When a message is new to you:

1. **Read the subject and sender information** without assuming the display name proves identity.
1. **Examine the complete message.** Notice the request, deadlines, and any claims about urgent action.
1. **Ask whether you expected the message.** A familiar company name or an official-looking logo does not prove authenticity.
1. **Separate information from action.** You can understand what a message claims without clicking its links, opening files, or responding.
1. **Verify important requests independently.** If a message says your bank, job, or school account has a problem, contact that organization through a previously known and trusted method.
1. **Decide what, if anything, is required.** Some messages are just information; others deserve a reply, a calendar entry, or review later.

If a message is clearly suspicious, do not interact with its links or files; use your email service's report-spam or report-phishing controls as appropriate. Never provide a password or verification code in response to an unsolicited email.

### Replies, Reply All, and Forwarding: a first look

Three commands commonly appear when reading a message:

- [[reply|Reply]] starts a response addressed to the sender of the message (with behavior subject to mail headers and app settings).
- [[reply-all|Reply All]] generally includes multiple participants in the original message. It can expose your response to people you did not intend; check all recipients before using it.
- [[forward|Forward]] prepares a copy of the message for additional recipients. It may include previous correspondence, addresses, or attachments, so consider privacy and permission before sharing it.

The specialized lesson *Replying, Forwarding, and CC* explains these operations in depth. For this first lesson, recognize the buttons and do not use **Reply All** or **Forward** in practice unless you fully understand the audience.

### Sending is not the same as delivery or reading

| State | What it usually indicates | What it does **not** prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Draft | You have composed and saved a message without sending it | That a recipient received anything |
| Sending or Outbox | The app is attempting or waiting to transmit | That delivery has succeeded |
| Sent | Your service considers the message sent or submitted | That the recipient opened, read, or acted on it |
| Delivery failure or bounce | A delivery problem was reported | That every possible message to this person will always fail |
| Reply received | Someone or a system responded | That every original detail was read correctly |

A [[bounce|bounce message]] or delivery failure notice may say an address could not be reached, the mailbox was full, the message was rejected, or another problem occurred. Read the notice carefully. If the address is wrong, verify the correct address **from an independent trusted source**. Do not click an unexpected “fix your account” link within a suspicious failure notification.

If an important message receives no reply, that alone does not prove the message failed. The person may be unavailable or may not check email frequently. Choose a reasonable follow-up time and, when appropriate, a separate contact channel.

### A simple daily email habit

1. Open your account using your usual trusted route.
1. Review the sender, subject, and urgency of new messages.
1. Read messages you recognize and verify unexpected requests before acting.
1. Respond to appropriate messages or save a draft when more information is required.
1. Check Sent after sending an important message.
1. Sign out if you are using a shared or public device.

An adult learner may check mail at convenient times rather than constantly. A work or school role may have its own response-time expectations. Email notifications are useful, but their absence does not guarantee you have no new mail. Some devices mute notifications or temporarily lose connectivity.

### Common problems and careful first responses

| Situation | Possible explanation | Safe first response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You do not see a new message | Different account, delayed arrival, filtering, or search issue | Confirm account, refresh, search by known subject or sender, and check appropriate folders |
| You cannot sign in | Mistyped credentials, connection issue, account restriction, or verification requirement | Use the provider's legitimate recovery guidance; do not share recovery codes |
| A message remains in Drafts | It was not sent | Reopen, verify, and send only if appropriate |
| A message is stuck in Outbox | App is offline or transmission has not completed | Check connectivity and account status; avoid creating duplicates |
| You receive a delivery-failure notice | Invalid address, rejection, or technical issue | Read notice, verify recipient independently, retry only when justified |
| Someone says they did not get your email | Delays, filters, wrong address, or communication mismatch | Check Sent and the verified address; ask them to check appropriate mail folders |
| A message demands urgent payment or a login code | Possible fraud or account takeover attempt | Stop; verify using a known contact method; report suspicious mail |

> **Security principle:** Reading a message is not an obligation to comply with it. A request for secrecy, money, passwords, identity documents, or immediate action deserves independent verification.

### Phones and accessibility: same principle, different controls

On a phone, the inbox may be a scrolling list; Compose may appear as a small floating button; Send may be a paper-airplane symbol. On a desktop, the account may have a left navigation rail and wider message panel. Even when controls are displayed differently, you can use the same mental model: **choose the account, identify the message, check the recipient, read the content, and act deliberately**.

If using a screen reader or keyboard, identify actions by their accessible names (for example, “Compose,” “Send,” or “Inbox”). If the control is unlabeled or uncertain, use the provider's support documentation rather than guessing. If you use dictation, reread dictated text before sending because speech recognition can mishear names, numbers, or addresses.

### Where the next lessons begin

This first lesson is about basic navigation and one simple message. The next topics build specialized competence: writing effective messages, attaching and downloading files safely, selecting Reply/Reply All/Forward/Cc/Bcc, organizing inbox contents, detecting scams, and using calendars, reminders, and invitations. Those topics deserve separate practice rather than being rushed into a single first exercise.

## 7. Guided practice: build an email routine

These five activities are arranged from observation to independent performance. All can be completed with paper, a note-taking app, or a real email account. **No activity requires sharing real account details, sending anything to a stranger, following an unfamiliar link, opening an attachment, or changing your account settings.** Activities marked *optional live step* are only for learners who have suitable accounts and consent to send a test message.

### Activity 1: Identify an email address (about 5–8 minutes)

**Goal:** Recognize the parts of an address and avoid common address mistakes.

1. Study the fictional address `alex.student@example.com`.
1. Write down the part before `@`, the `@` symbol, and the part after it.
1. Examine the following examples. Decide which looks like a properly formed **basic email address**, which is a website address, and which is missing an essential part. The examples are for analysis, not for sending.
1. Explain why two people whose display names are “Alex Student” might still have different email addresses.

| Example | Your classification | Why? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `alex.student@example.com` | ________ | ________ |
| `https://www.example.com` | ________ | ________ |
| `alex.studentexample.com` | ________ | ________ |
| `sam.helper@example.org` | ________ | ________ |
| `@example.com` | ________ | ________ |

:::answer Show Activity 1 answers
1. `alex.student@example.com` — has the familiar local part, `@`, and domain structure of an email address.
1. `https://www.example.com` — a website URL, not an email address.
1. `alex.studentexample.com` — missing the separating `@`; not a basic email address.
1. `sam.helper@example.org` — has the familiar email-address structure, but is only an example and not verified as a reachable mailbox.
1. `@example.com` — missing a local part before the `@` symbol.

A display name is simply a label. Always inspect the actual address before sending an email. These simple examples do not describe every rule in formal email-address standards.
:::

### Activity 2: Read an example inbox (about 8–10 minutes)

**Goal:** Find important information without clicking on untrusted content.

Imagine that an email interface contains these three fictional messages:

| Sender shown | Subject | Preview | Time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Community Library | Room reservation question | We received your question... | 9:14 AM |
| Sam Helper | Weekend walk | Are you available Saturday? | Yesterday |
| Account Service | URGENT: verify your password now | Send us your code to keep access... | 8:02 AM |

1. Name the information presented in each of the four table columns.
1. Identify which message appears related to a room question and which appears to be a personal invitation.

## 8. Common misconceptions

### “An email address and a website address are the same thing.”

They are different. A typical email address contains a local part, an `@` symbol, and a domain. A website URL is used to locate a web page or service. A provider may use the same domain for a website and for email, but the complete addresses have different purposes.

### “If the sender name looks familiar, the email must be genuine.”

No. The sender display name can be misleading or imitated, and visible sender details may be forged. Verify an unusual or important request independently, even when it appears to come from a familiar organization.

### “Opening an email automatically tells the sender that I agree with it.”

No. Merely reading an email does not mean you accept its claims. However, some messages use tracking features that may report an apparent open depending on your application and privacy settings. Avoid treating message-open status as meaningful consent.

### “Every email arrives instantly.”

Not always. Delivery may be delayed by network conditions, filtering, account limitations, or server issues. For urgent situations, use another appropriate contact method rather than relying on one email.

### “If a message is in Sent, the person definitely read it.”

No. Sent means the sending service considers it sent or submitted. Delivery and reading are separate events, and many services do not provide dependable proof that someone read a message.

### “A draft is an email that has already been delivered.”

No. A draft is an unsent or incomplete message. Finding it in Drafts does not indicate that anyone else received it.

### “Deleting an email from my Sent folder takes it away from the recipient.”

Generally not. Deleting your own copy does not remove the recipient's copy. Some systems support a brief delay or limited recall functionality, but you should never assume universal recall is possible.

### “A message in my inbox is safer than a message in Spam.”

Not necessarily. Filters make mistakes, and dangerous messages can appear in Inbox. Examine requests carefully regardless of their folder. Likewise, a legitimate message can sometimes be filtered as spam.

### “I must click every link in a message to find out what it says.”

No. Read the visible text first. If the message is unexpected or asks for sensitive action, verify through a known website, app, or contact method instead of following its link.

### “Reply and Reply All are interchangeable.”

No. Reply usually addresses the sender, while Reply All can include multiple people. Selecting the wrong command can distribute information more widely than you intended. A later lesson explains the details.

### “Cc and Bcc make the email encrypted and private.”

No. They are addressing features, not guarantees of secrecy. Cc recipients are ordinarily visible; Bcc conceals some recipient addresses from other recipients but does not make the content confidential.

### “An email address must include someone's full legal name.”

No. Email addresses can use nicknames, department names, assigned usernames, or other mailbox identifiers. The displayed name is not the same as the address and is not guaranteed proof of identity.

### “If I am using a phone, the email rules are different.”

The main concepts are the same. The screen layout changes, and the buttons may be icons rather than words, but addresses, messages, drafts, sending, and safety principles still apply.

### “I need to open a real account to learn email.”

No. You can learn the parts of a message, practice composing text, identify risks, and complete this lesson's knowledge check through simulation. Sending a real test message is optional.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Terms marked with dotted-underlined links earlier in the lesson can be selected to show a short definition. The definitions below are also available for ordinary reading, screen readers, and offline study.

- **Email:** Electronic mail; a system for exchanging messages through connected computer services.
- **Email address:** The address used to direct a message to a mailbox, commonly written as a local part, an `@` symbol, and a domain.
- **Local part:** The section of an email address before the `@` symbol.
- **At sign (@):** The symbol that separates the local part from the domain in a typical email address.
- **Domain name:** A name used to identify an internet domain, appearing after `@` in an email address.
- **Email service/provider:** A system or organization that hosts or helps manage email accounts and messages.
- **Email account:** Your individual access to an email service, which commonly includes an address and sign-in credentials.
- **Webmail:** Email used through a website in a web browser.
- **Email application:** Software that displays, sends, and organizes email through a connected account.
- **Web browser:** Software used to open websites and web applications.
- **Internet:** The global network through which many digital services exchange information.
- **Website:** A collection of online pages or a web application reached through web addresses.
- **Username:** An account identifier used for signing in, sometimes the email address.
- **Password:** A secret used to help verify access to an account.
- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA):** An added sign-in protection that requires more than one form of verification.
- **Recovery method:** A provider-approved way to regain access to an account when normal sign-in fails.
- **Synchronization:** The process of keeping account information or message states updated across services or devices.
- **Inbox:** A normal location for received messages that the service places there.
- **Compose/New message:** A command that begins a new email.
- **From field:** The displayed sender information associated with a message.
- **To field:** The field indicating the intended recipients of a message.
- **Recipient:** A person or mailbox addressed by a message.
- **Sender:** The person, mailbox, or service represented as sending a message.
- **Display name:** A readable name shown beside an email address; not proof of identity.
- **Subject line:** A short heading describing the email's topic.
- **Message body:** The main content of a message.
- **Greeting:** An opening such as “Hello” used to address the reader.
- **Closing/sign-off:** A concluding expression and name, such as “Thank you, Alex.”
- **Date/time:** Information displaying when an email was sent or received.
- **Attachment:** A file included with a message; files should be opened only when trusted and appropriate.
- **Link/hyperlink:** Clickable text or an element that may open another resource.
- **Draft:** A prepared message that has not been sent.
- **Drafts:** The location where unfinished or unsent messages are commonly stored.
- **Send:** The action requesting that the email service process an outgoing message.
- **Sent/Sent Items:** A location holding copies of messages the service treats as sent.
- **Outbox:** A location where messages may wait during transmission or until connectivity is restored.
- **Delivery:** The process of transferring a message toward or into a receiving email system.
- **Bounce/delivery failure notice:** A response reporting a problem delivering a message.
- **Reply:** A response to a received message, ordinarily addressed to its sender.
- **Reply All:** A response that can include multiple original participants.
- **Forward:** A command that prepares an existing message to be sent to another recipient.
- **Cc:** Carbon copy; an additional addressing field with recipients ordinarily visible to others.
- **Bcc:** Blind carbon copy; an addressing field that normally conceals its recipients from other recipient lists.
- **Thread/conversation:** A display grouping related emails, often a message and its replies.
- **Read status:** A marker indicating whether the system considers a message read or unread.
- **Unread:** Not marked as read by the service or application.
- **Archive:** A command that removes a message from ordinary inbox view without necessarily deleting it.
- **Spam/Junk:** Unwanted or suspicious mail, or a location where a provider filters such messages.
- **Phishing:** An attempt to trick someone into disclosing information or taking a harmful action by impersonating a trustworthy person or organization.
- **Trash/Deleted Items:** A location for messages marked for deletion, often retained temporarily.
- **Search:** A feature that finds emails matching names, text, subjects, or other terms.
- **Notification:** An alert that reports new activity, possibly including incoming email.
- **Auto-complete:** A feature that suggests recipient addresses or contacts as you type.
