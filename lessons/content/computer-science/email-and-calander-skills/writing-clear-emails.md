## Lesson 2: Writing Clear Emails

An effective email helps the reader understand **why you are writing**, **what information matters**, and **what should happen next**. You do not need advanced vocabulary, perfect grammar, or a formal job title to write a useful message. You do need a clear purpose, an accurate subject line, enough context, a respectful tone, and a careful final review. This lesson teaches a repeatable writing method for everyday messages to teachers, employers, service providers, classmates, coworkers, and other people.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 75–105 minutes (including practice) · **Prerequisites:** Lesson 1, *Email Basics*, recommended · **Practice equipment:** A device with a text editor or email application (optional); every writing activity can be completed on paper or in an unsent draft

> **Essential idea:** A clear email usually does five things: **identifies the topic, states the purpose, supplies the needed facts, makes the next step clear, and ends courteously**. Review the message and recipient before sending. Shorter is useful only when it remains understandable.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Identify the audience, purpose, and desired outcome of an email before writing.
1. Create a specific, accurate [[subject-line|subject line]] that helps the recipient recognize the topic.
1. Organize a message using a suitable greeting, opening, essential details, request or next step, and closing.
1. Replace vague, overly long, or emotionally charged writing with clear, courteous [[plain-language|plain language]].
1. Ask a question, request an action, share an update, or provide a deadline without causing unnecessary confusion.
1. Choose a tone appropriate to a friend, teacher, coworker, manager, or unfamiliar professional contact.
1. Proofread names, dates, addresses, requests, spelling, and privacy-sensitive information before sending.
1. Revise sample messages and complete a safe, independent writing exercise without needing to send real email.

## 1. How do you plan an email before writing?

Many unclear emails begin with typing before the writer has decided what they need. A better approach is to take a few moments to identify three things: **who will read the message**, **why you are sending it**, and **what result would make the message successful**. This is called identifying the [[audience|audience]], [[purpose|purpose]], and [[desired-outcome|desired outcome]].

For example, imagine you need to ask a training center whether its computer class has space. Your audience is the center's registration staff. Your purpose is to ask about availability. Your desired outcome is an answer about whether you can enroll and what you should do next. You do not need to include the history of every class you have considered; you need the class name, relevant session, and one clear question.

### The three-question planning test

Before typing, answer these questions in ordinary words:

1. **Who is this for?** Do I know the person, their role, or the department that handles the topic?
1. **What am I trying to communicate?** Am I asking, informing, confirming, thanking, correcting, or following up?
1. **What should happen next?** Do I need an answer, a decision, an action, or no action at all?

If you cannot answer the third question, your message may still have a legitimate purpose: perhaps you are sending a brief update that does not require a reply. State that plainly, such as, “For your information; no response is needed.” Do not create a request when none exists.

### Match the message to a communication purpose

| Purpose | Useful opening | Clear desired result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ask a question | “I am writing to ask whether the Tuesday class has openings.” | A specific answer |
| Request an action | “Could you confirm my registration by Friday?” | A confirmation or other named action |
| Share information | “The meeting location has changed to Room 204.” | The recipient knows the update |
| Confirm an agreement | “I am confirming our appointment for May 12 at 10:00 a.m.” | Shared understanding; reply if incorrect |
| Thank someone | “Thank you for helping me locate the form.” | Appreciation; usually no action required |
| Correct an error | “I need to correct the date in my earlier message.” | Recipient uses the accurate information |
| Follow up | “I am following up on my registration question from Monday.” | A status update or response |

One email can contain several related points, but avoid combining unrelated requests merely because they go to the same person. For example, an email about a room reservation should not abruptly switch to a question about a payroll problem. Separate messages make the topics easier to understand, route, search, and answer.

### Identify what the reader already knows

Good writing does not assume too much, but it also does not repeat unnecessary background. Think about what the reader needs to understand your message. If the reader already spoke with you yesterday, a short reference may be enough: “Thank you for speaking with me yesterday about the orientation schedule.” If the person has never met you, introduce your role and relevant connection: “I am enrolled in the Wednesday beginner computer class.”

Ask yourself whether the reader would know **which class, appointment, order, application, event, document, or project** you mean. A sentence such as “I was wondering what happened with it” leaves “it” unclear. A better sentence is “Could you tell me whether my workshop registration has been confirmed?”

> **Remember:** The most helpful email is not the longest or the shortest. It gives the intended reader enough accurate information to understand and act without guessing.

### Keep one primary subject whenever possible

A [[topic|topic]] is the matter the email discusses. Its [[main-point|main point]] is the most important information about that topic. For beginners, one primary topic is a strong default. A two-part message may still be appropriate when the parts are closely connected, such as confirming a meeting time and asking which entrance to use.

If you have several related questions, tell the reader how many and list them clearly. Do not conceal the most important question in the last line of a long paragraph. When the email has no request, make the update or conclusion apparent near the start.

### When email may not be the right channel

Email works well for nonurgent written messages and information the reader may need to review. It is less reliable for an emergency, an urgent situation requiring an immediate response, or sensitive information that must be handled through an approved secure process. A school, employer, clinic, or other organization may provide an appropriate portal or phone number for those situations. Choosing the correct channel is part of communicating clearly.

## 2. How do you write a helpful subject line?

The [[subject-line|subject line]] is the short heading shown with an email in the inbox. It helps the reader know the topic before opening the message and helps both people find it later. An effective subject is **specific enough to identify the matter** but **brief enough to scan**. The subject should accurately describe the content; it is not a place for a long explanation or emotional pressure.

Compare these examples:

| Weak subject | Better subject | Why the revision helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Hi` | `Question about Tuesday computer class` | Names the actual topic |
| `Important!!!` | `Schedule confirmation requested for May 12` | Describes the requested action without unsupported urgency |
| `Help` | `Help with workshop registration form` | Identifies the specific problem |
| `Question` | `Question about library hours on Saturday` | Gives useful context |
| `Meeting` | `Thursday staff meeting: room change` | Tells the reader what changed |
| `PLEASE READ NOW` | `Response requested by Friday: room reservation` | States a real request and deadline without shouting |
| `About that thing` | `Follow-up: volunteer orientation location` | Uses searchable words and identifies the subject |
| *(blank)* | `Request for course completion confirmation` | Makes the message identifiable in the inbox |

### A simple subject-line formula

One useful pattern is **topic + important detail or action**. You do not have to use every part each time.

- `Library orientation — question about arrival time`
- `Community workshop — attendance confirmation`
- `Application 2026-17 — request for status` (use a reference number only when appropriate and not sensitive)
- `Thursday meeting — revised start time`
- `Class assignment — clarification on instructions`

The subject line should not expose private information simply to be specific. Avoid putting passwords, full financial account numbers, medical details, or other sensitive content in the subject. Subjects can appear in notifications, previews, and other places where people may see them.

### Helpful dates, but only when they are correct

A date may help when a subject concerns an appointment, class, or deadline. Use a month name when a numeric date could be read two ways. For example, `May 6` is easier for a mixed audience to interpret than `5/6`. State the year when it is needed to distinguish events or avoid uncertainty. Check the real calendar: the example is not a reason to assume any particular weekday or year.

If a time zone matters because recipients are in different places, identify it accurately in the message. For instance, “2:00 p.m. Pacific Time” communicates more than “2:00” to a remote participant.

### Avoid misleading urgency

Terms such as `Urgent`, `Final notice`, or `Action required` should reflect a genuine time-sensitive matter, not a method of attracting attention. If action is required by a specific date, say what action and when. The recipient may have competing priorities, and your desired response time may not be the recipient's obligation.

### Review subjects when the topic changes

If you are starting a different matter, a **new message with a new subject** is often easier to follow than adding an unrelated question to an old email conversation. Later, the *Replying, Forwarding, and CC* lesson explains how conversations and reply options work. In this lesson, remember the principle: **the visible topic should match the actual message**.

> **Quick check:** Could a person understand the general purpose of the email by reading only the subject line? If not, make the subject more specific without revealing private details.

## 3. What structure makes an email easy to read?

Most ordinary emails can follow an uncomplicated five-part structure. You may use all five parts in a formal message or shorten them for a familiar contact. The structure is a tool for understanding, not a rigid rule that every email must look identical.

1. **Greeting:** Address the recipient appropriately.
1. **Opening and purpose:** Explain why you are writing, preferably in the first one or two sentences.
1. **Necessary details:** Give the facts the recipient needs to understand or act.
1. **Request or next step:** Say what response, action, or decision you want, if any.
1. **Closing and name:** End politely and help the reader identify you.

### Example: A complete everyday email

The following is a **fictional practice message**. The addresses use reserved example domains; do not send mail to them.

**To:** `registration@example.com`  
**Subject:** `Question about beginner computer class registration`

> Hello Registration Team,
>
> I am interested in the beginner computer class scheduled for May 12. I checked the course listing, but I could not tell whether registration is still open.
>
> Could you please confirm whether places are available and explain how to register?
>
> Thank you for your help.
>
> Jordan Lee

This message gives the recipient useful information without unnecessary background. The reader knows the topic, the relevant class, what has already been checked, and the two related answers being requested. The closing is courteous but not elaborate.

### The greeting

A [[greeting|greeting]] helps establish the relationship between writer and reader. Examples include:

- `Hello Ms. Rivera,` when the name and title are known and appropriate.
- `Hello Dr. Chen,` when that title is accurate and appropriate.
- `Hello Registration Team,` when contacting a department rather than a specific person.
- `Hello,` when a suitable name is not available.
- `Hi Taylor,` for a familiar colleague where a friendly tone is appropriate.

Use the person's preferred name and title when known. Do not guess a title, gender, marital status, or name pronunciation. A neutral greeting is better than an incorrect or overly familiar one. Overly formal openings can sound awkward in routine messages, but local workplace or school expectations may differ.

### The opening and reason for writing

Try to make the purpose apparent quickly. These openings are useful because they tell the reader what follows:

- “I am writing to ask about the workshop schedule.”
- “I am contacting you to confirm my appointment.”
- “Thank you for sending the updated instructions.”
- “I would like to request a copy of the event agenda.”
- “I am writing to correct a detail in my previous message.”

Compare the vague opening “I hope everything is fine. I have some things to ask you about, and I wanted to reach out” with “I am writing to ask whether the Thursday orientation has changed locations.” The second sentence is more useful. A short friendly introduction can still be appropriate, particularly in established relationships.

### The body and essential context

The [[message-body|message body]] contains the core information. Use one short paragraph for the purpose and another paragraph or short list for a separate point. A paragraph break tells the reader where one thought ends and another begins. Large unbroken blocks are especially difficult to scan on a phone.

For example:

> I registered for the evening workshop on May 12. My confirmation shows Room 110, but the latest notice says Room 204.
>
> Which room should I use when I arrive?

This contains the conflict and a precise question. It does not require a lengthy description of how the writer discovered the different room numbers.

### The request or next step

A [[call-to-action|call to action]] is the step you ask the recipient to take. In ordinary email, it can be as simple as “Could you confirm the room number?” or “Please let me know whether you can attend.” If no action is required, state the purpose as an update and consider saying no reply is needed.

When a deadline is necessary, include a specific, accurate date and explain why the timing matters when appropriate: “If possible, could you confirm by May 8 so that I can finalize transportation?” A deadline should not be invented to pressure someone.

### The closing and identification

A [[closing|closing]] ends the message courteously. Common options include:

- `Thank you,`
- `Best regards,`
- `Sincerely,` for more formal contexts
- `Thanks,` for familiar or routine exchanges

Below the closing, type your name or the identifier the recipient needs to recognize you. An [[email-signature|email signature]] may also include a suitable role or organization, but beginners do not need an elaborate signature. Limit personal contact information to what is needed and approved.

### A shorter message for a familiar contact

**Subject:** `Friday meeting — arrival time`

> Hi Taylor,
>
> I can arrive at 9:30 a.m. Friday. Does that still work for you?
>
> Thanks,  
> Jordan

A short email is not incomplete merely because it omits a long introduction. The recipient, topic, and request are clear. If Jordan and Taylor have not previously discussed a meeting, they should add the relevant event or location.

### Use simple formatting

For basic emails, use readable text and ordinary paragraph breaks. A short list can help when there are two or three related questions. Avoid decorative fonts, large colored text, complicated tables, and excessive bold formatting. These can distract readers and sometimes display poorly on small screens or with accessibility tools. A clear message should remain understandable even when displayed as plain text.

## 4. How do you make your meaning and next step clear?

A message can sound polite and still leave the recipient uncertain about what you want. [[clarity|Clarity]] means that the reader can understand your meaning with minimal guesswork. [[conciseness|Conciseness]] means removing unnecessary words while preserving relevant information. Both matter, but clarity comes first.

### Replace vague references with specific nouns

Words such as “this,” “that,” “it,” “something,” and “the thing” are sometimes useful, but they can confuse a reader who does not know what you mean.

| Less clear | More clear | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Did you get it?` | `Did you receive the workshop schedule I sent Monday?` | Names the item and date |
| `Can you fix this?` | `Could you correct the spelling of my name on the registration form?` | Names the problem and action |
| `I need the information.` | `Could you send the course start time and room number?` | Names the specific information |
| `I can't do that day.` | `I am unavailable May 12. Would May 14 work instead?` | Gives a concrete alternative |
| `Everything is wrong.` | `The receipt lists two tickets, but I purchased one.` | States the observable discrepancy |
| `Let me know soon.` | `If possible, please reply by May 8.` | Gives a meaningful response date |

The “more clear” examples are models, not facts about your own circumstances. Check dates, names, and other details before copying any sentence into a real message.

### Put the main point first

This is sometimes called the [[main-point-first|main-point-first approach]]. It does not mean being abrupt. It means giving the reader a helpful orientation before supplying background.

**Less effective:**

> Yesterday I was looking at my calendar and remembering the schedule we were discussing, and then I thought about the change from last week and realized that I should probably ask a question.
>
> Is the meeting still on Thursday?

**Clearer:**

> Could you confirm whether the meeting is still scheduled for Thursday? I noticed that last week's notice listed a different date.

The revised version introduces the question first, then gives its reason. This approach is especially useful for requests and corrections, because it helps the recipient decide what response is needed.

### Give enough context to avoid a second email

A precise message often answers these useful questions:

- **What?** Which class, event, order, request, or document?
- **Who?** Which person or department is involved, if important?
- **When?** What date or time is relevant?
- **Where?** Is a location or virtual meeting information relevant?
- **What now?** What answer or step is needed?

You rarely need all five. Include the details necessary for that particular task, not a checklist of irrelevant facts. For example, if asking about a course start time, the course name and session may matter; your home address ordinarily does not.

### Ask one question clearly; group related questions visibly

A reader should not have to search through a paragraph for a hidden question. For one request, put it in a sentence by itself when helpful:

> Could you please confirm whether registration is open?

For two or three related requests, a short numbered list can be easier to answer:

> Could you help me confirm two details about the orientation?
>
> 1. What time should participants arrive?
> 2. Which entrance should we use?
>
> Thank you.

The numbers are part of the message example, not technical instructions. Do not turn a routine note into a long questionnaire when a single sentence would be easier.

### Express a deadline responsibly

A [[deadline|deadline]] is a date or time by which an action is expected or requested. Say whether it is a requirement, your preferred timeline, or a flexible request. For instance:

- `I need to submit the registration by May 8. Could you reply by then if possible?`
- `The application closes May 8, according to the instructions I received. Could you confirm this date?`
- `There is no urgency; a response next week is fine.`

These statements have different implications. Avoid presenting a preference as a rule imposed by someone else. If the recipient is in another time zone, specify which time zone applies. Use a complete calendar date when relative terms such as “tomorrow” might be misread later.

### Avoid unnecessary jargon and unexplained abbreviations

[[jargon|Jargon]] is specialized language a group uses. Sometimes it is useful within that group; other times it makes an everyday email hard to understand. Compare “Please advise re: ETA on the deliverable” with “When do you expect the revised schedule to be ready?” The latter is clearer to many readers. Explain an unfamiliar abbreviation the first time you use it, or replace it with ordinary words.

Avoid forcing elaborate vocabulary into a message to sound professional. A simple sentence like “Could you confirm the appointment time?” is fully suitable for workplace communication.

### Avoid ambiguity in numbers and dates

Check quantities, currency, dates, time zones, and negative words such as “not.” These small details can change the entire meaning of a message.

- `2 p.m.` may be ambiguous without a time zone for remote meetings.
- `05/06` can mean different dates under different regional conventions.
- `I can attend` and `I cannot attend` communicate opposite conclusions.
- `15` and `50` are easy to mistype; verify amounts against a reliable record.

When the facts are uncertain, say so: “I believe the room is 204, but I am checking the updated notice.” Do not present a guess as a confirmed fact.

### A usable five-line writing template

You can begin with this model for many routine messages:

> **Subject:** [Specific topic]
>
> Hello [appropriate name or team],
>
> I am writing to [purpose].
>
> [Relevant detail: what, when, or why.]
>
> Could you please [specific question or action]? [Optional realistic deadline.]
>
> Thank you,  
> [Your name]

The bracketed items are **placeholders** to replace in your own draft. Do not send them literally. Adjust the template when a request is unnecessary, when a message needs more detail, or when a shorter format fits the situation.

> **Remember:** A recipient should be able to answer: “What does this message concern?” and “What, if anything, do I need to do?” after a quick read.

## 5. How do you choose the right tone and format?

[[tone|Tone]] is the attitude or feeling conveyed by your words. The same request can sound helpful, neutral, impatient, or hostile depending on how it is written. Email does not transmit facial expressions or voice inflection reliably, so straightforward, respectful language prevents many misunderstandings.

The right tone depends on the reader and your relationship. Writing to a close friend may be casual. Writing to a hiring manager, instructor, government office, or service provider usually calls for a more formal or neutral tone. In all settings, **respect and accuracy matter more than elaborate phrases**.

### Match the language to the reader

| Audience or situation | Suitable tone | Example opening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Familiar friend | Friendly and direct | `Hi Morgan, can we move our visit to Saturday?` |
| Classmate | Friendly and clear | `Hi Avery, do you know which chapter is assigned?` |
| Instructor | Courteous and specific | `Hello Professor Chen, I have a question about the assignment instructions.` |
| Coworker | Professional and practical | `Hi Jordan, I am confirming the revised meeting time.` |
| Unknown department | Neutral and respectful | `Hello Registration Team, I am writing to ask about enrollment.` |
| Customer service | Factual and solution-oriented | `Hello Support Team, my receipt shows an incorrect quantity.` |

Do not assume a person prefers a particular title. If you are unsure, use an accurate name without an uncertain title or address the relevant team.

### Polite requests are direct, not weak

A respectful request can be straightforward:

- `Could you please confirm the appointment time?`
- `Would you be able to explain the next step?`
- `Please let me know whether the revised schedule is correct.`
- `Thank you for reviewing this request.`

Avoid several sentences of apology before every ordinary request. Saying “Sorry to bother you” is not always wrong, but routine requests do not require a long explanation of why you deserve to ask.

### Compare three tones

**Accusatory or overly heated:**

> You never give people the right information. Fix this immediately!!!

**Vague or overly indirect:**

> I was perhaps wondering whether there might possibly be an issue with something I received.

**Firm, factual, and respectful:**

> My registration confirmation lists May 12, but the course page lists May 14. Could you confirm which date is correct?

The third version identifies the discrepancy and requests a concrete response. It does not excuse an error or prevent you from escalating an issue through appropriate channels if the matter remains unresolved. A respectful tone can still be firm.

### Emotions and difficult messages

When upset, avoid sending a message immediately. Write a draft, identify the facts, and revisit the wording after you have had an opportunity to review it. Distinguish **what happened**, **what effect it had**, and **what resolution you are requesting**.

Instead of “Your office is incompetent,” write “I submitted the form on Monday, but the confirmation still shows the wrong date. Could you correct the record or tell me whom to contact?” Avoid threats, insults, or accusations unsupported by facts. If the matter involves urgent danger or a protected reporting process, use an appropriate immediate or formal channel instead of relying only on a routine email.

### Use capitalization and punctuation thoughtfully

Excessive CAPITAL LETTERS, repeated exclamation marks, or strings of question marks can make an email seem aggressive or confusing. Ordinary punctuation improves readability. Standard sentence capitalization and periods are adequate for most situations. An occasional exclamation mark in friendly correspondence is not inherently wrong; the issue is whether it fits the audience and purpose.

Emojis and informal abbreviations are not universally understood. They may be suitable among friends or in some teams but less suitable in unfamiliar or formal contexts. They can also be misinterpreted or pronounced differently by assistive technology. When clarity is important, express the meaning in words.

### Write for people using different devices and abilities

[[accessibility|Accessibility]] means making communication usable by people with different abilities and tools. A recipient may read on a small phone, use enlarged text, rely on a screen reader, or translate the message into another language. Help them by:

1. Writing a meaningful subject line and putting the purpose near the beginning.
1. Using short paragraphs, descriptive words, and ordinary punctuation.
1. Keeping essential information in text, not exclusively in an image or decorative graphic.
1. Writing meaningful [[link-text|link text]] when including a link, such as “Course schedule,” rather than an unexplained “click here.”
1. Avoiding long sequences of all-capital words, unusual symbols, or visual formatting as the only way to communicate meaning.
1. Using short, simple lists when they genuinely improve comprehension.

Links and unexpected files can carry risks. Before inserting or opening one, confirm it is needed and comes from a trustworthy source. You will study attachments and suspicious email in later lessons.

### Respect people's identities and circumstances

Use names, pronouns, and titles appropriately when known; do not invent them. Avoid assumptions about someone's schedule, technology, fluency, or ability to respond immediately. If you need a reply, state the requested timing and allow for the possibility that the recipient has other responsibilities. Respectful communication also means not sharing someone else's private information without authorization.

### Be honest about certainty and responsibility

Write “I have submitted the form” only if you actually submitted it. Write “I plan to submit the form” if it has not yet happened. Write “I cannot attend” rather than promising attendance you have not confirmed. If you made a mistake, acknowledge it clearly and provide the corrected information. Accurate statements build trust more effectively than polished language that obscures facts.

## 6. How do you proofread and review before sending?

[[proofreading|Proofreading]] means reviewing a draft for mistakes before someone else receives it. Editing the meaning and organization is also called [[revision|revision]]. Both are important: an email can be correctly spelled but unclear, or clearly written but contain the wrong appointment date.

### The three-pass review

A dependable approach is to review the message in three short passes:

1. **Meaning:** Is the purpose clear? Does the message include the facts needed? Is the request or next step easy to identify?
1. **Accuracy and tone:** Are names, dates, times, amounts, and statements correct? Is the language suitable for the audience?
1. **Delivery and privacy:** Is the **To** address correct? Are the subject and body complete? Am I disclosing anything unnecessary or sensitive?

The third pass matters even when the words are perfect. A good message sent to the wrong recipient is still a serious mistake.

### A practical pre-send checklist

| Item to inspect | Question to ask | Example problem prevented |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recipient | Does the actual email address belong to the intended person or team? | Sending to an unrelated contact with a similar name |
| Subject | Does it name the real topic without revealing private details? | Vague or misleading inbox entry |
| Purpose | Is the reason for writing clear near the beginning? | Reader cannot identify the main point |
| Request | Is the expected answer or action specific? | “Let me know” without saying what to confirm |
| Facts | Are dates, times, amounts, names, and other details verified? | Incorrect appointment date |
| Tone | Would the message seem respectful if read aloud? | Accidental accusation or pressure |
| Privacy | Does the message avoid unnecessary sensitive information? | Exposing account, identity, or medical details |
| Formatting | Are paragraphs readable, including on a phone? | Dense block of text |
| Links or files | Are any mentioned resources necessary, approved, and correctly selected? | Missing or inappropriate supporting material |
| Final action | Have I decided whether to save a draft or actually send? | Sending practice text accidentally |

### Fix meaning before fixing small errors

Suppose a draft says:

> I am writing about the thing from last week. The date I got was not correct and something needs to change.

Changing its punctuation will not make it sufficiently clear. The first revision should supply the missing meaning:

> My workshop registration lists May 12, but the updated schedule lists May 14. Could you confirm the correct date?

Now check spelling, names, dates, and tone. There is little value in polishing an ambiguous sentence while leaving its meaning unresolved.

### Read once from the recipient's perspective

Imagine you know only what the recipient knows. Ask: “Would I understand which course or meeting this refers to? Is there enough information to answer? Am I being asked to do something? Does the subject match the body?” If the reader would have to ask a question merely to understand your question, add the missing context.

Reading the message aloud can help you find missing words, long sentences, and harsh-sounding phrases. You can also use a device's text-to-speech or screen-reading features if available. These are optional aids, not prerequisites.

### Spellcheck is useful, but it is not a fact-checker

[[spellcheck|Spellcheck]] may catch misspellings but not every problem. A sentence can contain correctly spelled words and still say the wrong thing. For example, “The class is on May 15” may be grammatically correct even when the class is on May 16. An automated grammar or writing assistant may also change your intended meaning. Always review suggested corrections, especially names, dates, negative words, and promises.

Do not paste private email content, account details, or confidential workplace information into an unapproved third-party writing tool. Follow the policies of your organization, school, or service provider where applicable.

### Recipient safety, autocomplete, and sensitive information

An email application's [[autocomplete|autocomplete]] feature may suggest a name as you begin typing. Suggestions can be helpful, but several people may have similar names. Inspect the actual address, not just the display name, before sending. Consider whether the recipient is entitled to see the information. If unsure, save a draft and verify the correct destination using an independently known contact method.

Routine email may be inappropriate for passwords, one-time sign-in codes, full payment-card data, detailed medical information, government identifiers, or confidential organizational records. Use the approved secure system when one is required. No exercise in this lesson calls for transmitting personal or confidential information.

### Saving a draft versus sending

A [[draft|draft]] is an unfinished or unsent message. Writing a draft is an excellent way to practice safely. In many services, drafts are saved automatically, but confirm that your particular application has saved your work before closing it. The **Send** command is the action that submits a message for delivery. Some services offer a short **Undo Send** cancellation window; it is not a dependable way to recall an email after delivery. Your best safeguard is reviewing before you send.

### When you discover a mistake afterward

If you sent a message with the wrong non-sensitive detail, consider sending a short correction rather than hoping the recipient notices. Use a specific subject if a new message is needed, identify the incorrect statement, and supply the accurate information. If you accidentally disclosed private information or sent to the wrong person, follow your organization's incident-reporting or privacy process promptly; an apology alone does not secure the disclosed information.

**Example correction:**

> **Subject:** Correction: workshop date in my previous email
>
> Hello Registration Team,
>
> I need to correct the date in my earlier message. I meant May 14, not May 12. I apologize for the confusion.
>
> Thank you,  
> Jordan Lee

> **Safety during practice:** Use invented scenarios and fictional names; never enter a real password, financial account number, medical record, government ID, or someone else's private information. Practice drafts do not need to be sent to demonstrate learning.

## 7. Guided practice: write and revise clear emails

These exercises use **fictional situations** and are designed so you can succeed **without an email account**. You may write in a notebook, a local text editor, or an unsent draft. If you use a real email service, keep all exercises in Drafts unless you deliberately choose the optional self-email practice described below. Do not send messages to the example addresses or contact real organizations as part of these exercises.

### Activity 1: Improve a subject line (about 10–12 minutes)

**Goal:** Turn unclear inbox headings into accurate, searchable subjects.

Read each situation and write a subject line in the blank space. Include the most useful topic and, where necessary, an action or detail. Do not invent confidential information.

| Situation | Weak subject | Your improved subject |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ask whether the Saturday library class is still open | `Question` | __________ |
| Confirm whether Tuesday's appointment starts at 9 a.m. | `Hello` | __________ |
| Tell a coworker a meeting has moved to Room 204 | `Important!!!` | __________ |
| Request a replacement participation certificate | `HELP ME` | __________ |
| Correct a workshop date in an earlier message | `Oops` | __________ |
| Ask which building hosts volunteer orientation | *(blank)* | __________ |

**Review:** Does each subject indicate the topic accurately? Would it help the recipient locate the email later? Does it avoid an unsupported claim of urgency?

:::answer Show sample answers for Activity 1

1. `Saturday library class — registration availability`
1. `Tuesday appointment — please confirm 9:00 a.m. start`
1. `Meeting update — moved to Room 204`
1. `Request for replacement participation certificate`
1. `Correction: workshop date in earlier message`
1. `Volunteer orientation — which building?`

Other clear, accurate subjects are also acceptable. A good answer does not have to match this wording exactly.
:::

### Activity 2: Build a complete email from notes (about 12–15 minutes)

**Goal:** Organize the necessary facts into a complete message with a clear request.

**Fictional scenario:** You are Jordan Lee. You want to attend an introductory computer workshop. You found an announcement for the May 14 session, but it does not state the beginning time. The training center uses the practice-only address `classes@example.com`. You want the center to confirm the time and tell you whether registration is still open.

Your rough notes are:

- Beginner computer workshop; session May 14.
- Unsure when class starts.
- Unsure whether registration is open.
- Need a reply; no artificial deadline.
- Be courteous and include your name.

Fill out the plan first:

| Part | Your response |
| --- | --- |
| Recipient or team | __________ |
| Main subject | __________ |
| First sentence: purpose | __________ |
| Relevant detail | __________ |
| Specific request | __________ |
| Suitable closing | __________ |

Then turn the notes into a complete email. Include a useful subject, greeting, two short paragraphs if helpful, and a closing. **Do not send** the email to the practice-only address.

:::answer Show one model email for Activity 2

**To:** `classes@example.com` (fictional; do not send)  
**Subject:** `May 14 beginner computer workshop — time and registration`

> Hello Classes Team,
>
> I am interested in the beginner computer workshop on May 14. The announcement did not list the start time.
>
> Could you please confirm when the workshop begins and whether registration is still open?
>
> Thank you for your help.
>
> Jordan Lee

**Why it works:** It identifies the session, states the missing information, asks two closely related questions, and closes respectfully. No real personal information is needed. The model does not claim that the workshop exists outside the fictional scenario.
:::

### Activity 3: Revise unclear or unhelpful messages (about 12–15 minutes)

**Goal:** Improve clarity, specificity, brevity, and tone while preserving the writer's legitimate purpose.

Read each weak example. Rewrite it as a short, accurate message that a recipient could act upon. Assume the relevant facts stated in the scenario are true **only within the fictional exercise**.

**A. Too vague**

> Hey, what about that thing? I need to know.

**Scenario facts:** You are asking the training center if the Tuesday evening computer class still has seats.

**B. Too emotional**

> THIS IS RIDICULOUS!!! You sent the WRONG ROOM. FIX IT NOW!!!

**Scenario facts:** Your confirmation says Room 110, but a later notice says Room 204. You need to know where to go.

**C. Too indirect**

> I was wondering if perhaps you might possibly be able to let me know at some time in the future if you would happen to have some information about when the assignment is due.

**Scenario facts:** You need the assignment deadline from your instructor.

**D. Missing action**

> Our meeting is on Thursday. Thanks.

**Scenario facts:** You need Taylor to confirm attendance at a 2:00 p.m. meeting. Both people are in the same local time zone.

**E. Inaccurate certainty**

> I definitely completed registration. See you in class.

**Scenario facts:** You filled out the online form but never received confirmation, and you do not know whether registration succeeded.

:::answer Show sample revisions for Activity 3

**A.** “Hello Registration Team, could you tell me whether seats are still available in the Tuesday evening computer class? Thank you.”

**B.** “Hello, my confirmation lists Room 110, but the latest notice lists Room 204. Could you confirm which room is correct? Thank you.”

**C.** “Hello Professor Chen, could you please confirm the due date for the assignment? Thank you.”

**D.** “Hi Taylor, our meeting is scheduled for Thursday at 2:00 p.m. Could you confirm that you can attend? Thanks.”

**E.** “Hello Registration Team, I submitted the registration form but have not received confirmation. Could you tell me whether my registration was received? Thank you.”

**What to notice:** Each revision keeps the relevant issue, removes unnecessary emotional language or hedging, and names an answer or action. Example E distinguishes submitting a form from having confirmed enrollment.
:::

### Activity 4: Choose tone and audience (about 10–12 minutes)

**Goal:** Keep the meaning consistent while adapting the language to different readers.

**Scenario:** You cannot attend a meeting that is currently planned for Thursday afternoon. You want to ask whether it can be moved to Friday. Write three versions of the **same basic request**:

1. A short friendly email to a friend.
1. A courteous message to an instructor or manager.
1. A neutral request to an unfamiliar scheduling department.

For each version, include a descriptive subject line, suitable greeting, clear request, and closing. Compare the language and ask whether each reader would know what you need. Do not invent a reason for your unavailability if you do not wish to share one.

| Audience | Greeting | Request style | Closing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Familiar friend | __________ | __________ | __________ |
| Instructor or manager | __________ | __________ | __________ |
| Scheduling department | __________ | __________ | __________ |

:::answer Show sample answers for Activity 4

**Friend**  
**Subject:** `Thursday plans — can we switch to Friday?`

> Hi Casey,
>
> I can't meet Thursday afternoon. Would Friday work instead?
>
> Thanks,  
> Jordan

**Instructor or manager**  
**Subject:** `Request to reschedule Thursday meeting`

> Hello Dr. Chen,
>
> I am unable to attend our Thursday afternoon meeting. Would it be possible to reschedule for Friday? I understand if another time would be more convenient.
>
> Thank you,  
> Jordan Lee

**Scheduling department**  
**Subject:** `Meeting reschedule request — Thursday to Friday`

> Hello Scheduling Team,
>
> I am writing to request a change to my Thursday afternoon appointment. Is a Friday appointment available instead?
>
> Thank you for your assistance,

**Comparison:** The purpose stays consistent across audiences; the greeting, level of detail, and phrasing change. None of the versions requires an invented personal excuse.
:::

### Activity 5: The full draft-and-review cycle (about 15–20 minutes)

**Goal:** Use the complete workflow independently: plan, draft, revise, proofread, and make a safe send-or-save decision.

Choose **one** of these fictional situations:

- **A: Class question.** Ask an instructor what materials are needed for next week's introductory class.
- **B: Work update.** Inform a coworker that a routine meeting will begin at 10:30 a.m. instead of 10:00 a.m., and request acknowledgement.
- **C: Service correction.** Explain that a fictional workshop ticket shows the wrong session date and ask how to correct it.
- **D: Appointment confirmation.** Ask a community center to confirm the day, time, and location of a scheduled appointment.

**Step 1 — Plan.** Write the audience, purpose, important context, and desired next step in one sentence each.

**Step 2 — Draft.** Write an appropriate subject, greeting, concise body, specific request, closing, and name. You may invent harmless names and dates to make the scenario complete. Do not insert account numbers or sensitive identifiers.

**Step 3 — Revise for meaning.** Remove irrelevant background. Make the main point easy to find. Replace vague pronouns with the actual class, meeting, or date.

**Step 4 — Proofread.** Check all invented details for consistency, and verify that the subject accurately matches the body. Read the draft as though you were the recipient.

**Step 5 — Protect privacy.** Verify that no real person is named without a reason, no real recipient address is entered by accident, and no password, sign-in code, or private record is present.

**Step 6 — Save or discard safely.** Keep the exercise in your notebook, local document, or unsent draft. If you used an email app, confirm the draft is saved before closing it.

**Self-assessment worksheet**

| Checkpoint | Yes / Not yet | What I would improve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The subject identifies the main topic | __________ | __________ |
| The first lines explain my purpose | __________ | __________ |
| The important details are present | __________ | __________ |
| The requested response or next step is explicit | __________ | __________ |
| The language fits the audience | __________ | __________ |
| Dates, times, and names are consistent | __________ | __________ |
| The message contains no unnecessary private information | __________ | __________ |
| I made a deliberate save-or-send decision | __________ | __________ |

:::answer Show an example completed draft for Activity 5

**Scenario B: Work update (fictional)**  
**Subject:** `Routine team meeting — new start time 10:30 a.m.`

> Hi Taylor,
>
> The routine team meeting has moved from 10:00 a.m. to 10:30 a.m. on Thursday. The meeting location has not changed.
>
> Could you please confirm that you received this update?
>

> Jordan Lee

**Review notes:** The subject explains the change; the first paragraph gives the original and revised times; the request asks for acknowledgement; and no unrelated information is included. In a real workplace, the writer should verify that the revised time is authorized and that recipients are the correct meeting participants before sending.
:::

### Optional Activity 6: A real, authorized self-email test (about 5–10 minutes)

If you already have access to your own email account and want to practice the actual interface, compose a **harmless message addressed only to your own verified email address**. Use `Practice: clear subject and message structure` as the subject. In the body, write two or three sentences explaining one skill you learned, then add a courteous closing.

Before selecting **Send**, inspect the To address, subject, body, and account you are using. Send only when you are certain that the recipient address belongs to you and that account rules allow this practice. If you prefer, **save a draft instead**; this is equally acceptable for completing the lesson. Do not use an organization's address or a fictional `example.com` address.

Afterward, evaluate your result: Did the subject match the message? Was the purpose evident? Were there unnecessary details? Can you tell the difference between a draft and a sent item? The earlier *Email Basics* lesson explains these folder distinctions.

### Reflection: three habits worth repeating

Finish by writing a short explanation in your own words for each question:

1. What information belongs in a helpful subject line?
1. How can I make a request easy for another person to answer?
1. What are the three most important things I will check before sending an important message?

**Suggested completion standard:** You can draft a complete message without assistance, improve at least three weak examples, and explain why a review helps protect accuracy and privacy. You do not need to send a real email to meet that standard.

## 8. Common misconceptions

### “A good email has to sound complicated or use big words.”

No. Simple words and accurate details are usually more helpful than elaborate vocabulary. Formality should fit the situation; complexity does not prove competence.

### “The longer the email, the more professional it is.”

No. Length should match the task. Extra background can hide the point, but omitting essential details can cause extra questions. Write enough for the reader to understand and respond.

### “The subject line does not matter because the reader will open the email anyway.”

A vague or blank subject makes it harder to recognize, prioritize, and find the message. A clear subject helps the reader before the body is opened.

### “Every email needs a long introduction.”

No. A short greeting and direct purpose work well in many ordinary messages. A longer introduction is useful when the reader needs to know who you are or why you are contacting them.

### “Being direct is rude.”

Not necessarily. “Could you confirm the room number?” is both clear and courteous. Directness becomes a problem when the language is disrespectful, misleading, or threatening, not merely because the question appears early.

### “Being polite means apologizing repeatedly.”

Not necessarily. A sincere apology is useful after a real mistake. Routine questions and requests can be written respectfully without several apologies or unnecessary explanations.

### “Using all capital letters makes a message more important.”

Not reliably. Excessive capitals can be harder to read and may appear to be shouting. Describe the actual purpose and any real deadline instead.

### “A spelling checker guarantees the message is correct.”

No. Spellcheck cannot reliably verify appointment times, names, intended meaning, or whether a statement is true. Review the facts yourself.

### “If the name appears in the To field, I know it is the right person.”

No. Contact autocomplete can suggest the wrong person, and display names may be duplicated. Inspect the actual address and verify the destination before sending.

### “Sending a message proves that it was read.”

No. A message placed in Sent does not guarantee delivery, reading, understanding, or agreement. A necessary acknowledgement or decision may still require a reply or an authorized follow-up.

### “I should include all of my account information to help the recipient.”

No. Disclose only what is necessary through an approved channel. Ordinary email is generally not the place to send passwords, one-time sign-in codes, full payment-card details, or similarly sensitive records.

### “Every request needs to say ‘urgent.’”

No. Urgency should describe a genuine time requirement. When a response date matters, state it clearly and accurately without exaggeration.

### “I need to send real messages to strangers to learn to write email.”

No. Paper exercises, text documents, and unsent drafts offer useful and safe writing practice. A self-addressed practice email is optional.

### “A good template can be copied without checking it.”

No. Templates contain placeholders and example information. Replace those with accurate details, and confirm that the resulting wording matches the actual situation before sending.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Words marked with a dotted underline earlier in the lesson can open short definitions inside the MGU dashboard. The glossary below repeats the important meanings for review. Some terms describe writing rather than email software, and not every term needs its own encyclopedia page.

- **Audience:** The person or people for whom a message is intended.
- **Purpose:** The main reason for writing a message.
- **Desired outcome:** The result the writer wants, such as an answer, decision, or confirmation.
- **Topic:** The matter discussed in an email.
- **Main point:** The most important idea or information in the message.
- **Subject line:** The short heading that identifies an email's topic in the inbox.
- **Email address:** The destination identifier used to direct an email to an intended mailbox.
- **Recipient:** The person or mailbox intended to receive the message.
- **Sender:** The person or account presenting the message as outgoing.
- **Greeting:** The opening words that address the recipient.
- **Opening sentence:** The first sentence or sentences that introduce the purpose.
- **Message body:** The principal text of an email, including facts and requests.
- **Context:** Background information necessary to interpret the message.
- **Plain language:** Writing designed to be easy for the intended reader to understand and use.
- **Clarity:** The quality of expressing an idea so readers can understand it without guessing.
- **Conciseness:** Expressing the necessary information without avoidable words or repetition.
- **Main-point-first approach:** Organizing a message so the purpose or key request appears early.
- **Specificity:** The use of concrete, relevant details instead of vague references.
- **Call to action:** The particular answer, action, or decision requested of the reader.
- **Deadline:** The date or time by which a response or action is requested or required.
- **Confirmation:** A message or statement verifying that information or an arrangement is correct.
- **Follow-up:** A later message asking about or continuing an earlier topic.
- **Tone:** The attitude or feeling expressed through a writer's wording.
- **Formality:** The degree to which wording follows more official or conventional language.
- **Courtesy:** Respectful and considerate communication.
- **Professionalism:** Appropriate, accurate, and responsible behavior in a work or formal setting.
- **Jargon:** Words or abbreviations mainly understood by a specialized group.
- **Ambiguity:** Wording that can reasonably be understood in more than one way.
- **Abbreviation:** A shortened form of a word or phrase.
- **Paragraph:** A group of sentences that develop a related thought.
- **Bullet list:** A short list that separates related points into visible items.
- **Formatting:** The visual arrangement of words, paragraphs, and other message elements.
- **Accessibility:** Designing information so people using different abilities and tools can understand it.
- **Screen reader:** Assistive software that conveys information through speech or another accessible format.
- **Link text:** The words describing a link's destination or purpose.
- **Closing:** The words used to end an email courteously.
- **Email signature:** A name, title, or other chosen information added at the end of a message.
- **Draft:** A message being composed or stored but not sent.
- **Revision:** Changing a draft's content or structure to improve meaning and organization.
- **Proofreading:** Reviewing text to find mistakes before sharing it.
- **Spellcheck:** Software that identifies some possible spelling errors.
- **Grammar checker:** Software that suggests possible changes to grammar or style; its output can be incorrect.
- **Autocomplete:** A feature that suggests contacts, words, or entries while typing.
- **To field:** The visible message field that identifies intended recipients.
- **Send:** The command used to submit an outgoing message for attempted delivery.
- **Sent folder:** A view containing messages that the email system treats as sent.
- **Response time:** The time between a request and the reply or action taken.
- **Time zone:** A regional convention for local clock time, important when scheduling across places.
- **Sensitive information:** Personal or confidential data that requires additional care or an approved channel.
- **Confidentiality:** The restriction of information to people authorized to receive it.
- **Correction:** A message supplying accurate information in place of an earlier error.
- **Template:** A reusable outline or sample with blanks to replace with accurate details.
- **Placeholder:** Temporary example text that must be replaced before sending a real message.
- **Readability:** How easily a person can read and follow a piece of writing.
- **Acknowledgement:** A reply or statement confirming receipt or recognition of information.

> **Lesson completion checklist:** I can plan for an audience and purpose; write a meaningful subject line; build an email with a clear structure; make a specific, polite request; improve vague or emotional wording; and check meaning, accuracy, recipients, and privacy before sending or saving a draft.

plain-language | Plain language | Writing designed to be easy for the intended reader to find, understand, and use. |
