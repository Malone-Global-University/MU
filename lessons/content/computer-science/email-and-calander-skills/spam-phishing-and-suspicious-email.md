## Lesson 6: Spam, Phishing, and Suspicious Email

Email helps people communicate with schools, employers, family members, businesses, and public services. It also gives dishonest senders a way to reach many people. Some unwanted messages are simply advertisements; others try to persuade you to reveal passwords, send money, open harmful files, or give a stranger control of a device. **You do not need to become a technical expert to handle suspicious mail safely.** You need a repeatable decision process: recognize warning signs, avoid interacting with an unverified request, check important claims through a separate trusted channel, and report the message when appropriate.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 90–120 minutes (including practice) · **Prerequisites:** *Email Basics* recommended; *Writing Clear Emails* and *Inbox Organization* helpful · **Practice equipment:** A computer, tablet, or smartphone, or a paper worksheet; **no real suspicious message, live link, or account is required**

> **Essential idea:** A message is not trustworthy merely because it looks professional, displays a familiar logo, names someone you know, or says that you must act immediately. **Stop, verify independently, and only then decide what to do.** The safest practice activities in this lesson use fictional messages rather than real threats.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish [[spam|spam]], [[phishing|phishing]], ordinary unwanted advertising, and legitimate mail.
1. Recognize common warning signs without relying on spelling mistakes alone.
1. Explain how a [[display-name|display name]], [[sender-address|sender address]], and [[domain|domain name]] can create a misleading impression.
1. Identify requests for passwords, [[verification-code|verification codes]], personal data, payments, or remote access as situations requiring special caution.
1. Use [[independent-verification|independent verification]] rather than links, phone numbers, or reply addresses supplied by the suspicious message.
1. Handle unexpected [[attachment|attachments]], [[link|links]], and [[qr-code|QR codes]] without opening them during practice.
1. Describe how to use [[report-phishing|Report phishing]], [[report-spam|Report spam]], and [[junk-folder|Junk or Spam folders]] appropriately.
1. Choose proportionate next steps if you clicked a link, entered information, opened a file, or sent money.
1. Apply a five-step safety routine to realistic beginner-level scenarios.

## 1. What are spam, phishing, and suspicious email?

Not every unwanted message has the same purpose. Understanding a few basic distinctions helps you choose a response without treating every email as an emergency.

[[spam|Spam]] is unwanted email, often sent in bulk. Some spam contains promotions for products or services; some is deceptive or malicious. [[phishing|Phishing]] is an attempt to trick someone into revealing information, transferring money, installing something dangerous, or taking another action that benefits an attacker. Phishing may arrive by email, text message, messaging app, or another communication channel. The word describes **the deceptive method**, not a particular appearance.

A [[suspicious-message|suspicious message]] is one that raises a reasonable question about its origin, purpose, or safety. It might turn out to be harmless, mistakenly delivered, or fraudulent. A message can be suspicious even when it has no obvious spelling errors, and a genuine message can sometimes look unusual because its sender is new or its formatting is poor.

### Four common categories

| Message type | Typical example | Appropriate first response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expected legitimate email | A class update you were expecting from a known instructor | Read, evaluate, and respond normally |
| Unwanted but recognizable marketing | A newsletter from a store where you knowingly subscribed | Use the trusted unsubscribe or preference process if desired |
| Unsolicited spam | A mass promotion from an unfamiliar sender | Do not engage; mark as spam or delete |
| Suspected phishing | An unexpected demand to confirm your password through a supplied link | Do not use the link; verify through a trusted channel and report as appropriate |

These are practical categories rather than a perfect technical classification. A marketing message may violate your preferences without being a phishing attempt. Spam may also contain phishing. What matters is the sender's behavior and your response, not finding a single perfect label.

### Why email can be convincing

Email messages combine text with names, images, logos, buttons, addresses, and other presentation details. A dishonest sender may imitate the branding of a bank, delivery company, school, employer, or public agency. They may refer to a real event, such as tax season, a recent order, a scheduled interview, or a shared document. This makes the email appear relevant to your life.

The message may also claim a problem: “Your account will be suspended,” “Your package cannot be delivered,” or “A payment has failed.” Another scam promises a reward, refund, job, scholarship, or unexpected inheritance. Whether the story appeals to fear, curiosity, kindness, or opportunity, the goal may be to move you **from reading** to **acting** before you have checked the facts.

### The simplest safe decision

Before taking an action, ask three questions:

1. **Was I expecting this?** An unexpected email is not automatically fraudulent, but an unexpected request involving money, credentials, or files deserves careful checking.
1. **What does it want me to do?** Pay, sign in, disclose information, open a file, approve a prompt, reply, or make contact using an unfamiliar number?
1. **Can I verify the claim without using the message's instructions?** If a real account needs attention, you can usually check it by opening the service's known application or a bookmarked website yourself.

> **Remember:** **“Suspicious” means “verify before acting,” not “you must solve the mystery by clicking.”** You can leave a questionable message alone while checking information through another channel.

### Opening an email versus acting on it

Viewing the subject or opening a message for reading is not the same as downloading a file, opening a link, or submitting information. Modern email services provide protections, but messages may contain external images or tracking features, and vulnerabilities are possible. For this course, **you are never required to open a real suspicious message**. The safe approach is to use the message list or its built-in reporting action when you can, and do the exercises with fictional examples.

## 2. What warning signs should you recognize?

There is no single sign that proves an email is fraudulent. Good judgment comes from looking at the **combination** of what the message claims, what it requests, and whether the request fits your real situation.

### Sudden urgency and pressure

A message might demand action within ten minutes, threaten to close an account, or claim that a payment must be made today. Real organizations sometimes send urgent notices, but **the urgency of the request does not establish the sender's identity**. A fraudster benefits when you respond quickly instead of checking.

Examples of claims worth verifying include:

- “Your mailbox will be deleted unless you sign in now.”
- “You will lose your benefit unless you provide your identification number.”
- “A manager needs you to purchase gift cards immediately.”
- “There has been suspicious activity; call the number below before your account is frozen.”

If the issue could be important, it is reasonable to check it promptly—**through a known app, saved bookmark, official account portal, or trusted telephone number**—without following the email's link or number.

### Requests for sensitive information

[[sensitive-information|Sensitive information]] includes passwords, account recovery codes, one-time security codes, financial account details, personal identification numbers, and other information that could enable fraud or identity theft. A message that unexpectedly asks you to **send a password or verification code in an email reply** is a major warning sign. Even if the message claims to be from a help desk, bank, family member, or colleague, do not disclose the information until you have verified the request; passwords and sign-in codes should not be shared with a person requesting them.

A [[multi-factor-authentication|multifactor authentication (MFA)]] request is another important situation. If you receive a sign-in approval prompt you did not initiate, do not approve it. An attacker may have your username and password and be attempting to persuade you to finish the login for them. Check the account through its usual trusted settings, and follow your organization's security process if applicable.

### Surprising changes in payment or contact instructions

A scammer may pretend to be an employer, vendor, landlord, relative, or business partner and claim that a payment destination has changed. This is sometimes called [[business-email-compromise|business email compromise]] when it targets workplace or business payments. Even an email in a familiar conversation can be compromised or imitated. For financial changes, use a known telephone number or established approval channel **separate from the message** and verify the exact account or recipient details.

### Unexpected files, invoices, and shared documents

An email may contain a document called “Invoice,” “Schedule,” “Photos,” “Payroll,” or “Secure Notice.” A familiar title does not prove the file is safe. Some scams impersonate shared-document invitations and lead to a fake sign-in page; others attempt to deliver harmful software. Do not open an unexpected attachment or document invitation just to find out whether it is real. Verify that the sender actually intended to send it.

### Unusual wording and appearance

Spelling errors, odd fonts, unfamiliar greetings, and inconsistent logos can be clues. But sophisticated scams may use polished writing, correct grammar, translated text, or accurate-looking branding. **A perfect-looking email can still be fraudulent.** Likewise, a brief or awkward message from someone you actually know is not automatically a scam. Evaluate the requested action and independently verify when the consequences could be serious.

### Warning signs versus proof

| Possible clue | What it may suggest | What it does **not** prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Act within 15 minutes” | Pressure to bypass careful checking | That the sender is definitely fraudulent |
| A display name matching your bank | An attempt to appear familiar | That the address or message is authentic |
| An unexpected login link | Possible credential theft | That opening the link is necessary |
| A polished logo and correct grammar | Professional-looking design | That the request is safe |
| A payment account change | Possible impersonation or compromise | That a familiar sender's account has not been compromised |
| An unexpected attachment | Possible fraud or malware risk | That every attached file is dangerous |
| A message in the Spam folder | The provider detected something questionable | That every message there is malicious |

> **Practice principle:** The safest response to an important, uncertain request is often **“I will check this through the normal channel.”** That statement is useful whether the request comes from an unknown sender or appears to come from a close contact.

## 3. How can sender names, addresses, and links mislead you?

An email has several identifying details, and they do not all mean the same thing. You should learn to examine them, while recognizing that **their appearance alone cannot authenticate a message**.

### Display name versus email address

An email application may show a large or prominent [[display-name|display name]] and place the actual [[sender-address|sender address]] in smaller text. For example, a fictional message might display **Student Services** while its address is `records@helpdesk-notice.example`. This example uses a reserved teaching domain, not a real school. The important point is that the familiar-looking name and the actual address are **different fields**.

A legitimate sender may also use a service provider or mailing platform with a less familiar domain. A mismatched-looking address therefore provides a reason to verify, not a final verdict. The safest method for a consequential request is to use the organization’s previously known contact method or account portal.

### What is a domain name?

In `alex@school.example`, the part after the `@` is the email address's [[domain|domain]]. Websites also use domains, such as `school.example`. Email domains and website domains can sometimes differ for legitimate operational reasons. Attackers may choose misleadingly similar words, extra labels, character substitutions, or unrelated domains that merely mention a trusted brand.

**Fictional teaching examples (not live destinations):**

| Displayed item | What a learner should notice | Safe interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `support@campus.example` | Mailbox name and domain are separate components | Verify using the campus's established channels if the request matters |
| `Campus Support <alert@campus-help.example>` | The display name says Campus Support, but the address uses a different example domain | Do not assume affiliation from the name |
| `https://campus.example/account` | Example URL uses the illustrative domain `campus.example` | A familiar-looking URL still needs independent context |
| `https://campus.example.login-notice.invalid/` | The domain is `login-notice.invalid`, not `campus.example` | An earlier trusted-looking word may be a distraction |
| `https://support-campus.invalid/verify` | A word resembling an organization appears in an unrelated example domain | Do not treat a familiar word as proof |

The suffixes `.example` and `.invalid` are reserved for illustrations. **Do not visit, scan, or type any example address as an exercise.** They exist only to teach the shape of addresses.

### Link text and actual destination

A [[hyperlink|hyperlink]] can show one phrase while sending the browser somewhere else. A blue button reading **Review Account** does not disclose who controls the site behind it. On some desktop devices you can preview a link's destination by placing the pointer over it without selecting it; on touchscreens this may not work the same way, and long-press behavior varies. A preview can help reveal contradictions, but it is **not sufficient proof of safety**, and trying to inspect a link is never required for this lesson.

For sensitive account matters, avoid the uncertain link entirely. Instead, open the correct service using an existing app icon or a bookmark you established earlier. If typing a website address, use one you already know is official; avoid copying the address from the suspicious email. Do not trust a result solely because it appears first in a search engine; fake customer-service numbers and sponsored search results can be misleading.

### QR codes, shortened links, and redirects

A [[qr-code|QR code]] can contain a website address or other data. Scanning a QR code in an unexpected message may direct a phone to an unsafe destination. [[shortened-link|Shortened links]] hide portions of the final destination; legitimate services sometimes use them, but they give you less information to evaluate. A [[redirect|redirect]] can also send a browser from one address to another.

The beginner's decision is straightforward: if the message is unexpected or requests a sensitive action, **do not scan, open, or follow its directions**. Verify through the official service independently. The fact that a phone offers to open a QR-code destination does not make it trustworthy.

### Website appearance, padlocks, and HTTPS

A site can look exactly like a familiar login page. A website with `https://` normally uses encryption for its connection, but encryption **does not mean that the company shown on the page is real or that the site is honest**. Attackers can also operate encrypted websites. Learn to treat a padlock or HTTPS as a connection property, not an identity guarantee.

### Familiar sender, unfamiliar request

If someone you know unexpectedly asks you to buy a gift card, send private records, or open a file, do not assume the request is safe because the message appears in a previous conversation. An account may have been taken over, or the displayed identity may have been impersonated. Contact that person using a separate, previously trusted method—not the new contact information inside the suspicious message.

## 4. What kinds of requests, attachments, and invitations need extra care?

A suspicious email is dangerous mainly because it tries to **change what you do**. The wording, image, or document title is the packaging. The request is the important part.

### Requests to sign in or verify an account

A message may ask you to enter your email address and password on a page labeled “Security Verification.” A phishing site may be designed to collect credentials. Another scam asks for a [[one-time-code|one-time code]] generated during an actual login attempt. **Do not send one-time codes, recovery codes, or MFA approvals to someone who contacted you unexpectedly.** A real authentication process should occur inside the account's established sign-in experience, not in a reply to an unverified message.

### Requests for money, gift cards, or changed bank details

Treat unexpected requests for [[gift-card|gift cards]], [[wire-transfer|wire transfers]], [[payment-app|payment app transfers]], cryptocurrency payments, or changes to payroll or vendor banking instructions as high-risk. Do not interpret apparent authority as permission to bypass normal payment approvals. If the request claims to come from a supervisor, family member, or service provider, independently call a number you had **before** the message or use a known established communication channel.

If a real organization has a valid payment due, checking through its ordinary app or account website will usually let you confirm the balance without acting on a questionable email. A scammer may also claim that you need to move money to “protect” it; a protective-sounding story does not make the transfer safe.

### Unexpected attachments

An [[attachment|attachment]] is a file included with an email. Documents, photographs, PDFs, spreadsheets, and compressed archives are all possible attachments. The file extension helps describe a format, but it does **not** guarantee safety. An attacker may place harmful content in a document, an archive, or a file pretending to be something else.

A safe beginner routine is:

1. Ask whether you were expecting this specific file from this specific person.
1. If not, **do not open or download it** merely to investigate.
1. Verify with the sender using a separate, known channel, especially if the attachment requests a sign-in or software installation.
1. Let your email service and device security protections run; do not disable them to open a file.
1. If it is a school or work account, follow the organization's policies before opening or distributing unusual files.

Do not rely on the extension alone. A PDF or image may appear safer than a program, but no file type is an absolute guarantee. This course has a separate lesson on **Attachments and Downloads** for ordinary file handling; the present lesson focuses on **recognizing and avoiding unverified files**.

### Shared document and meeting invitations

Some phishing emails imitate cloud-document invitations, shared folders, voicemail notifications, online meeting notices, or calendar events. They may lead to fake login forms or pressure you to accept a permission request. A familiar platform's logo does not authenticate an invitation. If you were expecting a document, verify in the platform's official app or ask its owner through a trusted conversation. If you were not expecting an invitation, do not sign in merely because an email says somebody shared something with you.

### Fake support and remote access

A scammer may say that your device is infected, your antivirus has expired, or an account refund requires a “technician.” They might instruct you to install software or grant [[remote-access|remote access]] to your screen. Do not grant unsolicited remote access or install unfamiliar tools at the direction of an unexpected email. If you need support, find the official service through a known app, original purchase documentation, or an independently established customer-support channel.

### Unsubscribe carefully

For **legitimate newsletters you knowingly joined**, a provider-managed unsubscribe function or a sender's clearly established preference center can help reduce mail. For **suspicious or unsolicited messages**, do not click the message's unsubscribe link merely to stop the mail; it may send you to an unsafe site or confirm that the address is active. Use the email service's Report spam or Report phishing option instead, when available.

### Consider privacy beyond money

Fraud is not limited to stealing credit-card numbers. A suspicious message might request photographs of identification, school records, employee information, health data, home addresses, contact lists, or security answers. You should protect these even if no immediate financial request appears. A legitimate organization may need information in a proper context; the task is to verify **who is asking, why, and through which approved method** before providing it.

## 5. How do you check a suspicious message safely?

Use a simple routine called **STOP–CHECK–CONTACT–DECIDE–REPORT**. It is a teaching framework for this lesson, not the name of a feature in any particular email application.

### Step 1: STOP — do not interact with the request

Do not rush. Avoid clicking links, scanning QR codes, opening files, replying with information, calling the number shown in the message, or approving an unexpected MFA request. If you feel alarmed, angry, excited, or pressured, that reaction is a reason to slow the decision, not to comply immediately.

### Step 2: CHECK — state the claim and the risk

Ask, in plain language: “This email says my account is locked and wants me to sign in through its button.” Identify the **claim** (“account locked”) and the **requested action** (“use this button to sign in”). Ask whether the message is expected, whether its request fits an ordinary process, and what information or money could be lost if it were a trick.

Do not spend unlimited time analyzing fonts, sender headers, and technical details. These may help experts, but a beginner can reach a safe decision without them. Remember that good spelling, a familiar logo, and a known display name do not settle the issue.

### Step 3: CONTACT — use an independent trusted channel

Open the institution's official application directly, use a bookmark created before the message, call the number on a physical card or previously saved record, or speak to the contact using a trusted existing conversation. For school or workplace accounts, use your known IT or administrative contact and established reporting process.

An email claiming to be from a bank should not be verified by **replying to that email** or calling the number it supplies. Verification is independent only when the contact method does not originate from the questionable message.

### Step 4: DECIDE — handle the real issue, not the unverified instructions

If the official account or trusted contact confirms a genuine issue, resolve it through the **official process**. A legitimate issue and a phishing email can exist at the same time. If the claim is false, there is no reason to comply. If you cannot verify and the request is sensitive, leave it unfulfilled while you seek qualified support. You can set a reminder to revisit a genuine deadline after checking.

### Step 5: REPORT — alert the right party

Use the email service's reporting controls where appropriate, and follow school or workplace policy. Reporting can help providers improve detection, though it does not guarantee immediate removal of every similar message. Once a suspicious message is reported and any institutional evidence-preservation step is complete, delete it or leave it in the designated Spam/Junk location according to the provider's process.

### A one-minute decision card

| Ask yourself | If the answer is yes | Your next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is this asking for a password, code, money, or personal records? | Sensitive action | Do not comply from the email; verify separately |
| Is there an unexpected file, QR code, or sign-in link? | Unverified interaction | Do not open it; check with the sender independently |
| Is the message pressuring me to rush or keep a secret? | Potential manipulation | Stop and involve a trusted person or official contact |
| Was I expecting an ordinary update with no unusual request? | Possibly routine | Evaluate normally, while remaining attentive to context |
| Did I already follow the message's instructions? | Possible exposure | Use the response steps in Section 6 immediately |

### Example: an unexpected school notice

Suppose an email says, “Your school account expires in 30 minutes. Click **Verify Now** and enter your password.” The safe response is **not** to test the supplied link. Instead, open the school's normal student portal from your usual bookmark, check official announcements, or contact student support using a number obtained earlier. If your account works and support says the message was not sent by them, use the mailbox's phishing-report function. If support confirms a real account issue, resolve it through their known process rather than the original message.

### Example: a familiar relative asks for help

Suppose an email appears to be from a relative, claims their phone is broken, and asks you to buy gift cards and send the codes. First, stop. Contact the relative using your established phone number or another trusted method. Do not use the new number supplied by the email. If you cannot reach the relative, do not treat silence as confirmation. The apparent personal connection is precisely why checking matters.

### Accessible and low-technology verification

The safest process does not require a mouse, a high-resolution display, or the ability to read tiny website addresses. Screen-reader users can use message details and reporting commands available in their mail application; if an interface does not expose useful information, an independently contacted trusted support person can help. People using mobile devices can open a familiar app instead of previewing links. A paper list of official service numbers or bookmarks can make independent verification easier. **No one must hover over a link to prove they learned this lesson.**

## 6. How do you report suspicious email and respond to mistakes?

Recognizing and avoiding a scam is the preferred outcome. But people sometimes click before realizing a message was suspicious. The response should depend on **what happened**, because merely reading a message, entering a password, transferring money, and granting remote access are not the same event.

### Reporting through the email service

Most major email services provide built-in options to report unwanted or suspicious mail. The exact buttons vary by provider, version, and device. On a school or work account, use the organization's approved phishing-report tool or IT process if one exists.

| Service or setting | Usual reporting method | Important caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gmail | Select a message and use **Report phishing** or **Report spam** where offered | Reporting and moving messages may share content with Google for analysis; labels vary by device |
| Outlook | Select a message and choose an available **Report phishing** or **Report junk** control | Options depend on Outlook version and organizational setup |
| Apple Mail or iCloud Mail | Use **Move to Junk**, **Mark as Junk**, or the applicable mail service reporting process | Mail and server-side junk filtering may use different controls |
| Workplace or school email | Follow the institution's security reporting procedure | Report promptly; do not independently circulate the malicious email to coworkers |

**Spam versus phishing:** Use Report spam for unwanted unsolicited email when there is no clear deceptive attack. Use Report phishing when it appears designed to impersonate or trick you into giving up information, money, or access. Some applications combine or rename these options. If unsure, you can ask your service's legitimate support channel or follow the organization's policy.

### If the email was merely viewed

If you opened a message to read it but did **not** follow links, open files, grant permissions, submit information, or make payments, do not assume an account takeover occurred. You can report or remove the message and continue using your device normally. Because email clients and tracking features differ, opening a message is not an absolute guarantee of zero risk, but it does not by itself mean a password was stolen.

### If you clicked a suspicious link

1. **Do not enter information, approve prompts, download files, or continue interacting** with the page.
1. Close the suspicious page or tab.
1. If the page initiated a download, do not open it; follow your device's and organization's safety procedures.
1. Update your browser and device protection software through their normal official mechanisms, and use a trusted security scan if you suspect a harmful download or installation.
1. If the page requested credentials or you suspect account compromise, go to the real service through a trusted route and check account security.

Clicking alone does not always mean credentials were exposed, but it is a reason to assess what followed. A workplace user should notify the approved IT/security contact promptly, even if no obvious damage occurred.

### If you entered a password or authentication code

1. From a trusted device and the service's **official sign-in route**, change the affected account's password immediately; use a unique password.
1. If the same password was reused elsewhere, change it on those accounts too.
1. Enable or review multifactor authentication and recovery settings; where supported, favor secure, phishing-resistant methods such as passkeys or security keys.
1. Review recent sign-ins, active sessions, connected apps, forwarding rules, and account recovery information for unfamiliar changes; use the provider's account security tools.
1. Report the incident to the provider or your school/work security team. If you cannot sign in, use official account recovery rather than numbers from the suspicious message.

A leaked one-time code may enable an unauthorized login attempt. Do not assume that changing a password alone resolves every possible session or account-setting issue; use the platform's security review and trusted support.

### If you opened a suspicious attachment or installed software

Do not keep opening the file or follow on-screen instructions from it. Stop interacting with unfamiliar software and seek help through a trusted device support provider or your organization's IT team. Use a reputable, up-to-date security scan or the official recovery instructions for your device. If remote access was given, notify the relevant account providers and follow qualified guidance to remove access and secure affected accounts. Do not pay a person who unexpectedly claims they can clean the infection.

### If you sent money or financial information

Contact the **bank, card issuer, payment service, or institution involved immediately** through an official number or application. Explain what was sent, when, and to whom; ask about possible reversal, dispute, account protections, or replacement of credentials. Reversals are not guaranteed, and the options depend on the payment method. In the United States, you can also report suspected fraud to the Federal Trade Commission at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/). If identity information was exposed, [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) offers recovery guidance. Report an impersonation to the institution being imitated through its normal contact channels.

### If you suspect your email account was taken over

Warning signs can include messages in Sent that you did not write, unfamiliar login notices, missing messages, unrecognized forwarding settings, or changes to account recovery details. These signs are not conclusive by themselves. Use the provider's official account-security tools and follow its recovery process. Check whether automatic forwarding or filters were changed, because an attacker might use them to continue accessing mail. Warn affected contacts through a trusted communication channel if someone may be receiving scam messages from your account.

### External reporting and privacy

In the United States, the FTC advises forwarding suspected phishing emails to the Anti-Phishing Working Group at **reportphishing@apwg.org** and reporting scams through **ReportFraud.ftc.gov**. For a beginner, the email provider's built-in Report phishing option is usually the simplest starting point. **Do not forward confidential workplace messages, personal records, or sensitive attachments to an outside address in violation of policy**; use institutional reporting procedures instead. Reporting sends information to another party, so be deliberate about where it goes.

> **Recovery principle:** Match the response to the exposure. **Viewed only → report and disengage. Entered a password → secure the account. Sent money → contact the financial institution urgently. Installed software or granted access → seek trusted technical assistance.** Report relevant incidents through the correct channel rather than attempting risky experiments.

## 7. Guided practice: Make safe email decisions

These activities are designed for a **fictional practice environment**. You can answer on paper, in a text editor, or by discussing your choices aloud. They do not ask you to open a suspicious message, visit any unfamiliar site, click a link, or share real personal information. The sample addresses use reserved example domains. If you practice in an actual email account, **do not manufacture phishing messages or send misleading messages to other people**.

### Activity 1: Sort a sample inbox (about 10–15 minutes)

Read the eight fictional message previews below. Mark each one as **routine**, **unwanted marketing/spam**, **suspicious—verify**, or **uncertain—ask for help**. More than one response may be reasonable for an ambiguous example. For each message, identify the **safest next action**.

| Message preview | Your classification | Your safe next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. A teacher whose class you attend confirms tomorrow's lecture time; no links or unusual requests | ________ | ________ |
| B. “WIN A PRIZE NOW!” from an unfamiliar store you never joined | ________ | ________ |
| C. “Your bank account closes in 10 minutes. Log in with this button.” | ________ | ________ |
| D. Your expected volunteer coordinator sends a plain-text schedule update | ________ | ________ |
| E. “The principal needs $300 in gift cards. Reply with the codes; keep this confidential.” | ________ | ________ |
| F. A newsletter you knowingly subscribed to provides an ordinary update | ________ | ________ |
| G. An unfamiliar sender shares a “Payroll Report” file and says to enable editing | ________ | ________ |
| H. A friend unexpectedly sends an invitation requiring an unfamiliar sign-in process | ________ | ________ |

Before revealing the suggested answers, explain why the **requested action** matters more than the sender's logo or writing style. Also explain why a genuine teacher's routine message does not require a formal security investigation every time.

:::answer Show Activity 1 suggested classifications and reasoning
1. **A — Routine, assuming the sender and class context fit.** Read and record the time through ordinary class procedures; no unusual action is requested.
1. **B — Unwanted marketing or spam.** Do not respond; use the mailbox's spam control or delete appropriately.
1. **C — Suspicious: verify.** Do not click. Use the bank's established app or number to check the account.
1. **D — Routine, assuming the context matches.** Review the schedule; if something materially unusual appears, independently confirm it.
1. **E — Suspicious: verify.** Do not buy gift cards or send codes. Contact the institution through a trusted existing channel.
1. **F — Routine marketing.** Read if desired; use a trustworthy unsubscribe process if you no longer want it.
1. **G — Suspicious: verify.** Do not open the file or enable editing. Ask through an established channel whether it is expected.
1. **H — Suspicious or uncertain: verify.** Contact your friend through a known conversation or number. Do not enter credentials at the linked page.

The exercise is about **reasonable safe decisions**, not diagnosing a sender's actual identity from fictional evidence. A genuinely important notice can still require checking; checking does not require using the notice's link.

### Activity 2: Examine a fictional sender and URL (about 10 minutes)

Read these **text-only illustrations**. They are not live mail or sites:

- Display name: `Campus IT Desk`
- Sender: `help@campus-security-alert.invalid`
- Subject: `RE: Student account suspension`
- Body: `Your campus.example account will be disabled within 20 minutes. Sign in immediately at https://campus.example.verify-account.invalid/login and enter the code we just sent.`

Answer these questions without opening any address:

1. What does the display name claim, and what is the actual sender-address domain?
1. What part of the sample website address is meant to look like the school's domain?
1. What is the actual example domain at the end of the site address?
1. Name **two** pressure tactics or sensitive requests.
1. What independent channel would you use to confirm a genuine school-account problem?
1. What is your safe response if a one-time code arrives although you did not try to log in?

:::answer Show Activity 2 worked answers
1. The display name claims to be **Campus IT Desk**; the sender-address domain is `campus-security-alert.invalid`.
1. The early text `campus.example` gives the appearance of an institutional address.
1. The actual illustrative site domain is `verify-account.invalid`; the earlier words do not change that.
1. The message uses a **20-minute deadline** and requests an account sign-in plus a **one-time code**. Both demand caution.
1. Open the school's normal official portal using an existing bookmark, or contact known student IT support through a number you already trust.

### Activity 3: Rewrite unsafe reactions as safe actions (about 10–15 minutes)

1. “The email has the company logo, so I should use its sign-in button.”
1. “My supervisor's name appears at the top, so I'll buy the gift cards now.”

1. “I already clicked, so there's nothing more I can do.”

Use the sentence starter **“Instead, I will...”**. A useful rewrite names a positive, practical next step rather than merely saying “Be careful.”

:::answer Show Activity 3 example rewrites
1. “Instead, I will open the company's usual app or bookmarked site and look for a real account notice.”
1. “Instead, I will call my supervisor through our established work contact and follow payment approval procedures.”

### Activity 4: Choose the response after a mistake (about 15 minutes)

For each situation, name the **first useful response**, one **follow-up response**, and one step that would **not** help. Do not conduct any real tests or simulate an account compromise.

| Fictional situation | First useful response | Follow-up response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. You read a suspicious message but took no other action | ________ | ________ |
| B. You clicked an unexpected link but entered nothing | ________ | ________ |
| C. You typed a password into a page linked by a suspicious email | ________ | ________ |
| D. You sent a one-time sign-in code to someone by email | ________ | ________ |
| E. You purchased a gift card and gave someone the redemption code | ________ | ________ |
| F. You installed software after a fake support notice | ________ | ________ |
| G. You notice unfamiliar forwarding settings in your email account | ________ | ________ |

:::answer Show Activity 4 response guide
1. **A —** Report or remove the message using the provider's controls; do not assume that reading alone exposed credentials. An unhelpful step would be providing personal information to “confirm” the sender.
1. **B —** Leave the page without further interaction; assess whether anything downloaded or ran. If relevant, use trusted device security tools or inform organizational IT. Do not return to the page to test its other buttons.
1. **C —** Visit the genuine service through a trusted route and change the password immediately; review sessions, recovery settings, and reused passwords. Do not type a new password into the suspicious page.
1. **D —** Treat the code as potentially disclosed; use official account security and recovery steps, check recent access, and report the incident. Do not share additional codes to “undo” the first one.
1. **E —** Contact the gift-card issuer or retailer and any payment institution through official channels as quickly as possible; report the suspected fraud. Recovery is not guaranteed. Do not send more codes to a person promising a refund.
1. **F —** Stop following the fake support person's instructions and seek qualified, trusted technical help; secure affected accounts through official channels. Do not grant the same person more remote access.
1. **G —** Review and remove unauthorized changes through the account provider's official security tools; change credentials, examine active sessions, and seek trusted support. Do not assume that deleting one suspicious email fixes changed settings.

### Activity 5: Practice an independent verification script (about 10–15 minutes)

You receive a fictional message claiming to be from your school: “You must send your student identification number and password today or lose access.” You choose to contact the school using **a telephone number from your existing student handbook**, not from the email. Read the role-play aloud or write your answer. One person may play the learner and another the office employee.

**Learner's goal:** Ask whether there is a real account problem **without disclosing a password or one-time code**.

**Useful script:**

> “Hello. I received an unexpected email claiming my student account is at risk. Before I act, I want to confirm whether the school sent an account notice. Could you tell me the official process for checking my account and reporting suspicious email? I will not give a password or verification code over this call.”

Try a second round in which the message appears to come from an employer requesting a new bank account for vendor payments. Ask the appropriate internal contact to validate the request **using the existing approval process**. A role-play succeeds when the learner identifies the request, uses an established independent contact method, and avoids providing sensitive information prematurely.

**Self-review:** Did you explain the claim accurately? Did you keep your verification method separate? Did you avoid handing over private credentials? Did you learn how to report the message?

:::answer Show Activity 5 completion criteria
A strong response states the email's claim **without assuming it is true**, contacts the organization through an independently established channel, asks for the normal process, and refuses to share passwords, one-time codes, or payment credentials. If the organization confirms a real issue, the learner follows its official procedure instead of returning to the unverified email.
:::

### Optional Activity: Create a personal safe-email routine (about 5–10 minutes)

Write a short checklist you can keep beside your device. Start with these five words: **Stop, Check, Contact, Decide, Report**. Add one trusted way to reach each institution you frequently use (for example, “open my bank's existing app” or “call the number on my physical card”). **Do not write passwords, recovery codes, full account numbers, or private identifying information on the checklist.** You may also list whom to ask for help at school or work.

### Mini-capstone: A mixed message

A fictional email says: “Your delivery is delayed. The attached invoice explains a $3 redelivery fee. Open it, confirm your payment, and do so within an hour.” The sender has the delivery company's name as its display name; however, you were not expecting a package.

Explain, in three or four sentences, why this message deserves checking. Identify at least **three possible warning signs** and state an independent way to verify whether any delivery exists. Your answer should not involve opening the attachment, clicking a payment link, or calling a phone number contained in the email.

:::answer Show mini-capstone example answer

I will not open the attachment or pay through the message. ## 8. Common misconceptions

### “Spam and phishing mean exactly the same thing.”

No. Spam generally means unwanted messaging, often in bulk. Phishing is a deceptive attempt to manipulate someone into providing information, money, or access. A phishing message can be spam, but not every unwanted newsletter is a phishing attack.

### “A professional-looking email is trustworthy.”

No. An attacker can copy branding, use correct grammar, add an official-looking signature, and send a convincing message. Judge the requested action and verify the sender through independent channels.

### “My friend's name appears as the sender, so it must be safe.”

No. Display names can be imitated, and genuine accounts can be compromised. An unexpected request from someone you know may still require checking through a previously trusted contact method.

### “If a message says urgent, I must follow its instructions immediately.”

No. A claim of urgency can be part of the manipulation. A genuine urgent issue can usually be checked through a real official application, known number, or established support channel.

### “A web address with HTTPS or a padlock is definitely legitimate.”

No. HTTPS is a connection security feature, not proof of the site's owner or intent. A fraudulent site can use HTTPS. The safest route for account checks is the trusted service you already know.

### “If a file is a PDF, photograph, or spreadsheet, it cannot be harmful.”

No. Any unexpected file needs context and verification. Modern software and security protections reduce some risks, but file type alone cannot promise safety.

### “A suspicious email has to contain a link.”

No. A scam may ask you to telephone a fake support number, reply with confidential information, buy gift cards, approve a login prompt, or change a payment account. Look at the requested behavior rather than the presence of a link.

### “I can ask the sender whether the message is fraudulent.”

Replying to the questionable address does not provide independent verification. A fraudster can simply say the message is genuine. Reach the supposed sender through a trusted channel you obtained separately.

### “Reporting spam and deleting an email are always identical.”

No. A report may share the message with the email provider's detection systems; deletion usually places mail in a deletion location. Reporting is often more useful for identifying threats, and workplace procedures may require a particular method.

### “I should use every unsubscribe link to reduce junk mail.”

No. Use established unsubscribe controls for mail you recognize from legitimate subscriptions. For dubious messages, use the email service's reporting function rather than interacting with an unfamiliar link.

### “If I clicked one link, my entire computer is definitely hacked.”

Not necessarily. The consequences depend on what happened after the click, such as entering credentials, allowing a download, installing software, or approving permissions. Stop interacting and assess the specific exposure. Ask trusted support for help when uncertain.

### “If I already gave away my password, nothing can be done.”

No. Prompt action can reduce harm. Change the affected password through the real provider, secure related accounts, review recovery and session settings, and report the incident. The appropriate next step depends on the kind of information disclosed.

### “MFA makes phishing impossible.”

No. MFA adds valuable protection, but attackers may try to obtain one-time codes or trick users into approving sign-ins. Do not approve a prompt you did not initiate; use the strongest supported authentication method and verify unusual account activity.

### “Anything in the Spam folder must be dangerous; anything in the Inbox must be safe.”

No. Automated filters can make mistakes in both directions. A legitimate message may land in Spam, and a harmful one may reach the Inbox. Use contextual judgment and treat important requests consistently wherever you find them.

### “I can prove that a message is safe by clicking around the linked website.”

No. Exploring an unverified site creates exposure without establishing authenticity. Independent verification through a trusted app, known address, or established contact is safer and more useful.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Words marked with a dotted underline earlier in the lesson open concise definitions in the MGU lesson dashboard. You can also study the glossary below without using the interactive tooltips.

- **Spam:** Unwanted electronic messages, often promotional and sent widely; some spam is malicious.
- **Phishing:** Deceptive communication designed to persuade a person to give up information, money, or access.
- **Suspicious message:** A communication whose origin, content, or requested action warrants verification.
- **Display name:** The human-readable sender name an email application presents.
- **Sender address:** The email address displayed for a message's apparent sender.
- **Domain name:** The identifying part of an internet address, such as the portion after `@` in an email address.
- **Impersonation:** Pretending to be a trusted person, company, or institution.
- **Spoofing:** Making message-identifying details appear to come from another source.
- **Link or hyperlink:** Clickable text or a control that opens another location, which may not be obvious from its label.
- **QR code:** A machine-readable visual code that may contain an internet address or other information.
- **Attachment:** A file included with an email message.
- **Malware:** Software designed to compromise, disrupt, damage, or misuse a computer or data.
- **Verification code:** A temporary security code used during certain account authentication processes.
- **Multifactor authentication:** Signing in with two or more different kinds of evidence of identity.
- **Independent verification:** Confirming a claim through an established trusted source instead of the questionable message.
- **Report phishing:** A provider or organizational function for flagging messages that attempt deception or credential theft.
- **Report spam:** A provider control for flagging unwanted messages.
- **Spam or Junk folder:** A mailbox area used to hold messages a service regards as unwanted or potentially suspicious.
- **Account recovery:** The provider's procedure for regaining access to an account you cannot use normally.
- **Business email compromise:** A fraud pattern involving impersonated or compromised business communication, often to redirect payments.
- **Remote access:** A technology that lets someone interact with a device from another location; unsolicited requests for this are risky.
- **Password manager:** Software that helps create, store, and use unique passwords.
- **Passkey:** A sign-in credential using cryptographic methods that can reduce exposure to conventional phishing when supported.
- **Recovery code:** A special credential used to regain account access, which should not be shared with an unexpected requester.
- **Identity theft:** The misuse of another person's identifying information, often for fraud.
- **Red flag:** A sign that merits caution or verification; not necessarily conclusive proof.
- **False positive:** A legitimate message incorrectly classified as suspicious or spam.
- **False negative:** A suspicious message not detected by a protective filter.
