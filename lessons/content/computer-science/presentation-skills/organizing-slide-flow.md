## Lesson 5: Organizing Slide Flow

A neighborhood library has prepared nine slides to introduce its fictional Saturday Reading Hour. The slides contain useful information: an opening welcome, a schedule, who can attend, the activities, accessibility arrangements, cost, and how to participate. Yet the deck begins with registration instructions, moves to the final thank-you, then returns to the welcome. The facts may be correct, but the audience has to work out the order for itself. An organizer can improve the presentation without adding a single photograph or changing the color theme: **put the ideas in an order that makes sense.**

A [[presentation|presentation]] is a planned message communicated through [[slide|slides]] and often through a speaker's explanation. [[slide-flow|Slide flow]] is the logical progression from one slide's idea to the next. Effective flow gives an audience enough context, moves through related information in a deliberate sequence, and reaches a clear conclusion. This lesson teaches both the **thinking work** of planning a sequence and the **software work** of moving, grouping, adding, removing, and reviewing slides.

Lessons 1–4 introduced presentations, slide creation, themes, and content insertion. Lesson 5 builds on that foundation. Later lessons address detailed design rules, speaker notes, presenting, and exporting. **Organizing a presentation is not the same as making individual slides attractive.** A beautifully formatted deck can still be confusing if its story jumps backward without explanation.

Microsoft PowerPoint on Windows 11 is the primary desktop reference. Google Slides and LibreOffice Impress are included because their fundamental slide-order tools are similar, although available commands and shortcuts vary by version and device. All essential practice uses a fictional library event. You can complete the ordering, planning, and evaluation exercises on paper without an account, internet connection, or presentation application.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 100–135 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Presentation Skills Lessons 1–4 recommended · **Practice equipment:** Paper and pencil or an available presentation editor; internet optional · **Safety:** Use invented event details and nonprivate examples; keep an editable copy before major reorganization; do not publish a class exercise or alter a shared deck without authorization.

> **Essential idea:** **Identify the audience's question → choose the main message → arrange a beginning, middle, and ending → connect related slides → check the sequence from the audience's viewpoint.** Software can move slides, but it cannot decide the clearest explanation for you.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain [[slide-flow|slide flow]] and distinguish logical organization from a visual [[slide-transition|slide transition]].
2. Identify a presentation's purpose, audience, [[takeaway|takeaway]], and main audience questions before arranging slides.
3. Build a simple beginning–middle–ending [[outline|outline]] with one main idea per slide.
4. Recognize chronological, step-by-step, problem–solution, comparison, and question–answer organization patterns.
5. Use informative slide titles, [[signpost|signposts]], section groupings, and transition statements to connect ideas.
6. Distinguish moving a slide from duplicating, deleting, hiding, or temporarily skipping it.
7. Describe slide-order workflows in PowerPoint, Google Slides, and LibreOffice Impress, including the roles of [[thumbnail-pane|slide thumbnails]] and [[slide-sorter|Slide Sorter]].
8. Identify gaps, repetition, misplaced evidence, unexplained changes of topic, and unnecessary slides.
9. Explain why sequence, timing, visible titles, and meaningful reading order matter for accessibility.
10. Organize and review a short fictional presentation while preserving an editable copy.
11. Evaluate whether the resulting deck leads a beginner audience toward one understandable outcome.

## 1. What is slide flow, and why does order matter?

[[slide-flow|Slide flow]] describes how one slide's meaning leads to the next. It is more than the numbered position of a file's pages. A presenter chooses which idea an audience needs first, which details can follow, and which conclusion brings the message together. When this progression is clear, listeners spend their attention understanding the information instead of repeatedly asking, *Why are we talking about this now?*

Picture an introduction to a community reading program. A learner may reasonably want to know: **What is it? Who is it for? What happens there? When does it meet? How do I join?** An explanation that answers these questions in a sensible progression feels easier than an explanation that introduces an attendance rule before naming the program. The individual rules may be important, but the audience first needs to understand the setting.

### Slide order, slide flow, and slide transition are different

These concepts sound similar and should not be confused:

| Concept | Meaning | Practical example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slide order | The physical sequence of slides in a deck | Welcome is Slide 1; Activities is Slide 3 |
| Slide flow | The logic that connects the ideas | The audience learns the purpose before the steps |
| Slide transition | A visual effect or method for changing from one slide to another | The next slide appears with a simple fade |
| Verbal transition | A spoken bridge between ideas | “Now that we know who can attend, here is what happens.” |
| Animation | Movement or change of an item on a slide | A diagram reveals one part at a time |

You can put slides in the correct numeric order yet still have weak flow. For example, a slide titled **Three Reasons to Participate** may list three advantages that the following slides never explain. A slide titled **What You Need to Bring** may appear before the deck establishes that the activity takes place in person. The sequence is technically numbered; the audience still experiences gaps.

A visual transition effect, such as a wipe or fade, does not supply missing reasoning. A clear sentence explaining why the next idea matters is more useful than an elaborate animation. The software's transition controls influence appearance and playback; slide flow is an editorial decision.

### The audience encounters your information over time

In a written page, a reader can often scan ahead or look back immediately. During a spoken presentation, the audience normally sees the slides in an order chosen by the presenter. Participants may have differing knowledge, reading speeds, language backgrounds, vision or hearing access, and familiarity with the subject. A novice cannot be expected to supply definitions that an experienced presenter has left out.

Suppose a fictional training talk contains these slides:

| Current position | Slide title | Audience experience |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | How to Register | “Register for what?” |
| 2 | Final Questions | “Are we already finished?” |
| 3 | Welcome to Reading Hour | “This should have come first.” |
| 4 | What Happens Each Week | “Now the program makes sense.” |
| 5 | Who Can Attend | “This answers another basic question.” |
| 6 | When and Where | “Useful, but it came after several details.” |

The problem is not incorrect information. It is the [[sequence|sequence]]. The speaker can restore context by moving the welcome to the beginning, placing the audience and activity descriptions next, introducing logistics after the program is understood, and leaving questions at the end.

### Sequence creates expectations

An opening title prepares the audience for a subject. A roadmap or short [[agenda|agenda]] tells listeners what is coming. A middle section gives essential information. A conclusion connects the details and identifies the next step. These patterns reduce the need to explain repeatedly where the talk is heading.

A good sequence does not require a formal agenda in every presentation. A three-slide class update may work with **Question → Answer → Next action**. A longer training presentation may benefit from a short agenda because it covers several stages. The right structure depends on the [[purpose|purpose]] and the [[audience|audience]].

### Order also affects meaning

Consider three statements: “The room is accessible,” “Register online,” and “The program is open to everyone.” If the registration slide comes first, some participants may assume online registration is the only way to attend. If the presentation later explains alternatives, the first impression may have caused unnecessary uncertainty. Sequencing information helps avoid implying requirements that have not been established.

Likewise, a comparison of two options should define the options and the criteria **before** displaying the conclusion. A recommendation presented before its reasons can seem arbitrary. Sometimes opening with a conclusion is appropriate—especially for an executive update—but the audience still needs the supporting logic in a clear order afterward.

> **Practical habit:** Read only the slide titles from top to bottom. Can someone unfamiliar with the topic explain what journey the titles describe? If not, the deck may need a different order or clearer titles.

### A worked example: from shuffled slides to a coherent briefing

An organizer receives eight slides: **How to Join; Thank You; Weekly Activities; Welcome; When and Where; Who It Is For; Cost and Supplies; Questions**. The following order is more coherent:

1. **Welcome to Saturday Reading Hour** — name and purpose.
2. **Who Can Attend** — intended participants.
3. **What Happens Each Week** — the main activities.
4. **When and Where** — logistics.
5. **Cost and Supplies** — practical preparation.
6. **How to Join** — action after the necessary facts.
7. **Questions** — invite clarification.
8. **Thank You and Next Step** — finish with a clear reminder.

Other sensible orders exist. For example, **When and Where** might precede **What Happens Each Week** if time and location are the audience's most urgent concern. The measure is whether the order follows an intelligible principle, not whether every deck has an identical formula.

## 2. How do you plan a clear beginning, middle, and ending?

Before rearranging thumbnails, decide what you want people to know, feel prepared to do, or decide. The most important planning question is not *How many slides should I make?* It is **What should this audience be able to understand or act upon by the end?** That intended result is the [[takeaway|takeaway]].

For the library example, a useful takeaway could be: **“Participants can explain the program and know how to attend a session.”** That sentence guides which slides are necessary. An unrelated slide about the history of public libraries might be interesting but not required for this short orientation.

### Start with four planning questions

| Planning question | What to write down | Reading Hour example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who is the audience? | Prior knowledge, needs, and context | Adults and families new to the program |
| What is the purpose? | Inform, teach, compare, request, or persuade | Introduce participation options |
| What is the main takeaway? | One sentence describing success | Know what happens and how to join |
| What questions must be answered? | The minimum supporting information | Who, what, when, where, cost, accessibility, how |

Do not confuse *topic* with *purpose*. **Reading Hour** is a topic. **Help first-time visitors decide whether and how to attend** is a purpose. A topic can generate hundreds of possible facts; the purpose selects the few needed now.

### Build a three-part outline

A [[beginning-middle-ending|beginning–middle–ending structure]] is a reliable starting point, not an unbreakable rule. The beginning answers why the audience should listen. The middle develops the necessary ideas. The ending restates the main point and tells the audience what happens next.

| Part | Job | Example slides | Audience question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beginning | Establish context and relevance | Welcome; Who Can Attend | “What is this, and is it for me?” |
| Middle | Supply the explanation and practical details | Activities; Schedule; Cost; Access | “How does it work?” |
| Ending | Bring the message together and invite action | How to Join; Questions; Next Step | “What should I do now?” |

A beginning does not have to be dramatic. For a simple workplace report, **“This presentation explains the new supply request procedure”** may be enough. Avoid promising more than the presentation will deliver. If the agenda says **Five steps**, ensure the middle actually contains five distinct steps or update the agenda.

### Turn a list of ideas into a storyboard

A [[storyboard|storyboard]] is a simple plan showing one card or box for each slide. You can use index cards, sticky notes, paper rectangles, or digital thumbnails. Give each card a working title and a one-sentence message. Shuffle the cards until their order is understandable.

Try this miniature storyboard:

| Card | Working slide title | Main point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Welcome | The library offers a weekly reading activity |
| B | Who Can Attend | The event is open to specified participants |
| C | Activities | People listen, read, and discuss together |
| D | Schedule | The session occurs at the stated time and place |
| E | Preparation | Participants know what to bring and any cost |
| F | Participation | Attendees know how to join and get assistance |

You can reorganize a storyboard without risking the actual file. This is especially helpful when working with a partner: both people can discuss the message and order before someone opens a complex editing interface. A good planning card describes what a slide communicates, not merely a visual theme such as **Blue Slide** or **Picture Slide**.

### Distinguish an informative title from a vague label

An effective [[slide-title|slide title]] helps the audience anticipate the point and lets the editor review the deck as an outline. Compare:

| Vague title | More informative title | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Information | Who Can Attend Reading Hour? | Announces the question being answered |
| More Details | Sessions Meet on Saturdays | States the central fact |
| Process | How to Join in Three Steps | Identifies both topic and structure |
| Results | Participants Practiced Reading Together | Communicates the outcome |
| Conclusion | Choose a Session and Ask Questions | Names a next action |

Not every title must state a conclusion. **What Happens During Reading Hour?** is a good question title for a beginner presentation. The goal is clarity. Avoid inventing facts in a title simply to make it sound impressive; the content must support the wording.

### Use one main idea per slide—without turning it into a rigid formula

A useful beginner guideline is [[one-main-idea|one main idea per slide]]. That means viewers should recognize one primary message, even when a slide includes two or three supporting details. It does not require every slide to contain a single sentence or prevent a comparison slide from showing two options.

Suppose one slide attempts to explain who attends, why reading matters, parking instructions, a long list of books, budget totals, and the registration process. The problem is not merely the quantity of words. Those topics answer unrelated audience questions. Dividing them into two or more purposeful slides improves flow. Conversely, three slides containing one trivial sentence apiece may create unnecessary interruptions. Use judgment about what belongs together.

### Plan length around the audience and task

There is no universally correct number of slides per minute. Some slides need only a quick acknowledgement; a process diagram or comparison may require careful explanation. An audience new to the topic may need more time for definitions and questions. Consider:

- the length of the available meeting or lesson;
- the audience's prior experience;
- how much explanation each slide requires;
- demonstrations, discussions, and accessibility accommodations;
- time for questions, pauses, and technical interruptions.

A ten-minute orientation that includes a live demonstration cannot reliably devote every minute to presenting numbered slides. Leave enough time for the non-slide portions of the activity. Use a rough [[timing-plan|timing plan]] rather than treating a slide-count formula as a guarantee.

### Worked example: planning a five-slide deck

A beginner has five blank slides for a short orientation. Their audience has never attended the fictional program. A workable outline is:

1. **What Is Saturday Reading Hour?** — brief purpose.
2. **Who Can Attend?** — intended participants and welcome.
3. **What Happens During a Session?** — three simple activities.
4. **When, Where, and What to Bring** — relevant practical details.
5. **How to Participate and Get Help** — action and contact route.

This is a complete beginning, middle, and ending without separate agenda and thank-you slides. If the presentation expands, the fourth slide may become two: schedule and preparation. If audience questions require a dedicated closing slide, add one. The structure should serve the message rather than a fixed numerical target.

## 3. Which organization pattern fits the information?

A [[organization-pattern|organization pattern]] is the principle used to arrange ideas. Choosing a pattern gives the presentation a predictable logic. Different purposes call for different patterns. A report of events typically uses time order; a set of instructions uses step order; a recommendation may use a comparison or problem–solution structure.

### Common patterns and when to use them

| Pattern | Typical sequence | Useful for | Pitfall |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chronological | Earlier → later → current | Project timeline, history, event recap | Unexplained jumps in time |
| Step by step | Start → action 1 → action 2 → finish | Software instructions, safety processes | Steps shown out of order |
| Problem–solution | Problem → effect → response → next action | Improvement proposal, troubleshooting | Solution before the problem is defined |
| Comparison | Criteria → option A/B → tradeoffs → conclusion | Choosing software or equipment | Inconsistent criteria for each option |
| Question–answer | Audience question → answer → next question | Orientation, FAQ, introductory teaching | Questions that overlap or omit essentials |
| Category or topic | Overview → topic A → topic B → summary | Program overview, department briefing | Topics grouped without a meaningful connection |
| Claim–evidence | Main point → supporting facts → interpretation | Short recommendation or findings briefing | Claims unsupported or evidence unexplained |

You may combine patterns, but avoid switching silently. For example, an orientation might use question–answer for its major sections and step-by-step for a single **How to Join** slide. The presentation's overall pattern remains understandable because the smaller procedure is clearly introduced.

### Chronological order: when did things happen?

A timeline of a school's reading program might use **Planning → First Session → Growth → Next Year**. Dates should be accurate and consistently represented. If one slide jumps from the current year back to the first year, the presenter should explain why. Chronology helps people understand change, but a strict timeline is not always necessary for a practical orientation. The reader may care more about the current schedule than the founding date.

### Step-by-step order: what must happen first?

A brief instruction on registering for a community program could use:

1. Find the program's confirmed information page.
2. Check eligibility and available attendance options.
3. Select the approved registration method, if registration is required.
4. Review the information and follow the stated confirmation process.
5. Keep the correct participation details.

Do not imply that every service uses the same steps. The sequence is an illustrative workflow, not a promise about a real organization's current policy. An instructional deck should clearly distinguish prerequisites from optional actions. If Step 3 depends on Step 2, reversing them makes the directions harder to follow.

### Problem–solution order: why should something change?

A fictional community center has long registration lines. A deck might start with **Visitors Wait Too Long**, then explain **Where the Delay Happens**, propose **A Clearer Check-In Process**, and close with **How We Will Test the Change**. This pattern connects a recommendation to its reason. It should not exaggerate a problem or conceal alternative explanations. When evidence is unavailable, identify observations as examples rather than presenting them as measured facts.

### Comparison order: how do the choices differ?

A presenter must help an audience select between two fictional scheduling options. Establish the evaluation criteria first—ease of attendance, capacity, and needed resources—then explain each option using the same criteria. A comparison becomes confusing if the presenter describes Option A's cost, Option B's convenience, and never compares the same dimensions. A side-by-side slide may be appropriate, provided the text remains readable and the alternatives are correctly labeled.

### Question–answer order: what will beginners ask?

This pattern is particularly useful for the library orientation:

| Slide question | Why the question comes here |
| --- | --- |
| What is Reading Hour? | Establishes context |
| Who can attend? | Establishes relevance |
| What happens during a session? | Makes the experience understandable |
| When and where does it happen? | Enables planning |
| What should participants bring? | Enables preparation |
| How do participants join? | Provides a next action |

A question–answer sequence is not automatically good merely because titles contain question marks. The questions must build toward the intended decision. If the last slide raises a major question that should have been answered before registration, move or revise it.

### Recognize a broken pattern

A presentation often loses coherence when it changes organization patterns without a bridge. For example, **Step 1 → Step 2 → History of the Organization → Step 3** interrupts a procedure. That history may be relevant in a separate introduction, but placing it between dependent instructions makes the procedure harder to follow. Likewise, **Option A's advantages → Option B's disadvantages → Conclusion** is an unbalanced comparison. Correct the order and restore missing criteria before adding visual polish.

> **Pattern check:** Write the structure as a sentence: **“First I will explain ___, then ___, and finally ___.”** If the blanks describe unrelated ideas, revise the plan.

## 4. How do you connect slides into a coherent story?

A sensible list of slide topics is a good start. A coherent presentation goes further: its [[connection|connections]] between ideas are apparent. This does not mean every slide must be entertaining or dramatic. It means listeners can tell what each slide contributes and how it relates to the slides before and after it.

### Build continuity with slide titles

Read a sequence of titles without opening the full slides:

- **Why Reading Hour Exists**
- **Who the Program Serves**
- **What Happens in a Session**
- **When and Where to Attend**
- **How to Participate**

The titles alone communicate a basic journey. Compare a deck titled **Overview**, **Details**, **More Information**, **Other Items**, and **The End**. Those titles make it difficult to find or remember a specific idea. Informative titles support both the audience and the editor reviewing the deck.

### Use transition statements, not just transition effects

A [[verbal-transition|transition statement]] is a short sentence that connects the meaning of the previous slide to the next. For example:

| Previous slide | Next slide | Possible spoken transition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who Can Attend | What Happens Each Week | “Now that we know who the event is for, let's see what a session includes.” |
| What Happens Each Week | When and Where | “Those are the activities; here's how to plan a visit.” |
| When and Where | How to Join | “Once the time works for you, here is the participation process.” |
| Common Questions | Next Step | “With those questions addressed, here is the action to take.” |

The transition statement can be spoken without being printed on a slide. A presenter might also use an informative heading such as **From Schedule to Registration** when the change is especially important. The key is the logical bridge. Adding a software fade between two unrelated slides does not make the content related.

### Group slides into sections when the deck grows

A [[section|section]] is a meaningful group of slides covering one stage of the presentation. A longer orientation might group slides into **Getting Started**, **During a Session**, and **Joining and Support**. Grouping helps the presenter find information, lets collaborators review sections, and gives the audience a clearer roadmap.

| Section | Slides within it | Goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Getting Started | Welcome; Who Can Attend | Establish context |
| During a Session | Activities; Schedule; Preparation | Explain the experience |
| Joining and Support | Registration; Accessibility; Questions; Next Step | Explain action and assistance |

A [[section-divider|section-divider slide]] is a visible slide that marks a change of topic. A [[powerpoint-section|PowerPoint section]] is an editing feature that groups slides inside the application. These are **not the same thing**. PowerPoint allows editors to create named sections. In Google Slides and LibreOffice Impress, a simple divider slide or deliberate title structure can provide similar audience-facing navigation, even if an identical editing-level section control is unavailable.

Do not add a divider before every slide in a five-slide talk; that can make the presentation feel longer without improving understanding. Reserve dividers for substantial changes of topic or a long deck whose audience needs orientation.

### Decide whether an agenda helps

An [[agenda|agenda]] can help when a talk has multiple distinct parts, when participants may need to return to certain topics, or when the audience wants to know how the meeting will proceed. It need not list every slide. A three-item agenda—**Program Overview; What to Expect; How to Participate**—is more useful than a 17-item table of contents for a 17-slide introductory talk.

An agenda creates a commitment. If you change the sequence later, update the agenda and any section numbering. A slide titled **Part 3: Questions** should not come before **Part 2: Participation** without explanation. Consistency between navigation and content is more important than the presence of an agenda itself.

### Remove repetition without losing necessary reminders

[[redundancy|Redundancy]] is information repeated without adding value. In a short presentation, four slides containing the same welcome sentence may crowd out more useful content. However, purposeful repetition can help: a time and location might appear once during logistics and again in a concise final reminder. Ask whether the repetition helps the audience remember or act, or merely fills space.

A slide does not need to be deleted just because two slides use the same word. **Who Can Attend?** and **How to Join** both discuss participation but answer different questions. A flow audit should compare their actual message, not only their labels. Keep separate slides when their roles are distinct.

### Control the pace and avoid sudden changes

[[pacing|Pacing]] is the rate at which information is introduced and discussed. A deck with three slides of simple headings followed by one slide containing an entire policy manual will feel uneven. Break substantial explanations into understandable stages, reserve time for complicated material, and remove details better handled in a handout or follow-up reference.

An [[audience-question|audience question]] is also a pacing signal. If a point is likely to raise confusion, place its explanation or answer nearby. Do not expect the audience to hold a major unanswered question while the deck takes an unrelated detour. Where a question belongs later, say that explicitly: **“We will cover registration after the schedule.”**

### Check the audience's path through the presentation

Use a simple test for each slide: **What does the audience know before this slide? What new idea do they learn on it? What will they need to know for the next slide?** If a new slide uses an unexplained abbreviation, add a definition or introduce the term earlier. If it asks the audience to choose between options before giving the criteria, move the criteria forward. If it includes an important condition discovered only at the end, consider whether that condition belongs earlier.

The best order is not necessarily the order in which the presenter created the slides. People often draft the easiest slide first, then add background information later. The creation sequence reflects the writer's workflow; the final sequence should reflect the audience's learning needs.

## 5. How do you reorder, group, or hide slides in common applications?

Once the outline makes sense, use the presentation editor to place slides in the intended order. This is a file-editing task. The safest sequence is **identify what needs to change → save a working copy if appropriate → move one slide → check its new position → continue → review the entire deck**. Dragging quickly without checking can move the wrong slide, accidentally select an object inside a slide, or disturb an otherwise correct sequence.

The controls described below reflect documented desktop workflows. Microsoft PowerPoint, Google Slides, and LibreOffice Impress change over time, and some web, mobile, organization-managed, or older versions may expose different menus. Look for the application's current help if the instructions on your screen do not match. None of the exercises requires you to buy a product or sign into a new service.

### Understand the main slide-order controls

A [[thumbnail-pane|thumbnail pane]] (also called a filmstrip or Slides pane) is a strip of small slide previews arranged in their current order. Select a thumbnail to choose the slide; drag the thumbnail to a different position to reorder it when supported. A [[slide-sorter|Slide Sorter]] or [[grid-view|grid view]] shows many thumbnails at once. It is particularly helpful when a longer presentation has more slides than fit in the side panel.

An [[outline-view|outline view]] focuses on titles and, in supported applications, the text content of slides rather than their full visual appearance. It helps evaluate the wording and progression of ideas. Do not assume it represents every chart, image, or text box equally. Check the actual slides too.

| Editing operation | What changes | What normally stays the same | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Move a slide | Its position in the sequence | The slide's content and design | Check the insertion position |
| Duplicate a slide | Adds an additional copy | Original remains in its place | Update the copy so it is not an accidental repeat |
| Delete a slide | Removes a slide from the deck | Other slides remain | Recover from Undo or a prior copy if needed |
| Hide or skip a slide | Excludes the slide from ordinary playback | The slide remains stored in the file | Hidden does not mean secret or access-restricted |
| Add a slide | Inserts a new slide | Existing slide content remains | Place it where its content belongs |
| Rename a section | Changes an editor's group label | Grouped slide content remains | Not always visible to the audience |

### PowerPoint on Windows 11: reorganize with thumbnails

1. Open your **own practice copy** of the presentation. If editing an existing important deck, use **Save As** or another suitable version-saving method before major changes.
2. In **Normal** view, locate the slide thumbnails along the left. Select the slide you want to move by clicking its thumbnail rather than a text box on the full-size slide.
3. Drag the selected thumbnail to the intended position. Watch for the location indicator, then release the mouse or touchpad.
4. Inspect the thumbnails immediately above and below the moved slide to confirm the intended order.
5. Repeat one deliberate move at a time. Use **Undo** when necessary, but verify what it actually reversed.
6. For a longer deck, switch to **View → Slide Sorter** to see a broad overview of the sequence, then move slides in that view.
7. Return to Normal view or begin a slide show from the start to confirm the audience-facing sequence, not merely the thumbnail arrangement.

PowerPoint supports selecting multiple slides in its thumbnail areas. In common Windows workflows, **Shift** can select a continuous range and **Ctrl** can assist with selecting separate items, depending on view and context. Observe which thumbnails are selected before moving a group. Do not assume the entire group will remain together until the new order is checked.

**Example:** Slide 6, **Who Can Attend?**, belongs after Slide 1, **Welcome**. Select the thumbnail for **Who Can Attend?** and move it immediately after **Welcome**. Confirm that the next slide is now the program overview or activities, not the closing thank-you. Merely moving the slide does not rewrite its title, update an agenda, or correct references to old slide numbers.

### PowerPoint: use named sections for longer decks

PowerPoint has a built-in [[powerpoint-section|section]] feature for grouping slides in the editor. For a long deck:

1. Identify the first slide of a meaningful group, such as **During a Session**.
2. Select its thumbnail in Normal view.
3. Choose **Home → Section → Add Section** in the supported desktop interface.
4. Name the section so collaborators understand its purpose.
5. Repeat at major boundaries rather than creating many one-slide sections.
6. Collapse or expand sections to review the deck at a higher level.
7. In an appropriate view, move a section by its section heading when needed, and verify that the slides within the group moved as expected.

Microsoft also documents adding sections in **PowerPoint for the web** from Slide Sorter view, using the selected slide's context menu. Commands and layout may vary; check the current version rather than assuming every screenshot is identical. An editor section heading is an organizational convenience, **not automatically a visible title or section-divider slide**. If the audience needs an on-screen divider, create a purposeful slide for that role.

### Google Slides: reorder with the filmstrip or grid

1. Open a practice presentation that you have permission to edit.
2. Locate the thumbnails at the left in the [[filmstrip-view|filmstrip]] view.
3. Click the thumbnail of the slide you want to move. For a continuous group, use the supported multi-selection method; Google's desktop documentation describes using Shift when selecting multiple slides.
4. Drag the selected thumbnail or selected group to its new position.
5. Check the resulting sequence. If the destination is wrong, use Undo and move it again deliberately.
6. Switch to **grid view** using the view control when you want to review many thumbnails at once.
7. Present the deck from the beginning to ensure that its logical flow matches the order of the thumbnails.

Google Slides provides **Duplicate slide** and **Delete** commands for slide thumbnails. It also offers **Skip slide** in a slide's context menu. A skipped slide remains in the presentation document and can be visible to someone with access to that file, even though ordinary playback bypasses it. Do not use Skip slide to protect private or confidential information. If information must not be distributed, remove it from the version you will share and verify the actual shared file.

In this lesson, use a clearly named divider slide if you need an audience-visible section boundary in Google Slides. Do not assume there is an editing-level named-section feature identical to PowerPoint's. The learning goal is to organize the message, whether you accomplish it with labels, divider slides, a short agenda, or consistent headings.

### LibreOffice Impress: use the Slides pane and Slide Sorter

1. Open a practice presentation in [[impress|LibreOffice Impress]].
2. In **Normal** view, find the **Slides pane** containing slide thumbnails. If it is hidden, use the available **View** controls to show it.
3. Select the thumbnail of a slide that needs to move.
4. Drag it to the position where the idea belongs, and verify the order after releasing it.
5. For an overview, select **View → Slide Sorter** or use the Slide Sorter workspace tab, depending on the layout and version.
6. Select one or more slides in Slide Sorter and drag them to a new position.
7. Use **Outline** view when useful to review the textual progression, then return to Normal or Slide Sorter to verify visual content and the final sequence.

Impress also provides slide-level actions such as duplicate, delete, and hide, typically available from slide menus or thumbnail context menus. As in PowerPoint and Google Slides, hiding does not remove the content from the document. For a deck you plan to distribute, remove information that should not be included and inspect the resulting export or copy.

### Compare the main workflows

| Task | Microsoft PowerPoint | Google Slides | LibreOffice Impress |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Move a single slide | Drag a thumbnail in Normal or Slide Sorter | Drag in filmstrip or grid | Drag in Slides pane or Slide Sorter |
| Review many slides | Slide Sorter | Grid view | Slide Sorter |
| Review main text | Outline view where available | Read titles in filmstrip or inspect slides; do not assume identical Outline view | Outline view |
| Group a long deck | Named sections in supported versions | Consistent titles and purposeful divider slides | Consistent titles and purposeful divider slides |
| Temporarily omit slide during playback | Hide Slide | Skip slide | Hide slide |
| Permanently remove from practice copy | Delete Slide | Delete slide | Delete slide |

Menus and functionality may differ across platforms or account types. Do not treat **Hide/Skip** as a form of deletion, and do not assume the software updates an agenda, a visible slide-number reference, or a written description after moving a slide.

### What happens when you duplicate or delete a slide?

[[duplicate-slide|Duplicating]] creates another slide whose content usually begins as a copy of an existing one. It can be convenient when a sequence needs the same general arrangement. For example, a three-step instruction may use three slides with consistent titles and visual placement. However, every duplicate should be revised for its new purpose. Leaving two identical slides by accident breaks the flow.

[[delete-slide|Deleting]] removes a slide. It may be appropriate when the content is obsolete, repeated without purpose, or outside the presentation's objective. But do not delete the only copy of information you may need later without an appropriate backup. In an organization-managed or shared deck, consider whether the slide is required for compliance, review, or the work of another collaborator. Confirm permissions before removing it.

### Hidden, skipped, or backup slides are not private

[[hide-slide|Hiding]] or [[skip-slide|skipping]] changes what the audience ordinarily sees during playback. It does not guarantee that viewers with access to the original file cannot inspect that slide. A hidden slide may still be included when the editable deck is shared, depending on software and file handling. It may also appear when the file is edited later. Keep unpublished private details, passwords, account numbers, and confidential records out of distributed practice files entirely.

Some advanced presenters keep optional [[backup-slide|backup slides]] with extra data after the formal ending, which they may navigate to during questions. A beginner does not need this technique to complete the lesson. If you use it in authorized work, be clear about what belongs in the ordinary presentation sequence, which slides are optional, and what the final distribution format reveals.

### Choosing a safe operation

A slide titled **Parking Instructions** is useful but currently appears before **Welcome**. **Move** it. A slide titled **Parking Instructions** appears twice with exactly the same material. **Review the duplicates**; remove the accidental one or revise it if each has a distinct purpose. A slide is no longer relevant to the audience but might be needed in a later edit. Consider **temporarily skipping** it within a private working file, then decide what belongs in the final shared version. A slide contains private participant data that must not be distributed. **Remove the sensitive material from the distributable copy** rather than merely hiding the slide.

## 6. How do you test and improve slide flow?

A deck can look orderly in the editor but still confuse an audience. A [[flow-review|flow review]] tests the complete sequence before presenting or distributing it. The simplest review is to run the slides in the intended order while pretending you are seeing them for the first time. Read the titles, identify the main point of each slide, and ask whether the next slide feels logically connected.

### Perform a five-pass review

| Review pass | Question to ask | Corrective action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Purpose | Does every slide serve the stated outcome? | Remove or reposition irrelevant material |
| 2. Sequence | Does the beginning provide context before details? | Move slides; define unfamiliar terms earlier |
| 3. Connections | Can the audience understand each change of topic? | Add a verbal bridge or clearer title |
| 4. Completeness | Are essential questions answered before the next action? | Add missing explanation or consolidate slides |
| 5. Delivery | Does the deck play through the intended order? | Test from the beginning and correct skipped or duplicated slides |

This is an editorial review as well as a software review. An editor can report that there are eight slides and that all thumbnails load, but those facts do not prove that the presentation answers its central question. The content must be examined for meaning and relevance.

### Use the title-only test

Cover or ignore the body content and read each [[slide-title|slide title]] in order. Can the titles tell a short story? If one title says **How to Join**, the next says **The Founder in 1998**, and the following says **Step Two**, the interruption should be examined. Either the historical material belongs in a different position, or the process needs a clearer introduction.

For a practical orientation, titles should give novices enough context to navigate. **Eligibility**, **Schedule**, and **Cost** may be adequate if the subject has just been introduced. **Additional Notes** and **Other Things** usually reveal less. Titles should describe the slide truthfully rather than oversell its content.

### Identify missing prerequisites and unsupported conclusions

A [[prerequisite|prerequisite]] is information an audience needs before it can interpret a later idea. A slide saying **Choose the Best Option** is premature if the preceding slides never defined the options or how they are evaluated. A slide saying **Submit Your Form** may need an earlier explanation of who should submit, whether alternatives exist, and where official instructions are found.

Similarly, a slide that claims **This Program Doubled Participation** needs evidence if it is presented as a real outcome. Flow cannot substitute for factual accuracy. If you are using a fictional scenario for practice, label the numbers as fictional. A slide with an impressive result may be more persuasive when it follows the method and supporting information, or an executive deck may lead with the result and immediately explain the evidence afterward. In both arrangements, the connection must be explicit.

### Check for loops and repeated explanations

An [[unnecessary-loop|unnecessary loop]] occurs when the presentation repeatedly returns to a point without adding context. Examples include **Schedule → Registration → Schedule → Costs → Registration** when no new detail is supplied. Some repetitions are intentional: a final reminder about time and location may help people act. Ask whether each return serves a specific purpose. If not, consolidate the material and revise the sequence.

### Confirm that slide numbers and navigation still match

After moving slides, review any visible [[slide-number|slide numbers]], agenda references, internal [[hyperlink|hyperlinks]], instructions such as **“See Slide 7”**, section labels, or printed materials that refer to the old order. Some automatic slide-numbering features update as the software changes the deck, but manually typed numbers and cross-references may not. Never assume every reference recalculates correctly.

If you have a separate handout listing **Slide 4: Cost** and move Cost to Slide 6, that handout may now be wrong. Update it or avoid unnecessary references to exact slide numbers. In a collaboration setting, advise reviewers that the sequence changed so comments and discussion refer to the correct content.

### Review visual and assistive-technology reading order

The sequence of slides is only one kind of [[reading-order|reading order]]. Within an individual slide, screen readers and other assistive tools may encounter elements in a different order from the visual layout. A slide can be physically fifth in the presentation yet have a confusing internal reading order. Use the application's accessible layout and reading-order or accessibility inspection features where available.

A clear flow supports accessibility when it includes:

- meaningful slide titles that identify the topic;
- definitions before specialized terms are relied upon;
- one recognizable primary message per slide;
- sufficient time to understand complex content;
- explicit spoken or written explanations for changes of topic;
- access to information that is not conveyed only through an animation or image;
- a reliable order for elements within each slide.

Do not assume that a particular screen reader always announces content exactly as the presenter expects. Use the relevant application's accessibility checker and test with the available assistive technology when appropriate. Avoid requiring an audience to understand meaning solely from color, direction, flashing effects, or a fast sequence of timed slides.

### Test from the first slide—not only from the editing view

A [[slide-show|slide show]] is the presentation's playback mode. For a final flow check, start at the beginning, advance one slide at a time, and check what actually appears. This reveals accidentally hidden or skipped slides, repeated content, unexpected transitions, and misplaced closing slides. A slide that looks correct in the editor may still fail during playback if media or navigation links behave unexpectedly. Testing the first, middle, and last slides alone is not a substitute for a complete run-through.

Ask a peer to describe the main point after each major section. If a beginner cannot explain why a slide followed the previous one, revise the arrangement or the bridge. The peer's confusion is useful diagnostic evidence, not proof that the audience is inattentive. It may reveal missing context or an unfamiliar term.

### Allow sensible pacing, breaks, and questions

Some audiences need pauses to read, ask questions, follow instructions, or use assistive technologies. A presentation that races through a crowded deck is harder to follow even when the slide titles are well ordered. If a demonstration takes several minutes, include that time in the overall plan. Avoid relying on an automatic advance timer for material that requires discussion unless the timing has been tested and accommodations are available.

A [[question-break|question break]] may belong after a major section or near the end. Its position is an editorial choice. In a brief classroom exercise, it may be enough for the presenter to invite questions verbally. In a long workshop, a visible **Questions Before We Continue?** slide can create a useful pause. Match the choice to the audience, duration, and task.

### Troubleshoot common reorganization problems

| Symptom | Likely cause | Safe first response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slide appears in the wrong place | Dragged to the wrong insertion point | Inspect thumbnails, Undo if appropriate, move deliberately |
| Two identical slides appear | Accidental duplication | Identify the original and remove or revise the extra copy |
| Needed information disappears during playback | Slide hidden or skipped | Check the slide's playback setting and restore it if appropriate |
| Agenda names the wrong section order | Deck changed after agenda was written | Revise the agenda and section labels |
| Slide says “See Slide 4” but content moved | Manually written cross-reference is outdated | Replace or update the cross-reference |
| An image-heavy slide breaks the explanation | Important context exists only in a visual | Add a clear explanation and check accessible alternatives |
| Opening is too long | Too much background before the main topic | Move optional history to later or to a handout |
| Presentation ends abruptly | No conclusion or next action | Add a clear summary or action statement |
| Collaborator's sequence differs from yours | Copies or versions are out of sync | Confirm which editable file is authoritative |

### Save, verify, and distribute only an appropriate version

Reorganizing slides changes the editable file. Save your work using the application's expected method, then verify the saved deck retains the correct sequence by reopening it when practical. A local file may require a deliberate **Save** operation; a cloud service may save changes automatically but still require checking sync or access status. Avoid claiming a cloud save has completed merely because you moved a slide.

Before sharing, inspect the actual version that recipients will receive. A document may still contain hidden slides, presenter notes, embedded comments, old filenames, or other details that are not obvious during an ordinary slide show. The topic of exporting and sharing is addressed in Lesson 9, but it is already important to know that **a presentation's playback view is not the same as its complete editable contents**.

### Worked example: repair a confused six-slide training deck

A fictional workplace presentation has this order: **Submit the Request; Welcome; Approved Items; Thank You; How Requests Are Reviewed; Where to Get Help**. The intended outcome is that staff know how to submit a supply request.

A clearer order is:

1. **Supply Requests: What This Training Covers**
2. **Which Items Can Be Requested**
3. **How to Submit a Request**
4. **What Happens After Submission**
5. **Where to Get Help**
6. **Summary and Next Step**

This version establishes context, explains eligibility before action, describes the steps, and ends with support. It does not guarantee that the organization's procedure is accurate; the presenter must check the actual workplace policy. **Good flow helps explain correct facts, but it does not make invented or outdated instructions trustworthy.**

## 7. Guided practice: build and revise a clear sequence

These six activities use fictional material. You can complete each with index cards, written lists, or a presentation application. The objective is **to make a defensible ordering decision and explain it**, not to memorize a particular keyboard shortcut. If a computer is available, work in a practice file you own. Do not change someone else's real presentation or use actual private student, employee, or customer information.

### Activity 1: restore the order of an orientation deck

**Goal:** Identify the main message and place eight shuffled slides into a sensible order.

**Scenario:** The fictional Reading Hour coordinator receives these titles in the order listed:

| Original position | Slide title |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | How to Join |
| 2 | Final Reminder |
| 3 | Weekly Activities |
| 4 | Welcome to Reading Hour |
| 5 | When and Where |
| 6 | Who Can Attend |
| 7 | Cost and Supplies |
| 8 | Questions |

**Instructions:**

1. State in one sentence what a first-time attendee should know after seeing the deck.
2. Place the slide titles in a new numbered sequence. On paper, write the sequence; in software, drag the thumbnails into place.
3. Divide the new sequence into beginning, middle, and ending.
4. Explain why **How to Join** belongs where you placed it.
5. Identify two other orders that would make the audience's job unnecessarily difficult.
6. Read your new slide titles aloud without the body content. Decide whether the path is understandable.

**Success criterion:** Someone unfamiliar with the program can identify what it is, whether it is relevant, what happens, and how to participate in that order or another clearly justified progression.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answer
**One possible takeaway:** “A first-time visitor can explain Reading Hour and understand the steps for attending.”

A sensible eight-slide order is **Welcome to Reading Hour → Who Can Attend → Weekly Activities → When and Where → Cost and Supplies → How to Join → Questions → Final Reminder**. The first two establish context; the next three explain the experience and logistics; the last three support action and closure.

**How to Join** belongs near the end because the audience should first understand the program, basic eligibility, schedule, and preparation. Starting with **Final Reminder** would be confusing because there is not yet anything to remember. Placing **Questions** before the welcome would also break the intended introduction. An alternative order might put **When and Where** before **Weekly Activities** when attendees are chiefly concerned about schedules; this can be equally coherent if the speaker provides a logical bridge.
:::

### Activity 2: choose the right organization pattern

**Goal:** Match a presentation's objective to an appropriate organizational pattern.

**Instructions:** For each scenario, name a primary pattern from Section 3 and write three slide titles in the intended order. Some scenarios permit more than one sound answer; explain your choice.

| Scenario | Audience's task | Candidate ideas |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. A new club member learns to submit an event request | Follow a procedure | Prepare details; submit form; receive confirmation |
| B. A manager reports a project's last three milestones | Understand what changed over time | First pilot; revised process; current status |
| C. A community group considers two meeting times | Compare options | Attendance needs; Option A; Option B; decision |
| D. A workshop addresses repeated registration delays | Understand a proposed improvement | Problem; cause; new workflow; evaluation |
| E. First-time visitors ask about a recreation event | Decide whether to attend | What; who; when; where; how |

**Extension:** Write one sentence bridging the second and third slide in Scenario D. Is an elaborate slide transition animation necessary to make that bridge logical?

:::answer Show Activity 2 model answer
**A. Step-by-step:** **Prepare the Request → Submit It → Check the Confirmation**. The order reflects dependencies.

**B. Chronological:** **First Pilot → Process Revision → Current Status**. The timeline helps listeners recognize development.

**C. Comparison:** **What Matters to Attendees → How the Two Times Compare → Recommended Time and Rationale**. Keep evaluation criteria consistent.

**D. Problem–solution:** **Where Delays Occur → Proposed Check-In Method → How We Will Test It**. A possible spoken bridge is **“The delays arise at the first check-in step, so the proposal starts there.”** A visual transition effect is not needed to supply this reasoning.

**E. Question–answer:** **What Is the Event? → Who Can Attend? → When, Where, and How to Join?**. A longer deck can give each practical question its own slide.

Other answers are acceptable when they respect prerequisites and answer the audience's central question.
:::

### Activity 3: find repetition, gaps, and misplaced details

**Goal:** Diagnose a deck before editing it.

A fictional six-slide talk about safe computer lab use has the following titles and messages:

| Slide | Title | Main message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Welcome | Learn how the computer lab works |
| 2 | Who May Use the Lab | Check the center's participation rules |
| 3 | Rules | Do not share account passwords |
| 4 | Password Reminder | Do not share account passwords |
| 5 | Sign Out | Sign out of personal accounts when finished |
| 6 | Thank You | Ask staff for help if needed |

**Instructions:**

1. Identify one pair of slides that likely repeats information.
2. Decide whether they should be combined or whether one should gain a distinct purpose.
3. Identify one useful missing question a first-time computer lab visitor might ask.
4. Propose a title and position for a slide that answers that question. Do not invent the lab's actual rules.
5. Explain how you would verify rules before presenting them as fact.
6. Read the resulting titles as a coherent sequence and defend your changes.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model answer
**Likely redundancy:** **Rules** and **Password Reminder** contain the same instruction. They can be combined into **Protect Your Account**, unless **Password Reminder** is revised to cover a distinct concept such as identifying legitimate support without giving away passwords.

A reasonable missing question is **“How do I request a computer or get help?”** A slide such as **Check In and Request Assistance** could follow **Who May Use the Lab**. The resulting deck might be **Welcome → Who May Use the Lab → Check In and Get Help → Protect Your Account → Sign Out → Thank You and Questions**. The local check-in rules must be verified from the center's actual published or staff-approved procedures. A practice scenario does not establish real policies.

A different answer—such as adding **What Equipment Is Available?**—may also be reasonable if it supports the defined purpose and the content is verified.
:::

### Activity 4: create clear sections and transitions

**Goal:** Organize a longer fictional orientation into three meaningful groups and build logical bridges.

Use these nine slides, which are already in a plausible order: **Welcome; Who Can Attend; Program Goals; Weekly Activities; What to Bring; Schedule and Location; Registration; Accessibility and Assistance; Questions and Next Steps**.

**Instructions:**

1. Divide the nine slides into three groups of your choosing.
2. Give each group a concise [[section-name|section name]].
3. Decide whether a visible section-divider slide is useful for this short deck. Explain why.
4. Write a sentence connecting the last slide of the first section to the first slide of the second section.
5. Write a second sentence connecting the middle section to the final section.
6. If using PowerPoint, describe where you would add named sections in the editor. If using Google Slides or Impress, explain a comparable organizational approach without assuming an identical feature.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answer
**Example grouping:** **Understanding the Program** includes Welcome, Who Can Attend, and Program Goals. **What to Expect** includes Weekly Activities, What to Bring, and Schedule and Location. **Joining and Getting Help** includes Registration, Accessibility and Assistance, and Questions and Next Steps.

**Bridge 1:** “We have explained who the program serves and what it aims to do; now let's look at what attending a session involves.”

**Bridge 2:** “With the schedule and preparation covered, the remaining question is how to take part and where to request help.”

For nine simple slides, visible section dividers may be unnecessary because descriptive slide titles and spoken bridges provide orientation. In a longer deck, dividers might be useful. PowerPoint can group slides using editor sections beginning at selected slides. Google Slides and Impress can use clear headings, a compact agenda, and optional divider slides to communicate the same conceptual groups to the audience.
:::

### Activity 5: make safe software editing decisions

**Goal:** Choose an editing operation that fixes a problem without unnecessarily losing material.

Imagine you have an editable ten-slide practice deck. For each situation, select **move, add, duplicate, revise, hide/skip, delete, or verify before changing**. More than one action may be necessary.

| Situation | Your decision |
| --- | --- |
| A. The welcome slide is after the conclusion | ? |
| B. Two slides accidentally repeat the same text | ? |
| C. You need three similar step slides | ? |
| D. The editor has a confidential contact list | ? |
| E. An optional advanced slide is not needed for the short talk | ? |
| F. The deck says “See Slide 8,” but that slide was moved | ? |
| G. Another person's shared presentation looks incorrectly ordered | ? |

**Instructions:** State the safest operation for each case. For A, describe exactly how you would use thumbnails in one presentation editor. For D, explain why hiding or skipping the slide is insufficient if the file will be shared. For G, describe a permission check before making changes. End by listing three ways you would verify that the saved deck still works.

:::answer Show Activity 5 model answer
**A. Move** the welcome slide to the beginning and test playback.

**B. Review and revise or delete** the redundant slide; keep any unique information.

**C. Duplicate** a suitable layout if it helps consistency, then change each slide's title and content to its own step.

**D. Remove** the confidential information from the distributable copy and verify the actual shared file. Hide/Skip does not ensure privacy.

**E. Consider hide/skip** only within a controlled practice or presenter copy, or remove it from a version intended for a simpler audience; inspect what the final file contains.

**F. Revise** the outdated cross-reference after confirming the intended destination. A manually typed slide number may not update automatically.

**G. Verify** that you have editing authority and understand the agreed presentation purpose before modifying a shared deck. Communicate significant reordering when appropriate.

For A, one valid PowerPoint workflow is to open a practice copy, select the welcome thumbnail in Normal view, drag it to the first position, check the surrounding slides, save, and run from the beginning. Three verification actions are to read titles in order, test the slide show including skipped-slide status, and reopen the saved version to confirm the sequence.
:::

### Activity 6: complete a six-slide organizing project

**Goal:** Plan, create or rearrange, review, and save a complete fictional presentation.

**Scenario:** A local learning center wants to introduce a new **Community Technology Practice Hour** to first-time visitors. All details are hypothetical. The purpose is to let participants understand the activity and the next step for attending. You are given the content cards below in an intentionally mixed order.

| Card | Draft message | Suggested content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | How to join | Ask the center for its participation procedure |
| B | What to bring | Bring a question and, when available, a practice device |
| C | Welcome | A supervised session for practicing basic computer tasks |
| D | What participants practice | Files, browsing, and common software tasks |
| E | Who it is for | People learning or reviewing basic digital skills |
| F | Final reminder | Confirm details through the center before attending |

**Instructions:**

1. Write one clear sentence stating what a first-time visitor should understand by the end of this deck.
2. Order all six cards into a beginning, middle, and ending. You may rename titles to improve clarity, but do not turn invented details into real announcements.
3. Give each slide one main idea and no more supporting detail than its role requires.
4. Add two verbal transitions between adjacent slides that would otherwise feel disconnected.
5. Create the deck in a presentation editor if available. Otherwise, draw six numbered rectangles on paper and write the titles and messages inside them.
6. Deliberately move one middle slide to the wrong position, observe why the explanation becomes worse, and then return it to the intended position. In software, use Undo or a careful move; on paper, rearrange the cards.
7. Read only the final titles aloud. Then describe the complete sequence as if you were a new participant.
8. Review privacy and accessibility: fictional details only, meaningful titles, correct sequence, and no critical meaning conveyed solely by appearance.
9. If using software, save the editable file as a clearly named practice deck, such as `community-technology-practice-hour-practice.pptx` or an equivalent supported format. Verify where the file is stored and whether you can reopen it.
10. Explain in two sentences why the final order serves the intended audience better than the mixed card order.

**Self-check:** Can a beginner answer *What is it? Who is it for? What happens? What should I prepare? How do I join? What should I do next?* without needing to jump backward through the slides? If not, revise before marking the exercise complete.

:::answer Show Activity 6 model answer
**Possible takeaway:** “Visitors can describe Community Technology Practice Hour and know how to confirm participation details.”

A coherent six-slide order is **C → E → D → B → A → F**:

1. **Welcome to Community Technology Practice Hour** — describe the practice session.
2. **Who Can Participate?** — describe the intended learners.
3. **What Will We Practice?** — introduce the example computer tasks.
4. **How Should You Prepare?** — explain the fictional preparation guidance.
5. **How Do You Join?** — direct visitors to confirm the center's actual participation procedure.
6. **Your Next Step** — remind visitors to verify dates, access arrangements, and requirements with the center.

**Bridge 1:** “Now that you know who the session is for, here is the kind of computer practice it may include.”

**Bridge 2:** “After reviewing what to bring, the next step is to check how participation works.”

An intentionally poor version places **How Do You Join?** before the welcome, because the audience does not yet know what the program is. Moving it near the end produces a more natural action sequence. In the final review, keep the fictional example clearly separate from any real center's policies. A successful deck uses meaningful titles, contains no private records, and can be found and reopened if created digitally.

There is no single mandatory order for every informative presentation. Alternative sequences may pass if the learner can explain the progression, preserve necessary prerequisites, and support the intended outcome.
:::

### Practice completion checklist

Use these questions after completing any of the activities:

- Can I state the audience and intended outcome in one sentence?
- Does the opening establish context before details?
- Can I explain the main organizational pattern?
- Does each slide contribute a distinct and relevant idea?
- Are transitions between sections understandable?
- Are any slides repeated, missing, misplaced, or accidentally skipped?
- Are agenda items and slide references consistent with the new sequence?
- Is the file safe to save or share, with no unintended private information?
- Did I check the final order in playback or through the completed paper storyboard?

If an answer is **no**, revise that item and check again. The aim is dependable organization, not perfection on the first attempt.

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

A presentation's sequence often looks simple after someone has organized it. The following misconceptions can cause confusion during planning, editing, or delivery. Review each statement and the more accurate practice.

| Misconception | Better understanding |
| --- | --- |
| “If the slides are numbered, the flow is good.” | Numbers show physical order, not whether the ideas connect. |
| “A dramatic transition makes unrelated slides fit.” | Visual effects do not replace explanation or editorial structure. |
| “Every presentation must have an agenda.” | A short deck may be clearer without a separate agenda slide. |
| “The first slide I created must be the opening.” | Drafting order and audience-facing order are different. |
| “One idea per slide means only one sentence.” | A slide may have supporting details as long as it communicates one primary point. |
| “Every topic needs a divider slide.” | Dividers are most useful at meaningful changes in longer decks. |
| “A skipped slide has been deleted.” | It remains in the editable file and may still be inspectable. |
| “Hiding a slide protects private information.” | Hidden content is not a privacy or access-control measure. |
| “Duplicating makes a new step automatically.” | A copy must be given a distinct title and appropriate content. |
| “Moving a slide updates every reference to it.” | Manually typed slide numbers, agendas, and links may need review. |
| “All three applications organize sections in the same way.” | Editing-level section tools vary; clear titles and dividers remain broadly useful. |
| “More slides necessarily make a presentation more complete.” | Extra slides can distract if they do not advance the purpose. |
| “Shorter is always better.” | Removing essential explanations can leave a beginner confused. |
| “A conclusion must say only ‘Thank you.’” | An effective conclusion often restates the takeaway and a next action. |
| “Good flow means nobody will ask questions.” | Questions may signal engagement, needed clarification, or a useful pause. |
| “The slide order is the same as the screen-reader reading order.” | Slide sequence and object reading order are separate accessibility concerns. |

### Why a logical outline should come before decoration

Imagine you have spent an hour adjusting colors, borders, and animations, but the presentation still answers **How do I register?** before **What is the program?** The decorative work did not solve the underlying communication problem. Organize the ideas first, then refine the visual design. This prevents repeated work and helps identify whether new content is actually necessary.

### Why “delete everything that is repeated” is also a mistake

Some repetition has a useful role. A reminder of attendance time at the end can reinforce the next action. Repeating an essential safety instruction in the correct procedural context can help learners avoid errors. The distinction is whether the repeated material serves a new function. An identical slide inserted accidentally should be reviewed; a purposeful reminder may be retained.

### Why moving slides is not the end of the process

After a structural revision, test the entire deck, not merely the affected thumbnails. New relationships may require a different opening sentence or a revised conclusion. A former introduction may now contain “as discussed earlier,” even though it has become Slide 1. A previous section ending may promise a topic that no longer comes next. Correct those statements so the spoken explanation and the visible sequence match.

## 11. MGU Library connections and next steps

### Key takeaways

A presentation is a guided sequence of ideas. Clear flow begins with knowing the audience and intended takeaway. Arrange the introduction before unfamiliar details, make related points follow each other, and close with an appropriate summary or action. Use slide titles as a compact outline; choose a pattern that matches the task; and provide short bridges when the subject changes.

PowerPoint, Google Slides, and LibreOffice Impress can move and manage slides, but they do not automatically decide the best argument or teaching sequence. **Move** a slide to change its position; **duplicate** to create a new copy; **delete** to remove it; and **hide or skip** only when you want the slide retained in the editable file but not normally shown during playback. Hiding does not provide privacy.

Finally, run the presentation in its intended order, check titles and supporting material, review accessibility, confirm navigation references, and verify the saved version. A deck is ready to use when a reasonable new viewer can follow the explanation and identify the intended next step without needing the presenter to repair the order on the fly.

### Readiness check

You are prepared for the next lesson if you can explain slide flow in one sentence, choose a suitable organization pattern, put six fictional slide cards in a meaningful sequence, demonstrate or describe how to move a thumbnail, distinguish hiding from deletion, and perform a complete review of your resulting deck. You should also be able to explain why a hidden slide is not a secure location for sensitive information.
