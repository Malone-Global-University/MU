## Lesson 6: Basic Design Rules

Imagine a community center preparing a five-slide presentation about its fictional Saturday Digital Skills Workshop. The organizer has useful information, but the first slide contains three different typefaces, the schedule is printed in pale yellow on a white background, the photographs cover part of the instructions, and the final slide is so crowded that no one knows where to look. The presentation might be factually correct and logically ordered, yet the audience may struggle to understand it. The difficulty is not a shortage of decorative effects. It is a shortage of consistent [[design|design]] decisions.

In a slide presentation, design means arranging and presenting information so people can **find, read, understand, and remember it**. Basic design rules are practical guidelines for deciding what deserves attention, how to make text legible, when to use color and images, and how to place objects without creating confusion. They are not rules requiring every slide to look identical or every speaker to have artistic training. They help an ordinary presenter make a deck that serves its audience.

Lessons 1–5 explained presentations, creating slides, layouts and themes, adding content, and slide flow. This lesson concentrates on **the design of individual slides and consistency across a deck**. Later lessons cover speaker notes, live delivery, and exporting or sharing. You can practice every principle in this lesson on paper. If you have access to Microsoft PowerPoint, Google Slides, or LibreOffice Impress, you can apply the same ideas in a real slide editor. Feature labels may vary by version and device.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 100–140 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Presentation Skills Lessons 1–5 recommended · **Practice equipment:** Paper and pencil or an available slide editor; internet optional · **Safety:** Use only fictional content in exercises; do not upload confidential material or reuse unlicensed images; save a separate editable copy before a major redesign.

> **Essential idea:** **Decide what matters → make it readable → arrange it clearly → repeat the same visual conventions → test it as the audience will see it.** A good design directs attention to meaning instead of competing with it.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain what slide design does and distinguish visual appeal from effective communication.
2. Identify the main message and create [[visual-hierarchy|visual hierarchy]] with titles, text size, position, and emphasis.
3. Use readable [[typography|typography]], reasonable text lengths, and plain-language wording.
4. Explain [[contrast|contrast]] and check whether text stands out clearly from its background.
5. Apply [[alignment|alignment]], [[spacing|spacing]], [[margin|margins]], [[proximity|proximity]], and [[negative-space|negative space]] to organize objects.
6. Recognize when color, images, icons, charts, and labels help or hinder comprehension.
7. Keep fonts, colors, slide structures, and other recurring choices consistent without making every slide identical.
8. Distinguish design guidelines and starting estimates from rigid universal requirements.
9. Describe accessibility checks for reading order, alternative text, color-independent meaning, and projected readability.
10. Make basic design adjustments in PowerPoint, Google Slides, and LibreOffice Impress.
11. Diagnose and improve a crowded, low-contrast, inconsistent fictional presentation.

## 1. What makes slide design effective?

[[slide-design|Slide design]] is the deliberate arrangement of words, images, shapes, colors, and other visual elements on a slide. It supports a [[communication-goal|communication goal]]: helping an intended audience understand something, decide something, or take an appropriate next step. Attractive details can be useful, but decoration alone is not the goal. An audience should not have to work harder because the slides look elaborate.

Suppose a workshop slide must answer the question **When does the class begin?** A plain, prominent statement such as **Saturday, 10:00 a.m.** communicates the answer immediately. A crowded slide with seven text boxes, tiny schedule details, a moving illustration, and the same start time buried near the bottom may conceal that answer. Both slides contain the information; only one makes the information easy to find.

A practical test is to ask, **What should someone notice first, second, and third?** If the order is unclear, the design is not yet doing its job. Effective design establishes priority before decoration.

### A slide is not a printed page

A slide is commonly viewed for a limited time on a projection screen, shared monitor, video call, or personal device. A speaker may be explaining the slide while the audience is reading it. That setting differs from a document designed for independent, detailed reading. A document can contain many paragraphs that a reader examines at their own pace. A projected slide usually needs a more concentrated message.

Do not assume a full paragraph is forbidden. Some training, accessibility, reference, and self-paced uses legitimately require more written detail. Rather, match the amount of on-screen text to the way the material will be used. If participants need to study detailed policy language, consider a separate handout or an accessible document instead of shrinking everything until it fits.

| Communication need | Sensible slide approach | Possible supporting material |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Announce a workshop time | One prominent date and time | Registration details in a handout |
| Explain three steps | Three numbered steps in sequence | Detailed instructions in a separate guide |
| Compare two options | Two clearly labeled groups with matching criteria | Complete comparison notes |
| Introduce a complex procedure | A high-level process overview | Accessible written procedure |
| Present a data finding | One chart with an explanatory takeaway | Source table and methodology |
| Share a long quotation | A short relevant excerpt when permitted | Original document with proper attribution |

### Begin with the audience and message

Before changing colors, identify the [[audience|audience]], the purpose, and the [[main-message|main message]]. A beginners' workshop may need everyday language and clear procedural steps; a specialist audience may recognize a technical label. An audience watching from the back of a room may need larger text than one using individual laptops. A recorded course might need captions and an accessible supplementary description. Good design adjusts to those conditions instead of using a single decorative template for every task.

Use a simple planning sentence: **After this slide, viewers should know ______.** The blank can hold an answer, a comparison, or a next action. If you cannot complete the sentence, consider removing or splitting material until the purpose is clear.

For example, instead of titling a slide **Information**, use **The workshop begins at 10:00 a.m.** A descriptive title tells the audience what the slide is about; a [[takeaway-title|takeaway title]] goes further by stating its principal conclusion. Not every slide needs a conclusion-shaped title—a slide that introduces a question or group of tasks may work better with a descriptive heading—but every slide should have an identifiable purpose.

### The five design questions

Review any slide by asking these questions in order:

1. **Message:** What is the single most important idea or action?
2. **Readability:** Can an ordinary viewer read and distinguish the essential content?
3. **Organization:** Are the elements arranged so their relationships make sense?
4. **Consistency:** Does this slide belong visually with the rest of the presentation?
5. **Access:** Can people with different visual, hearing, cognitive, and device needs obtain the same core information?

A slide can succeed in four areas and still fail in the fifth. A clean, minimal slide with pale-gray text may be inaccessible. A high-contrast slide with random placement may be hard to follow. A visually consistent deck may repeat the same confusing design over many slides. Review each dimension rather than awarding a slide a single vague label such as *professional*.

### Design principles are not universal measurements

Recommendations such as “use few words,” “leave space around objects,” or “prefer larger fonts” are **starting points**, not mechanical laws. A title size suited to one screen may be unreadable from the back of another room. A high-quality chart may require more labels than a title slide. A theme may provide attractive combinations, but you must still verify whether the resulting content is legible.

In this lesson, example sizes and sample layouts are **illustrative**, not guarantees of accessibility or official requirements. Test the actual environment whenever possible. If a viewer cannot read the slide, the fact that it followed a popular numerical rule does not solve the problem.

### Worked example: choose what deserves attention

A fictional community center slide contains the following five lines:

- Workshop Registration and Participant Information
- Our Saturday session is designed to help people practice computer basics with staff support.
- Date: Saturday, April 18
- Time: 10:00 a.m.–11:30 a.m.
- Register at the front desk before Thursday.

For a slide whose purpose is to announce attendance details, the title might become **Join Saturday's Digital Skills Workshop**, with **Saturday, April 18 · 10:00–11:30 a.m.** set prominently below it. The brief registration instruction follows in a smaller but still readable line. The longer background sentence can be spoken, moved to a program-overview slide, or included in a handout if useful. The facts remain intact; their [[visual-priority|visual priority]] becomes clearer.

> **Remember:** A design decision is successful when the audience can identify the relevant information more readily—not merely when the slide looks different.

## 2. How do you create hierarchy and readable text?

[[visual-hierarchy|Visual hierarchy]] is the arrangement of information so viewers recognize its relative importance. Usually, the audience first encounters a title or main number, then a supporting image or grouped statement, and finally optional detail. Hierarchy can be established through size, placement, weight, contrast, and spacing. Using all available effects at once can make everything compete for attention.

### Give the slide a meaningful title

A [[slide-title|slide title]] names or summarizes the topic. A clear title often uses direct words rather than a vague label. **How to Register** is more useful than **Details**. **Three Supplies to Bring** is more useful than **Additional Information**. Titles can also communicate a finding, such as **Most attendees prefer morning sessions**, but such claims must be supported by actual evidence. Never invent a statistic merely to make a title sound decisive.

Some layouts contain a built-in [[title-placeholder|title placeholder]]. Using the intended title field can preserve consistent formatting and improve navigation by assistive technology. A title manually drawn as an unrelated decorative shape may look correct but be less well structured. If a slide needs a visually hidden title for a specialized purpose, use the application's supported accessible method rather than deleting meaningful navigation information.

### Use type size and weight with a purpose

[[typography|Typography]] refers to choices about text appearance and arrangement: [[typeface|typeface]], [[font-size|size]], [[font-weight|weight]], line spacing, capitalization, and more. A beginner often needs just one readable typeface family, a consistent heading style, and a consistent body-text style. Bold can emphasize a small number of important words. Making an entire slide bold, underlined, italicized, and uppercase removes the distinction between primary and secondary content.

For an ordinary projected deck, you might **begin experimenting** with titles around 28–36 points and body text around 20–28 points, then adjust after testing the room and display. These are common planning estimates, not universal minimums. A self-paced slide viewed close up, a very large projected screen, or a detailed reference deck may need different choices. Prioritize the real viewing test over any fixed number.

| Text role | Example | Design intention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slide title | Workshop registration closes Thursday | Establish the primary subject or finding |
| Key figure | 10:00 a.m. | Make the required fact easy to locate |
| Supporting statement | Arrive ten minutes early | Give context after the key fact |
| Short list | Bring a notebook; borrow a device on site | Present actions in an easy-to-scan sequence |
| Source note | Fictional demonstration data | Identify evidence or example status without hiding crucial facts |
| Optional extended detail | Full program conditions | Move to a handout or supporting document when density is too high |

Keep [[line-length|line lengths]] manageable. A slide with one very long line may force the eye to travel across the screen repeatedly. Break text where it changes idea, not in random places. Avoid squeezing text horizontally to force it into a small box, and avoid an unexplained shift between different text alignments. [[left-alignment|Left alignment]] is often a practical starting choice for multi-line text in left-to-right languages because each line begins at a predictable place; other language directions and formats may call for different alignment.

### Prefer clear language over visual tricks

A design cannot repair confusing wording by making it bright. Compare two fictional instructions:

- **Before the initiation of the commencement of the scheduled digital literacy session, participants must effectuate their presence at the predetermined venue.**
- **Arrive at the computer room ten minutes before class.**

The second version is shorter, specific, and actionable. Use [[plain-language|plain language]] wherever it preserves the necessary meaning. Explain unfamiliar acronyms on first use and distinguish between a headline and an instruction. If a slide contains a critical date, amount, exception, or warning, keep it visible and unambiguous rather than hiding it in tiny [[fine-print|fine print]].

A text box that automatically reduces font size as words are added may look convenient but can defeat readability. If the font shrinks too far, **shorten the message, split the slide, or move detail elsewhere**. Do not assume the fit feature has decided what the audience can read.

### Bullet points should organize, not replace thinking

[[bullet-list|Bullet lists]] work when several items belong to one idea and do not require strict sequence. If the order matters—such as three registration steps—use numbers instead. If a comparison matters, use a clearly labeled two-group layout. If a full sentence is best, do not force it into a list only because a template provides bullets.

| Information | Better starting form | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Three independent supplies | Short bullet list | Items belong together without sequence |
| First, second, third actions | Numbered steps | Sequence is part of the meaning |
| Two membership plans | Paired labeled groups or comparison table | Common criteria support comparison |
| One important reminder | Prominent statement | No list structure is needed |
| A decision and supporting reasons | Takeaway heading with concise evidence | Shows conclusion and its basis |

One idea per slide does **not** mean one word or one object. It means the content works together to support one main message. A labeled chart, a title, and one sentence can form a coherent slide; five unrelated topics in equal-sized boxes do not.

### Use emphasis sparingly

[[emphasis|Emphasis]] is a change in treatment that makes one item stand out. Use it for meaningful distinctions: a deadline, a key conclusion, or a next action. Bold text or a larger font may work better than adding another color. If you emphasize every noun and every number, no element has priority.

In the workshop example, it is sensible to emphasize **Registration closes Thursday** if that deadline matters. It is less sensible to make **Registration**, **Closes**, and **Thursday** each use a different typeface, size, and color. The viewer needs a reliable cue, not three competing signals.

### Worked example: reduce without losing substance

**Crowded slide draft:** “Please be reminded that the Saturday beginner computer workshop has been scheduled for April 18, will begin promptly at 10:00 a.m., will take place in Computer Room B on the second floor, and participants are strongly encouraged to visit the front desk in advance to register for attendance. Participants should also be aware that loaner devices are limited.”

**Revised slide:** Title **Saturday Computer Workshop**. Main line **April 18 · 10:00 a.m. · Computer Room B**. Supporting lines **Register at the front desk** and **Loaner devices are limited**. The presenter can explain the second-floor location, or a location line can be retained if the audience needs it. For an independent-viewing slide, include all essential details rather than assuming a narrator will provide them.

Ask whether the revised design preserved the [[essential-information|essential information]] required for the use case. A slide for live delivery and a slide emailed to participants may need different amounts of text. Simplification is not permission to omit an essential accessibility instruction, safety warning, or eligibility condition.

## 3. How do you use color, contrast, and visuals responsibly?

[[color|Color]] can establish identity, group related information, distinguish categories, and direct attention. It should serve a task instead of merely making the slide more colorful. A beginner can start with a restrained palette: one neutral background, one main text color, and a small number of accent colors. A theme may provide these choices, but a theme's existence does not guarantee good contrast or accurate meaning.

### Contrast is about distinguishability

[[contrast|Contrast]] describes a visible difference between elements. For reading, [[text-contrast|text contrast]] is the difference between a letter's appearance and the background behind it. Dark text on a light background or light text on a dark background can both work if the contrast is sufficient. Pale yellow on white, gray on a similar gray, or thin text laid over a busy photograph can be hard to read.

A quick visual inspection is useful but not complete. When possible, use an established [[contrast-checker|contrast checker]] or the slide application's accessibility tools. For ordinary digital text, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines ([[wcag|WCAG]]) provide a useful reference of at least **4.5:1** contrast for normal text and **3:1** for large text under their AA criteria, with defined exceptions. Those ratios are not a guarantee that a projected presentation will be legible, and slides may require additional practical adjustments for room lighting, distance, font weight, display quality, and visual needs. These numbers belong to a web accessibility specification; apply them as an informed baseline, not as a replacement for testing the presentation.

| Text-background combination | Likely concern | Practical response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dark navy on white | Usually clearer than similar light shades | Check actual text size and display |
| Pale yellow on white | Low distinguishability | Choose a much darker foreground |
| White on a busy photograph | Uneven contrast across the image | Add a solid contrasting panel or move the text |
| Light gray on medium gray | Weak separation | Increase contrast deliberately |
| Bright colors on saturated colors | Color vibration or visual strain | Test with a calmer palette and simpler background |
| Dark text on solid light background | Potentially effective | Verify with the intended room and screen |

Do not rely only on printed or projected appearance in your own workspace. A laptop in a bright office, a classroom projector, and a participant's phone may show the same slide differently. A design that looks acceptable while zoomed in may fail when displayed as a full-screen presentation from across a room.

### Color must not carry meaning alone

Someone may have [[color-vision-difference|color-vision differences]], may see a low-quality display, or may receive a monochrome printout. If a chart labels two groups only by a red and green legend, the distinction may be unclear. Pair color with words, symbols, data labels, patterns, or directly named categories. For example, instead of saying **The green activities are required**, label them **Required**. Use **Optional** as a separate label rather than communicating the distinction solely with a yellow fill.

[[semantic-color|Semantic color]] uses color to suggest a meaning, such as warning or approval. Such associations vary by culture and context; do not assume one color alone always means the same thing to everyone. If a deadline is urgent, write **Deadline: Thursday**. If a step is unsafe, state the caution clearly. Color may reinforce the message but should not be its only carrier.

### Images should explain, not obstruct

A [[visual|visual]] may be a photograph, diagram, screenshot, icon, map, or chart. It should earn its place by helping explain the main idea. A clear photograph of the computer room may help attendees recognize the venue. A decorative photograph of unrelated office equipment may occupy space without improving the explanation. One informative visual can be better than several decorative ones, but a visual is not required on every slide.

Before inserting a picture, ask whether it adds information, context, or useful orientation. Then inspect its [[resolution|resolution]], [[crop|crop]], proportions, and position. Stretching a photograph until people look unnaturally wide changes its appearance. Cropping may remove irrelevant surroundings but can also cut away the essential object. Text placed directly over a busy image may become difficult to read even if the image itself is attractive.

| Visual element | Helpful use | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Workshop-room photograph | Shows where participants will meet | Cropped so the entrance is no longer visible |
| Process diagram | Makes three dependent steps understandable | Decorative arrows imply an inaccurate sequence |
| Simple chart | Shows a genuine comparison | Truncated scale or missing labels misleads viewers |
| Icon | Reinforces an explicitly labeled category | Unexplained icon becomes the only instruction |
| Screenshot | Demonstrates a software control | Tiny labels cannot be read in presentation mode |
| Decorative background | Supports tone without interfering | Busy pattern obscures text |

### Data visuals need honest labels

A [[chart|chart]] is useful when visual comparison supports a claim. It is not automatically more truthful than a written number. A chart should identify what is being measured, the categories or time period, the units, and any important qualification. For a small example, a labeled table or simple text statement may be clearer than an elaborate graph. A misleading axis, a decorative three-dimensional effect, or omitted source information can distort the audience's understanding.

Consider fictional attendance figures for three weeks: **12, 15, 18**. A bar chart could compare the weeks, but viewers should know these figures are made up for practice. The slide might state **Attendance increased across three fictional sessions**. Do not show the data as genuine evidence about an actual community program. If presenting real measurements, preserve the underlying numbers and identify an appropriate source.

### Alternative text and captions

[[alternative-text|Alternative text]], often called *alt text*, describes the meaningful information conveyed by an image for people who cannot see it. For a screenshot of a registration button, a useful description may identify the location and purpose of that button. A decorative flourish can be marked decorative through the editor's supported tools, while an informative chart may need a concise description and a nearby text summary of the actual data. Do not repeat a long paragraph word for word merely because an image is present; describe its function and essential content.

[[captions|Captions]] and transcripts may be needed for recorded speech, video, or audio instruction. Captions must accurately represent the spoken content and meaningful sounds, not merely appear as decorative text. These needs were introduced in the media lesson; here the design priority is that important information must remain available through more than one visual or auditory channel.

### Worked example: repair color-only directions

**Original slide:** “Go to the green station, then the yellow station.” A photograph contains three tables, each identified only by a colored card. A viewer with a color-vision difference or a low-resolution printout may not know which location is meant.

**Improved slide:** “1. Check in at Station A — Registration. 2. Continue to Station B — Device Pickup.” Put the same written labels next to the actual tables or in a floor map. Colors may remain as extra cues but are no longer the only directions. The revised slide is also more useful for someone describing the route aloud.

> **Color principle:** **Use color to reinforce meaning; use readable text or another distinct cue to establish meaning.**

## 4. How do you arrange objects and maintain consistency?

A slide can use a readable font and good colors yet feel confusing when its components have no clear relationship. [[alignment|Alignment]], [[spacing|spacing]], [[proximity|proximity]], and [[negative-space|negative space]] help the viewer understand what belongs together and where to look next. These principles are often easier to improve than an elaborate color scheme: a small movement of a text box can make an entire slide easier to scan.

### Alignment gives objects an underlying structure

[[alignment|Alignment]] means placing objects in an orderly relationship—for example, lining up several headings by their left edges. You need not draw visible grid lines. Instead, imagine an invisible [[layout-grid|layout grid]] or a few consistent guide lines behind the slide. When related items share an edge or center, their relationship is usually easier to perceive.

Misalignment can be intentional for a design purpose, but beginner decks frequently contain accidental near-misses: one title begins slightly farther right than every other title; two picture cards have different widths; a label floats too far from the number it describes. Correcting these inconsistencies helps the audience recognize patterns without extra effort.

| Object arrangement | What the viewer may infer | Improvement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Three headings share a left edge | They belong to a common structure | Keep the shared alignment |
| An image and its caption are far apart | The relationship is uncertain | Move the caption closer |
| Two comparison cards have unequal widths | One may seem more important unintentionally | Use consistent dimensions unless emphasis is deliberate |
| Repeated titles begin in different positions | Slide-to-slide orientation changes | Apply a consistent title position |
| Boxes overlap important text | Information may be hidden | Reposition, shorten, or separate the objects |
| A large blank margin surrounds all content | Space may support easy scanning | Preserve it if nothing important is missing |

### Proximity groups related information

[[proximity|Proximity]] means that items placed near each other usually appear related. If each workshop step has an icon, a title, and a description, keep those three components together. Leave more space between complete steps than between an icon and its own label. The audience can then recognize three groups instead of nine unrelated objects.

Consider three station labels: **Registration**, **Device Pickup**, and **Practice Room**. If the descriptive sentences are scattered beneath the wrong headings, the audience may connect the wrong information to the wrong station. A well-designed layout uses proximity, labels, and spacing to keep every description next to its own heading. The principle is especially important in comparison slides and diagrams where the visual location carries meaning.

### Negative space is not wasted space

[[negative-space|Negative space]] is the unoccupied area around and between elements. It gives text and images room to be recognized. A beginner might fill every open corner with an icon, border, or quotation because the empty area feels incomplete. That can increase [[visual-clutter|visual clutter]] without adding useful information.

A useful rule of thought is **leave room for the audience to distinguish groups**. If two paragraphs touch, increase the gap. If a title is crowded against a photograph, add a margin or move the photograph. If a chart barely fits between the slide edges, simplify or reduce its complexity rather than simply compressing it. There is no universal percentage of empty space that works for every slide. The required amount depends on its content and screen.

### Margins and distribution

A [[margin|margin]] is a deliberate boundary between the slide edge and its content. Important text placed too close to an edge may be clipped by a projector, obscured by a video conferencing overlay, or difficult to see from an angle. Use generous, consistent margins, and verify the final presentation mode. A [[safe-area|safe area]] is an informal planning zone that keeps essential material away from risky edges; software may also provide guides or other layout aids.

[[distribution|Distribution]] means arranging multiple objects with consistent spacing. Three equal cards with inconsistent gaps can look accidental even when each card is aligned. Many slide editors have commands such as **Align**, **Distribute**, or **Guides**. These tools are conveniences; their usefulness still depends on selecting the correct objects and choosing a layout that makes sense.

A pair of examples illustrates the difference:

- **Misleading equality:** A slide lists three different prices, but one price is placed in a much larger card for no stated reason. Viewers may assume it has special importance.
- **Deliberate priority:** A slide lists three available classes and highlights **Registration is open for Class B** because the audience must act on that item. The visual difference has a clear communicative purpose.

Symmetry can be useful, but an effective slide does not require perfectly symmetrical objects. What matters is whether the arrangement communicates relationships and importance accurately.

### Repetition builds consistency

[[consistency|Consistency]] means using similar treatments for similar purposes. If all section titles use the same font and position, viewers learn where to find them. If action steps are always numbered, readers can recognize an instruction sequence quickly. If informational callouts use the same restrained styling, the audience can distinguish them from warnings or questions.

Choose a small number of reusable conventions:

1. A predictable title style and location.
2. A readable body-text style.
3. A limited palette of purposeful colors.
4. Similar margins and spacing for similar slide types.
5. Consistent formatting for charts, source notes, and important labels.
6. A shared convention for identifying actions, requirements, and optional details.

A [[theme|theme]] or [[template|template]] can support repeated choices, but a deck can become inconsistent when individual slides are manually changed. A [[slide-layout|slide layout]] controls a particular arrangement, while a [[slide-master|slide master]] or corresponding theme-editing system may control design conventions used across several slides. The exact capabilities differ across applications. Lesson 3 covered these concepts more fully; here the design rule is to prefer repeatable, manageable styles over one-off decorative exceptions.

### Consistency does not mean sameness

A title slide, an illustrated process slide, a comparison slide, and a closing slide have different jobs. Their arrangements may vary while still sharing the same typeface family, color logic, and title treatment. A deck with every object in the exact same position can be just as unhelpful as a deck with no common conventions.

Think of a document that uses headings, paragraphs, and occasional tables. The elements differ, but the document feels unified because headings have a predictable role and text formatting follows a system. A presentation can be designed in the same way: varied slide arrangements operating inside one coherent set of conventions.

### Worked example: align three workshop steps

The original slide contains three text boxes for **Sign Up**, **Check In**, and **Start Practicing**. Each box uses a different width, the captions have inconsistent space from the titles, and the third box overlaps the bottom border. To repair it, first identify three equal-priority steps and choose a numbered layout. Align the headings, make their widths consistent where appropriate, put each explanation near its step, and leave a comparable amount of space between the groups. If the explanatory sentences are too long to remain legible, shorten or move them to a supporting page. Recheck the slide in full-screen mode. The result communicates the sequence without relying on decoration.

## 5. How do you apply design rules in presentation software?

The same principles apply whether you edit a deck in PowerPoint, Google Slides, or LibreOffice Impress. A specific button's location is less important than the decision you are trying to make. Start with the message, choose a suitable layout, apply consistent text styles, adjust colors and spacing, and then test the resulting slide. **Save a separate editable copy before major changes**, especially if the original is shared, used for training, or already approved.

### General editing workflow

Use this sequence in a familiar slide application:

1. **Open an editable copy.** Confirm that you are allowed to modify it and that its filename identifies the practice version.
2. **Select one slide.** Read its title and determine the most important audience takeaway.
3. **Inspect the layout.** Identify the title field, text boxes, images, labels, and any off-slide or overlapping objects.
4. **Simplify content before shrinking it.** Remove duplication, split unrelated ideas, and retain essential information.
5. **Set readable text styles.** Choose a small number of type sizes and weights that communicate hierarchy.
6. **Check the color pairings.** Ensure essential text stands out from the background and that meaning is not color-only.
7. **Align and space.** Move related objects together, restore margins, and fix inconsistent edges.
8. **Review visuals and accessibility.** Verify useful images, alternative text, data labels, object reading order, and captions where appropriate.
9. **Present or preview the slide.** Examine it at the size and distance of actual use, not only while zoomed in for editing.
10. **Save and verify.** Reopen or recheck the copy if necessary and keep the original intact.

An editor may provide automatic layout suggestions, artificial intelligence design assistance, or theme recommendations. Treat these as proposals to review, not verified solutions. Such suggestions can omit critical content, create weak contrast, reorganize meaning, or change the placement of information. The author remains responsible for accuracy and accessibility.

### Microsoft PowerPoint on Windows 11

In PowerPoint, open the editable file and select the slide thumbnail. A built-in layout under **Home → Layout** can provide organized placeholders. The **Design** tab provides theme-related choices in typical desktop versions. When text is selected, the **Home** tab includes common font, paragraph, and alignment controls. Select shapes or pictures to access context-specific formatting options. To align multiple objects, select the intended objects and use the appropriate **Align** commands, often available through **Shape Format** or a similar ribbon location. Commands can vary depending on version and selected object type.

When an object seems hidden or crowded, inspect its position and text box size before reducing type size. You can also use guides, gridlines, and the selection tools where available. For an accessibility review, use the **Review** or **Accessibility** tools, including **Check Accessibility** in supported versions. Look for missing titles, inadequate contrast, object order issues, and images lacking meaningful alternative text. Microsoft provides a **Reading Order** or selection-related interface in supported PowerPoint editions; verify that a screen reader would encounter the content in the intended sequence rather than assuming the visible left-to-right arrangement controls it.

For example, suppose an image partially covers the sentence **Register by Thursday**. Do not merely change the image's transparency and hope the text becomes readable. Decide whether the image is necessary. Move it away from the instruction, reduce its footprint while preserving its proportions, or place the instruction on a clear contrasting panel. Then run a presentation preview and accessibility review.

### Google Slides in a browser

In Google Slides, open an authorized editable presentation and select a slide. Use the **Layout** controls or **Slide** menu to choose a suitable arrangement, depending on the interface version. The toolbar provides fonts, text size, text color, alignment, and other common formatting options for selected objects. For themes or background choices, use the relevant **Theme**, **Background**, or **Slide** controls. For positioning, select the desired objects and consult the **Arrange** menu for available alignment and distribution commands.

Google Slides may automatically fit or resize text in particular placeholders or text boxes, depending on settings. If text suddenly appears much smaller than on neighboring slides, inspect the fitting behavior and redesign the content rather than accepting an unreadable result. Use the editor's accessibility options and appropriate alternative-text commands for meaningful visuals, and check current Google guidance for supported keyboard and screen-reader behavior. A shared Slides file can have other collaborators or permission limits; avoid changing a live group presentation without coordination.

Because Google Slides is often used in a browser, consider your working zoom separately from slide text size. Zooming in makes editing easier for you, but does not necessarily make the eventual projected slide more readable. Test the presentation in its actual playback or display mode.

### LibreOffice Impress

In LibreOffice Impress, open a practice copy and select a slide. Use the available **Properties** or formatting controls to change text, shapes, lines, and fills. Choose an appropriate slide layout, then use object selection and the **Format** alignment controls, guides, or rulers where supported. Aligning objects does not automatically repair excessive text or an unclear message. First decide what belongs together, then use the tools to position it precisely.

Impress may use a different interface organization from PowerPoint or Google Slides, and the names or availability of commands may differ by release. If a command described for another application is missing, look for its equivalent concept instead of assuming the program lacks the capability. PowerPoint and Impress also do not preserve every visual detail identically when files are exchanged. If opening a deck in a different editor changes a font, shifts text, or alters a media element, compare it against the intended design and repair the content rather than assuming the conversion was perfect.

### What changes from application to application?

| Design task | PowerPoint | Google Slides | LibreOffice Impress |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Choose slide structure | Home / Layout in many desktop versions | Layout or Slide controls | Slide layout controls |
| Change font and size | Home formatting controls | Toolbar text controls | Properties / formatting controls |
| Change theme appearance | Design tab and theme tools | Theme and related controls | Master slide / style controls |
| Align selected shapes | Align commands for selected objects | Arrange / align tools | Format / alignment tools |
| Review full-screen readability | Slide Show / presentation view | Slideshow / present view | Slide Show view |
| Inspect accessibility | Supported accessibility checker and order tools | Accessibility features and manual review | Manual review and supported accessibility features |

These are representative paths, not guarantees that every platform or subscription exposes the same commands. Do not change global settings or install additional software merely to complete the practice activities. The paper exercises are equally valid for the design concepts.

### Selecting versus editing text

Many beginner errors occur because a user changes the wrong object. Clicking a text box border may select the entire box; clicking inside the text may place the insertion point in the text. Formatting the object can differ from formatting characters. If the wrong color or font changes, undo the action, confirm what is selected, and try again. For multiple objects, use the application's supported multi-selection procedure and verify that the group contains the intended elements only.

**Undo** can recover some recent edits, but it is not a substitute for maintaining a copy. Automatic saving and version history may be available, but they depend on the application, storage location, permissions, and service state. Confirm that the expected file or version exists before continuing.

### Worked example: apply a three-part design standard

A staff member receives three fictional slides. Their titles appear in three places; body text is 14 points on one slide and 30 on another; the third slide has gray writing on an orange photograph. The staff member first creates an editable copy, chooses one consistent title position, and selects a readable body-text range based on the actual display. Next, they remove the photograph from behind the text and use a solid high-contrast area. Finally, they preview all three slides together and check that each title has its intended heading role. The staff member has not introduced a complex visual identity: **three repeatable decisions** have made the deck substantially easier to understand.

## 6. How do you review design for real audiences?

A slide that appears neat in editing view may fail during a live presentation. A good review uses an [[audience-view|audience view]]: look at the complete slide as a viewer will see it, not only as the person who built it. Review is not limited to checking spelling or whether the file opens. It asks whether the design works under the likely conditions of use.

### The full-screen and distance test

If you have access to the presentation environment, show the slide full screen. Sit or stand at a representative viewing distance, including a position toward the back of a room if relevant. Check titles, instructions, chart labels, and crucial details. Ask someone unfamiliar with the material to state the slide's main message after a short glance. If they cannot recognize it, the hierarchy may need work.

Do not conduct a forced “five-second test” as a pass/fail certification. Complexity varies, and some slides require careful inspection. The goal is to find unexpectedly hard-to-locate information and unnecessary friction. A slide demonstrating complex evidence may reasonably take longer to understand, but essential labels must still be legible.

A laptop-screen preview is a useful first pass, not a substitute for checking the intended projector, video meeting, or mobile display. In a hybrid meeting, video tiles, captions, or screen-share interface elements may reduce the visible area. Be especially cautious with information placed near outer edges.

### An accessibility-first review

[[accessibility|Accessibility]] means designing so people with different abilities and access methods can obtain and use the content. The relevant checks extend beyond contrast. Visual order may not match [[reading-order|reading order]] for a screen reader. A diagram may require meaningful [[alternative-text|alternative text]] or an accompanying textual explanation. A video may require captions. A chart should not rely solely on colors. A link's visible text should describe its destination instead of saying only **Click here**.

| Review question | Why it matters | Corrective action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is each slide's purpose identifiable? | People need orientation | Add or clarify a meaningful title |
| Is essential text legible in full-screen view? | Small or faint text may be missed | Increase clarity, shorten or split content |
| Does color carry an instruction by itself? | Some viewers cannot reliably distinguish it | Add words, labels, or another cue |
| Do screen readers encounter objects logically? | Visual position may differ from object order | Inspect and adjust supported reading-order controls |
| Do meaningful images have suitable descriptions? | Nonvisual users need equivalent information | Add alt text or a nearby explanation |
| Do media elements have needed captions or text alternatives? | Audio-only meaning excludes some viewers | Supply accurate captions or transcripts |
| Are charts labeled and accurately scaled? | Visual exaggeration can mislead | Restore labels, units, and honest scales |
| Is the deck readable in the delivery environment? | Screens and distances vary | Preview and revise in actual conditions |

An automated accessibility checker can identify certain problems, but it cannot fully evaluate whether a message is understandable, whether alternative text describes the important information, or whether a color distinction is meaningful in context. Perform a manual review even after an automated check reports no issues.

### Check content accuracy and design together

A visually strong slide can still misinform. If a workshop deadline is inaccurate, making it large and bold only amplifies the error. If a chart uses fictional data, identify it as fictional; if a chart uses real data, verify its source and scale. Design should not make one option appear safer, cheaper, or more effective unless the evidence supports that distinction. Avoid using misleading icons or emotionally loaded imagery to imply facts that have not been established.

When revising, make one substantial change at a time and review the effect. For example, shortening a sentence may create extra space, making it easier to increase font size. Increasing font size may cause a text box to extend past its boundary, requiring layout changes. Redesign is an iterative process: **edit → inspect → compare with goal → revise**.

### A practical design audit

Use this structured review on every finished practice deck:

1. **Purpose:** Each slide has an obvious job in the presentation.
2. **Title:** Each slide has an informative title and the title style is consistent.
3. **Main message:** Important content receives more attention than secondary decoration.
4. **Text:** Fonts, sizes, wording, and line breaks support reading at the intended distance.
5. **Contrast:** Text and essential shapes are visually distinguishable from their backgrounds.
6. **Color:** Meaning does not depend on the ability to name or distinguish a color alone.
7. **Alignment:** Related objects share clear edges or positions when appropriate.
8. **Grouping:** Explanations, labels, and visuals are near the items they describe.
9. **Space:** Margins and gaps reduce crowding without removing necessary information.
10. **Consistency:** Similar functions receive similar visual treatment across the deck.
11. **Visuals:** Every chart, image, and icon supports the intended message and is not misleading.
12. **Access:** Reading order, alt text, captions, and delivery methods are considered.
13. **Delivery:** The presentation has been reviewed at realistic playback size and conditions.
14. **Preservation:** A usable editable copy remains available and the original is not accidentally overwritten.

### Before-and-after example: a six-slide workshop deck

A fictional workshop deck has these issues: Slide 1 uses six typefaces; Slide 2 buries the starting time; Slide 3 puts white text over a photograph; Slide 4 uses unlabeled red and green categories; Slide 5 has three misaligned steps; and Slide 6 ends without telling participants what to do. The design audit converts these into identifiable changes.

| Slide | Observed problem | Repair guided by design rules |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Welcome | Six conflicting typefaces | Use a consistent title style and one body-text family |
| 2. Schedule | Main time buried in a paragraph | Present the time prominently; retain complete essential details |
| 3. Location | Text overlays a visually busy photo | Place text on a clear contrasting background |
| 4. Choices | Only red/green distinguishes options | Add explicit Required and Optional labels |
| 5. Steps | Uneven boxes and unclear grouping | Align steps and associate each caption with its label |
| 6. Closing | No next action | Add a clear, accurate registration or contact instruction |

The repaired slides may look simpler than the originals. Their improvement comes from clarity, consistency, and completeness—not the number of visual effects added. The final check is whether someone can identify what the program is, when and where it happens, and what action follows. If those answers are available and accessible, the deck is closer to its purpose.

### When to seek another review

A person who did not design the deck may notice problems the author has learned to overlook. Where feasible, ask a peer to identify the main message, locate an important detail, and describe the order in which they would read the content. For high-stakes or public-facing material, obtain appropriate subject-matter review and accessibility review. Do not ask someone to share private information just to test a fictional example.

If a deck will be viewed without the presenter, check that slides contain enough explanatory information on their own or are distributed with a suitable accessible handout. **Slides made for live narration and slides intended as independent documents have different communication requirements.** Design decisions must account for the actual setting.

> **Completion standard:** A slide is not ready merely because every object fits. It is ready when its essential message is **accurate, legible, organized, consistent, and accessible for its intended use**.

## 7. Guided practice: redesign a fictional deck

The six activities below form a progression from recognizing individual design problems to improving a complete short presentation. **All names, deadlines, quantities, and program details are invented for training.** You do not need an actual event, an online account, or presentation software. For the paper option, sketch rectangular slides and write the proposed titles, text, image descriptions, and labels. For the software option, use a new editable practice file and save it under a descriptive name such as `fictional-workshop-design-practice.pptx` or the application's native format. Avoid sharing the practice deck publicly.

There is usually more than one defensible design. The model answers show a reasonable method and explain the decision; they are not the only artistic arrangements permitted. Evaluate whether the message is clear, facts remain accurate, and viewers can access the important information.

### Activity 1: Find the main message and visual priority

**Scenario:** A fictional library has prepared a slide titled **General Workshop Information**. It includes these six items: a welcome sentence; **Saturday, April 18**; **10:00–11:30 a.m.**; **Computer Room B**; a decorative clip-art keyboard; and **Registration closes Thursday**. Every item uses the same size of text, and the keyboard is the largest element.

**Your task:** Identify the primary audience need: a new participant must recognize the meeting time and required registration action. Write a clearer title, decide what should be most prominent, and describe one change to reduce distraction. Do not delete essential logistics. Use the decision table below to plan a slide.

| Item | Keep on the slide? | Proposed visual priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Workshop title | Yes | Title or strong heading |
| Date and time | Yes | High: required logistics |
| Room | Yes | Readable supporting location |
| Registration deadline | Yes | High: required action |
| Long welcome sentence | Optional | Supporting explanation or spoken introduction |
| Decorative keyboard | Optional | Remove if it competes with essential details |

**Check yourself:** Could a newcomer identify the date, time, room, and action without listening to a separate explanation? If not, the design still hides necessary information.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answer
A reasonable title is **Saturday Digital Skills Workshop**. A prominent line reads **April 18 · 10:00–11:30 a.m.**. A second line gives **Computer Room B**, and a clearly labeled action reads **Register by Thursday**. The keyboard is removed or reduced because it was drawing attention away from information needed to attend. The welcoming sentence can be shortened or used in the presenter's spoken introduction. Different arrangements are acceptable if the key details remain clear and easy to read.
:::

### Activity 2: Repair typography and overcrowded text

**Scenario:** A fictional slide uses a tiny title, a long sentence in all capital letters, four typefaces, and a large paragraph of instructions. It is intended to appear briefly during a live orientation. The paragraph reads:

> ALL INDIVIDUALS WHO WISH TO PARTICIPATE IN THE DIGITAL SKILLS PRACTICE EVENT ARE HEREBY REQUESTED TO COMPLETE THEIR REGISTRATION PROCESS BY THURSDAY AND TO PRESENT THEMSELVES AT THE DESIGNATED COMPUTER ROOM TEN MINUTES PRIOR TO THE SCHEDULED BEGINNING OF THE SESSION.

**Your task:** Rewrite the message in plain language, keeping the two actionable facts. Propose a simple title/body style, explain how the text should be grouped, and identify whether a smaller font is the best solution. Sketch the revised slide or edit the text in a practice editor. Check for an accidental change in meaning.

**Hint:** The sentence contains a registration deadline and an arrival-time instruction. Those are two actions, not one decorative paragraph.

:::answer Show Activity 2 model answer
Use **Before the Workshop** as the title, then show two numbered lines: **1. Register by Thursday. 2. Arrive at the computer room ten minutes early.** Keep one readable typeface family, a consistent large heading style, and a body style legible in the intended setting. Place enough space between the two steps to make each action clear. If exact location details are essential to independent viewing, add them in readable form rather than assuming the audience knows the room. Shrinking the original paragraph would preserve its confusing wording and make it harder to read; editing the message is the more effective first step.
:::

### Activity 3: Fix contrast and color-only meaning

**Scenario:** A fictional event slide has light-gray text on a pale-blue background. It presents three attendance categories, but the only distinction is that the category boxes are red, green, and yellow. A narrator is expected to say, **The green category may register now**. Viewers have no written labels explaining which category can act.

**Your task:** Identify at least three issues. Propose a new text-background relationship, give meaningful category labels, and rewrite the narrator's instruction so it can be understood without distinguishing colors. If using software, you may add labels and a clearly contrasting solid background; do not make this exercise depend on a specific color picker.

| Original device | Risk | Improvement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light text on similarly light background | Reduced readability | Use a clearly contrasting color pair |
| Categories distinguished by color only | Meaning unavailable to some viewers | Add explicit category names |
| Spoken instruction references a color only | Difficult to follow without color recognition | Refer to the named category |
| No written next action | Standalone slide lacks instruction | Write the required action on-screen |

:::answer Show Activity 3 model answer
A possible redesign uses dark text on a solid light background, with each category labeled **Open for Registration**, **Waitlist**, or **Not Yet Available**. The instruction becomes **Participants in the Open for Registration category may sign up now**. The labels remain understandable even if printed without color. The revised text should be checked in presentation view, and any real registration category must be verified rather than assumed from the fictional example. Color can be retained as a secondary cue, but it is no longer the only source of meaning.
:::

### Activity 4: Apply alignment, proximity, and negative space

**Scenario:** A slide explains a three-step fictional process: **Check In**, **Collect a Loaner Device**, and **Begin Practice**. Its three icons sit at different heights. The caption for the second step appears closer to the third icon than the second. The last step touches the slide's right edge. Decorative stars occupy the remaining empty spaces.

**Your task:** Sketch three consistent labeled groups. Decide whether the steps should be displayed left-to-right, top-to-bottom, or in another justified arrangement. Explain how you would align the groups, how you would associate captions with the correct steps, and why you would change the margin or decorative elements.

**Accessibility extension:** Write the process as a numbered text sequence that would still make sense if no icons were visible.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answer
For a left-to-right layout in an English-language presentation, place three equally prominent groups along a common horizontal guide, with consistent spacing and a comfortable margin at both edges. Each group contains a number, a readable step label, and its short explanation directly below. Remove the decorative stars if they compete with the steps. If a narrow display makes the groups too small, stack the steps vertically rather than compressing everything. The text-only sequence is **1. Check in. 2. Collect a loaner device. 3. Begin practice.** The meaning does not depend on seeing icons.
:::

### Activity 5: Perform a design review in your chosen editor

**Scenario:** You are given an editable copy of a fictional three-slide training deck, or you sketch it on paper. Slide 1 has an inconsistent title position, Slide 2 has too much text, and Slide 3 contains an unlabeled photograph plus text that blends into the picture.

**Your task:** Perform the same review in PowerPoint, Google Slides, LibreOffice Impress, or on paper. First preserve the original. Then use the checks in the table. Record **one visible change and one verification action for each slide**. At least one change must improve accessibility rather than merely appearance.

| Slide | Diagnosis | Proposed fix | Verification |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Title position differs from other slides | Apply consistent layout or reposition title | Compare all three slides together |
| 2 | Text is too dense | Shorten, group, or split content | View at presentation size |
| 3 | Text overlaps photograph | Move text to a solid high-contrast area | Test readability and image meaning |

**Software note:** If using a shared editor, work only in an authorized practice copy. An automatic layout or text-fit feature may change formatting; confirm that it did not remove essential information or shrink the text excessively.

:::answer Show Activity 5 model answer
A suitable result preserves the original in a separate file, aligns the first slide's title with the others, reduces the second slide to a clear main message plus essential supporting information, and moves the third slide's text onto a solid contrasting background. A meaningful photograph receives suitable alt text or a nearby explanation; a decorative image is identified as decorative through supported tools. The author previews the slides as an audience would see them and checks the reading order of important elements where the editor supports it. If no software is available, a paper before-and-after sketch with the same decisions fully meets the activity.
:::

### Activity 6: Capstone project — redesign six workshop slides

**Scenario:** Your fictional neighborhood center needs a beginner-friendly presentation introducing **Saturday Digital Skills Workshop**. The deck will be shown on a screen during a live welcome session and may later be shared with attendees. Invent no additional attendance rules or registration restrictions. Use the following fictional facts consistently:

| Required information | Fictional details |
| --- | --- |
| Event | Saturday Digital Skills Workshop |
| Date | Saturday, April 18 |
| Time | 10:00–11:30 a.m. |
| Location | Community Center, Computer Room B |
| Registration | At the front desk by Thursday |
| What participants do | Practice opening files, using a browser, and saving documents |
| Equipment | A limited number of loaner laptops are available |
| Closing action | Confirm registration with the front desk |

**Your task:** Produce six slide sketches or a six-slide editable deck. Use the following structure as a starting point, modifying it only when your alternative flow remains clear:

1. **Welcome and purpose:** Tell viewers what the workshop offers.
2. **When and where:** Make the schedule and location prominent.
3. **What participants will practice:** Group three activities into a clear sequence or labeled set.
4. **Equipment:** Explain the limited loaner laptops accurately without inventing a reservation policy.
5. **How to register:** Explain the front-desk method and Thursday deadline.
6. **Next step and questions:** Repeat the required action and allow room for clarification.

Apply a coherent title style, readable body style, a restrained color approach, clear margins, and deliberate alignment. Do not place instructions directly over a busy photograph. Label any visuals, give accessible descriptions when relevant, and keep the registration details visible if the file is shared without a speaker. You may use no images at all: accurate text and a simple diagram are sufficient.

**Review rubric:** Give yourself one point for each successful check below. A total of eight points shows that all foundational checks were addressed; the score is an exercise reflection, not a formal certification or disability-access compliance determination.

| Check | One point when... |
| --- | --- |
| Message | Every slide has one clear principal purpose |
| Readability | Essential text is readable in the intended display setting |
| Contrast | Important text and objects stand out from the background |
| Grouping | Labels, images, and descriptions are associated correctly |
| Consistency | Repeated heading and body styles follow a coherent system |
| Accuracy | All fictional details are preserved without unsupported additions |
| Accessibility | Meaning is not color-only and relevant alternatives are considered |
| Review and preservation | The complete deck is previewed and an editable copy is retained |

**Final reflection:** Choose the weakest slide. Explain what a participant might misunderstand and name one specific edit that would correct it. Aesthetic preference alone is not an adequate explanation; link the decision to communication or access.

:::answer Show Activity 6 model answer
A strong six-slide result might use a consistent heading style, dark text on a light solid background, and one accent color for headings or important deadlines. Slide 1 reads **Practice Everyday Computer Skills** and identifies the fictional program. Slide 2 gives **Saturday, April 18 · 10:00–11:30 a.m.** and **Community Center, Computer Room B** in a clear grouping. Slide 3 lists or depicts three labeled activities: **Open files**, **Use a browser**, and **Save documents**. Slide 4 states **Limited loaner laptops available**, without implying that every participant will receive one. Slide 5 says **Register at the front desk by Thursday**. Slide 6 repeats **Next step: Confirm registration at the front desk** and provides an opportunity for questions. All slides keep essential content away from unsafe edges, use purposeful spacing, and remain understandable without color recognition. If any slide contains a meaningful photo, add an appropriate alternative description. The editor copy is saved separately and checked at presentation size. The review rubric documents which principles were actually tested.
:::

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

Design habits improve quickly when you can identify what an apparently attractive slide may still be missing. The table below contrasts common assumptions with more reliable practices.

| Misconception | More accurate understanding | Useful action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The most colorful slide is the best slide | Color serves communication rather than decoration | Identify the slide's main message first |
| Every slide must contain a picture | Images are useful when they explain something | Remove irrelevant stock imagery |
| A theme guarantees accessibility | Theme choices still require content-specific testing | Check contrast, titles, reading order, and alt text |
| If text fits, it is readable | Fitting can shrink text too far | Test at actual presentation size |
| Every slide must follow an identical layout | Consistent rules can support multiple appropriate arrangements | Repeat styles while varying layouts for purpose |
| White space is wasted space | Empty space helps separate information | Preserve useful margins and group boundaries |
| Color is enough to label categories | Some viewers may not distinguish the colors | Add explicit words, symbols, or direct data labels |
| More animations create more understanding | Movement can distract or obscure meaning | Prefer a clear explanation and restrained effects |
| A chart is automatically objective | Scale, source, and labels influence interpretation | Verify values, axes, units, and context |
| Small text is fine because I can read it on my laptop | Delivery environments change readability | Preview with the actual screen and distance |
| Automatic accessibility checks find every problem | Software identifies only some issues | Perform human judgment and audience testing too |
| Design repair requires starting from scratch | Many defects can be fixed by changing priorities and placement | Edit one problem, preview, then continue |

### Mistake: solving a content problem with a visual effect

A slide crowded with six unrelated points does not become more coherent when its text fades in one line at a time. Animation may control pacing, but the underlying list still needs an organized purpose. Similarly, adding shapes around every sentence may create more boundaries without clarifying relationships. Whenever a visual change seems complicated, return to the main message and ask whether the slide should be rewritten or divided instead.

### Mistake: trusting visual appearance alone

A visually centered title may be located in an unexpected reading sequence for assistive technology. A photograph may depict the correct location but lack alternative text. A category distinction may be clear to someone who recognizes colors but not to someone viewing a monochrome printout. Inspect the file's semantic organization as well as its appearance. If the editor has accessibility guidance, use it, but evaluate its output rather than treating the tool as a final authority.

### Mistake: using an arbitrary font-size rule as proof of success

A beginner may hear that all text should be a fixed number of points and assume any deck satisfying that number is readable. Type appearance depends on the font, projector, viewing distance, weight, and amount of information. A guideline is useful for beginning the design, but meaningful review asks **can the intended audience actually read it?** If not, revise, even when the deck conforms to a popular rule of thumb.

### Mistake: changing one slide without checking the rest

If you restyle a title on one slide, compare it with other titles. If you change the theme, inspect older slides for altered line breaks, contrast, and image positions. If you replace a typeface, verify that the replacement is available on devices used to present or share the file. One improvement can create a different problem elsewhere; finish by previewing the whole deck.

### Troubleshooting quick reference

| Symptom | Likely cause | First response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Text vanishes behind an image | Layering or object position | Inspect object placement and move content to a clear area |
| A slide becomes unreadably small | Excess content or text auto-fit | Shorten, divide, or enlarge the content area appropriately |
| A title shifts after a theme change | Layout or font substitution | Check title placeholder, font, and slide layout |
| Colors look washed out on a projector | Different display conditions or low contrast | Increase practical contrast and preview on the equipment |
| Objects no longer align | Manual movement or inconsistent guides | Select related items and realign deliberately |
| A chart loses its meaning in black-and-white | Color-only categories | Add data labels and distinct patterns where useful |
| Screen-reader sequence seems wrong | Object reading order differs from visual order | Review and adjust the supported reading-order settings |
| A shared slide looks different on another computer | Font, editor, or conversion differences | Recheck with the destination software and preserve an editable original |

> **Troubleshooting sequence:** **Identify the communication problem → preserve the original → change the smallest meaningful set of features → test the full slide → inspect related slides.**

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The following terms summarize design ideas that can be used in an ordinary slide-editing workflow. Select contextual vocabulary throughout the lesson to review concise definitions. The entries also help you distinguish principles, software features, and accessibility responsibilities.

| Term | Everyday meaning | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design | How information is arranged for communication | Decisions should support a real audience goal |
| Visual hierarchy | What viewers notice first, second, and later | Makes priorities recognizable |
| Typography | Choices about text appearance and arrangement | Determines readability and emphasis |
| Contrast | Difference between neighboring visual elements | Helps people distinguish text and content |
| Alignment | Orderly positioning of objects relative to each other | Creates structure and predictable scanning |
| Proximity | Placing related elements near one another | Shows which caption belongs to which item |
| Negative space | Useful unoccupied area around content | Reduces crowding |
| Margin | Space between content and the edge | Protects important information from clipping |
| Consistency | Similar treatment for similar functions | Helps the audience follow a deck |
| Color-independent meaning | Information understandable without distinguishing colors | Supports broader access and reliability |
| Alternative text | Text describing meaningful visual information | Supports nonvisual access |
| Reading order | Sequence in which assistive tools encounter objects | May differ from the visible arrangement |
| Theme | Coordinated appearance choices across slides | Helps maintain a repeatable visual identity |
| Layout | Arrangement of placeholders and objects on a slide | Supports different kinds of content |
| Presentation preview | Viewing slides in their intended display mode | Reveals issues hidden by editing zoom |
| Accessibility check | Inspection for usability barriers | Helps identify problems before delivery |

**Self-explanation exercise:** Choose three vocabulary terms and explain how they apply to one slide. For example: **Contrast** makes the registration deadline distinguishable; **proximity** keeps the deadline near the registration instruction; **visual hierarchy** makes the action easier to notice than a decorative photograph.

A practical vocabulary test is whether you can use a term to make a design decision rather than merely repeat its definition. If you understand alignment, you should be able to identify which objects need a common edge. If you understand visual hierarchy, you should be able to choose which information deserves the strongest emphasis. If you understand accessibility, you should recognize when a seemingly attractive treatment blocks access for part of the audience.

? Why might a title such as “Registration closes Thursday” be more effective than “Information”?
- [x] It conveys the slide’s main point directly
- [ ] It automatically proves that the deadline is correct
- [ ] It eliminates the need for any other slide content
! A meaningful title helps people orient to the message; the fact must still be verified.

? What is the best first response when too much text shrinks automatically?
- [ ] Add a new typeface to every sentence
- [x] Simplify, regroup, or divide content before accepting tiny text
- [ ] Make the background a brighter color
! Text fitting is not the same as audience readability.

? When does a numbered list usually work better than bullets?
- [ ] When the list items have no relationship at all
- [ ] Whenever the presenter wants more decoration
- [x] When the steps must be performed in a particular order
! Numbers communicate sequence when sequence matters.

? What is contrast in the context of slide text?
- [x] The distinguishability of text from its background
- [ ] The number of slides in the presentation
- [ ] The speed at which the slides advance
! Readable text needs adequate visual separation from the background.

? Which pairing is most likely to make ordinary text difficult to read?
- [ ] Dark navy lettering on white
- [x] Pale yellow lettering on a white background
- [ ] White lettering on a sufficiently dark solid background
! Very similar light foreground and background colors often have insufficient contrast.

? What is an appropriate use of the 4.5:1 normal-text contrast reference?
- [ ] Treat it as a guarantee that every projected slide is readable
- [ ] Ignore the presentation’s actual room and screen
- [x] Use it as a useful WCAG AA baseline while also testing the real display
! WCAG helps assess contrast; viewing conditions and other accessibility issues still matter.

? How should an instruction originally stating “Choose the green box” be improved?
- [x] Add an explicit category or action label rather than relying only on green
- [ ] Replace the word green with another color word
- [ ] Remove every label and rely on pictures alone
! Words and secondary visual cues allow meaning to survive color differences.

? What is the best reason to include an image on a slide?
- [ ] The slide has empty space that must be filled
- [x] It helps explain or orient viewers to the subject
- [ ] Every slide must have at least one image
! Visuals should support the slide’s communication task.

? A photograph covers the instructions. What is the most reliable first fix?
- [ ] Assume the audience will guess the hidden words
- [ ] Reduce all text to the smallest available font
- [x] Move the instructions to a clear readable area or remove the obstructing image
! Essential text should not be obscured by decorative content.

? What should a real chart communicate in addition to the plotted shapes?
- [x] Clear categories, values or units, and relevant context
- [ ] Only a color legend with no labels
- [ ] A dramatic effect regardless of the accuracy of the data
! Data visuals must remain understandable and honest.

? What does alternative text help provide?
- [ ] A decorative second color theme
- [x] A description of meaningful visual information to someone who cannot see the image
- [ ] Automatic copyright permission for every image
! Alt text or equivalent explanation makes relevant nontext content understandable.

? What does alignment mean in slide layout?
- [ ] Making every object the same color
- [ ] Adding a border around every sentence
- [x] Placing objects in a deliberate relationship such as sharing an edge
! Common guides or edges can make visual relationships clear.

? Why should a caption usually appear near its image?
- [x] Proximity makes their relationship easier to recognize
- [ ] All captions must touch the outer slide border
- [ ] A distant caption always improves readability
! Group related visual and explanatory material.

? How should useful negative space be treated?
- [ ] Fill it with decorative stars until no space remains
- [x] Retain it when it separates information and reduces clutter
- [ ] Treat it as an automatic error in the slide editor
! Unoccupied space can improve visual organization.

? Why are margins important?
- [ ] They guarantee that every image has appropriate copyright
- [ ] They remove the need to check the projector
- [x] They keep essential content away from risky slide edges
! Consistent margins help avoid clipping and crowding.

? What best describes consistency across a deck?
- [x] Similar design treatments for similar communication roles
- [ ] Every slide must have the exact same arrangement
- [ ] The use of a different typeface on each slide
! A repeatable system creates coherence while permitting different layouts.

? What is the best use of a theme or template?
- [ ] As proof the finished deck has no accessibility issues
- [x] As a starting system to apply and test consistent styles
- [ ] As a reason to avoid reviewing slide content
! Templates can support consistency but do not verify each finished slide.

? Which practice is appropriate before redesigning an approved deck?
- [ ] Overwrite the only approved copy immediately
- [ ] Publish the file publicly to test the colors
- [x] Make a separate editable copy and confirm permission to modify it
! Preserving the original and respecting permissions reduces avoidable risk.

? If an automatic design suggestion changes the meaning of a slide, what should you do?
- [x] Reject or revise it to preserve accurate essential information
- [ ] Keep it because software suggestions are always correct
- [ ] Remove the original source details from every slide
! Automated suggestions are drafts requiring human review.

? Why can working zoom in Google Slides be misleading?
- [ ] Zoom changes the actual meeting room dimensions
- [x] Editing zoom is not the same as the final audience display
- [ ] Zoom automatically fixes all low-contrast text
! Test the slide in its intended presentation view.

? What is a sensible response if a slide looks different in another presentation program?
- [ ] Assume all programs render every file identically
- [ ] Delete the editable original to avoid confusion
- [x] Inspect text, fonts, placement, and content after conversion
! Cross-application conversions can change formatting.

? What should an accessibility review check besides visible contrast?
- [x] Reading order, meaningful image descriptions, and color-independent information
- [ ] Only the number of decorative shapes
- [ ] Only whether the presentation is shorter than ten slides
! Access involves multiple forms of perception and interaction.

? What should you do after an automated checker reports no issues?
- [ ] Assume all captions and charts are accurate automatically
- [x] Still perform a manual content and audience review
- [ ] Remove all alternative descriptions from pictures
! Automated checkers do not replace human judgment.

? A chart uses invented practice figures. How should it be presented?
- [ ] Describe them as official attendance figures
- [ ] Omit context so the graph looks persuasive
- [x] Clearly identify the figures as fictional examples
! Distinguishing fictional examples from observed facts protects accuracy.

? For a slide shared without a speaker, what matters most?
- [x] Essential explanatory details must be available to independent viewers
- [ ] Every instruction can be omitted because the speaker knows it
- [ ] The file should contain only decorative photographs
! Standalone access requires adequate on-slide or accompanying information.

? What is the best way to correct a crowded slide?
- [ ] Add more animations to hide the crowding
- [x] Clarify the message, simplify or split content, then check the layout
- [ ] Make all objects smaller until they fit anywhere
! Structural editing usually works better than adding more effects.

? What constitutes a successful final design review?
- [ ] Every pixel of the slide is occupied
- [ ] All slides contain a different transition effect
- [x] The important message is accurate, legible, organized, and accessible in context
! Communication quality and practical accessibility are the actual finish criteria.

### Lesson summary

Effective slide design begins with a communication goal. Use visual hierarchy to establish priority; readable typography to keep text clear; sufficient contrast and explicit labels to preserve meaning; alignment, proximity, and negative space to organize objects; and repeated visual conventions to make the deck coherent. Use visuals when they communicate something useful, preserve accurate labels and data, and provide accessible alternatives where needed. Finally, preview the presentation as the audience will experience it. **Your design is effective when people can obtain and use the intended information—not simply when the file appears polished in an editor.**
