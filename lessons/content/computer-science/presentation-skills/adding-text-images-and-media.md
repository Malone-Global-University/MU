## Lesson 4: Adding Text, Images, and Media

A neighborhood library is preparing a short presentation for its fictional Saturday Reading Hour. The organizer has already created five slides, chosen a simple layout for each one, and applied a consistent theme. The slides are still incomplete. One needs a short description of the activity, another would benefit from a picture of books, and another might use a short recording of a sample reading. If the organizer adds every available picture, paragraph, sound, and video, the audience may become less informed rather than more informed.

A [[presentation|presentation]] communicates a selected message through a sequence of [[slide|slides]]. Each slide can contain [[text-box|text]], [[image|images]], [[shape|shapes]], and sometimes [[audio|audio]] or [[video|video]]. Adding these materials is not simply a question of finding the **Insert** command. You must choose material that supports the message, place it deliberately, keep it readable, verify that it works, and respect other people's information and creative work.

Lesson 1 introduced the purpose of presentations; Lesson 2 explained how to create and manage slides; Lesson 3 distinguished layouts and themes. **This lesson focuses on the content you place inside those prepared slides.** Later lessons address the overall slide sequence, detailed design rules, speaker notes, delivery, and sharing. You do not need to know how to edit videos or create professional illustrations to succeed here.

Microsoft PowerPoint on Windows 11 is the main desktop example. Google Slides and LibreOffice Impress provide comparable beginner workflows, but controls, capabilities, and supported [[file-format|file formats]] vary by application, device, account, and release. You may complete every required activity using fictional descriptions and paper slide plans. Using live audio or video is optional. No personal photographs, copyrighted downloads, recordings of other people, public uploads, or new software subscriptions are required.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 100–135 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Presentation Skills Lessons 1–3 recommended · **Practice equipment:** Paper and pencil or a presentation editor; optional self-created or properly licensed sample image; optional audio/video only when available and authorized · **Safety:** Use fictional names and locations; obtain permission before using recognizable people, private information, or recordings; do not download suspicious media or publish a practice deck.

> **Essential idea:** **Choose content for its purpose → insert it in a suitable place → make it readable and accessible → check that it behaves as intended → save the editable file.** Text, pictures, sounds, and videos are supporting evidence or explanation, not decorations that must appear on every slide.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish slide titles, body text, [[placeholder|placeholders]], and manually inserted text boxes.
2. Add and revise concise slide text without confusing the editable text area with the slide itself.
3. Explain the difference between inserting, moving, resizing, and [[crop|cropping]] an image.
4. Identify appropriate sources for images and describe basic permission, attribution, and privacy checks.
5. Recognize common presentation media, including audio, video, and animated images, and decide when each adds value.
6. Describe ordinary insertion workflows in PowerPoint, Google Slides, and LibreOffice Impress without assuming their interfaces are identical.
7. Distinguish an [[embedded-media|embedded media]] item from a [[linked-media|linked media]] item at an introductory level.
8. Explain how to write useful [[alt-text|alternative text]] and why captions or transcripts may be necessary.
9. Troubleshoot misplaced objects, distorted images, missing media, and playback problems using a safe sequence.
10. Build and review a five-slide fictional presentation using concise text and meaningful visual content.
11. Apply a final content-quality check before presenting, exporting, or sharing a deck.

## 1. What kinds of content can a slide contain?

A presentation slide is a digital workspace. You can place words and visual or audiovisual material onto it, but not every slide needs every kind of material. The question to ask is **what information must the audience understand here?** A title and a single sentence may be sufficient. A comparison may need two images. A demonstration may be clearer with a brief video. A crowded slide that includes all three does not automatically communicate better.

### Content has different jobs

The slide's [[slide-title|title]] identifies the main subject. [[body-text|Body text]] explains a few supporting ideas. An [[image|image]] might show an actual object, clarify a process, or supply evidence. A [[diagram|diagram]] explains relationships. An audio recording might demonstrate pronunciation. A video can show a process that is difficult to explain with still images. A chart may represent quantities, though making and interpreting charts is beyond the main scope of this lesson.

| Content type | Appropriate beginner use | Example in a library presentation | Main risk to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Title | Identify the slide's subject | What Happens at Reading Hour? | An unclear or missing heading |
| Short body text | Communicate essential details | Listen, read, and discuss together | Too many words or tiny text |
| Photo | Show a relevant place or object | A properly licensed picture of books | Unclear source or private details |
| Illustration or icon | Support a simple idea | A book symbol beside reading time | Meaning communicated only by an icon |
| Diagram | Explain steps or relationships | Choose a book, read, discuss | Confusing labels or flow |
| Audio clip | Provide sound needed for learning | A permitted short sample reading | No transcript, excessive volume |
| Video clip | Demonstrate an action or event | A short authorized reading demonstration | Playback failure or missing captions |
| Animated image | Illustrate movement when useful | A short instructional motion sequence | Distracting or inaccessible animation |

The useful distinction is between **meaningful content** and **decoration**. A clear photograph of a physical object can convey information. A collection of randomly selected clip-art pictures may merely consume space. A decorative icon might help with visual interest, but the slide should not become impossible to understand when the icon is not visible.

### One message, selected supporting material

Suppose Slide 3 is titled **When and Where?** and contains the fictional details **Saturday, 10:00 a.m.** and **Community Room**. The slide already communicates its key point with text. A large photograph of an unrelated library building might compete with that information. A small schematic map could help only if the actual route were necessary and accurate; because the example location is fictional, a map would be misleading. Good design includes the ability to leave unnecessary material out.

For Slide 2, **What Happens at Reading Hour?**, a meaningful image of a book and three short steps may help audience comprehension. The words explain the activity; the picture offers context. There is no requirement to insert a video merely because a video button is available.

### Recognize slide objects and selection

A [[slide-object|slide object]] is an element that can usually be selected and changed separately from the slide background. Text boxes, photos, icons, and media players can be separate objects. To edit the words inside a text box, you generally need the [[text-cursor|text cursor]] inside that box. To move or resize the entire box, you generally select its [[object-border|object border]] or handles instead.

This distinction matters because clicking the same area in slightly different ways can cause different results. When the whole text box is selected, pressing Delete might remove the box and its contents. When the text cursor is active, pressing Delete may remove one character. Before making a change, look for the visible selection state. The exact handles, borders, and command names depend on the editor.

### Placeholders and freely inserted items

A [[placeholder|placeholder]] is a prepared area supplied by the [[slide-layout|slide layout]]. For example, a Title and Content slide may already have a place for a title and another for information. A manually inserted text box is added separately, independent of the prearranged placeholders. Either can hold useful text, but a layout's title placeholder may provide more predictable title styling and [[reading-order|reading order]] than several manually stacked boxes.

| Question | Placeholder | Manually inserted item |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where does it come from? | Existing slide layout | An insertion command or paste operation |
| What is it useful for? | Expected title and content positions | Extra labels, explanatory notes, special arrangements |
| What happens after a layout change? | Its position or formatting may change | It may stay where it was, possibly overlapping other elements |
| What should you check? | Correct type and content | Placement, legibility, and reading order |

A beginner can usually start with placeholders and add manual objects only when there is a clear reason. Extra control is useful, but extra objects also create more work during resizing, accessibility review, and later theme changes.

### Worked example: a content decision

A neighborhood library wants to introduce **Book Discussion**. The planned slide has a title, two sentences, five pictures, a short song, and a two-minute online video. The lesson objective is simply that participants will **share what they liked about a book**. A better version may have this title, one sentence, and one relevant picture. The song does not demonstrate book discussion, and the video requires time, reliable playback, captions, and permission. Removing them makes the slide easier to understand and deliver.

> **Content selection rule:** Ask what the audience should remember from the slide. If an object does not support that point, it may not belong there.

## 2. How do you add and edit slide text?

Text is often the first material entered into a new presentation. Well-chosen words can communicate exact details that pictures cannot: a date, location, person responsible, action to take, or distinction between two options. Presentation text is different from writing a full report because the audience may need to **read and listen at the same time**. The most useful slide text usually highlights the message while the presenter or surrounding context explains the details.

### Identify the text area before typing

When you open a new slide with a title-and-content layout, you might see instructions such as **Click to add title** or **Click to add text**. These are placeholder prompts, not finished slide content. Click or activate the intended area, then enter the real title or body information. If the editor is in read-only mode, changes may not be permitted; do not mistake that for a broken keyboard.

A simple text-entry sequence is:

1. Open the intended slide in the normal editing view.
2. Locate the title or content placeholder, or use the editor's text-box insertion control when a separate box is needed.
3. Activate the text area and confirm the insertion cursor is visible.
4. Type a short heading or statement using the fictional practice information.
5. Read the result and remove duplicate, unnecessary, or misspelled words.
6. Click outside the text area to inspect the overall slide and ensure nothing is cut off.
7. Save the document or confirm the online editor's save status.

The specific command may appear as **Insert > Text Box**, a toolbar button, or a placeholder. If the editor presents a different interface, use its own help for **text box** rather than repeatedly pressing unrelated buttons.

### Titles, body text, and emphasis

A title should tell the audience what the slide is about. A body sentence should develop that point. Use emphasis selectively to draw attention to an important action or value, not to make every word compete for attention. [[font-size|Font size]], [[font|font]] choice, and [[contrast|contrast]] affect legibility. Detailed design standards belong in the later Basic Design Rules lesson; here your responsibility is to avoid obvious text problems while adding content.

| Less effective wording | More useful slide wording | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduction | What Happens at Reading Hour? | Communicates the subject |
| We have a very nice and fun and wonderful time here | Read together and share ideas | Removes unsupported praise and repetition |
| INFORMATION ABOUT THE TIME AND LOCATION | Saturday at 10:00 a.m. | Gives the audience the needed fact |
| All participants should please make sure that they remember | Please bring a book, if you have one | States the action directly |
| This slide is about how to join our program | Ask library staff for current details | Avoids inventing registration instructions |

An exact schedule or address should be verified before publication. In these exercises, times and names are fictional. If using the lesson as the basis for a real event, replace every sample detail with confirmed information.

### Use short lists deliberately

A [[bulleted-list|bulleted list]] can help when the items belong to one category and the order does not matter. A [[numbered-list|numbered list]] is more useful when the order is significant, such as a procedure. You do not need six or seven bullets merely to fill space. If the slide contains a complete essay, decide whether the detail belongs in speaker notes, a handout, or separate slides.

Compare two ways to explain what participants do:

| Long paragraph on one slide | Focused on-slide list |
| --- | --- |
| At Reading Hour, we will first ask all the people who are there to choose a book, and then afterward each person will take some time to read, and finally people may talk about something they enjoyed reading. | Choose a book; read quietly; share one idea |

The shorter list does not provide every possible instruction, but it identifies the sequence the audience needs. If the purpose is a self-study handout that must stand alone, a little more explanation may be warranted. **Concise** means appropriately selective, not incomplete.

### Move and resize a text box safely

If text overlaps a picture, first consider whether the slide contains too much material or uses an unsuitable layout. Moving a box may help, but movement alone cannot solve overcrowding. When you move an object, keep it on the slide's visible canvas and separate it from unrelated material. When you resize the box, inspect where lines wrap and whether the font size changed unexpectedly. Do not drag a box so narrow that an ordinary word becomes a vertical stack of letters.

A common mistake is to drag a corner while believing you are editing text. Another is to select the entire object and accidentally delete it. [[undo|Undo]] often reverses a recent supported change. If you are unsure what is selected, click away, select the correct object again, and make one small change at a time. Saving a separate editable practice copy gives you an additional recovery option.

### Copying and pasting text

[[copy-paste|Copy and paste]] is useful when you have approved text from a trusted source. It can also bring unexpected formatting, line breaks, oversized fonts, or hyperlinks. Some editors offer paste choices that match the destination design or retain the source formatting. These options can produce very different-looking slides. Inspect pasted content for correctness, length, and consistency; do not assume that a successful paste means the material fits or is accurate.

If you copy information from a website, check its reliability, relevant date, and whether reproduction is appropriate. Quoting a short sourced statement for a legitimate purpose is different from copying an entire article into a slide. The audience should be able to distinguish your own explanation from material borrowed from someone else.

### Links and text on slides

A [[hyperlink|hyperlink]] can connect text or an object to a website, file, or another slide, depending on the editor. Links are useful when viewers will have access to the editable or shared presentation and can activate them. They are less useful when the deck is displayed on a projector and the audience cannot click anything. For projected material, a clear resource name, handout, or appropriate short address may be more practical.

Never use a suspicious or unverified link simply because it looks like the name of a familiar organization. Check the actual destination before embedding a link. If you are sharing a deck beyond your own computer, remember that a link to a private local file may work only on the machine where it was created.

### Worked example: writing Slide 4

The fictional Reading Hour slide titled **What to Bring** starts with this paragraph: *We would like all interested individuals to bring a favorite book if they happen to have one available, and we want everyone to understand that participation is still possible even if they do not have a personal book to bring.*

A more accessible on-slide version could be:

- **Bring a favorite book, if available.**
- **No book? You can still participate.**

The underlying message has not changed. The text communicates a choice rather than inventing a requirement. A book image is optional; the written explanation remains understandable without it. This is an example of **editing for meaning**, not merely making the font smaller.

## 3. How do you add, resize, and position images?

An [[image|image]] is visual information: a photograph, drawing, diagram, screenshot, or other picture. Inserting one into a slide can provide context, show an example, or support a comparison. A picture is not automatically necessary. If the image does not clarify a point or illustrate something the learner needs to see, words may be more effective.

### Choose the source before the picture

A program may offer pictures stored on your computer, a built-in stock collection, cloud storage, an online search, or a URL. These sources are not interchangeable. A photograph of a book that you took yourself may be straightforward to use if it reveals no private material. A picture copied from a commercial website may be protected by [[copyright|copyright]]. A publicly searchable image is not necessarily free to copy, modify, or republish.

Common image-source categories are:

- **Self-created image:** A photograph or drawing you made, subject to other people's privacy and any rights associated with depicted material.
- **Approved organization image:** An illustration or photograph supplied by an authorized school, library, or workplace under its rules.
- **Licensed or stock image:** Media distributed under a specific permission or [[license|license]] describing allowed uses and restrictions.
- **Public-domain image:** Material not subject to copyright restrictions in the relevant context; other legal or ethical restrictions may still apply.
- **Random internet search result:** A lead that requires verification of the source and usage rights before reuse, not permission in itself.

| Source situation | First decision | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You drew a simple book on paper | You can use a scan or digital drawing if the context is appropriate | You created the illustration |
| An editor offers approved stock icons | Check the terms and permitted uses | Product and license conditions vary |
| A search engine shows a beautiful photo | Open the source and review rights | Search visibility is not a reuse license |
| A friend sends a photo of several children | Ask about consent and institutional rules; prefer not to use it in beginner practice | Privacy and permissions may be unclear |
| A business website has a logo | Use only with an appropriate reason and permission where required | Logos may involve trademark and copyright considerations |
| A site claims an image is Creative Commons | Verify the exact license and requested attribution | Different CC licenses have different conditions |

For required practice, a **text description of an imagined picture** is enough. You can write, for example, *Insert a simple book drawing here* rather than downloading an unfamiliar file. The exercise tests whether you know where an image would belong and why, not whether you possess an image library.

### Inserting is different from choosing a background

Using **Insert Picture** or **Insert Image** generally adds a selectable picture object onto the current slide. Setting an image as a background changes the surface behind the slide content. A photo placed on top of the slide may move or overlap text. A background image can make the text difficult to read. Do not confuse the two operations simply because both use a photo.

A sensible beginner sequence for adding a local picture is:

1. Save an editable copy of the practice presentation.
2. Select the slide intended to contain the picture.
3. Choose the application's image insertion command.
4. Select a trusted local file or properly licensed source.
5. Confirm the picture appears on the expected slide.
6. Adjust its placement and size without hiding essential text.
7. Add alternative text when the picture communicates information.
8. View the slide at presentation size and verify the result.

The program may let you insert through a ribbon, toolbar, menu, picture placeholder, or context command. Do not assume the editor automatically knows which picture is relevant or where it should go.

### Understanding resize, crop, rotate, and replace

These operations do different jobs. [[resize|Resizing]] changes the displayed dimensions of a picture. [[crop|Cropping]] hides or removes portions of the picture from the visible composition, according to the tool's behavior. [[rotate|Rotating]] changes orientation. **Replace image** or **Change picture** may preserve some existing positioning while using a different source, though exact results differ among editors.

| Action | What changes on the slide? | Beginner check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Insert | A new image object appears | Is it on the right slide? |
| Move | The image changes position | Does it cover words or other objects? |
| Resize | The picture becomes larger or smaller | Is it still clear and proportionate? |
| Crop | Only a selected portion remains visible | Was important information cut away? |
| Rotate | The picture turns | Is its orientation intentional? |
| Replace | A different image takes the selected role | Does the replacement convey the same meaning? |
| Delete | The image is removed | Was it decorative or important? |

### Keep the image's proportions

A picture has an [[aspect-ratio|aspect ratio]]: the relationship between its width and height. If you stretch a photograph horizontally but not vertically, a circular clock can become an oval. This is called [[distortion|distortion]]. Many presentation editors help preserve proportions when resizing from a corner or when **Lock aspect ratio** is enabled, but do not depend on a universal shortcut. Inspect the picture after every significant resize.

Sometimes the image's natural shape does not match the placeholder. A wide photograph may not fit a tall column without cropping. Ask which part carries the meaning. For a photograph of a complete bookshelf, cropping the center could be acceptable if only the books matter. For a diagram with labels along the edges, cropping could destroy its meaning. **Do not crop away labels, units, explanatory markings, or people in ways that make the resulting message misleading.**

### Clarity, pixelation, and file size

A [[pixel|pixel]] is one small unit of digital image information. A small, low-resolution picture may become visibly blurry or blocky when enlarged. This effect is often called [[pixelation|pixelation]]. Enlarging a picture does not automatically create the missing detail. Choose an image with enough quality for the intended display, and avoid excessive enlargement. An image that looks sharp in a tiny editor preview may look poor on a projected screen.

Larger, high-resolution pictures may increase the presentation's file size. Video and audio can increase it much more. File size matters because a deck must be stored, opened, transmitted, and sometimes shared over slow connections. The objective is sufficient quality without unnecessarily enormous files. Compressing media may help, but aggressive compression can reduce clarity or audio quality. Save the original source if your organization permits and inspect the final result.

Some common image file extensions are [[jpeg|JPEG]] (`.jpg` or `.jpeg`), [[png|PNG]] (`.png`), [[gif|GIF]] (`.gif`), and [[svg|SVG]] (`.svg`). These files are not identical. JPEG is frequently used for photos; PNG is useful for many illustrations and screenshots; GIF may be animated; SVG is a vector format. **Support for any given format depends on the specific presentation editor and version.** A file extension alone is not proof that a file is safe or compatible.

### Avoid text trapped inside a picture

A screenshot of a paragraph may look like text, but to the editor it can be one image object rather than editable slide text. That can make the words difficult to resize, search, translate, or read with assistive technology. If words carry essential meaning, place them as genuine slide text whenever possible. If an image contains necessary labels, provide an accessible equivalent or explanation. The visual does not replace the need for accurate, readable content.

The same principle applies to charts. A picture of a chart may communicate a pattern visually, but the title, units, and key finding should not disappear when the picture cannot be seen. If it matters that attendance rose from 12 to 18 people, those numbers are worth stating in text as well.

### Image placement and layering

Objects on a slide may overlap. The [[layer-order|layer order]] determines which is in front of another. A program may provide commands such as **Bring Forward** or **Send Backward**. These controls can be useful when a photo sits behind a label, but they can also hide content. If a picture covers the slide title, do not assume the title was deleted. Try selecting the picture, moving it, or adjusting the order. Use Undo if you have just made an accidental change.

A clear layout typically separates the image from the explanatory text. For a two-column arrangement, place a small, readable picture in one region and the relevant statement in the other. For a photograph that demonstrates a particular object, position a short label near that object. Consistency matters: a label far from the image it describes may lead the audience to connect it with the wrong object.

### Worked example: choosing one useful visual

Slide 2 of the Reading Hour presentation currently says: **Participants choose a book, read quietly, and share one idea.** Three candidate images are available in a fictional image catalog:

| Candidate picture | Evaluation | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A simple licensed illustration of an open book | Relevant, visually quiet, and easy to describe | Appropriate |
| A dramatic city skyline at night | Does not explain the reading activity | Omit |
| A recognizable child reading in a private classroom | Could be relevant, but rights and consent are unknown | Do not use without proper authorization |

The first image is a sensible choice. Its alternative text might be **Illustration of an open book** if the drawing identifies the subject. If the slide already says **Bring a book** and the illustration provides no additional meaning, the image might instead be decorative and be marked appropriately in a tool that supports that distinction. Alternative text should reflect purpose, not merely identify every visible shape.

## 4. When should you use audio, video, and other media?

[[multimedia|Multimedia]] combines more than one content type, such as text, pictures, sounds, and moving images. A slide with a small photograph is already visual, but inserting audio or video creates new requirements for playback, timing, file compatibility, accessibility, and permission. Use media when it provides evidence or demonstration that a still image and sentence cannot convey as effectively.

### Audio: what does sound contribute?

An [[audio|audio]] clip may contain music, voice, an interview excerpt, a pronunciation example, or a sound associated with an instructional topic. In a language class, an authorized short recording can demonstrate how a word is pronounced. In a safety lesson, an authorized sample of a warning signal might clarify what listeners should recognize. In the library's invitation slide, unrelated background music could simply compete with speech.

Before adding audio, ask:

1. Does hearing the sound help the audience learn something necessary?
2. Do I have permission to use this recording and any performance within it?
3. Is the volume suitable for the viewing environment?
4. Can a person unable to hear the sound obtain the meaningful information through a [[transcript|transcript]] or equivalent text?
5. Can I start, pause, or stop playback without disrupting the presentation?
6. Will the audio still work on the device and connection used for delivery?

Audio files may have extensions such as `.mp3` or `.wav`. A format familiar from a music player is not guaranteed to play in every presentation application. The file's [[codec|codec]], operating system, application version, and how it was added may affect compatibility.

### Video: when motion conveys meaning

A [[video|video]] clip can demonstrate a physical procedure, a sequence of actions, or a real process. It should serve the learning objective rather than fill time. A 15-second, correctly explained demonstration may be more useful than a long recording that the presenter cannot control. When a video contains speech, provide accurate [[caption|captions]] or another appropriate accessible equivalent. If meaningful visual actions are not described in speech, additional verbal description may be needed.

Video can also introduce risk: playback may require internet access, the linked source may change or disappear, advertisements or unrelated recommendations may appear, and a recording may reveal people who did not consent to being shown. Avoid surprising the audience with an unfamiliar clip. Review the entire intended segment in advance, check whether it starts at the right point, and confirm that its contents remain appropriate.

### Embedded and linked media

A media item can be **embedded** in the presentation file, **linked** to a separate local file, or accessed as an online resource, depending on the editor. With [[embedded-media|embedded media]], the actual material may be packaged with the presentation. With [[linked-media|linked media]], the presentation may depend on another file or service remaining available. The distinction affects what happens when the deck moves to another computer.

| Media arrangement | Potential benefit | Main dependency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Embedded local clip | May travel with the presentation file | File size and compatible player/codec |
| Linked local clip | Can keep the deck smaller | Original file path and availability |
| Online or cloud video | May avoid storing the entire video in the deck | Connection, permissions, provider, and content availability |
| Google Drive audio in a Google Slides deck | Integrates with supported Google Slides audio workflow | Drive access, file availability, and correct sharing permissions |

Do not assume that every program embeds the same way. Google Slides, for example, can reference selected Drive-hosted audio or video rather than bundling a standalone multimedia file into a conventional offline PowerPoint package. Moving or downloading a presentation may affect playback behavior. **Test on the actual destination device or in an equivalent environment.**

### Playback controls, timing, and autoplay

A media object may offer [[playback|playback]] controls such as Play, Pause, volume, timing, or whether to start automatically. The available choices vary. [[autoplay|Autoplay]] means media begins according to a configured event rather than waiting for a deliberate click. For a beginner, starting media on purpose is often easier to manage, especially when you are speaking at the same time.

An automatically playing sound can interrupt the presenter or start at excessive volume. A video can continue when the speaker is ready to discuss a different slide. The mere fact that a program offers an automatic option is not a reason to enable it. If playback timing matters, rehearse with the actual presentation sequence.

Animated images create similar concerns. A moving GIF may appear simple to insert, but its movement can distract readers and some animation can present accessibility concerns. Avoid unnecessary flashing, rapid motion, or effects that prevent the audience from concentrating. Prefer a stable picture when movement is not essential.

### Media format and reliability questions

If a video does not play, the cause could be an unsupported format or codec, a missing linked file, an expired or unavailable online resource, a permission problem, a muted device, or an interruption in network access. The sensible response is not to download random 'codec packs' or media converters from a pop-up. Instead, check the editor's official supported formats, verify the source and permissions, use a trusted conversion workflow if authorized, or provide a reliable fallback.

A [[fallback|fallback]] is an alternative way to communicate the same important information when the intended media cannot play. For a video demonstrating three steps, a slide with a short list and three properly authorized still images may work. For a pronunciation sample, a written phonetic guide can supplement a recording, though it is not a perfect substitute for audible speech. Plan fallbacks for important messages rather than relying on one fragile clip.

### Worked example: should the library add a video?

A presenter proposes a three-minute internet video showing an unknown group of children reading. The presentation lasts only four minutes. The video source has not been checked, it contains speech without captions, and the library room's network connection is uncertain. The video is not yet appropriate for the deck. A single book illustration plus a brief explanation of the activity meets the purpose without adding those risks. If the program later needs a demonstration, the library can seek permission, make an accessible short clip, and test it in the actual venue.

> **Media decision rule:** When the audience can learn the point without sound or motion, select the simpler option unless there is a clear instructional benefit to the media.

## 5. How do you insert text, images, and media in common applications?

The basic workflow stays similar across editors: select a slide, insert the chosen content, adjust its placement, review accessibility and permissions, and test the result. The names and locations of commands can differ, particularly between desktop, web, and mobile editions. This section describes representative desktop paths, not universal instructions that will remain identical in every release.

### Microsoft PowerPoint on Windows

PowerPoint commonly groups insertion tools on the **Insert** tab of the [[ribbon|ribbon]]. A slide's built-in title or content placeholder may already be suitable for text. If you need a separate text object, **Insert > Text Box** is a common path in modern desktop editions. After you select a text box, click inside it to edit the characters; select its border to move the whole object. Keep track of which slide thumbnail is selected so the text appears in the intended place.

For an image, use **Insert > Pictures** and choose a source such as **This Device**, supported stock imagery, or another available location. The available source names vary by account and release. Once inserted, select the picture to access its formatting controls. You can adjust its size and position and may be able to crop or apply a picture layout. If a suggested design change obscures the text or changes the visual meaning, reject or undo it.

To insert sound or video, use the relevant **Insert > Audio** or **Insert > Video** controls in an edition that supports the desired media. You may have local-file, recording, stock, or online choices depending on the version. After insertion, review the playback controls and test the clip in Slide Show mode. **A media object visible in editing mode is not proof that playback will succeed during delivery.** Save the deck as an editable presentation file before exporting a static version.

A typical introductory workflow is:

1. Select a content slide in the thumbnail pane.
2. Activate the title placeholder and type a concise heading.
3. Fill an appropriate body placeholder with one or two short points.
4. Add a picture only if it demonstrates or supports the point.
5. Resize the picture proportionately and place it away from essential text.
6. Supply appropriate alternative text, or mark a purely decorative image appropriately where supported.
7. If adding a permitted media sample, test volume, start conditions, and playback.
8. Run the available accessibility check and inspect the presentation in Slide Show mode.
9. Save and reopen the editable deck, checking that the object remains where intended.

Do not assume that PowerPoint for the web, PowerPoint on macOS, and PowerPoint on a phone provide exactly the same media insertion or export capabilities as Windows desktop. If a feature is unavailable, use a supported alternative rather than trying to bypass account or device restrictions.

### Google Slides in a desktop browser

Google Slides provides editing tools in a web-based presentation interface. Click a prepared placeholder to enter text. To create a separate text box, a common route is **Insert > Text box** or the text-box toolbar control. Use **Insert > Image** to choose an available image source, which may include your computer, Drive, Photos, stock or web options, or another supported source. Available options and their labels can change.

For video, Google Slides supports insertion through its **Insert > Video** workflow, subject to currently available supported sources and permissions. For audio, its desktop workflow supports inserting eligible audio stored in [[google-drive|Google Drive]], commonly `.mp3` or `.wav`, through **Insert > Audio**. The audio object may appear as an icon. Select it to inspect playback and other formatting controls. This is not necessarily the same as embedding an MP3 into an offline PowerPoint file.

When using Drive-hosted media, check more than the slide itself. Verify the selected file still exists, the account has access, and intended viewers have the necessary permission. Inappropriate access changes can expose private files, while restrictive permissions can cause playback failures for legitimate viewers. **Do not make an unrelated private folder public merely to fix one slide.** Use approved sharing methods or choose an authorized alternative.

The browser's save and synchronization state also matters. An online editor may save automatically, but a lost connection, missing permission, or signed-out session can prevent edits from reaching the intended copy. Review the file title and current save state before you close it. Downloading or exporting may change what embedded or linked media can do; test that exact exported version.

### LibreOffice Impress

LibreOffice Impress is a desktop presentation editor. It commonly provides **Insert > Text Box** for a separate text object and **Insert > Image** for a picture. You can also click a layout-provided text area to enter text. Once an image is selected, use supported position, size, and cropping controls as appropriate. LibreOffice provides **Insert > Audio or Video** for media in supported environments; available playback depends partly on the platform and installed multimedia components.

LibreOffice presentations commonly use the [[odp|ODP]] format for editable files, and Impress can work with certain other formats. A presentation may look or behave differently after conversion. Do not assume that every animation, image effect, font, or media relationship will carry into another editor without changes. For an assignment that requires a particular output format, save a working native copy and inspect the exported copy separately.

If a media file works on one computer but not another, investigate compatibility and file location with official documentation. The correct response may be to use a supported local media format, adjust the authorized file arrangement, or present the essential steps with text and pictures. Installing an unknown extension downloaded from a questionable website is not a sound troubleshooting step.

### Comparison of representative tasks

| Task | PowerPoint desktop | Google Slides desktop browser | LibreOffice Impress desktop |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Enter text in a prepared area | Click the layout placeholder | Click the layout placeholder | Click the layout text area |
| Insert a separate text box | Insert > Text Box | Insert > Text box | Insert > Text Box |
| Add a picture | Insert > Pictures | Insert > Image | Insert > Image |
| Add video | Insert > Video, where supported | Insert > Video, where supported | Insert > Audio or Video |
| Add audio | Insert > Audio, where supported | Insert > Audio from eligible Drive files | Insert > Audio or Video |
| Edit an object's position | Select object and reposition | Select object and reposition | Select object and reposition |

These are conceptual directions. The particular interface, supported source choices, and accessibility controls can vary. A school-managed application might also disable a feature. For current information, use the official resources at the end of the lesson.

### Desktop, web, phone, and tablet differences

Presentation editors on a phone can often show and make basic changes to slides, but a small touch screen may make precise object placement difficult. Some editing and media functions may be unavailable or found in different menus. A learner who can view a presentation on a phone has not necessarily gained full editing access. Where possible, perform complex placement, media compatibility testing, and accessibility inspection in an appropriate desktop interface.

The keyboard and mouse workflows above are only examples. Learners using a [[screen-reader|screen reader]], keyboard navigation, switch device, speech input, or touchscreen may use different selection methods. The goal is the same: reach the intended object, change it without unintended effects, and review the result. Consult the application's accessibility help for supported alternate interactions.

### Saving and exporting are separate decisions

An [[editable-file|editable presentation file]] preserves slides and supported objects so you can revise them in an editor. A [[pdf|PDF]] export is generally a fixed-document representation and may not preserve working audio, video, or editable objects. A video export can preserve a recorded presentation as video, but it is not equivalent to an editable slide deck. An image export may lose the separate text and object structure entirely.

| Output | Usually best for | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Editable presentation file | Continuing work or approved collaboration | Requires compatible editing software and permissions |
| PDF handout | Reading static slides without editing | Interactive media and animations generally do not transfer |
| Presentation video | Viewing a recorded sequence | Slide text and objects are not separately editable as slide content |
| Individual slide images | Thumbnails or approved visual references | Text may no longer be accessible as ordinary editable text |

Keep the source file until you have verified that the required export works. A PDF that displays every slide is not proof that a video was successfully embedded in the original deck. Test the version the actual audience will receive.

## 6. How do you check accessibility, rights, and reliability?

The content should be useful to people who read, listen, use assistive technology, or encounter the presentation in less-than-ideal conditions. Accessibility and reliability are not separate finishing touches. They influence which material to add and how to describe it. A well-constructed deck also respects the rights and privacy of people represented in images, recordings, or quoted materials.

### Write alternative text for meaningful visuals

[[alt-text|Alternative text]], often called *alt text*, is a short textual description associated with a nontext object so that people using assistive technologies can understand its relevant purpose or content. It is not a place to repeat a filename such as `IMG_0043.jpg` or write a vague phrase such as *picture of something*. A useful description captures what matters in the context of the slide.

| Visual and its purpose | Less useful alt text | More useful alt text |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Photo shows one open book for a slide about reading | Image | An open book lying on a table |
| Diagram communicates a three-step sequence | Diagram | Three steps in order: choose a book, read, then share an idea |
| Photo compares empty and full bookshelves | Bookshelf | Side-by-side shelves: the left has few books and the right is full |
| Decorative star beside a title already saying Welcome | Gold star | Mark decorative when the editor supports it |
| Image contains a written event time | Schedule picture | Put the event time in real slide text; describe other meaningful visual details as needed |

The best description is context-dependent. If a picture identifies a particular species of bird in a science lesson, *bird* may be too vague; if it merely illustrates the idea of nature, a specific species may be unnecessary. Do not invent facts about people or objects that the picture does not establish. Some tools can propose descriptions automatically; review them rather than assuming they are accurate.

### Captions, transcripts, and descriptions

[[caption|Captions]] present spoken words and sometimes meaningful audio information synchronized with video. A [[transcript|transcript]] is a text representation of spoken or otherwise relevant audio content. They are related but not identical. Captions help a viewer follow speech while the video plays; a transcript helps someone read or search the same content independently. A full accessible experience may also require a description of visual actions that are not expressed in the soundtrack.

A title and an audio icon alone do not provide access to what an audio recording says. When sound carries required information, supply an appropriate text alternative. If a video shows a person demonstrating how to place a book onto a shelf without speaking, captions of nonexistent speech will not explain the action. Add meaningful spoken description or an accessible written sequence as appropriate.

Media volume and playback settings should respect the environment. Avoid videos that rely on rapidly flashing content or unexpected loud audio. **A video being technically playable does not mean it is comfortable, accessible, or appropriate.** For essential instructional content, have a non-video fallback.

### Permissions and attribution

Before using someone else's image, video, audio, illustration, or written passage, identify the source and whether you have a lawful, appropriate basis for that use. [[attribution|Attribution]] gives credit to the creator or source where required or appropriate. It is not a substitute for obtaining permission when permission is necessary. A license may allow reuse only under certain conditions, such as providing credit, keeping modifications under the same terms, or restricting certain uses.

A useful attribution record may include the creator, title, source location, license name and URL, and whether you modified the material, as applicable. Do not add invented license claims to a picture. Some content is in the public domain or available under terms with different requirements; do not assume every image has identical rules. For educational and business presentations, check organizational policies and applicable licensing terms rather than declaring all educational uses automatically exempt from copyright requirements.

When a person is recognizable in a photograph or recording, also consider privacy, consent, and the purpose of the presentation. A technically licensed photograph can still be inappropriate in a particular setting. Avoid including private addresses, student names, confidential workplace screens, medical information, or other unnecessary personal details in practice materials.

### Avoid accidentally disclosing private information

A [[screenshot|screenshot]] is an image of what appears on a screen. Screenshots are convenient for demonstrations, but they may expose names, email messages, login status, phone numbers, document titles, browser tabs, or private notifications. Cropping or covering part of a screenshot does not always address the underlying privacy problem, and copies of the original may remain available elsewhere. Prefer a fictional screen or purpose-made example; check the entire image before adding it.

The same caution applies to recorded audio and video. A clip can capture a background conversation, a visible badge, a student's face, or the name of a restricted file. Review the complete material, not just the first frame. Do not record other people as part of this lesson.

### Work with trusted files and sources

A media file may be mislabeled, incompatible, damaged, or unsafe. Use trusted sources and the presentation editor's own supported insertion workflows. Do not run a downloaded executable just because a webpage claims it is needed to view a video. Avoid approving unexpected permission requests or signing into an unfamiliar site to obtain a picture. If a licensed image is not available, use a text-only slide or your own drawing instead.

Online sources can change. A clip embedded from a third-party platform may be removed, renamed, restricted, or replaced. An educational presentation should not rely exclusively on a fragile source for an essential point. Keep the relevant written explanation in the deck or an authorized handout so the lesson remains meaningful.

### A structured troubleshooting sequence

When an inserted object seems wrong, diagnose the actual problem before experimenting with unrelated settings. The basic process is: **identify what is wrong → determine whether it involves content, appearance, access, or playback → make one reversible correction → test again → save the working result.**

| Symptom | Possible cause | Safe first step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You type but nothing appears | Text box not active or file is read-only | Confirm edit permission and put the cursor in the text area |
| Entire text box moved | Object border was selected | Reposition it or use Undo |
| Text overlaps a photo | Too many objects or unsuitable layout | Simplify content or reposition without hiding information |
| Photo looks stretched | Proportions were not preserved | Restore and resize proportionately |
| Important part of an image is missing | Image was cropped | Reopen crop controls and inspect the original |
| Media icon appears but nothing plays | Source, compatibility, access, or device issue | Test the source and presentation environment |
| Online video works for editor but not audience | Viewer permission or network dependency | Verify legitimate viewer access and provide a fallback |
| Audience cannot understand an image with a screen reader | Missing or inadequate alternative text | Add or correct meaningful alt text |
| Background music obscures speech | Volume or timing is inappropriate | Reduce, pause, or remove unnecessary audio |
| Exported PDF has no playable clip | Static export does not preserve media playback | Share a suitable supported format or a separate approved clip |

Avoid changing multiple settings simultaneously because you may lose track of which action solved the problem. Documenting the intended effect of each inserted item can make troubleshooting and later revisions easier.

### Final slide and deck review

When you finish adding content, inspect the complete deck in normal editing mode and then in an audience-facing presentation mode. Verify the wording, titles, picture sources, captions, alt text, and accessibility. Check whether objects sit on the wrong slides, cover one another, or extend beyond the visible area. If the deck includes audio or video, play each permitted clip from start to finish on the intended device or a reasonable equivalent.

A beginner's final checklist is:

1. Each slide has a clear point and an accurate title.
2. Slide text is readable, concise, and not hidden.
3. Each picture contributes information or is appropriately decorative.
4. Pictures are not stretched, misleadingly cropped, or visibly damaged.
5. Sources, licenses, attribution, and privacy have been considered.
6. Meaningful images have suitable text alternatives.
7. Important audio and video have suitable accessible equivalents.
8. Playback works, or the slide has a practical fallback.
9. The editable file is saved in the intended location.
10. A final review uses the form in which the audience will see the presentation.

> **Reliability principle:** A presentation is ready only when the intended audience can receive and understand its message in the actual delivery format—not simply when the editor displays all its objects.

## 7. Guided practice: build and check a content-rich deck

The following activities can be completed using paper and pencil, a word-processing document, or a slide editor. When software is available, use a new fictional deck or a separate copy of an earlier practice deck. **Do not open, alter, or upload a real organization's slide file, photograph a real person, or publish a deck to complete these exercises.** Each activity provides an expandable model answer. Compare your reasoning with the example rather than assuming there is only one acceptable design.

### Activity 1: Match content to purpose

For each scenario, choose a reasonable content type: short text, still image, diagram, audio, video, or no additional media. A combination may be appropriate if you can explain why. Write one sentence describing the intended learning benefit.

| Scenario | Your content choice | Your reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A slide lists the library's fictional meeting time and room | ______ | ______ |
| A slide introduces the appearance of a book cover | ______ | ______ |
| A language class teaches the sound of a new word | ______ | ______ |
| A slide teaches the sequence of choosing, reading, and discussing a book | ______ | ______ |
| A four-minute invitation contains a decorative three-minute song | ______ | ______ |

Ask whether the audience needs precise facts, visual identification, a sequence, or actual sound or motion. Avoid selecting multimedia simply to make the slide appear sophisticated.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answers
| Scenario | Reasonable choice | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meeting time and room | Short text | Words communicate the exact details directly |
| Appearance of book cover | Authorized image with text label | The visual appearance is the information being taught |
| Pronunciation lesson | Authorized audio plus an accessible text equivalent | Hearing the pronunciation adds information that spelling alone may not convey |
| Three-step reading sequence | Short numbered text or simple diagram | The ordering matters more than motion |
| Decorative three-minute song | No additional media | The sound is not needed and consumes most of the allotted time |

Other answers can be sound if they explain an actual instructional benefit, a suitable alternative for accessibility, and relevant permissions. A video for pronunciation might be useful in a lesson where mouth movement is important, but audio alone often suffices for an introductory listening example.
:::

### Activity 2: Turn a paragraph into useful slide text

A fictional program notice says:

*We are very excited to let everyone know that our Saturday Reading Hour takes place in the community room at 10:00 in the morning, and everyone who might want to come should know that it is perfectly fine if they do not have a personal book of their own because they can still participate.*

Prepare a slide title and two or three short supporting lines. Preserve the essential information: the program name, day, time, location, and the fact that bringing a personal book is not required. Explain why your wording is easier to scan than the original paragraph. Do not invent a street address or registration process.

:::answer Show Activity 2 suggested rewrite
**Slide title:** Saturday Reading Hour

- **When:** Saturday, 10:00 a.m.
- **Where:** Community Room
- **Bring a book if you have one; everyone can participate.**

The shortened version preserves the core logistical and participation details without exaggerated praise or repeated language. The three lines can fit in a standard content layout more comfortably than the original paragraph. If the event were real, the organizer would need to verify every detail before publishing. A word such as *everyone* should also be checked against the actual program's eligibility policy.
:::

### Activity 3: Select and describe an image responsibly

A fictional library has three possible pictures for a slide explaining book discussions:

- **Picture A:** An approved, licensed illustration of a group of books, with the creator and license recorded.
- **Picture B:** A photograph copied from a personal social-media page showing identifiable children, with no permission information.
- **Picture C:** A popular photograph found through an online image search, with no source or license details.

Choose which picture you would use, if any. Write an appropriate short alternative-text description for Picture A, assuming it shows three books standing upright on a shelf. Describe what extra information you would need before using Pictures B or C. Explain whether simply crediting the photographer would settle the permission question.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model source and alt-text review
**Recommended choice:** Picture A, provided its actual license permits the intended use and the required credit is included. It fits the slide's subject without disclosing personal information.

**Possible alt text:** *Three books standing upright on a shelf.* If the illustration adds no information beyond an adjacent label and the program provides a decorative-image setting, it may be reasonable to mark it decorative instead. The decision depends on the slide's purpose.

Picture B requires appropriate authorization and privacy considerations before use, particularly because identifiable children appear. Picture C requires an identified original source and usage permission. Attribution does not automatically grant a right to use the work. The strongest solution may be to use no image rather than take an uncertain one.
:::

### Activity 4: Repair a distorted and overlapping picture

A learner has added an authorized book illustration to Slide 2. The book looks twice as wide as expected, its lower edge covers the final word of the sentence, and the picture's original caption has been cropped away.

Write a recovery plan of at least four actions. The goal is to restore the meaning without deleting the slide or sacrificing readable text. Include how you would check that the problem is solved.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model repair sequence
1. Select the picture rather than the entire slide or text box.
2. Use Undo if the distortion and crop were just introduced, or restore the picture's original proportions using the editor's supported size controls.
3. Reconsider the crop so that important details and captions are retained; if space is insufficient, use a different layout or a more suitable image.
4. Move the picture away from the text or shorten the wording without removing necessary information.
5. Inspect the picture and text at presentation size, not only in the thumbnail pane.
6. Check the picture's alternative text and source information, and save the editable deck.

A poor repair would be to make the sentence so small that it cannot be read or to cover the caption permanently. If the illustration conveys nothing essential, removing it may be the best option.
:::

### Activity 5: Decide whether media will actually work

A hypothetical presenter wants to place an online two-minute video on Slide 4. It shows how to choose a book. The presenter will use a borrowed computer at a community center. The clip is hosted on a site the presenter has not checked; its audio is important, but no captions or transcript are available. The room may not have dependable internet access.

Answer the following questions in complete sentences:

1. What is the presenter's educational reason for showing the video?
2. What rights, privacy, and content questions should be checked?
3. What accessibility and playback dependencies must be resolved?
4. What should happen if the clip cannot be made reliable and accessible?
5. What is one good fallback that uses no internet connection?

:::answer Show Activity 5 model media decision
The video might help viewers see the physical process of selecting a book, but that benefit must be compared with the deck's limited time and other ways to convey the steps. The presenter should check the clip's actual content, reuse permissions, any recognizable people, whether the intended segment is appropriate, and the accuracy of the demonstration. Captions or an appropriate accessible alternative are needed when speech conveys meaning; meaningful silent actions may need description.

The presenter should also verify whether the hosting service is accessible at the venue, whether the borrowed computer supports playback, and whether the correct account and viewer permissions exist. The source has not been checked and connectivity is uncertain, so it is premature to rely on the clip.

A practical fallback is a slide reading **Choose a subject → Find a book → Read the description → Select one**, optionally accompanied by authorized still illustrations. This alternative is understandable without internet access or sound. If a later authorized video is used, it should be tested in advance with the audience-facing presentation.
:::

### Activity 6: Build and audit a complete five-slide mini-presentation

Create a fictional five-slide **Saturday Reading Hour** deck in PowerPoint, Google Slides, Impress, or on paper. You may adapt the basic slide structure from earlier lessons. Add text to every slide and plan **one meaningful, appropriately sourced visual** for Slide 2. A real picture is optional; an image placeholder or written description is acceptable. You do not need audio or video unless it clearly helps your purpose and you have authorized, accessible content to use.

| Slide | Required purpose | Minimum content | Optional content |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Introduce the activity | Saturday Reading Hour; fictional library program | A simple approved book icon |
| 2 | Explain the activity | Choose a book; read quietly; share an idea | Licensed illustration of an open book |
| 3 | Give logistics | Saturday, 10:00 a.m.; Community Room | None needed |
| 4 | Reduce participation uncertainty | Bring a book if you have one; you can participate without one | None needed |
| 5 | State an appropriate next step | Ask library staff for current information | None needed |

Do the following:

1. Enter one clear title and the required text on each slide.
2. Use a consistent slide layout or theme suited to the message without changing fictional details.
3. On Slide 2, insert an authorized image if available or draw a clearly labeled placeholder.
4. Inspect the image's size, aspect ratio, and position. Write useful alt text if the image conveys information.
5. Explain whether audio or video is necessary. If you choose to include it, state what it teaches and how you will provide accessible alternatives and playback checks.
6. Review all five slides for accurate wording, readable text, reasonable spacing, and respectful content.
7. Save or label the editable practice work and verify that it can be found again. If your work is on paper, write down the steps you would use to save it in software.

**Completion evidence:** You should be able to show five titles, concise content, one well-reasoned image decision, one alternative-text decision, one media decision, and a short quality-check statement. You do not need a published URL, a real account, or a recording.

:::answer Show Activity 6 model five-slide plan and audit
**Slide 1 — Saturday Reading Hour**

Subtitle: *A fictional community library program.* The title is prominent; an icon is not necessary.

**Slide 2 — What Happens?**

- Choose a book.
- Read quietly.
- Share one idea.

Possible picture: a properly licensed illustration of an open book. If the picture identifies the activity, alt text might read *Open book on a table.* If it is purely decorative and repeats the text, it can be marked decorative where supported.

**Slide 3 — When and Where?**

- Saturday, 10:00 a.m.
- Community Room

Text is enough. A photograph would not add necessary information.

**Slide 4 — What to Bring**

- Bring a book if you have one.
- No book? You can still participate.

The wording avoids treating the book as a requirement. It is readable without pictures.

**Slide 5 — Learn More**

- Ask library staff for current program information.

No invented booking URL, contact person, or actual phone number is included.

**Media decision:** The deck's purpose is a brief invitation. Audio and video are unnecessary for the five required points. Omitting them avoids distracting sound, access dependencies, and additional captioning work.

**Model audit:** Each slide has a meaningful title; dates and places remain identified as fictional; no slide is overcrowded; the Slide 2 picture, if included, is properly sourced and described; content is understandable without sound; an editable copy is saved; the deck has been checked in presentation view. A different but equally clear design can also pass.
:::

### Optional application challenge

If you have access to suitable presentation software, duplicate the editable practice file and try replacing the Slide 2 picture with a different, equally relevant authorized image. Inspect whether cropping, scale, alt text, or positioning must change. Use Undo or return to the saved original if you do not like the result. Do not upload a sensitive or copyrighted picture to a new service just to try the feature.

### Self-review rubric

| Criterion | Not yet | Developing | Ready |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slide text | Essential point missing | Point present but hard to scan | Accurate, brief, and easy to read |
| Image selection | Unrelated or unverified source | Relevant but source information incomplete | Purpose and rights reasonably checked |
| Object placement | Overlap or distorted picture | Minor positioning problem | Clear separation and proportions |
| Accessible meaning | Important information only in image or sound | Some alternatives included | Meaningful alternatives available |
| Media decision | Added without purpose or testing | Purpose clear but dependencies unresolved | Media omitted appropriately or tested with fallback |
| File verification | No identifiable final work | File saved but not checked | Editable copy found and result reviewed |

You are ready to proceed when you can explain not only **how** to insert an item but **why** it belongs on the slide and **how you know** that the audience will be able to understand it.

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

### Misconception 1: Every slide needs a picture

A slide containing a precise meeting time may be clearer with only text. Pictures should answer a real communication need. Blank space can be useful.

### Misconception 2: A picture found online is free to use

Search engines help discover images; they do not themselves grant reuse rights. Check the source, license, permission, and relevant restrictions.

### Misconception 3: Giving credit makes any image legal to reuse

Attribution and permission are different. Some licenses require both compliance with usage conditions and attribution; credit alone does not override a copyright restriction.

### Misconception 4: A placeholder and a text box are identical in every situation

A layout placeholder can carry expected positioning and structural properties. A manually inserted text box may not follow a layout change in the same way.

### Misconception 5: A bigger text box always makes text easier to read

A larger box may prevent wrapping, but readability also depends on font size, contrast, wording, and actual display conditions. Sometimes shorter text is the better solution.

### Misconception 6: An image and its background are the same object

An inserted picture is usually selectable. A slide background is part of the slide's underlying visual surface and behaves differently.

### Misconception 7: Stretching a photograph makes it higher quality

Uneven stretching changes proportions; enlarging a low-resolution image may reveal pixelation. Neither operation manufactures the detail needed for a sharp picture.

### Misconception 8: Cropping only changes size

Cropping changes what the audience can see. A hidden label, person, date, or object may change the meaning of the image.

### Misconception 9: If the slide looks correct, it must be accessible

A visual arrangement can still have missing alt text, incorrect reading order, hard-to-read contrast, or media without an accessible equivalent.

### Misconception 10: Alt text should describe every pixel of a picture

Alternative text should communicate the image's relevant purpose and information. Decorative visuals may be marked decorative when supported.

### Misconception 11: Captions and transcripts are interchangeable

Captions are synchronized with media playback; transcripts are separate text representations. An accessible video may also require a description of meaningful silent action.

### Misconception 12: Every audio or video file works in every editor

Formats, codecs, permissions, and device configurations affect playback. A supported extension is a clue, not a complete guarantee.

### Misconception 13: A video visible in editing view will play during a presentation

The object may depend on a local file, a cloud account, internet connectivity, or a specific player. Test in the actual presentation environment.

### Misconception 14: Autoplay makes a presentation more professional

Unrequested sound can interrupt speech and distract the audience. Deliberate playback is often easier to manage.

### Misconception 15: Moving an inserted object is the same as editing its contents

Selecting a border changes the object; placing the text cursor inside edits characters. Watch what is selected before using keyboard commands.

### Misconception 16: Exporting to PDF preserves all audio and video

PDF is primarily a fixed document representation. Do not assume multimedia playback survives export; test the actual output and use an appropriate delivery format.

### Misconception 17: A presentation with more media always teaches more

Each media item adds technical and attention costs. The best choice is the simplest material that communicates the necessary information accurately and accessibly.

### Misconception 18: If something will not play, I should install the first codec offered online

Unknown downloads and pop-ups are risky. Check trusted application documentation, source access, and device configuration; use a known supported format or a fallback.

### Quick decision exercise

You have 60 seconds to improve a slide that contains a wall of text, an unrelated animated picture, and an unverified online video. Which problem should you address first? One reasonable sequence is: **identify the slide's actual message; shorten the text without losing needed facts; remove irrelevant motion; verify whether the video provides unique educational value; and replace it with a reliable, accessible alternative if not.** This sequence improves comprehension before adding technical complexity.

### Frequently confused terms

- **Resize versus crop:** Change dimensions versus change the visible portion.
- **Insert versus paste:** Add material through a source-selection command versus transfer selected copied material, often with different formatting consequences.
- **Embedded versus linked:** Content carried within the presentation versus content that depends on a separate location or service.
- **Alt text versus caption:** Descriptive text for a visual versus timed text supporting media audio.
- **Slide object versus slide background:** A separately selectable element versus the visual surface behind objects.
- **Copyright versus attribution:** A system of rights versus a way of acknowledging a creator; acknowledgement does not by itself grant permission.
- **Saving versus exporting:** Preserving an editable source versus making a particular distribution format, which may not preserve every feature.

### Lesson summary

Adding content to a slide is a series of deliberate decisions. Begin with the slide's main message. Enter a clear title and concise supporting text, select an appropriate visual only when it contributes useful information, and check its size, proportions, placement, and source. Add audio or video only when sound or motion teaches something relevant and the media can be accessed, understood, and played reliably. Verify alternative text, captions or transcripts, licenses, privacy, and final output behavior. Save the editable presentation before exporting or sharing it.
