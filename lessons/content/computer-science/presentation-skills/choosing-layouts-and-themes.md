## Lesson 3: Choosing Layouts and Themes

A neighborhood library has a five-slide presentation for its fictional Saturday Reading Hour. The file opens correctly, and every slide contains useful words. Yet the opening title looks like an ordinary bullet point, the schedule is cramped into a narrow corner, and the final invitation uses a different font and background from the rest of the deck. The information is not necessarily wrong. The problem is that the slides have not been given an appropriate visual structure.

A [[slide-layout|slide layout]] determines the arrangement of content areas on a slide. A [[presentation-theme|presentation theme]] provides coordinated visual choices—often colors, fonts, backgrounds, and design treatments—for a presentation. Learning to use the two together gives a beginning presenter a reliable way to make information easier to follow without designing every slide from scratch. **The main goal is communication, not decoration.**

Lesson 1 introduced the purpose of presentations. Lesson 2 covered building and managing slides. This lesson shows how to select useful layouts, apply a coherent theme, change visual choices safely, and inspect the result. The subsequent lessons address inserting images and media, organizing the narrative, applying more detailed design principles, speaker notes, presenting, and sharing. You can complete this lesson with paper sketches and fictional examples if presentation software is unavailable.

Microsoft PowerPoint on Windows 11 is the principal software example. Google Slides and LibreOffice Impress are also covered. Application commands and appearance can vary by version, platform, document type, or account. These are general workflows, not promises that every edition offers identical controls. No real personal information or public sharing is required.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 90–120 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Presentation Skills Lessons 1–2 recommended · **Practice equipment:** Paper and pencil or an available presentation application; a network connection is optional unless using a browser-based editor · **Safety:** Use fictional program information; retain an editable practice copy before making substantial visual changes; do not publish or share a deck simply to test a theme.

> **Essential idea:** **Choose the layout for the kind of information on each slide; choose the theme for a consistent appearance across the deck; then inspect every slide for readability and unintended changes.** A layout and theme are starting structures, not substitutes for judgment.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain the difference between a slide layout and a presentation theme in ordinary language.
2. Distinguish a theme, a template, a slide background, and a slide master at an introductory level.
3. Match common slide purposes to suitable layouts, including title, title-and-content, two-content, and blank layouts.
4. Apply or change a layout on the intended slide without confusing it with changing the whole deck's theme.
5. Identify the usual theme controls in PowerPoint, Google Slides, and LibreOffice Impress.
6. Recognize when a theme improves consistency and when its colors, fonts, or decorations reduce clarity.
7. Explain why placeholders, manually added objects, and custom formatting may behave differently after a layout or theme change.
8. Diagnose common layout problems without deleting content or rebuilding the entire presentation.
9. Review a short fictional deck for readable text, sensible hierarchy, consistent styling, and appropriate content placement.
10. Explain a safe workflow for previewing changes, checking slides, and saving an editable copy.

## 1. What are layouts, themes, and related design tools?

You can think of a presentation as a small set of rooms that all belong to one building. The **layout** describes where important things belong inside a particular room. The **theme** gives the rooms a related appearance. Both can be useful, but neither determines whether the facts inside the rooms are accurate.

A [[slide-layout|slide layout]] is a prepared arrangement of [[placeholder|placeholders]] or content positions within one slide. A placeholder is an area meant for a certain kind of material, such as a title, body text, image, or chart. Common layouts include **Title Slide**, **Title and Content**, **Two Content**, **Section Header**, and **Blank**. Their exact names and availability depend on the application and chosen design.

A [[presentation-theme|presentation theme]] is a coordinated set of design choices. It can supply [[theme-color|theme colors]], [[theme-font|theme fonts]], backgrounds, and other visual properties. In PowerPoint, a theme is also associated with a set of slide layouts. In other software, the theme and master-slide terminology may differ. Applying a theme usually changes more than one slide, while applying a layout ordinarily targets the slide or slides you select.

### One slide, two independent decisions

Suppose Slide 3 must show a program's time and location.

- **Layout decision:** Use a title-and-content arrangement with a heading above two short details.
- **Theme decision:** Use the same simple light-background, dark-text styling as the other slides.

Changing the layout may move the content areas. Changing the theme may alter the fonts and colors. Either change can affect how the slide looks. Neither should change **Saturdays at 10:00 a.m.** into **Sundays at 3:00 p.m.** The content still needs to be checked separately.

| Design concept | Main job | Typical scope | A beginner question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slide layout | Arrange content areas | One or more selected slides | Where should the title and supporting information go? |
| Theme | Coordinate visual appearance | Often the whole presentation | What consistent colors and type styles should the deck use? |
| Template | Provide a reusable starting deck or design | New or copied presentation | What prepared structure should I start from? |
| Background | Supply the surface behind slide content | One slide or multiple slides | What appears behind the words? |
| Master slide | Define shared design or layout properties | Slides using that master | What should remain consistent across related slides? |
| Manual formatting | Change particular text or objects | Individual selection | Does this item need a deliberate exception? |

### Themes and templates are related, not identical

A [[presentation-template|presentation template]] is a reusable starting file or design that may contain sample slides, headings, institutional branding, theme choices, or instructions. A theme generally describes visual styling; a template can include a theme **plus actual slide structures or example content**. Templates can be useful for a school orientation, team briefing, or recurring meeting. But a sample sentence in a template is not automatically true just because the design looks polished.

Some software presents prepared themes through a gallery that resembles its template gallery. The distinction is based on what the tool does, not where the button appears. Ask whether you are **changing the existing deck's appearance** or **starting from a prepared presentation**.

### Backgrounds are only one part of a theme

A [[slide-background|slide background]] is the fill or image behind a slide's content. You may encounter white backgrounds, solid colors, gradients, or pictures. A theme can specify a background, but **background and theme are not synonyms**. A slide with a dark blue background may use exactly the same content layout as a slide with a white background. Choosing a darker background can also make dark text hard to read, even when the original text was clear.

Background images deserve extra care. A photograph full of detail behind small text can make the text difficult to locate. Beginners usually achieve better results by using a plain or visually quiet background and placing the emphasis on the message. Later design lessons explore when illustrations and photographs are useful.

### What is a master slide?

A [[master-slide|master slide]] establishes shared design properties for slides that use it. In PowerPoint, a [[slide-master|Slide Master]] and associated layouts help define recurring positions and formatting. In Google Slides, theme editing offers related shared design functions. In Impress, master slides and templates supply recurring presentation styling. These mechanisms are not implemented identically.

At this stage you should recognize why master-level design exists, not try to build a complex custom master. If a school logo or a standard footer belongs on many slides, a master or approved template can help maintain consistency. Changing an individual text box on every slide is often harder to maintain. However, do not edit an institution's official master or brand template without authorization.

### What a layout is not

A layout is not a rule that all slides must have the same number of bullet points. It is not the same thing as a [[slide-transition|slide transition]], an effect that plays when moving between slides. It is not an [[animation|animation]], which can make content enter, move, or disappear. It is also not a guarantee of accessibility. A layout can provide good structure, but the creator must still check reading order, contrast, text size, and meaning.

> **Decision rule:** When content is awkwardly positioned, investigate the **layout** first. When many slides look visually unrelated, investigate the **theme** or shared formatting first. When text itself is excessive or unclear, revise the **content** before adding decoration.

### Worked example: three repairs

A five-slide Reading Hour deck has three distinct problems:

1. **Slide 1** says **Saturday Reading Hour**, but its title is small and placed inside an ordinary content list. Select a **Title Slide** layout or another layout designed for an opening title, then inspect its placement.
2. **Slide 3** has a schedule heading, but the time and location are squeezed into two narrow side-by-side text areas. Consider **Title and Content** so both details can be read in one clear region.
3. **Slide 5** uses bright yellow text on white while the other slides use dark text on a pale background. Keep the appropriate layout and repair the **styling** or choose a coherent theme rather than changing the information.

These changes solve different problems. Replacing every slide with the same layout would not be a thoughtful repair.

## 2. How do you choose a layout for a slide's purpose?

Every slide should have a recognizable job. Before opening a layout gallery, ask: **What should the audience understand or do after seeing this slide?** A title slide introduces. A comparison slide places two alternatives beside each other. A schedule slide communicates time and location. A conclusion slide reinforces the next action. Matching layout to purpose is more important than choosing the most elaborate arrangement.

### Common slide layouts

The exact labels vary, but presentation editors often include variations on these arrangements:

| Common layout | Typical structure | Useful for | Risk to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Title Slide | Prominent title with optional subtitle | Opening the presentation | Treating it like a paragraph page |
| Title and Content | Heading above one content region | A short explanation, list, or single visual | Filling the content region with too many words |
| Section Header | Strong heading with little supporting content | Separating major topics | Using many section breaks in a very short deck |
| Two Content | Heading plus two content regions | Two related ideas or alternatives | Forcing long text into narrow columns |
| Comparison | Two labeled areas under a heading | Before and after, option A and option B | Suggesting equal importance when items are not comparable |
| Title Only | Heading with open area beneath | Custom diagram or carefully arranged object | Leaving an unclear purpose beneath the title |
| Blank | No prepared visible content placeholders | Specialized illustrations or composition | Creating unnecessary manual work for ordinary text |
| Picture with Caption | Visual area with explanatory text | A relevant photograph or diagram | Relying on the image without meaningful explanation |

These are examples rather than a universal menu. **Do not invent a missing layout** if your application shows a different selection. Choose the closest suitable structure and inspect it.

### Why the title slide is different

The [[title-slide|title slide]] is usually the presentation's opening. Its title identifies the subject; a subtitle may name the audience, program, or purpose. A title slide does not need to carry the entire presentation's explanation. In our fictional example, **Saturday Reading Hour** is the main heading and **A neighborhood library program** is supporting information.

If the first slide uses an ordinary content layout, the title may look like a subpoint instead of a clear opening. A Title Slide layout helps establish [[visual-hierarchy|visual hierarchy]]: the audience first sees the most important information, then the secondary information. Hierarchy is about relative prominence—not about making every word as large as possible.

### One region or two?

A slide with a single purpose often benefits from one content region. A slide comparing two genuine alternatives may benefit from two. The number of regions should reflect **how the information relates**, not how many boxes you can fit.

Consider two fictional slides:

**Slide A — When and where?**

- Saturdays at 10:00 a.m.
- Community room
- Arrive a few minutes early

A single content region is usually sufficient. Placing each line in a different column makes the audience work unnecessarily hard.

**Slide B — Two ways to participate**

- **Read aloud:** Listen and take turns reading.
- **Quiet reading:** Choose a book and read independently.

A comparison or two-content layout could make these two approaches easier to compare. However, if one side has one short phrase and the other contains a long paragraph, the columns may not be balanced. Edit the material or choose a more suitable structure.

### What is a placeholder?

A [[placeholder|placeholder]] is an editable region provided by the layout. It might show **Click to add title** or display a symbol for inserting a picture. Once the user supplies content, the instructional prompt is not usually part of the delivered slide. A placeholder is different from a [[text-box|text box]] that a person inserts manually. Both can contain text, but they may not react identically when a layout changes.

A title placeholder communicates *this is the slide's title*. A manually positioned text box can hold the same words but may not have the same structural meaning for accessibility or for the application's built-in editing tools. This is one reason it is often better to use a suitable built-in layout rather than covering a blank slide with improvised boxes.

### When a blank layout is appropriate

**Blank** is not a superior or more professional choice simply because it offers freedom. It removes or avoids some prepared content areas, so you may need to add all text boxes and objects manually. A blank layout can be useful for an approved full-slide illustration or unusual diagram, but it may introduce extra work maintaining alignment, title semantics, or reading order. In introductory work, prefer a built-in layout with meaningful placeholders when it fits the job.

### When to choose a different layout

Change the layout when the slide's **communication task** changes. For example:

1. A title-only opening is now meant to explain an event schedule.
2. A planned side-by-side comparison no longer has two comparable categories.
3. A long list has been shortened to one key message and supporting detail.
4. A relevant image needs an associated explanation rather than a crowded paragraph beside it.

Do **not** change the layout merely because you are bored with the existing slide. Variation should have a reason. A short deck with a title slide and four title-and-content slides can be very effective.

### The five-slide Reading Hour plan

| Slide | Purpose | Suggested layout | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Saturday Reading Hour | Identify the program | Title Slide | Gives the opening title clear emphasis |
| 2. What is Reading Hour? | Explain the activity | Title and Content | One heading and a few short supporting points |
| 3. When and where? | Communicate logistics | Title and Content | Keeps time and place together |
| 4. What to bring | Show a short checklist | Title and Content | A short list needs one readable area |
| 5. Join us | Invite participation | Title Only or Title and Content | Prominent invitation plus one necessary detail |

This plan is a useful baseline, not a compulsory formula. **It is acceptable to reuse a layout** when several slides perform the same job. Consistency can reduce the audience's effort.

### Worked decision: identify the real problem

Suppose a slide has a title, three short bullet points, and plenty of empty space. The layout is Title and Content. Should you switch to Two Content? Not unless there are two meaningful groups to compare. Empty space can support reading. The more appropriate decision may be to keep the layout and leave the slide uncluttered.

Suppose a different slide has 14 long bullets in one content area. Switching to a two-column layout may simply create two crowded columns. A better first action may be to remove repetition, separate distinct ideas into two slides, or shorten the wording. **Layouts arrange information; they cannot repair an unclear message by themselves.**

## 3. How do you choose a theme that supports the audience?

A theme should make related slides look like part of one presentation. Consistent choices help the audience understand that the slides belong to the same speaker, topic, and event. But theme consistency does not require every slide to be identical, nor does it guarantee readability. Choose a theme as you would choose a clear document style: its role is to make the information easier to receive.

### Start with audience and setting

A presentation for a small staff meeting may appear on individual laptop screens. A community event deck may be projected on a wall in a brightly lit room. A school project might be read on a phone after class. The useful theme depends on the conditions in which people will view it.

Ask:

- **Who will view the presentation?** Beginners, colleagues, community members, or a mixed audience?
- **Where will they see it?** On a monitor, projector, shared screen, printed copy, or mobile device?
- **What is the message?** Instruction, schedule, comparison, invitation, or report?
- **Are there organizational requirements?** An approved brand template or color scheme may already exist.
- **What access needs should be anticipated?** Readers may need clear contrast, a predictable structure, and non-color cues.

The most useful style for Reading Hour is likely friendly, restrained, and legible. A highly decorative theme with busy textures and tiny pale lettering would undermine a simple public invitation.

### Elements a theme may control

| Theme element | What it affects | What to inspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary and accent colors | Text, shapes, emphasis, or charts | Can important items be distinguished without relying only on hue? |
| Theme fonts | Repeating title and body text styles | Are the letters easy to recognize at actual viewing size? |
| Default backgrounds | The surface behind content | Does any text blend into the background? |
| Decorative elements | Lines, shapes, or framing | Do decorations interfere with content? |
| Layout designs | Placement and appearance of content regions | Are placeholders appropriate to each slide's purpose? |
| Theme variants | Related style choices within a theme | Does the variation preserve clarity and consistency? |

A [[theme-variant|theme variant]] is a related alternative offered by some applications, such as a different coordinated color choice. A variant is not necessarily a completely separate template. It should still be evaluated as a design change because it can affect readability.

### Color and contrast

[[contrast|Contrast]] is the difference in appearance between foreground information and its background. Dark text on a plain light background is often a dependable beginner choice. Light text on a dark background can work too, provided the contrast is sufficiently strong and the slide is readable in its actual viewing setting. Avoid light gray on white or bright yellow on white. Decorative gradients and photographs can make contrast vary across a text area.

Do not assume a theme is accessible just because it is built in. Themes may have combinations that are visually attractive in thumbnails but hard to read on a projector or a small screen. The correct test is to inspect the slide at realistic size. Automated accessibility checkers, when available, can help identify some problems, but they do not replace human review.

**Color should reinforce meaning rather than carry it alone.** If two events differ by status, label them **Confirmed** and **Tentative**, rather than using only green and red. People may have color-vision differences, monochrome printing, or displays with altered colors.

### Font choices and visual hierarchy

The [[font|font]] is the design of the letters; [[font-size|font size]] affects how large they appear. A theme often gives the title a more prominent style than the body text. That supports [[visual-hierarchy|visual hierarchy]]. When selecting a theme, look for a clear title-to-body relationship instead of many unrelated typefaces.

A theme does not know whether your text will fit. A long title may wrap awkwardly; a large body paragraph may shrink or overflow. If text becomes too small, **simplify the wording or restructure the slide** rather than relying on automatic shrinking alone. Exact font sizes depend on audience distance, display conditions, application, and content. The next design lesson discusses more detailed readability decisions.

### Why restraint matters

An attractive deck can make a familiar message look professional. An overly elaborate deck can make a simple message harder to grasp. Effects such as colored stripes, gradients, shadows, illustrations, and special fonts should have a communicative role. You do not need to use every color in the theme. You also do not need to add pictures simply because a theme offers picture placeholders.

| Theme choice | Likely advantage | Potential concern | Sensible response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plain light background with dark text | Familiar and usually clear | Can look visually simple | Keep it if the content reads well |
| Plain dark background with light text | Can provide strong contrast | Light colors may not all be legible | Inspect each text color and projector view |
| Bright multi-color palette | Useful for distinguishing categories | May compete with the message | Use accents sparingly and label categories |
| Photographic background | Can establish context | Can obscure text or distract | Prefer a calm background or separate the photo from text |
| Elaborate title typography | Can create a strong opening | May be hard to read or inconsistent | Keep title wording concise and clear |
| Institutional template | Provides organizational consistency | May include obsolete sample content | Follow approved standards and verify every detail |

### Theme versus content accuracy

The theme can coordinate colors and fonts, but it cannot verify a date or ensure that an invitation is genuine. Suppose the slide says **Saturday, November 42**. A polished theme will not correct this impossible date. A simple slide with the right information is more useful than a luxurious slide with an error.

Theme selection is one step in production: **draft content → choose meaningful layouts → select a readable theme → inspect every slide → correct factual and visual errors**. It is not a substitute for the entire review process.

### Worked example: evaluating two theme options

Imagine a fictional **Community Reading Hour** slide with the heading **When and where?** and details **Saturdays at 10:00 a.m.** and **Community room**.

**Theme A:** Pale background, dark text, a consistent heading style, and a small blue accent. The information reads clearly and the accent does not carry essential meaning.

**Theme B:** Busy photograph of stacked books, thin yellow text, decorative lettering, and several colored shapes behind the schedule. The slide may look lively in a small preview but the words become difficult to distinguish.

Choose **Theme A** for this basic lesson. Theme B would require substantial changes before being appropriate. The choice is based on how reliably an audience can read the message, not whether one design seems more exciting.

## 4. How do you apply or change layouts and themes?

Applying a layout and applying a theme are different operations. Before you click, decide whether the change is intended for **one particular slide**, a **selection of slides**, or **the overall deck**. Then review the result. Commands can vary across releases, especially between desktop and browser editions. The following workflows describe the common approaches and point to the correct kinds of controls, not exact screen coordinates for every version.

### A safe starting workflow

1. Open the editable Reading Hour practice presentation from Lesson 2, or create a similar fictional deck.
2. Save an appropriately named practice copy if you need to preserve the original.
3. Select the slide you intend to change in the [[thumbnail-pane|slide thumbnail pane]].
4. Open the program's layout options and select a layout matched to the slide's purpose.
5. Confirm that every heading and detail is still present and readable.
6. Open the theme or design gallery, choose a simple theme, and inspect the entire deck.
7. Correct any unintended change; use [[undo|Undo]] promptly if necessary.
8. Save and verify the editable result.

These actions separate **structural choice**, **styling choice**, and **verification**. Avoid changing layout, theme, content, and background all at once if you are still learning. It becomes difficult to determine which action caused a problem.

### Microsoft PowerPoint: change a slide layout

For common desktop versions of PowerPoint on Windows:

1. Open the presentation and select the intended slide thumbnail.
2. Locate **Home** on the [[ribbon|ribbon]].
3. Choose **Layout** to display available slide arrangements. The control is commonly in the Slides group.
4. Pick a layout such as **Title Slide**, **Title and Content**, or **Two Content**.
5. Review text positions, headings, and other objects on that slide.
6. If content is unexpectedly relocated, inspect the slide and consider Undo before making further changes.

PowerPoint's layout gallery can reflect the theme and master designs associated with the file. A layout may look different from one presentation to another even when its name is similar. PowerPoint also provides layout-related tools through Slide Master view, but editing the master is not necessary for this beginner task.

If several slides are selected, a layout choice may affect all of them. Before changing anything, verify the selection in the left pane. Use one selected slide while learning so you can recognize cause and effect.

### Microsoft PowerPoint: change a theme

1. Locate the **Design** tab.
2. Find the available **Themes** gallery.
3. Point to or examine a candidate theme. Some versions show a [[live-preview|live preview]], while others require selection.
4. Choose a restrained theme with readable text and appropriate background colors.
5. Look through the slide thumbnails and open each slide for closer inspection.
6. If your version offers **Variants**, compare them cautiously rather than changing several unrelated properties at once.
7. Restore or revise any slide whose text wraps badly, becomes too small, or loses contrast.

A normal theme selection frequently affects the presentation as a whole. Some PowerPoint desktop editions also support applying themes to selected slides; that behavior depends on the app and command used. **Do not assume every PowerPoint version supports the same multi-theme workflow.** For beginner practice, using one coherent theme across the deck is the clearest option.

### Google Slides: change a slide layout

For Google Slides in a desktop browser, the usual workflow is:

1. Open the intended presentation with editing permission.
2. Select the slide thumbnail.
3. Open **Slide** in the menu and choose **Apply layout**, or use the layout control available in the toolbar or new-slide menu.
4. Select a suitable arrangement.
5. Examine the slide to make sure text and objects remain correctly placed.
6. Repeat only for slides that need different structural arrangements.

Google Slides offers layouts through its current theme. A title or body field may be linked to the theme's preset design, while manually inserted objects may retain individual choices. Therefore, a layout change should always be followed by a visual review.

### Google Slides: change the theme

1. Open the presentation.
2. Choose **Slide > Change theme**, or use the visible **Theme** control if your interface provides one.
3. Examine the themes displayed in the side panel or gallery.
4. Select a readable theme.
5. Review all slides for unwanted wrapping, reduced contrast, or repositioned content.
6. Confirm the document's [[sync-status|save or synchronization status]] before relying on the latest changes.

Google Slides also supports changing individual slide backgrounds. Changing a background is **not** the same as changing the whole theme. If one slide suddenly looks different, investigate whether its background was changed locally. Theme editing or importing themes may be available in some contexts; this lesson does not require those advanced functions.

Browser-based tools typically save through an account and internet-connected service. A browser tab that remains open is not proof that all changes are safely synchronized. Check the editor's status and reopen the presentation when necessary. If you do not have Google Slides access, use the offline activity instructions rather than creating an account for this exercise.

### LibreOffice Impress: choose a slide layout

LibreOffice Impress provides slide layouts using controls such as **Slide > Layout** or the **Layouts** panel in the [[sidebar|sidebar]], depending on the active interface. A general process is:

1. Open Impress and the intended presentation.
2. Select the correct slide in the Slides pane.
3. Open the slide layout choices from the menu or sidebar.
4. Choose an appropriate layout for the content.
5. Inspect placeholders and existing objects before continuing.

Impress may reposition content when a layout changes. This makes inspection particularly important. You should not assume that choosing a different layout will remove information, nor should you assume that every manual object will automatically move to a useful position.

### LibreOffice Impress: use a master or template for consistent styling

Impress commonly uses [[master-slide|master slides]] and [[presentation-template|templates]] to coordinate design. Open the relevant master-slide selection options in the sidebar or through the **Slide** menu where available. Select an appropriate master design and review the affected slides. An existing template may also be applied or used to begin a new deck, depending on the version and workflow.

Do not confuse **applying an available master slide** with **editing the master itself**. The latter changes shared design definitions and can affect many slides. Beginners are better served by selecting a suitable existing master and keeping the layout simple.

### Cross-application command guide

| Task | PowerPoint | Google Slides | LibreOffice Impress |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Change selected slide layout | Home > Layout | Slide > Apply layout | Slide > Layout or Layouts sidebar |
| Choose coordinated deck styling | Design > Themes | Slide > Change theme or Theme control | Master Slides panel or suitable template |
| Change only a slide background | Use background formatting controls | Slide > Change background or Background control | Slide/page background controls |
| Inspect slide sequence | Thumbnail pane | Slide thumbnail strip | Slides pane |
| Undo an accidental edit | Undo | Undo | Undo |

This table summarizes common desktop paths. Menu names and locations can vary. If the displayed interface differs, search the program's own help for **layout**, **theme**, or **master slide** rather than trying unrelated settings.

### What if the layout gallery seems unavailable?

Start by checking that you are in a slide-editing view rather than a full-screen presentation. Confirm that the file is editable and that you selected a slide. If the presentation is open as a static PDF or other non-editable export, you may need the original presentation file. A protected, shared, or read-only document may restrict editing; obtain legitimate permission rather than attempting to bypass it.

### A caution about instructions that look universal

Slide programs change. Their toolbars may collapse when the window is narrow, and mobile editions may have different controls from their desktop counterparts. Use the function's *purpose* as the stable guide: **choose content arrangement**, **choose coordinated appearance**, **inspect results**. Do not treat a missing icon as a failure if your version offers the function elsewhere.

## 5. How do you adjust a layout without losing consistency?

Built-in layouts are starting points, not cages. You may need to shorten a title, move a picture, or choose a different content arrangement. The challenge is to make necessary improvements without converting every slide into a separate, unrelated design. This section focuses on the reasoning behind small adjustments. More detailed typography, alignment, image handling, and accessibility technique will appear in later lessons.

### Use the smallest correction that solves the problem

If the title is too long, consider editing the wording before changing the entire theme. If two columns are cramped, consider a one-column layout before making the font smaller. If one slide uses an unrelated background, consider restoring the common theme background before rebuilding its objects.

| Problem you see | Likely cause | First sensible action | What to check afterward |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Schedule text is squeezed into narrow columns | Two-content layout unsuitable for short logistics | Choose Title and Content | Both details remain present and readable |
| Slide title looks like a small bullet | Content box used instead of title placeholder | Choose suitable title layout or restore title placeholder | Title is clearly identified and prominent |
| Multiple slides have mismatched colors | Mixed manual formatting or backgrounds | Reapply coherent theme styling as appropriate | Text and accent colors remain readable |
| New theme makes one paragraph wrap over an image | Font, placeholder, or object placement changed | Shorten text or adjust suitable layout | Nothing overlaps or disappears |
| A picture does not fit its area | Image dimensions and chosen placeholder conflict | Reassess image/layout pairing | Image is not confusingly cropped |
| All slides look identical despite different purposes | Same layout used without regard to content | Choose layout based on function | Differences improve communication, not decoration |
| A single heading has different font styling | Manual override or pasted formatting | Restore consistent title styling | Heading matches intended hierarchy |

The table offers diagnosis, not guarantees. Many problems have more than one cause. Inspect before and after changes rather than treating a command as a universal fix.

### Placeholder content versus manually inserted objects

A [[placeholder|placeholder]] belongs to a layout. A [[manual-object|manual object]] is an element placed directly on a slide by the editor, such as a separately inserted text box, shape, or picture. When a layout is changed, placeholder positions and behavior may change. Manually inserted objects do not necessarily follow the new placeholder arrangement.

This explains a common experience: after selecting a different layout, the title moves but a manually added text box stays where it was. The result can look inconsistent or overlap content. **Do not interpret this as proof the application has lost the information.** Examine the slide, use Undo if needed, and decide whether to move or replace the manual object or choose a better-fitting layout.

### Why copied slides sometimes look different

A slide copied from another deck may bring formatting, background settings, or theme associations from its source. After pasting, the text may use a different color or font from surrounding slides. Some applications offer choices to preserve the source formatting or adapt the slide to the destination design. These are not identical options.

Before choosing:

- If you need the slide to look like part of the current deck, consider matching the destination theme.
- If the slide must preserve an officially approved design, consult the appropriate requirements.
- If the deck contains mixed styles unintentionally, investigate the pasted slide's formatting before changing the entire presentation.

The content itself may also be outdated. A copied slide with the correct new font but an old event time is still wrong.

### Reading order matters

[[reading-order|Reading order]] is the sequence in which assistive technologies or readers may encounter slide elements. A logical slide often begins with the title and continues through supporting information. Built-in placeholders and clear structure can support this, although **a visually pleasing layout does not guarantee a correct programmatic reading order**.

When you create many manually positioned text boxes, the order visible to your eyes may differ from the underlying order that a [[screen-reader|screen reader]] uses. In editors that support accessibility inspection and reading-order controls, review those features. At minimum, keep a simple structure, use meaningful titles, avoid unnecessary overlapping objects, and do not use placement alone to imply relationships.

### Simple accessibility checks

At this introductory level, ask these questions on every slide:

1. Can I read the title without squinting at the expected display size?
2. Can I read all body text against the background?
3. Is important meaning available through words, not only color?
4. Does the layout match the content's purpose?
5. Can I explain the slide's message in one sentence?
6. Are labels, locations, and times explicit rather than only implied by symbols?
7. Is there anything on the slide that overlaps, spills off the edge, or looks accidentally hidden?
8. Where software tools permit, has the slide been reviewed for accessibility issues?

For an image that carries important meaning, later lessons will introduce [[alternative-text|alternative text]] and related accessibility practices. No images need to be inserted to complete this lesson.

### Avoid rebuilding the deck merely to correct a theme

A learner may change the theme, dislike the result, and feel compelled to recreate every slide. Usually that is unnecessary. Try Undo if the change was recent, choose another theme, restore an appropriate layout, or correct the affected objects. If working on an important original, make an editable copy first so you can test alternatives without losing the established design.

A useful repair order is: **identify the scope of the problem → check the layout → check theme or background → inspect manual overrides → verify content → save**. This order helps preserve useful work.

### Distinguish intentional exceptions from accidental inconsistencies

A final **Join us** slide may use a larger invitation heading than the informational slides. That could be an intentional difference serving a communicative purpose. It need not mean the deck is inconsistent. Conversely, a slide whose text uses an unrelated font because it was copied from another file may be accidentally inconsistent. The question is whether a difference helps the audience understand the material.

| Difference between slides | Intentional or suspicious? | Reasoning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening title is more prominent | Often intentional | An opening performs a different job |
| Two comparison items are aligned side by side | Often intentional | Placement supports a genuine comparison |
| One slide uses a faint font by accident | Suspicious | It reduces readability without serving the message |
| A concluding call to action is emphasized | Often intentional | The final slide may highlight the next step |
| A pasted slide has an unrelated color palette | Worth investigating | Source formatting may not match the deck |
| Every bullet list has different indentation | Worth investigating | Irregular formatting may distract readers |

> **Consistency is functional:** Keep the same kinds of things looking related. Permit a difference when it reflects a real difference in purpose—not merely because a different button was available.

## 6. How do you review and troubleshoot a designed deck?

You should be able to inspect a presentation before you share or display it. An editor's thumbnail view gives a quick sense of visual consistency, but small thumbnails can hide poor contrast, accidental wrapping, and text that does not fit. Review at both the deck level and the individual-slide level.

### A complete five-slide review

Use the fictional Reading Hour deck or a paper version. Read each slide in order and answer the following:

| Slide | Layout fit | Theme fit | Content check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Saturday Reading Hour | Title and subtitle are prominent | Matches the deck's general style | Program name is correct |
| 2. What is Reading Hour? | One clear explanatory region | Text is legible and not crowded | Activities are plausible and concise |
| 3. When and where? | Time and location are clearly grouped | Background does not obscure details | Saturday, 10:00 a.m., community room |
| 4. What to bring | A simple short list | List styling matches other informational slides | Items are consistent with fictional program |
| 5. Join us | Invitation is prominent | Intentional emphasis remains coherent | No unsupported claim or invented registration link |

An appropriate layout for one slide may be inappropriate for another. A consistent theme may still need local inspection because a long heading or imported object can behave differently on a particular slide.

### Review at two scales

**Deck overview:** View the slide thumbnails in order. Does any slide appear unexpectedly bright, dark, crowded, or stylistically unrelated? Are title and content slides distinguishable? Have you accidentally applied a theme or background to only one slide?

**Individual slide:** Open each slide at a useful size. Is the heading obvious? Does all text fit? Is the contrast strong enough? Are text boxes and images separated appropriately? Does the content still say what it should say?

If possible, view the deck in [[slide-show-mode|Slide Show]] or presentation mode, since editing view can differ from what an audience sees. This is a visual test, not a requirement to deliver a live presentation or enable sharing. Do not leave a deck in full-screen playback when you intend to keep editing; return to the editor through the program's normal controls.

### A small change log

When testing two themes, record what you tried rather than relying on memory:

| Version | Change | Observation | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Plain light theme with title-and-content slides | All text easy to read | Keep as baseline |
| B | Dark theme with pale title accent | Title readable, schedule too dim | Reject or revise |
| C | Baseline theme with Title Slide on Slide 1 | Clearer opening | Keep if all slides pass review |

This table is fictional; your observations might differ. The point is to make an intentional choice and record why. For a short personal exercise, a few handwritten notes are enough.

### Troubleshooting sequence

1. **Identify the problem.** Is the content incorrect, the content poorly arranged, or the overall styling inconsistent?
2. **Check selection.** Are you editing the expected slide or multiple slides?
3. **Check the layout.** Does its arrangement match the information's purpose?
4. **Check the theme and local background.** Are the intended colors and fonts actually being used?
5. **Check overrides and imported objects.** A manually formatted object may not follow the theme.
6. **Use a reversible correction.** Try an appropriate layout, restore consistent formatting, or use Undo when possible.
7. **Verify all slides.** A deck-wide theme change can affect more than the currently visible slide.
8. **Save and reopen.** Confirm the editable file contains the reviewed presentation.

### When the theme causes surprises

**Problem: A title moves or becomes smaller.** The theme may use different title typography or a different layout arrangement. Inspect that slide's layout and shorten a very long title if appropriate.

**Problem: A manually inserted picture overlaps a text area.** A layout or theme change may have repositioned placeholders without repositioning the picture. Move or resize the picture carefully if appropriate, or restore the previous layout. Do not delete the source picture without considering whether it is needed.

**Problem: One slide stays a different color.** Check for a local slide background or custom formatting. Reapplying the deck theme may not automatically remove every override.

**Problem: A pasted slide changes fonts.** The source and destination may use different formatting. Review paste options or restore consistent styling without altering the factual text.

**Problem: A layout appears to remove a heading.** The heading could be in an unexpected field or behind another object; check selection and Undo before assuming it has been deleted. If it was removed, recover using Undo or an earlier saved copy where available.

**Problem: The menu is different from the instructions.** Check whether you are using a mobile or browser version, a collapsed toolbar, or an exported non-editable file. Use official help for your specific application.

### How much is enough?

A short beginner presentation usually does not need custom slide masters, dozens of theme variants, or extensive graphic effects. For this lesson, success means you can **justify a layout**, **choose a consistent readable theme**, and **notice when the choice interferes with the content**. You do not need to become a professional graphic designer.

### Final quality checklist

- Every slide has a clear purpose and suitable layout.
- The opening slide presents the subject clearly.
- Informational slides use readable content arrangements.
- The deck's styling is coherent without unnecessary decoration.
- Text remains legible against every background.
- Essential meaning is not conveyed only through color.
- No text is hidden, overlapping, or accidentally cropped.
- Every title and critical detail is factually correct.
- Unintended pasted formatting and local overrides are addressed.
- The edited presentation is saved in an accessible, authorized location.
- The editable source is preserved; exporting or publishing is not required.

## 7. Guided practice: choose, apply, and evaluate

The activities use a fictional neighborhood library and can be completed on paper. If a presentation editor is available, work only in a copy of a practice file. **Do not post the deck online, invite collaborators, or use real attendee information to satisfy these exercises.** Each activity includes a success check and an expandable model response so you can compare your reasoning with a defensible approach.

### Activity 1: Classify the design decision

**Task:** For each situation below, decide whether the first change should concern the **layout**, **theme**, **content**, or **background**. There may be more than one eventual improvement, but choose the most direct starting point.

1. The first slide contains the program title as the third bullet in a list.
2. Every slide uses a different font and unrelated accent colors.
3. The event date is written as **February 31**.
4. A dark blue heading is difficult to see on a dark blue slide surface.
5. The time and location are squeezed into two separate narrow columns.
6. A scenic photograph behind the schedule makes some words hard to read.

**Success check:** You can identify the primary type of issue without suggesting that decoration fixes factual errors.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answers
1. **Layout:** Use an opening title layout; confirm that the title is entered in the intended title area.
2. **Theme:** Choose a consistent style, then investigate manual formatting that remains different.
3. **Content:** Correct the impossible date from an authorized source; no theme can verify the date.
4. **Background or styling:** Restore a readable foreground/background combination. If the problem was introduced by a new theme, reassess that theme too.
5. **Layout:** A single content region is likely more appropriate for short logistics.
6. **Background:** Replace or simplify the busy background or separate text from the photograph; check the final contrast.

Several answers could involve follow-up work. The important distinction is that a layout changes arrangement, a theme changes coordinated styling, a background changes the surface, and content correction repairs information.
:::

### Activity 2: Match layouts to five slide purposes

**Task:** Select a fitting layout for each slide in the five-slide Reading Hour deck. You may choose among **Title Slide**, **Title and Content**, **Two Content**, **Title Only**, and **Blank**. Then explain why a two-content layout is **not** automatically preferable simply because it looks more elaborate.

| Slide | Intended information | Your layout choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Saturday Reading Hour; neighborhood library program | Decide |
| 2 | Enjoy stories; ask questions; explore books | Decide |
| 3 | Saturdays at 10:00 a.m.; community room | Decide |
| 4 | Bring curiosity; choose a book if you wish | Decide |
| 5 | Join us; everyone welcome at the fictional program | Decide |

**Success check:** Your selections reflect the function of the information rather than the maximum number of visible boxes.

:::answer Show Activity 2 suggested layouts
| Slide | Suitable answer | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Title Slide | Separates the main title from the supporting subtitle |
| 2 | Title and Content | Gives a short list one clear content area |
| 3 | Title and Content | Keeps time and location together |
| 4 | Title and Content | Presents a concise invitation or list clearly |
| 5 | Title Only or Title and Content | Gives the closing invitation prominence and room for one necessary detail |

A two-content layout would become useful if a slide had **two meaningfully related categories**—for example, a genuine comparison between read-aloud and independent-reading activities. More boxes do not mean a stronger slide.
:::

### Activity 3: Test two fictional themes

**Task:** Compare these two theme descriptions using the same schedule slide.

- **Option A:** Plain ivory background, dark charcoal heading, dark body text, small blue accents, and familiar letterforms.
- **Option B:** Full-slide photograph, yellow body text across detailed imagery, decorative headings, and three different highlight colors.

1. Choose the better initial theme for an audience that may view the slide from the back of a community room.
2. Identify two risks in the other option.
3. Explain why choosing a built-in theme does not end accessibility review.
4. State one final check you would perform at realistic viewing size.

**Success check:** Your decision cites readability, audience conditions, and clear communication rather than personal taste alone.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model reasoning
**Option A** is the safer starting point because it uses a relatively calm surface, clear foreground text, and restrained accents. **Option B** risks poor local contrast where yellow lettering crosses bright parts of the picture and distracts from the schedule with decorative elements. A built-in or professionally designed theme does not guarantee that every slide, inserted object, or text combination is readable. Check the actual schedule slide at realistic size and verify that all words can be read without relying only on color distinctions.

A different dark theme could also be acceptable if it produced strong contrast and clear hierarchy. The lesson does not require a light background in every situation.
:::

### Activity 4: Apply a layout and theme safely

**Task:** Use an editable fictional deck in PowerPoint, Google Slides, or Impress, or describe the actions you would take on paper.

1. Preserve a separate practice copy if the original matters.
2. Select the opening slide alone.
3. Apply a Title Slide layout or the closest appropriate alternative.
4. Select the schedule slide and apply a layout with one clear content region.
5. Apply a consistent, legible theme or existing master-slide design.
6. Check the full slide sequence for changed colors, wrapping, or repositioned objects.
7. Record the theme choice and one observation about its effect.
8. Save and verify the editable result.

**Success check:** You can distinguish what you changed *on one slide* from what you changed *across the deck*, and you inspect more than the currently selected slide.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model workflow
**PowerPoint example:** Open the deck, preserve a copy if necessary, select Slide 1, choose **Home > Layout > Title Slide**, then select the schedule slide and use **Home > Layout > Title and Content**. Choose a restrained theme through **Design > Themes**. Inspect all thumbnails and each slide at readable size. Save and confirm the file.

**Google Slides example:** Select each intended slide, use **Slide > Apply layout**, then use **Slide > Change theme** or the available Theme control. Review the deck and confirm synchronization.

**Impress example:** Choose each slide through the Slides pane, use **Slide > Layout** or the Layouts sidebar, then select an available master-slide design or applicable template. Review the slides and save the edited file.

If working on paper, record two separate decisions: the opening slide uses a title arrangement, and the deck uses one coherent visual style. A paper plan satisfies the activity without requiring software installation.
:::

### Activity 5: Repair a disrupted slide

**Task:** After a theme change, the Reading Hour schedule slide has these problems:

- The heading reads **When and where?**, but it is now very small.
- The body says **Saturdays at 10:00 a.m.** and **Community room**, but the second line overlaps a decorative picture.
- Another manually inserted text box still uses pale gray text that is hard to read on the new background.

Explain a safe correction sequence. You may choose to undo the theme change or keep the theme and revise the affected slide. Identify what you should **not** do until you have checked the problem.

**Success check:** Your repair preserves correct information, addresses overlap and contrast, and checks the rest of the presentation.

:::answer Show Activity 5 model repair
1. Confirm that the expected slide is selected and the deck is still editable.
2. Inspect whether the slide uses an appropriate layout and a real title placeholder.
3. If the theme change was recent and makes the slide worse, try **Undo** and review the restored presentation.
4. If retaining the theme, adjust the layout or shorten text as appropriate; move or remove unnecessary decorative overlap without deleting the schedule facts.
5. Restore readable foreground/background contrast for the manually formatted object.
6. Check the exact time, room, and heading; a visual repair must not alter their meaning.
7. Inspect all other slides because the theme may have affected them too.
8. Save and verify an editable copy.

**Avoid** deleting the whole slide, recreating the presentation from scratch, shrinking essential information until it is unreadable, or assuming the slide's title and body are gone because they moved visually. Confirm before discarding work.
:::

### Activity 6: Perform a final five-slide review

**Task:** Use the proposed final Reading Hour deck. Write **Pass** or **Needs revision** for each condition and explain what would need repair. If you are using paper, apply the following fictional observations:

- Slide 1 has a clear main heading and smaller subtitle.
- Slide 2 uses the common theme and has three short points.
- Slide 3 uses pale yellow text against a white area and lists the time and room.
- Slide 4 uses the common theme but its title reads **What to brign**.
- Slide 5 uses a readable closing heading and a consistent color accent.

| Review dimension | Question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Layout | Is the arrangement appropriate to the slide's purpose? |
| Theme | Does the deck have a coherent visual identity? |
| Legibility | Is the text readable against the actual background? |
| Content | Are the program details and spelling correct? |
| Consistency | Are differences deliberate and useful? |
| File handling | Is an editable version preserved and verified? |

**Success check:** You find both the styling issue and the spelling issue; you do not approve a deck solely because the thumbnails look similar.

:::answer Show Activity 6 model audit
- **Slide 1: Pass**, assuming its title and subtitle are legible.
- **Slide 2: Pass**, assuming no text is clipped and the short points are accurate.
- **Slide 3: Needs revision.** Pale yellow text on white can be difficult to read. Change the text or background to provide strong contrast; then verify the schedule details.
- **Slide 4: Needs revision.** Correct **What to brign** to **What to bring**. A consistent theme cannot repair spelling.
- **Slide 5: Pass**, assuming the accent is not the only way to identify essential information and the invitation is accurate.

After making corrections, recheck all five slides. An electronic deck should be saved and reopened in the expected editable format. A paper-only learner should retain the annotated plan and explain how the same checks would apply in software.

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

### Misconception 1: A layout and a theme mean the same thing

**Correction:** A layout primarily controls the arrangement of content areas. A theme coordinates presentation styling. They can interact, but they answer different questions.

### Misconception 2: A theme improves whatever text I type

**Correction:** Styling cannot verify facts, simplify an overloaded explanation, or make contradictory statements agree. Content remains the presenter's responsibility.

### Misconception 3: Every slide should use a different layout

**Correction:** Reusing an appropriate layout is often a strength. Change it when the slide's function changes, not to create artificial variety.

### Misconception 4: Every slide must use exactly the same layout

**Correction:** An opening title, a two-item comparison, and a closing invitation can have different layouts while sharing the same theme.

### Misconception 5: A blank layout is always the most flexible and professional

**Correction:** Blank layouts may require more manual positioning and accessibility review. Built-in placeholders usually simplify beginner work.

### Misconception 6: An expensive-looking theme is necessarily clearer

**Correction:** Decorative effects can obstruct information. Clear, readable slides can be simple.

### Misconception 7: A theme guarantees accessibility

**Correction:** Contrast, reading order, image meaning, text size, and real viewing conditions still need inspection.

### Misconception 8: Changing a theme is exactly like changing a background

**Correction:** Background is one visual property. Themes can also influence coordinated fonts, colors, and other design elements.

### Misconception 9: A slide master is another name for a single slide

**Correction:** A master slide defines shared properties for related slides; an ordinary slide carries particular presentation content.

### Misconception 10: Changing the layout always moves every object perfectly

**Correction:** Placeholders and manual objects may respond differently. Inspect the result and repair overlap, misalignment, or unexpected wrapping.

### Misconception 11: The same menu path exists in every version of every app

**Correction:** Desktop, browser, mobile, and different software versions can have different controls. Look for the operation's meaning and use current official help.

### Misconception 12: If a slide looks good as a thumbnail, it will read clearly when projected

**Correction:** Zoomed-out previews can hide small text, clipping, and low contrast. Inspect at realistic viewing size.

### Misconception 13: Copying a slide from another presentation automatically makes its design match

**Correction:** Imported slides may preserve source formatting or receive destination formatting depending on software and paste choices. Review both appearance and content.

### Misconception 14: Different colors are enough to communicate different categories

**Correction:** Label important categories in words as well. Color alone may not communicate reliably to everyone.

### Misconception 15: A design change is successful as soon as I click the theme

**Correction:** The operation is not complete until the affected slides are inspected and the editable file is verified.

### Misconception 16: I must sign up for a new service to complete this lesson

**Correction:** The practice can be completed on paper or with an application already available. You need not register, upload information, or publicly share a deck.

### Quick decision exercise

When something looks wrong, name the type of problem before changing it: **information** (incorrect meaning), **structure** (unsuitable arrangement), **styling** (inconsistent appearance), **accessibility** (difficulty perceiving or navigating content), or **file state** (uncertain save or edit permissions). Some problems involve several categories; naming the primary issue makes the correction more deliberate.

## 11. MGU Library connections and next steps

### Lesson summary

A **layout** decides how a particular slide organizes its content; a **theme** helps the entire presentation share a recognizable appearance. A **template** can provide a prepared beginning, a **background** sits behind content, and a **master slide** supports recurring design properties. Choose each based on its purpose. Neither a suitable layout nor an attractive theme replaces accurate writing and thorough review.

### Readiness check

You are ready to proceed when you can choose Title Slide for a simple opening, explain why Title and Content fits an ordinary explanation, describe when Two Content supports a real comparison, and apply a coordinated readable theme without confusing deck-wide styling with a slide-specific layout. You should also be able to identify one contrast problem, recover from an awkward layout or theme change, and verify that all slides remain correct and editable.
