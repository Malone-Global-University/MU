Basic Computer Skills / Presentation Skills

## Lesson 1: What Is a Presentation?

Imagine that a community center asks you to explain a proposed weekend food-drive program to a small group. You could read a long document aloud, but people might have trouble remembering the main facts. Instead, you could speak for a few minutes while showing a title, a simple schedule, a picture of the collection location, and a short list of ways to volunteer. This is one example of a [[presentation|presentation]]: an organized way to communicate a message to an [[audience|audience]]. The visual pages supporting the talk are called [[slide|slides]].

A presentation is not just a colorful file or a screen full of words. It is a communication activity with a purpose, a message, and people who need to understand that message. Some presentations use slides; others use a demonstration, physical objects, a poster, or no visual aid at all. This course concentrates on the everyday computer skills used to plan, create, deliver, and share [[slide-deck|slide decks]].

You do not need to be an experienced speaker or graphic designer. This first lesson explains what presentations are, when they are useful, how slide decks are organized, what presentation software does, and how to judge whether a basic presentation communicates clearly. Later lessons teach the detailed mechanics of creating slides, choosing layouts, adding media, organizing flow, applying design rules, writing speaker notes, presenting slides, and exporting or sharing the finished work. All examples are fictional. No account, live audience, or paid software is required for the activities here.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 85–110 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Basic computer navigation and file awareness recommended; no prior presentation experience required · **Practice equipment:** Paper and pencil or a basic text editor; an optional presentation application · **Safety:** Use fictional people and information in practice; do not disclose private records or use copyrighted media without appropriate permission.

> **Essential idea:** A successful presentation makes **one clear message easier for a particular audience to understand**. Slides support that message; they do not replace good thinking, accurate information, or understandable speech.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Define a presentation, a slide, a slide deck, and presentation software in ordinary language.
2. Explain the relationship among the purpose, audience, main message, speaker, and visual aids.
3. Identify at least five everyday situations in which a presentation might be appropriate.
4. Distinguish an oral presentation from its supporting slide deck, handout, and written report.
5. Recognize common slide elements and the usual beginning, middle, and ending of a short presentation.
6. Describe what common presentation applications do and recognize basic presentation file formats.
7. Explain why readability, accessibility, truthful information, privacy, and preparation matter.
8. Plan a short fictional presentation before opening presentation software.
9. Assess a simple presentation scenario and recommend a practical improvement.

## 1. What is a presentation?

A [[presentation|presentation]] is an organized communication designed to inform, explain, teach, persuade, report, or demonstrate something to other people. It can happen in a classroom, workplace, community meeting, online call, training session, or informal conversation. A person giving a presentation is the [[presenter|presenter]], while the people receiving it form the [[audience|audience]]. The presenter typically plans what to say and selects information suited to the occasion.

Presentation is a broader idea than presentation software. A nurse can demonstrate a safety procedure without a computer; a student can explain an experiment using a poster; a manager can show project milestones using a few digital slides. In each case, what matters is an understandable message delivered for a purpose.

### Three things to remember

- **The communication:** what you want people to know, remember, decide, or do.
- **The delivery:** how you explain or demonstrate that information to your audience.
- **The supporting material:** the slides, diagrams, handouts, or examples that help people follow the explanation.

A slide deck is only the third part. If a presentation is delivered without any spoken words, such as an automatic information display, its slides must carry enough context to stand on their own. If a speaker delivers a talk without slides, that talk can still be a presentation. Neither situation changes the basic objective: communicate effectively.

### Everyday examples

| Situation | Main purpose | Possible visual support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Student describes a class project | Explain what was done and learned | Photos and a brief results slide |
| Employee gives a team update | Report progress and next steps | Milestone list or timeline |
| Community group recruits volunteers | Invite people to participate | Event schedule and location image |
| Small business explains a service | Introduce benefits and practical details | Simple service diagram |
| Instructor demonstrates a procedure | Teach a repeatable process | Step sequence and labeled example |
| Resident describes a neighborhood concern | Present evidence and request a decision | Map, observations, and proposed actions |
| Family member shares vacation photos | Tell a story | Photos with short captions |
| Nonprofit summarizes an annual program | Report outcomes and limitations | Headline figures and explanation |

The appropriate presentation depends on the audience and setting. A family photo slideshow need not resemble a workplace budget update. The same slide-making software may be useful for both, but the message, tone, and level of detail will differ.

### Presentation, speech, and slide show are not identical

A [[speech|speech]] is spoken communication, which may or may not involve visual aids. A [[slide-show|slide show]] is a sequence of slides displayed on a screen, possibly with automated timing. A complete presentation may combine speech, slides, questions, demonstrations, and discussion.

| Term | What it emphasizes | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Presentation | The overall communication experience | Explaining a proposal to a committee |
| Speech or oral presentation | Spoken delivery | Five-minute project explanation |
| Slide deck | A saved collection of slides | Eight-slide volunteer orientation file |
| Slide show | Displaying slides in sequence | Full-screen slides on a projector |
| Handout | Material an audience can keep or read | One-page schedule or notes |
| Report | A fuller written account | A detailed project evaluation |

A slide deck is not automatically a report. A report may contain complete arguments, methods, and evidence that readers can study independently. A projected slide must usually be understood quickly while listeners follow the presenter. This is why copying a long report directly onto slides often produces a poor result.

> **Key distinction:** The presentation is the communication. The slide deck is one tool used to help the communication.

## 2. Why do people give presentations?

People give presentations because they want an audience to understand information or take an informed next step. Before making slides, the presenter should be able to state the [[purpose|purpose]] and identify the [[audience|audience]]. Without those decisions, it is easy to create attractive pages that do not solve a communication problem.

### Common purposes

| Purpose | Question to answer | Sample objective |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inform | What should people know? | Explain the library's new opening hours |
| Teach | What should people learn to do? | Show the steps for booking a room |
| Report | What happened and why does it matter? | Describe progress on a team project |
| Propose | What choice should the audience consider? | Present an idea for a community garden |
| Persuade | What position is supported by reasons? | Explain why an accessibility improvement is needed |
| Demonstrate | How does something work? | Show how to organize a file folder |
| Celebrate | What achievement should be recognized? | Highlight volunteers' contributions |

These purposes sometimes overlap. A proposal may first inform listeners of a problem and then invite them to support a solution. A training session may mix explanation and demonstration. Still, the presenter benefits from choosing **one primary goal**.

### Know who will listen

A [[target-audience|target audience]] is the group the presentation is designed to serve. Ask what the audience already knows, what vocabulary will be familiar, what questions they may have, and what information they actually need. A beginner learning computer skills needs different explanations from a room of experienced technicians. A volunteer orientation may require a location and start time, while a grant committee may need evidence and cost information.

Also consider the audience's practical circumstances:

- Will people view a large projected screen, individual laptops, or small phones?
- Will they hear the speaker clearly, or rely on captions or text?
- Are they familiar with the topic, language, and abbreviations?
- Is there a strict [[time-limit|time limit]]?
- Does the audience need to make a decision, practice a procedure, or simply understand?
- Will people receive the slides afterward, and will the material still make sense without the speaker?

You cannot assume that every person can see small text, distinguish colors, hear every spoken explanation, or understand unexplained jargon. Audience awareness affects both the spoken words and the visual choices.

### Identify the main message

A [[main-message|main message]] is the single most important idea the audience should remember. For a food-drive presentation, a strong main message might be: **The community center will collect shelf-stable food on Saturdays, and volunteers can join using a simple sign-up process.** A weaker message would be: **Things about our project**, because it does not say what the audience is supposed to learn.

Try completing this sentence before building slides:

> After this presentation, my audience should understand **_____** and, if appropriate, be able to **_____**.

For example: *After this presentation, new volunteers should understand the Saturday collection schedule and be able to locate the sign-up desk.* That statement makes later content decisions easier. A decorative animation that does not clarify the schedule is not essential. A readable map showing the sign-up desk may be very useful.

### Think about whether slides are needed

Not every message needs a deck. A short personal reminder may be better as an email, message, or conversation. A complicated policy may require a written document that readers can review at their own pace. Slides are especially helpful when the speaker needs to show images, compare a small amount of information, outline stages, or keep a group focused on the same topic.

| Communication need | Often a good choice | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quick private reminder | Text message or email | Fast and easy to revisit |
| Long procedure with detailed rules | Written instructions | Readers can check exact details |
| Explain a process to a group | Short presentation with demonstration | Provides common visual reference |
| Share many records for independent review | Report or spreadsheet | Readers can inspect data closely |
| Announce a meeting time and address | Brief message or flyer | A full presentation may add no value |
| Summarize three findings in a meeting | Short slide presentation | Creates an organized verbal explanation |

**Good planning begins before software.** You can identify purpose, audience, and main message with only a pencil and paper.

## 3. What are slides and slide decks?

A [[slide|slide]] is one digital page or screen within a presentation file. A [[slide-deck|slide deck]] is the sequence of slides collected into a single presentation. Like pages in a document, slides can contain information; unlike ordinary document pages, slides are commonly designed to be displayed one at a time during a talk.

A typical slide might display a [[title|title]], a short statement, a photograph, a simple chart, or a diagram. Not all slides need all of these. The aim is to give the audience a useful visual reference without forcing them to read paragraphs while someone else is speaking.

### Common slide elements

| Element | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slide title | Identifies the point of the slide | Volunteer Schedule |
| Body text | Supplies a few important details | Saturdays, 9 AM–noon |
| Bullet list | Separates related short points | Drop-off, sorting, sign-up |
| Image | Shows an object, place, or example | Photo of the collection entrance |
| Diagram | Explains connections or steps | Donate → Sort → Distribute |
| Chart | Shows selected quantitative relationships | Donations by month |
| Caption | Explains an image or graphic | Entrance on the west side |
| Source note | Credits evidence or visual material | Source: fictional program records |
| Speaker notes | Help the presenter remember explanations | Mention volunteer parking instructions |

An [[image|image]] is useful when it supplies information, not merely because empty space looks uninteresting. A [[chart|chart]] may make a pattern easier to see, but it can also mislead if its labels, scale, or source are unclear. A [[diagram|diagram]] often helps describe an ordered process better than a long paragraph.

### Titles, layouts, and themes

The [[slide-layout|slide layout]] is the arrangement of areas or placeholders on a slide: for example, a title at the top and content underneath. A [[theme|theme]] is a coordinated set of styling choices such as colors and fonts that can help slides look consistent. A [[template|template]] is a reusable starting design or structure, sometimes including layouts, example slides, or preset elements.

These concepts are related but not interchangeable:

- **Slide:** an individual presentation page.
- **Layout:** how a slide's content areas are arranged.
- **Theme:** a coordinated overall visual style.
- **Template:** a reusable starting design for a new presentation.

A beginner does not need to customize every color or font to prepare a meaningful message. Many applications offer simple defaults. Detailed layout and theme instruction belongs in Lesson 3, *Choosing Layouts and Themes*.

### A simple five-slide deck

Suppose you need to introduce a fictional neighborhood reading program. A clear beginner deck could contain:

| Slide | Title | Main job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Neighborhood Reading Program | Introduce the topic and presenter |
| 2 | Why the Program Exists | Explain the community need |
| 3 | How the Program Works | Show when and where sessions happen |
| 4 | How to Participate | Explain what listeners should do |
| 5 | Questions and Next Steps | Summarize and invite questions |

This is only a planning example, not a universal rule. A quick update might need two or three slides; a more technical explanation might need more. **The number of slides should follow the message and allotted time**, not a fixed quota.

### The speaker supplies context

The visual slide **How the Program Works** might say *Tuesdays, 4 PM; Community Room B; ages 10–13*. The presenter can explain who supervises, why the schedule was chosen, and how guardians can ask questions. Those details may belong in [[speaker-notes|speaker notes]], spoken remarks, a handout, or supporting documentation rather than as several paragraphs on the slide.

A presentation can also be designed for independent reading, with more explanatory material in a [[self-guided-presentation|self-guided presentation]]. That requires deliberate planning: a deck without narration cannot assume someone is present to fill gaps. Distinguish the uses instead of treating every slide format as identical.

## 4. How is a simple presentation organized?

An effective short presentation usually has a recognizable [[introduction|beginning]], an explanatory [[body-section|middle]], and a [[conclusion|ending]]. These parts help people know where the speaker is going, what the important points are, and what to remember afterward. Detailed slide sequencing is taught in Lesson 5, *Organizing Slide Flow*; here the goal is to recognize the basic logic.

### Beginning: explain the topic and why it matters

The opening should establish the topic, identify the purpose, and give the audience a reason to listen. An introduction does not need a dramatic quotation or a complex animation. A direct beginning works:

> Our neighborhood reading program will offer free weekly practice sessions. Today I will explain how the sessions work and how families can participate.

This opening gives a subject, value, and roadmap. It is better than starting with a slide that only says **Welcome**, because the audience still has to guess what the talk is about.

### Middle: develop a small number of connected points

The main part should organize supporting information in a sequence that people can follow. For a beginner's short talk, two or three main points may be sufficient. These points might answer **why**, **what**, and **how**; or describe **problem**, **proposal**, and **next steps**.

[[transition|Transitions]] are the connections between ideas. A presenter can say, *Now that we have discussed why the program is needed, here is the proposed schedule.* In software, a [[slide-transition|slide transition]] also refers to a visual effect used when moving between slides. Those two meanings should not be confused. Clear spoken transitions are often more valuable than elaborate visual effects.

### Ending: reinforce the message and identify next steps

The ending should restate the key idea, describe a practical next action if there is one, and allow appropriate questions. A final slide that merely says **The End** may miss an opportunity to make the message useful. For example: *The program begins next month. Families can register at the center's front desk. What questions can I answer?*

A [[call-to-action|call to action]] is the specific next step invited by a presentation. Not every talk needs one: an informational history lesson might simply conclude with its main finding. If you do ask the audience to act, the request should be accurate, realistic, and within their authority.

### Worked example: turning a long announcement into a short deck

Consider this fictional announcement:

> The Eastside Community Center hopes to run a free reading practice program for children ages ten through thirteen. Sessions are planned for Tuesdays at four o'clock in Community Room B. Volunteers will help learners read short passages and discuss unfamiliar words. Families can request participation information from the front desk. Dates and staffing are not yet final, and the center will confirm the schedule before enrollment begins.

A weak deck could copy that whole paragraph onto one slide. People would try to read the screen and listen to the speaker simultaneously. Instead, first identify the message: *A proposed free reading program may be available, but enrollment is not open until the center confirms details.* Next, divide the material into meaningful visual units.

| Stage | Slide headline | Visual content | What the presenter explains |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | Proposed Reading Program | Free weekly reading practice | Purpose of the proposal |
| Need | What Learners Will Practice | Reading aloud and word discussion | How the sessions would work |
| Logistics | Planned Schedule | Tuesday, 4 PM; Room B | Time and place are provisional |
| Next step | Watch for Confirmation | Ask the center's front desk | Enrollment is not yet open |

**Accuracy matters:** Because the program is only proposed, the slide should not say *Registration now open*. A polished design cannot correct a false statement. If important arrangements are still uncertain, make that uncertainty visible and explain what will confirm them.

### Speaking versus reading

A presenter should understand the material rather than depend on reading every line exactly as written. A short list of [[talking-points|talking points]] can guide a natural explanation. Some situations require precise wording—legal notices, safety steps, quotations, or approved statements may need to be read carefully. The general objective is to avoid filling ordinary slides with a speech transcript.

### Plan and rehearse before delivery

A [[storyboard|storyboard]] is a rough plan showing what each slide will communicate. You can create it on paper as five labeled rectangles. A [[rehearsal|rehearsal]] is a practice run of the presentation, preferably checking the message, timing, and whether someone unfamiliar with the subject can follow it. You do not need sophisticated tools to do either one.

For a first attempt, sketch the slide titles, say aloud what you would explain, notice where information is missing, and simplify any slide that asks an audience to read too much. Do not treat the number of completed slides as evidence that the message is ready.

## 5. Which tools and files are used for presentations?

[[presentation-software|Presentation software]] is an application for creating, editing, organizing, and showing slide decks. Most presentation programs let users add slides, type text, choose layouts, place images, rearrange slides, save the work, and start a full-screen slide show. Some also support shared editing, comments, animations, recording, captions, and online delivery. The exact features and menu names vary by product and version.

### Common presentation applications

| Application | General description | Common way to work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Microsoft PowerPoint | Presentation application for Microsoft environments | Desktop or web, depending on access |
| Google Slides | Browser-based presentation application in Google's document suite | Online editing and collaboration |
| Apple Keynote | Presentation application associated with Apple platforms | Mac, other supported Apple devices, or supported web access |
| LibreOffice Impress | Presentation application in the LibreOffice suite | Desktop application with local files |

The purpose of learning presentation skills is **not** to memorize one commercial brand. Different programs often share basic concepts: slides, layouts, text boxes, images, slide order, a save command, and a presentation or slide-show mode. Their interfaces differ, and individual feature availability can depend on your device, subscription, account, organization, and software version.

### What an editing window often includes

An ordinary presentation editor may show a [[slide-thumbnail|slide thumbnail]] area at one side, a larger [[editing-canvas|editing canvas]] displaying the selected slide, and a [[toolbar|toolbar]] or [[ribbon|ribbon]] containing commands. A notes area may be available beneath the slide or in another view. The precise placement is not guaranteed across applications.

| Area or control | Common function |
| --- | --- |
| Slides or thumbnail pane | View slide order and select a slide |
| Main editing area | Change the currently selected slide |
| Insert command | Add text, images, shapes, or other objects |
| Layout or theme controls | Change the arrangement or styling |
| Notes area | Store presenter reminders |
| Present or Slide Show command | Display slides to an audience |
| Save or file controls | Keep, rename, download, or export work |

In Lesson 2, *Creating Slides*, you will use the editor more directly. In this lesson, simply recognize the overall workspace. You can learn the concept without creating a real account.

### A presentation file is different from a slide displayed on screen

A [[presentation-file|presentation file]] is a saved digital document containing the deck and other associated data. It may be stored in a local folder, on a removable drive, or in a [[cloud-storage|cloud storage]] service. The display on a projector is an output of the presentation; it is not a substitute for retaining the saved file.

Common formats include:

| Format | Typical purpose | Important limitation or note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `.pptx` | Editable PowerPoint-style presentation | Features may vary across programs |
| `.odp` | Editable OpenDocument presentation | Appearance may change in another program |
| `.key` | Keynote presentation document | Requires compatible tools or conversion |
| `.pdf` | Fixed-layout document for reading or distribution | Usually not suitable for editing a slide deck |
| `.png` or `.jpg` | Individual exported slide images | Images do not preserve normal slide editing |

Some online services keep an original presentation as a cloud-native document rather than a conventional `.pptx` or `.odp` file. Downloading an editable copy, exporting a PDF, or sharing an access link are different operations. **Do not assume a shared link gives everyone editing access, or that a PDF preserves animations and speaker notes.**

### Save, export, and share are different actions

To [[save|save]] is to keep your current work in a file or service. To [[export|export]] is to create a version in another output format, such as a PDF. To [[share|share]] is to let other people access the work through a copy, invitation, or link, subject to [[permissions|permissions]]. Presentation software may save automatically, but beginners should still know where the document is stored, what it is called, and whether it can be reopened.

A simple filename is `Community-Reading-Program-Draft.pptx`. Avoid names such as `final-final-really-final` that become confusing when revisions accumulate. The file should be identifiable without putting sensitive information into the name.

When using a public or shared computer, do not assume that signing out, closing a window, or deleting a local download handles every cloud copy. Follow the organization's rules, confirm the file's location, and avoid leaving confidential work exposed.

## 6. What makes a presentation clear and responsible?

The central standard is whether people can understand the message reliably. Attractive colors, transitions, and complex diagrams cannot compensate for a confusing argument, unreadable text, unsupported claims, or lost context. [[clarity|Clarity]] should be treated as a design and communication requirement, not as decoration.

### Keep one central idea in view

Each slide should have a clear job. A slide about **when volunteers arrive** should not also attempt to explain the program's budget, safety policy, and organizational history in tiny print. That information may belong on separate slides or in a handout. The [[one-idea-principle|one-idea principle]] is a planning aid, not an inflexible law: some diagrams and carefully labeled comparisons necessarily combine related elements to communicate one overall point.

A useful test is to ask, **Can I explain the purpose of this slide in one sentence?** If not, consider simplifying the content, splitting it, or changing the title to a specific claim.

### Readability is more important than decoration

[[readability|Readability]] means that the content can be visually read and understood under the actual viewing conditions. Text large enough for a laptop may be too small from the back of a classroom; thin pale lettering on a light background may disappear when projected. Clear [[contrast|contrast]] between foreground and background, familiar fonts, meaningful headings, and uncluttered space usually help.

| Less effective choice | More useful alternative | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full paragraph copied from a report | Short headline and selected points | Easier to read while listening |
| Tiny text across the entire slide | Fewer words and readable type | Supports people farther from the screen |
| Pale gray text on a white background | Stronger text-background contrast | Improves visual distinction |
| Several distracting animations | Simple movement only when useful | Keeps attention on information |
| Unlabeled chart | Chart with clear labels and context | Reduces interpretation mistakes |
| Image with no purpose | Relevant image with a short caption | Reinforces the message |

There is no single font size that suits every room, screen, or accessibility need. Test the presentation under realistic conditions rather than assuming that it looks readable because it is clear on your own monitor.

### Accessibility helps people receive the message

[[accessibility|Accessibility]] means designing communication so people with different needs and ways of interacting can use it. At an introductory level, good practices include using readable text, strong contrast, descriptive titles, not relying on color alone to show meaning, and explaining the main point of images and charts aloud. When the format allows, add [[alternative-text|alternative text]] for meaningful images and [[captions|captions]] or a transcript for relevant audio and video.

For example, if a chart uses red to indicate *late* and green to indicate *on time*, add the words **Late** and **On time**, not color alone. If a slide shows a photo of the meeting entrance, say which entrance it is and explain how to find it. A person listening without a clear view of the screen should still understand the main point whenever possible.

### Respect evidence, context, and intellectual property

A presentation can spread incorrect information very quickly because its message feels condensed and authoritative. Check factual claims against appropriate [[source|sources]]. A [[citation|citation]] identifies where evidence, quotations, images, or figures came from. The level of citation detail depends on the setting, but copying an image or number from the web does not automatically make it free to reuse or reliable.

Use the following habits:

- Verify important names, dates, figures, and quotations before presenting them.
- Label estimates, hypothetical numbers, and uncertain plans accurately.
- Distinguish facts from opinions, proposals, and predictions.
- Credit other people's words, photographs, graphics, and research as appropriate.
- Use media you created, media you are licensed or permitted to use, or resources with clearly applicable reuse terms.
- Avoid charts that omit context in a way that changes the apparent meaning of data.

A [[license|license]] grants specified rights under stated conditions; it is not the same as making every use permissible. A web image's availability does not prove that you may copy or republish it. When uncertain, omit the image, use an original illustration, or ask for guidance.

### Privacy and authorization still apply

Do not put addresses, contact details, account numbers, private workplace records, student information, or identifiable photographs into a presentation without a valid need and proper authorization. [[privacy|Privacy]] matters even when the audience is small. Before showing a live desktop or browser during a presentation, close unrelated windows and notifications that could reveal sensitive details.

If you distribute a deck, check whether it contains [[speaker-notes|speaker notes]], comments, hidden slides, or internal-only information. Removing visible text from one slide may not remove all underlying data from the file. Use the application's inspection tools and your organization's policies before external distribution. Details of exporting and sharing are covered later in Lesson 9.

### Prepare for ordinary technical problems

A good presentation includes practical preparation: check the saved file, inspect the display, confirm that embedded material can be opened, and practice any equipment you plan to use. A [[backup-plan|backup plan]] might be speaking from a short paper outline if the projector fails. For a high-stakes meeting, confirm that the room, file access, audiovisual equipment, and time available match what you planned.

| Possible issue | Practical first response |
| --- | --- |
| Text is unreadable from the back | Increase readability and reduce content |
| Slides appear in the wrong order | Review and reorder the deck before presenting |
| Audience cannot see an important chart label | Describe the information aloud and revise the visual |
| Internet-dependent content does not open | Use a prepared non-online explanation where permitted |
| You forget the next point | Refer to a simple outline or presenter notes |
| Projector is unavailable | Deliver the core message without slides if possible |
| Confidential window appears | Stop displaying it before continuing |

**Professional habit:** Prioritize a truthful, understandable, accessible message and a sensible fallback. Special effects are optional; communication is not.

## 7. Guided practice: plan and evaluate a presentation

The activities are designed for self-study. You can write answers on paper or in a text editor. If presentation software is available, you may explore it, but no paid application, account, internet connection, or public speaking is required. Use only the fictional scenarios shown here. Complete each activity before opening its answer panel.

### Activity 1: Is it a presentation?

**Goal:** Distinguish a presentation from its supporting materials.

For each situation, write **presentation**, **supporting material**, or **another communication format**. More than one category may seem possible, so explain your choice based on the main role.

1. A volunteer explains the new food-drive schedule to a group using five slides.
2. An organizer saves the five-slide file but does not show or discuss it.
3. A resident sends a one-sentence reminder by text message.
4. A teacher explains how to use a printer without displaying slides.
5. A visitor reads a printed instruction sheet independently.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answers
1. **Presentation:** the volunteer is delivering organized communication to an audience; the slides provide support.
2. **Supporting material:** the saved deck is a presentation resource. It could itself serve as a self-guided presentation if designed and used that way.
3. **Another communication format:** the simple text reminder is not ordinarily a presentation.
4. **Presentation:** the teacher communicates and demonstrates to an audience even without slides.
5. **Another communication format:** the printed instructions function primarily as a self-guided document or handout.

The lesson is not about forcing rigid labels on every situation. It is about noticing the difference between the *communication activity* and the *material* used for it.
:::

### Activity 2: Find the purpose, audience, and main message

**Goal:** Plan the communication before opening software.

A fictional neighborhood club wants to tell newly enrolled members about its weekly gardening meetings. Members have little gardening experience. Meetings are planned for Wednesdays at 5 PM at the community greenhouse. There is no participation fee. Members must confirm the schedule with the club coordinator before attending.

Write four short answers:

1. Who is the primary audience?
2. What is the main purpose?
3. Write one complete main-message sentence.
4. Which detail must be described as provisional or requiring confirmation?

:::answer Show Activity 2 model answers
1. New club members, especially people without gardening experience.
2. Inform members about the proposed meeting arrangements and tell them how to confirm attendance details.
3. **Example:** The club plans free beginner-friendly gardening meetings on Wednesdays at 5 PM, but members should confirm the schedule with the coordinator before attending.
4. The schedule must be confirmed; it should not be represented as guaranteed or final.

The main message should combine the useful facts with the important uncertainty.
:::

### Activity 3: Build a paper storyboard

**Goal:** Outline a short deck without using presentation software.

Using the fictional gardening club from Activity 2, draw four rectangles on paper. Label each rectangle with a slide title. Beneath each, write a few words showing what the audience would see. Do not write a paragraph on each slide. Check that the sequence has a clear beginning and ending.

:::answer Show Activity 3 sample storyboard
| Slide | Suggested title | Visible message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Beginner Gardening Meetings | New member orientation |
| 2 | What We Will Learn | Planting, watering, and basic tool safety |
| 3 | Planned Meeting Details | Wednesdays, 5 PM; Community Greenhouse |
| 4 | Confirm Before Attending | Contact the club coordinator for final schedule |

Other sequences can be valid if they preserve the same facts, do not invent confirmed arrangements, and make the next step clear.
:::

### Activity 4: Choose the better format

**Goal:** Decide when a slide deck adds value.

Select the most practical primary format for each fictional need: **short presentation**, **detailed written document**, or **short message**.

1. Explain a three-step recycling process to twenty new volunteers at a meeting.
2. Provide a twelve-page safety policy that staff must reference later.
3. Tell a coworker that tomorrow's meeting starts fifteen minutes later.
4. Summarize project achievements and next steps at a five-minute team meeting.
5. Provide a precise procedure people should follow after the meeting without relying on memory.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answers
1. **Short presentation**, perhaps with an actual demonstration.
2. **Detailed written document** so readers can review exact requirements.
3. **Short message** because there is one simple change.
4. **Short presentation** because organized spoken explanation and selected visuals may help a group.
5. **Detailed written document** because future users need steps they can check. A presentation can introduce the procedure but should not replace an authoritative reference.
:::

### Activity 5: Diagnose and improve a crowded slide

**Goal:** Recognize readability and information problems.

A fictional slide is titled **Important Things**. It contains a twelve-line paragraph in tiny light-gray text, a large unrelated decorative photograph, a chart without labels, and an animated symbol that moves repeatedly. The presenter intends to explain the program's volunteer shift times.

Identify **at least five problems** and redesign the slide in words. Give it a more useful title and name two or three pieces of information the slide should display.

:::answer Show Activity 5 model answer
Possible problems:

- **Vague title:** the audience cannot tell what the slide is about.
- **Excessive paragraph text:** people will struggle to read while listening.
- **Small text:** information may not be readable at a distance.
- **Weak contrast:** pale gray on a light background is difficult to distinguish.
- **Irrelevant photograph:** it takes space without improving understanding.
- **Unlabeled chart:** listeners cannot interpret the graphic accurately.
- **Continuous animation:** it competes with the central message.

**Improved slide title:** Volunteer Shift Times.

**Visible information:** Saturday setup, 8:30 AM; collection, 9 AM–noon; cleanup, noon–12:30 PM. These are invented practice times, not real event arrangements. Use an easily read list or timeline and explain any exceptions aloud.
:::

### Activity 6: Plan a safe five-minute presentation

**Goal:** Integrate the lesson's core concepts.

Create a mini presentation plan for a fictional public library introducing its free computer-help desk. The desk is proposed for Thursday afternoons, and its exact opening date has not been approved. Your audience is first-time library visitors who may have little computer experience.

Write:

1. The audience and communication purpose.
2. A one-sentence main message that reflects the unapproved opening date.
3. Three to five slide titles in a logical sequence.
4. One useful visual aid and one visual that would not help.
5. Two accessibility considerations and one privacy consideration.
6. One practical backup if a projector is unavailable.

:::answer Show Activity 6 sample plan
**Audience:** First-time library visitors, including beginning computer users.

**Purpose:** Explain what the proposed help desk would offer and how visitors can obtain confirmed information.

**Main message:** The library plans a free computer-help desk on Thursday afternoons, but the opening date is not yet approved, so visitors should ask library staff for confirmation.

**Suggested slides:** 1. Proposed Computer-Help Desk; 2. Questions the Desk May Help With; 3. Planned Thursday Schedule; 4. How to Get Confirmed Information.

**Useful visual:** A clear diagram or photograph showing the library information desk, with permission and an accurate caption. **Unhelpful visual:** An unrelated decorative image that does not explain where to go.

**Accessibility:** Use large readable text and describe visual directions aloud; do not rely on color alone. **Privacy:** Do not display real patron accounts, passwords, or individual help requests.

**Backup:** Explain the four points using a paper outline and point visitors toward the library's authorized information channel.

Many answers are acceptable. The essentials are clarity, truthfulness, a suitable audience focus, and a practical next step.
:::

## 8. Common misconceptions and problems

Beginners sometimes believe that presentation software can automatically solve the task of communicating. The application is useful, but software cannot independently decide what your audience needs or guarantee that your claims are supported.

| Misconception or problem | Why it is misleading | Better habit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A presentation must use PowerPoint | A presentation can use other software or no slides | Choose the right communication method |
| More slides always mean better work | Additional slides can add clutter or consume time | Include only necessary ideas |
| Every sentence I say belongs on the slide | Reading and listening compete for attention | Put key points on slides and explain aloud |
| A theme makes information accurate | Appearance does not verify factual claims | Check evidence and label uncertainty |
| Everyone can read what I see on my monitor | Viewing distance and abilities differ | Test readability and accessibility |
| A slide deck and a written report are identical | They support different modes of reading | Use the appropriate amount of detail |
| A beautiful image is always useful | Decorative imagery can distract or mislead | Choose visuals that communicate |
| If a photo is online, I may reuse it freely | Copyright and license conditions still apply | Verify permission and credit appropriately |
| Saving a PDF keeps the deck fully editable | PDF usually preserves a reading layout, not editing features | Keep the original presentation file |
| Sending a share link makes it private | Permissions may grant wider access than intended | Review recipient and access settings |
| A presentation needs an animation on every slide | Motion can increase distraction | Use effects only for a real purpose |
| If the projector fails, I must stop completely | The central message may be deliverable another way | Prepare a short paper or spoken fallback |
| Every presentation should ask for a purchase or vote | Many presentations are purely educational | Match the ending to the actual purpose |
| A proposed event date can be shown as confirmed | Uncertainty is meaningful information | Label tentative information accurately |

### A simple decision checklist

Before building a deck, ask these six questions:

1. **Purpose:** What is the communication trying to accomplish?
2. **Audience:** Who needs the message, and what do they already understand?
3. **Main message:** What is the one idea they should remember?
4. **Evidence:** Are the main facts, dates, and claims accurate?
5. **Format:** Would slides make this information easier to understand than a short message or written document?
6. **Practical conditions:** How much time is available, what equipment is needed, and what happens if it does not work?

If those six questions have useful answers, you are ready to begin creating slides in the next lesson.

## 11. MGU Library connections and next steps

### Lesson summary

A presentation is an organized effort to communicate to an audience. Effective planning starts with purpose, audience, and main message. A slide deck supports the explanation rather than substituting for it. Simple slide titles, a logical introduction and conclusion, meaningful visuals, readable text, accurate claims, and thoughtful accessibility make the information easier to understand.
