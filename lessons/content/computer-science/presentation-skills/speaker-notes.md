## Lesson 7: Speaker Notes

Imagine that you are giving a short presentation about a fictional community center's Saturday Digital Skills Workshop. One slide shows three short items: **Welcome**, **What to bring**, and **Where to get help**. The slide is easy for an audience to read, but it does not remind you to explain that registration is free, demonstrate where participants should sign in, or pause for questions. Adding every instruction to the visible slide would make the slide crowded. A set of [[speaker-notes|speaker notes]] can hold those reminders while the slide stays focused on the audience.

Speaker notes are written prompts associated with particular slides. They may contain talking points, explanations, evidence, transition phrases, timing reminders, and directions for demonstrations. In many presentation applications, the notes can appear in a separate editing area and, when supported and correctly configured, in a [[presenter-view|presenter view]] that the speaker sees during a presentation. **The notes are not the same as the text displayed on the slide.** They also are not automatically confidential: someone with access to the original presentation file may be able to inspect them.

Lessons 1–6 introduced the purpose of presentations, slide creation, layouts, media, slide flow, and design. This lesson teaches you to prepare and manage notes so you can speak naturally, deliver accurate information, and recover when a presentation does not go exactly as planned. **Lesson 8, Presenting Slides,** will examine the broader process of operating the slideshow and communicating with an audience. This lesson concentrates on notes as a practical preparation tool. Microsoft PowerPoint on Windows 11 is the principal desktop example, with Google Slides and LibreOffice Impress as alternatives. Menus, feature availability, and multi-screen behavior vary by version, browser, device, and organization settings. All activities can be completed with fictional information and paper if you do not have presentation software.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 100–140 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Presentation Skills Lessons 1–6 recommended · **Practice equipment:** Paper and pencil or an available slide editor; a second screen is optional · **Safety:** Use fictional details in exercises; do not place passwords, confidential conversations, private participant information, or unreleased organizational decisions in speaker notes. Assume notes could be visible to anyone receiving the editable file.

> **Essential idea:** **Keep the slide useful to the audience; keep the notes useful to the speaker.** Prepare a few clear cues for each slide, rehearse the message aloud, test how notes will appear, and check what information travels with the file before sharing it.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain what speaker notes are and distinguish them from visible slide text, comments, captions, and handouts.
2. Describe situations in which notes improve accuracy, organization, confidence, and accessibility.
3. Write clear notes using opening statements, talking points, examples, transitions, and timing cues.
4. Distinguish brief [[cue-notes|cue notes]] from an optional full [[script|script]] and decide when each is appropriate.
5. Add, edit, find, and review notes in PowerPoint, Google Slides, and LibreOffice Impress using version-appropriate controls.
6. Explain how a presenter display differs from an audience display and why screen-sharing settings must be tested.
7. Plan for a single screen, a projector, printed notes, or a presenter view without assuming any configuration is private.
8. Identify and remove information that is not suitable for sharing inside an editable presentation.
9. Rehearse with notes, manage timing, and revise notes after spotting unclear passages or unreliable facts.
10. Diagnose common problems such as missing notes, small text, notes on the wrong slide, and unexpected note visibility.
11. Prepare a five-slide fictional presentation with useful notes and verify that the audience-visible material and speaker guidance remain distinct.

## 1. What are speaker notes, and what are they for?

A [[presentation|presentation]] is a communication activity that may use a sequence of [[slide|slides]] to help an audience follow a message. A slide generally shows selected words, images, or diagrams. Speaker notes hold material meant to help the person delivering that slide. Some people call notes *presenter notes*, *talking notes*, or *slide notes*. The exact label depends on the application. **The function is more important than the label:** a note supports what you plan to say or do without forcing every reminder onto the screen.

Consider a slide entitled **Three steps to join the workshop**, with the visible items **Register**, **Bring a device if available**, and **Ask for assistance**. Your notes might say, “Explain that registration is free. Clarify that the center has a few loaner devices. Pause after the second step and ask whether anyone needs help finding the sign-in desk.” The visible slide provides a concise map of the subject; the notes preserve the explanation and delivery sequence.

### Notes belong to a slide, not to the projected words

Most slide editors store speaker notes in association with a specific slide. When you select another slide, its notes area changes to the notes for that slide. A slide can have an empty notes area. It may also have more note text than fits in the visible editing pane, in which case you may need to scroll or resize that pane. Notes can be changed without necessarily changing the content of the slide.

A [[notes-pane|notes pane]] is the area where you type and edit this supporting material. A [[notes-page|notes page]] is a layout used by some applications to show a slide and its accompanying note text together, particularly for review or printing. A [[slide-show|slide show]] is the running presentation. These are related views of the same work, but they are not interchangeable.

| Information | Usually belongs on the slide | Usually belongs in speaker notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main message | Yes, as a concise title or visual | May be restated as a speaking reminder |
| Three key steps | Yes, if the audience needs to remember them | Explanation and example for each step |
| Full opening greeting | Rarely necessary | Useful if you want an opening cue |
| Presenter timing | Generally no | “Allow about 45 seconds for this slide” |
| Source detail | Essential credit or source when needed | Additional checking information or contextual explanation |
| Audience action | Yes, if viewers must act on it | Prompt to repeat or demonstrate the action |
| Private password | No | No—do not put it in the file |
| Instructions for a live demonstration | Only if viewers need the steps on screen | Speaker's procedure, fallback, and timing |

### Notes, comments, captions, and handouts are different

It is easy to confuse notes with several other presentation features. A [[comment|comment]] often supports collaboration or editorial review: “Please verify this date” or “Could we simplify this chart?” A comment is not automatically a speaking cue. [[captions|Captions]] are visible text representing speech and meaningful sounds for the audience, either live or in recorded media. Speaker notes do **not** replace captions. A [[handout|handout]] is a document intended for audience use before, during, or after the talk. It may include fuller explanations, instructions, and resources that people can read independently.

| Feature | Main intended user | Main purpose | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Visible slide text | Audience | Communicate the central point | Too much text can overwhelm a slide |
| Speaker notes | Presenter | Support accurate speaking and delivery | Notes can be exposed in shared files or screens |
| Comments | Authors and reviewers | Discuss revisions or questions | Not a reliable speaking script |
| Captions | Audience | Make spoken information accessible as text | Notes do not create captions automatically |
| Handout | Audience | Provide durable, independent reference | Must be checked for completeness and accessibility |
| Transcript | Audience or reviewer | Record or supply the spoken content | A set of prompts is not necessarily a full transcript |

### Why notes help people at different experience levels

Beginners often believe notes are a sign that a person has not learned the material. The opposite may be true: notes are a method of preparing responsibly. Experienced speakers use them to protect the accuracy of names, numbers, dates, citations, and complex procedures. A novice might use them to remember how to start and end. A person speaking in a language they are still learning may use them for unfamiliar terms. A trainer may need a reminder to allow questions or explain how to complete an activity.

Speaker notes can also reduce pressure to memorize exact words. A list of prompts can make it easier to maintain [[eye-contact|eye contact]] when appropriate, speak at a sustainable pace, and focus on the audience rather than on a long paragraph of on-screen text. However, notes are not a substitute for understanding the subject. If you cannot explain a slide without reading a long paragraph word for word, you may need to simplify the message or practice it further.

### When not to use notes as your only source

Notes may be helpful in a small meeting, large lecture, online workshop, team briefing, or recorded demonstration. They should not be your only way to preserve critical information. A presentation may be opened by someone without the original file; presenter view might not be available; a screen-sharing setup might fail; or a printed copy might be needed. If the audience must retain an exact address, safety procedure, policy requirement, or deadline, provide it visibly or in an accessible audience document rather than keeping it solely in private notes.

A practical division is **slides for what the audience must see, notes for how the speaker will explain it, and a separate reference when people must keep the details**. That structure helps prevent the common mistake of using speaker notes as a hidden substitute for essential audience information.

### Worked example: turn a crowded slide into two useful layers

A draft slide contains the following paragraph: “Welcome to our Saturday Digital Skills Workshop, which begins at 10:00 a.m. in Room B. Sign in at the entrance. If you do not have a computer, a loaner may be available. Today's session covers browser navigation and safe account habits, and there will be time for questions at the end.”

For a live welcome slide, simplify the screen to **Saturday Digital Skills Workshop**, **10:00 a.m. · Room B**, and **Sign in at the entrance**. Then write notes: “Welcome everyone. Introduce the workshop topics in one sentence. Mention that a few loaner devices may be available; confirm with staff before promising one. Invite anyone who needs sign-in help to ask at the desk. Transition: ‘We will begin with the skills you will practice today.’” The audience sees what it needs to locate and begin the event. The speaker retains the contextual reminders. The example uses fictional facts and does not imply that a real center offers loaners.

## 2. How do you write useful speaker notes?

Useful notes begin with the [[audience|audience]] and the [[purpose|purpose]] of the presentation. Before entering text, complete two short sentences: “The audience needs to understand ______.” and “On this slide, I need to explain ______.” The first keeps the audience's needs visible; the second defines the task for the notes. The result should be a speaking aid rather than a duplicate of every line on the slide.

### A simple repeatable note structure

For most beginner presentations, four elements are enough:

1. **Main speaking point:** What is the central message of this slide?
2. **Supporting detail:** What example, fact, or demonstration helps explain it?
3. **Delivery cue:** Should you point, pause, ask, demonstrate, or check for understanding?
4. **Transition:** How will you lead into the next slide?

You may also include a time estimate, a citation reminder, an unfamiliar pronunciation, or a fallback plan. Do not add elements just because a template has room. A short note should remain short when the slide is straightforward.

| Note element | Example for a fictional workshop | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main point | “A browser opens and displays websites” | Reminds the speaker of the concept |
| Example | “Compare opening a website with opening a book to a page” | Provides an understandable explanation |
| Demonstration cue | “Point to the address bar” | Connects words with the projected image |
| Question prompt | “Ask: Where do you type a website address?” | Encourages participation |
| Time cue | “Approximately one minute” | Helps maintain the overall schedule |
| Transition | “Next: reading links before clicking” | Connects the explanation to the next topic |

### Cue notes versus a full script

[[cue-notes|Cue notes]] use fragments, keywords, or short sentences to remind you what to say. They usually suit familiar topics and conversational speaking. A [[script|script]] provides fuller wording. It can be appropriate for a recorded announcement, an exact legal or policy statement, an interpreter's preparation, or a novice who benefits from more structure. Neither format is inherently better. The important question is whether the format helps you communicate accurately without becoming dependent on reading the screen continuously.

For example, a cue note could say, “Explain what the address bar does; demonstrate with fictional address; pause for questions.” A fuller script could say, “The address bar shows the location of the page you are visiting. A webpage may also contain a search box, but a search box is not the same as the address bar. Let's identify both on this training image.” A useful mixed format uses exact words for a critical definition and brief cues for familiar supporting remarks.

Avoid writing extremely long notes just because the editor accepts them. Long notes can scroll away from the line you need, shrink in certain displays, or tempt you to speak too quickly. A long script may be better kept in a separately managed, accessible script document, subject to the same privacy and sharing rules.

### Write for speaking, not for silent reading

An ordinary report might say, “The organization has initiated a phased implementation of instructional resource modernization.” A speaker can usually say, “We are updating our teaching resources in stages.” Notes can preserve a precise technical phrase when necessary, but the spoken version should be comprehensible to the intended audience.

Try reading each note aloud. If you lose your place, split it into shorter cues. If a sentence sounds awkward, rewrite it in language you would actually use. Replace vague prompts like “Explain more” with a concrete task: “Give one example of a safe browser habit.” A [[delivery-cue|delivery cue]] should tell you what to *do*, not merely urge you to perform well.

### Use numbers, names, and pronunciation carefully

Speaker notes are particularly useful for details that can be misremembered. You might write a full date, distinguish two similar terms, or add a [[pronunciation-guide|pronunciation guide]] for an unfamiliar name. Check the source before adding it. Do not assume that an accurate note from last month remains accurate today. Mark information that needs reconfirmation rather than presenting it as verified.

Suppose your fictional presentation says registration ends “Friday.” Your notes might say “Verify date against the current event notice before delivery.” Once you have verified the fictional event schedule, write the full day and date to avoid ambiguity. For real-world material, confirm facts from the responsible official source before presenting them. The file itself should not become a repository of invented statistics or unchecked quotations.

### Give yourself timing and interaction cues

A [[timing-cue|timing cue]] is a planning estimate such as “30–45 seconds” or “pause for two questions.” It is not a guarantee that the talk will take exactly that long. Some audiences need more explanation or have more questions. The cue reminds you of your plan; rehearsal tells you whether the plan is realistic.

An [[interaction-prompt|interaction prompt]] may read, “Ask for one volunteer to explain the difference between a browser and a website.” Include an alternative if participation is optional: “If no one responds, provide the example yourself.” Do not assume that participants can read small text, raise a hand, speak aloud, or use the same interface. You can offer a verbal, written, or quiet response method depending on the environment.

### Transitions are part of the notes

A [[transition|transition]] links the current point to what comes next. A note that says “We have identified the address bar; next we will use it to recognize a website's address” helps the talk feel continuous. Without transitions, a presentation can sound like a disconnected list of slides, even when the order is logically correct.

Useful transitions may preview a question (“What happens when the browser does not load?”), contrast two ideas (“A password proves one kind of access, but it does not prove that a site is trustworthy”), or summarize a finding (“Now that the process is clear, let's practice”). Avoid repeating a stock phrase on every slide. The transition should express a genuine connection.

### Worked example: notes for a “What to bring” slide

**Visible slide:** “What to bring” followed by “Curiosity,” “Questions,” and “A personal device if available.”

**Unhelpful notes:** “Bring curiosity. Bring questions. Bring a device.” These simply repeat the slide and offer little speaking support.

**Useful notes:** “Start by saying that no prior computer expertise is expected. Explain that learners may bring a laptop, tablet, or phone, but they should confirm whether the workshop provides devices before arriving. Remind participants not to share passwords during partner practice. Ask whether someone needs an accessible way to participate. Transition: ‘Let's review the skills we will practice.’”

A final check is simple: **If you hid the notes, would the audience still see the essential information? If you hid the slide, would the notes still tell you what to explain?** Both layers should make sense for their separate tasks.

## 3. How do you connect notes to slide flow and evidence?

A presentation is a sequence, and speaker notes should move with that sequence. If a deck introduces a problem, explains possible actions, demonstrates a process, and concludes with next steps, the notes should support the same progression. A set of notes can serve as an internal [[run-of-show|run of show]]: a simple operational guide identifying what happens at each stage of a meeting or lesson.

### Match each note to the correct slide

Write notes after a preliminary [[storyboard|storyboard]] or outline is in place. You do not need to finish every design choice first. Start with an ordered list of slide titles and a one-sentence purpose for each. Then attach a note to the intended slide. When reorganizing slides, recheck the note text. In many editors notes remain associated with their slide during ordinary slide reordering, but references to slide numbers, “the previous chart,” or “the next example” may become inaccurate after a structural change.

| Slide | Audience-visible content | Speaker's job | Possible note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Welcome | Event title, time, room | Orient the audience | “Welcome; describe goal in one sentence” |
| 2. Today's plan | Three stages | Explain the route through the session | “Preview; reassure beginners that practice is guided” |
| 3. Browser basics | Simple labeled diagram | Explain and demonstrate | “Point to address bar; avoid real sign-in details” |
| 4. Safe choices | Three rules | Connect rules with examples | “Use fictional suspicious-message example” |
| 5. Next steps | Practice activity and assistance route | Summarize and invite questions | “State one action learners can try; thank participants” |

### Use a predictable note pattern across the deck

A consistent note structure makes rehearsal easier. You might use **Say**, **Show**, **Ask**, and **Next** as labels. Those labels are not software commands; they are a personal organization technique. Use whichever short format you can read quickly. For instance:

**Say:** “A browser displays webpages.” **Show:** “Point to the example address bar.” **Ask:** “Which part tells us where we are?” **Next:** “Explain what a link does.”

A more formal presentation may benefit from a **Claim**, **Evidence**, **Explanation**, and **Transition** structure. That pattern makes it easier to distinguish the finding you are reporting from the evidence that supports it. A teaching demonstration might use **Objective**, **Example**, **Check**, and **Next action**. The pattern should reflect the task, not an invented obligation to fill every field.

### Evidence and attribution belong in the right places

Speaker notes can store a [[source-check|source-check]] reminder, a full title of a report, an explanation of how a figure was calculated, or a note that the number must be updated before use. They are useful during preparation because they reduce the risk of misquoting a source. **But speaker notes are not automatically a substitute for audience-facing attribution.** If an image, chart, quotation, or factual claim requires a visible source credit or an accessible source list, include it where the audience or recipient can obtain it.

Suppose a slide contains a chart comparing attendance over three weeks. The speaker notes might record, “Explain that these are fictional practice figures; do not characterize them as real attendance.” The slide itself should also clearly say “Illustrative data” if a viewer might otherwise assume the values are factual. Notes can remind you to state the limitation aloud, but someone viewing the deck independently also needs sufficient context.

### Keep live demonstrations manageable

A [[demonstration|demonstration]] can disrupt timing if the presenter must find a file, change an application window, or solve a login problem. Notes can provide a simple list of prepared actions and a [[fallback-plan|fallback plan]]. For example: “Open the training browser. Point to the address bar. Use only a fictional example. If the network is unavailable, describe the labeled screenshot rather than entering a real account.”

Do not store real passwords, recovery codes, private links, or sensitive customer data in the note to make a demonstration easier. The better preparation is to use a test environment that contains no secrets. If a demonstration is likely to fail, include an audience-safe alternative on a prepared slide or printed instruction page.

### Use pacing as a guide, not a stopwatch

Notes can give a rough time budget by section: 45 seconds for a welcome, two minutes for a concept, three minutes for a demonstration, and one minute for questions. The value is to recognize the *relative* time available, not to force a beginner to deliver every word on an exact second. If a talk is five minutes long, giving four minutes to the opening leaves little room for the main teaching point.

Before a rehearsal, estimate how many speaking segments and activities are planned. Time yourself once at a natural pace. Revise the plan by reducing unnecessary explanation, allowing real pauses, or moving secondary material into a handout. Do not solve timing pressure by speaking too quickly or skipping the information the audience must understand.

### Notes and accessibility

Accessibility responsibilities extend beyond the screen. If you show a photograph that carries instructional meaning, your notes may remind you to describe its relevant content aloud. If you ask viewers to “look at the red box,” consider replacing that phrase with a location or label that does not require color perception. If a chart is complex, prepare a short summary of the meaningful trend. These are useful **speaking cues** but do not replace [[alternative-text|alternative text]], captions, accessible document structure, or other audience-facing accommodations when those are needed.

For a mixed audience, write the cue “Describe the diagram before discussing it” rather than assuming everyone sees the same details. If participants need instructions, provide them in formats they can use independently. If a question requires reading small projected text, read or paraphrase the relevant information aloud before inviting answers.

### Worked example: recover after moving a slide

You prepared a five-slide deck with a browser example on Slide 3 and a safety discussion on Slide 4. After editing, you move the safety slide before the browser example. The notes on the slides may follow their associated slides, but “As we just saw in the browser demonstration” is now incorrect. Review all forward and backward references. Change that line to “Before the demonstration, here are three safety rules,” and adjust the next slide's introduction. The repair is not merely renumbering: it restores the logic of the audience's experience.

## 4. How do you add and edit notes in presentation software?

The exact command names differ, but the core operation is similar in many editors: **select a slide, locate its notes area, enter speaking guidance, and verify the result by selecting another slide and returning**. A speaker note is not normally entered by adding an ordinary text box to the slide canvas. If a note appears on the visible slide itself, you probably typed into the wrong place.

Before making changes to an important presentation, preserve an [[editable-copy|editable copy]]. If the application is shared with others, check that you are working in the intended file and that edits will be visible to collaborators. The procedures below describe common desktop workflows rather than guarantees for every release or mobile device.

### Microsoft PowerPoint on Windows 11

PowerPoint's ordinary editing view is commonly called [[normal-view|Normal view]]. On a desktop installation, the Notes area is typically located below the slide. Depending on the version and window arrangement, it may be collapsed, and the **Notes** control may be available on the status bar or through the **View** tab.

1. Open a fictional practice presentation in PowerPoint and verify its title.
2. Select the first slide thumbnail in the left pane.
3. If the notes area is hidden, use the **Notes** control or a corresponding option in **View** to display it.
4. Click in the notes area below the slide, often labeled **Click to add notes**.
5. Type a short cue, such as “Welcome participants; explain the purpose of the workshop.”
6. Select the next slide and add a different note that belongs specifically to that slide.
7. Return to Slide 1 to verify the original note remains attached to it.
8. Save the file, then close and reopen your practice copy if you can do so safely.

If the text area is too small, expand the notes pane by dragging its upper boundary when the pointer changes appropriately. Long notes may scroll. PowerPoint also offers **Notes Page** through its view controls for reviewing notes with a larger page-style layout. Do not confuse Notes Page with an ordinary slide; it is a separate way to inspect or format a notes page.

For a desktop presentation with appropriate display configuration, **Slide Show → Use Presenter View** can permit the speaker to view notes, the current slide, and upcoming content while the audience sees the intended slide output. The display must be tested in its actual physical or virtual arrangement; merely checking the box does not guarantee privacy in a meeting or screen-sharing service.

**Important version distinction:** Microsoft's published help describes support for authoring notes in PowerPoint for the web but notes that viewing them while presenting requires the desktop application in the documented workflow. Do not promise that the web edition has the same live notes capabilities as desktop PowerPoint. If you use a browser edition, verify the specific features available to your account and prepare a safe alternative.

### Google Slides in a browser

Google Slides commonly provides a speaker notes area below the slide in editing mode. In some interface layouts it may be collapsed or hidden. The location and menu labels can change, so use the application's current help instructions when the screen differs from this description.

1. Open a fictional practice deck in Google Slides.
2. Select the slide that needs a speaking cue.
3. Locate the area marked **Click to add speaker notes** below the slide, expanding the notes section if necessary.
4. Enter two or three short prompts related to that slide.
5. Change to another slide, add separate notes, and return to confirm both sets remain distinct.
6. Observe the application's save status and, if appropriate, reopen the file to verify the notes persisted.

On a computer, Google's presentation help describes opening the menu next to **Slideshow**, choosing **Presenter view**, and then selecting **Speaker notes**. This typically involves a separate presenter interface or window. **A separate window is not automatically a private window.** You must know which display, window, tab, or desktop is being sent to the audience. Do not assume the notes are hidden merely because they are labeled “speaker notes.”

Google Slides is often cloud based. Changes may save automatically, but syncing and access can depend on connectivity, account permissions, and the application's status. Observe what the interface actually reports rather than assuming every edit is immediately available to every viewer. If the deck is shared for collaborative editing, permitted collaborators may be able to inspect its notes.

### LibreOffice Impress

LibreOffice Impress uses a distinct [[notes-view|Notes view]] rather than relying solely on a notes pane beneath the normal editing canvas. Its documentation identifies **View → Notes** as a standard way to reach the notes area. Interface layout and menus can differ by version.

1. Open a practice Impress presentation and select the slide you want to annotate.
2. Choose **View → Notes** or the **Notes** view control when available.
3. In the notes page, select the box inviting you to add notes beneath the slide image.
4. Enter a short explanation, delivery reminder, or transition.
5. Select a different slide and give it its own note.
6. Switch back to **Normal** view and check that the visible slide has not been filled with your speaking prompts.
7. Save a practice file in an appropriate editable format, then reopen it for verification.

Impress can display notes through its [[presenter-console|Presenter Console]] in supported multiple-display setups. LibreOffice documents the Presenter Console as a place to see notes and other presentation controls on the speaker's screen while slides appear on the audience display. The feature's behavior depends on the operating system and available display configuration. Verify the actual screen before addressing an audience.

### Compare the three applications

| Task | PowerPoint desktop | Google Slides | LibreOffice Impress |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Add notes | Notes pane under slide in Normal view | Speaker notes area under slide | Notes view and notes text box |
| Read during preparation | Notes pane or Notes Page | Editor notes area | Notes view |
| Show while presenting | Presenter View in supported setup | Presenter view with speaker notes in supported browser setup | Presenter Console in supported setup |
| Depend on a second display? | Useful for private live presenter view | Display/window behavior must be checked | Presenter Console generally expects multiple displays |
| Store in presentation file | Yes, subject to format and editing behavior | Generally part of cloud presentation | Yes, subject to format and export behavior |
| Share risk | Notes can accompany editable files | Collaborators may inspect notes | Notes may be present in editable files |

The table is a navigation guide, not a promise that every feature exists on every subscription, browser, phone, or operating-system release. If a control is missing, check whether you are in editing mode, whether a pane is collapsed, whether the file is read-only, and whether the platform supports that feature.

### Working on a phone or tablet

Mobile presentation apps may allow viewing or editing some notes, but the available controls and presentation modes can differ greatly. A mobile app may place notes behind a menu, provide limited formatting, or handle presenter screens differently from a desktop app. Avoid copying a Windows procedure exactly onto a phone. Use the platform-specific help and rehearse the intended device. If you cannot access the notes reliably, a printed or separately accessible cue sheet may be safer than assuming the mobile interface will behave like the desktop.

### Notes can move with a slide, but their wording can become stale

After adding notes, try a small controlled edit in your practice deck: duplicate a slide, move it, and then inspect the notes. Determine what your chosen application actually did. Even when the notes remain attached to their slide, the instruction “Tell the audience to look at Slide 4 next” can become wrong if you change the order. Review references to positions, surrounding material, or future demonstrations after rearranging the deck. When copying slides between applications or file formats, inspect the notes again; conversions may change or omit features.

### Worked example: accidentally typing notes on the slide

You select Slide 2 and type “Remember to ask about prior experience” into a text box in the slide canvas. The phrase now appears in the audience-facing slide. **Undo or remove the unintended slide text**, locate the actual notes area for Slide 2, and reenter the cue there. Preview the slide in its audience-facing mode. The purpose of the check is not to hide information that the audience needs; it is to place private speaking instructions in the appropriate layer.

## 5. How do you view and use notes while presenting?

Preparing notes in an editor and seeing them during a live presentation are two separate tasks. A note may exist in the file but never appear while the slide show is running. A [[presenter-display|presenter display]] is an interface intended for the speaker; the [[audience-display|audience display]] is what viewers are supposed to see. Correct configuration and testing matter more than the software's naming convention.

### The two-display arrangement

A common setup uses a laptop screen for the speaker and a projector or secondary monitor for the audience. The laptop may show notes, a timer, a preview of the next slide, and navigation controls. The projector shows the current slide. Some operating systems allow [[extended-display|extended desktop]] mode, in which each screen can show different content. In [[mirrored-display|mirrored or duplicate display]] mode, the same screen content may appear on both displays. If you try to use presenter notes while screens are mirrored, the projected result may not match your intention.

| Display setup | Benefit | Risk or limitation | Safer preparation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Laptop plus projector, extended display | Presenter controls can occupy one screen while slides occupy another | Wrong display assigned; windows may swap | Test the actual projector and select the intended outputs |
| Two monitors for a remote meeting | Speaker can keep references on a separate screen | Sharing the wrong monitor can reveal notes | Share only the intended slide output, then verify from a participant view |
| Single monitor | Simple equipment | No guaranteed separate private presenter screen | Use a compact printed cue sheet or a verified supported arrangement |
| Mirrored screens | Both displays receive identical content | Private notes may be mirrored too | Avoid assuming presenter view is private in mirror mode |
| Phone or tablet presentation | Portable setup | Controls and notes access vary by device/app | Test the exact device and have an offline fallback |

The exact results can vary, particularly during a video call or when windows are moved after a slideshow begins. **Never test privacy for the first time in front of an audience.** If necessary, perform a rehearsal with another person viewing the audience display or meeting feed.

### Screen sharing introduces another layer

In a [[screen-sharing|screen-sharing]] meeting, the audience sees whatever content the meeting software sends. Sharing an entire desktop may transmit notification banners, other windows, notes, or documents that you did not intend to show. Sharing a specific window can reduce exposure but does not eliminate it if the window changes, a system dialog appears, or the presentation tool uses a second window that you select by mistake. Some meeting tools behave differently during full-screen mode.

Use a simple preflight procedure:

1. Close unrelated windows and turn off avoidable pop-up notifications where appropriate.
2. Open a presentation that contains no sensitive practice content.
3. Start presenter mode using your chosen slide software.
4. Choose the intended screen or window in the meeting software.
5. Ask a trusted test participant to state exactly what they can see, including any notes or private windows.
6. Change slides and repeat the check, because the output may change when a new display mode activates.
7. Stop sharing, then confirm that the meeting tool reports sharing has ended.

If the setup does not isolate notes reliably, switch to ordinary slide presentation and use a safe paper cue sheet. Do not continue under the assumption that the audience probably cannot see the notes.

### How to use cues without becoming dependent on them

A useful rhythm is **glance → explain → reconnect**. Glance at a short cue, turn attention back to the audience or camera, explain one idea, and then consult the next cue when needed. You can speak from notes without reciting them word for word. Pauses are normal. A person who cannot look directly at the audience for disability, cultural, or situational reasons can still communicate effectively; the objective is clarity and engagement rather than enforced eye contact.

Do not write a cue such as “Talk for exactly 43 seconds” and then try to fit the entire explanation into that interval regardless of audience comprehension. Time estimates are flexible planning aids. If a question is essential, allow enough time to answer it. If the presentation has a hard time limit, reduce optional material during rehearsal rather than rushing the core explanation.

### Rehearsing is different from reading silently

A [[rehearsal|rehearsal]] is a practice run of what you will actually say or demonstrate. Read notes aloud at a realistic speed. Turn slides when you intend to turn them. If the slide has a diagram, practice describing the diagram so the spoken words match what the audience sees. When a demonstration requires switching windows, rehearse that switch with the same display setup you plan to use.

Use a [[rehearsal-log|rehearsal log]] to make improvements. It may be as simple as “Slide 2 took twice as long as expected” or “Forgot to explain why the example is fictional.” Then change the notes deliberately. If a sentence is difficult to pronounce, revise it or add a pronunciation cue. If the notes are too long to scan quickly, divide them into shorter prompts. If the slide itself lacks essential information, fix the slide too; notes alone should not compensate for a confusing deck.

### The paper backup

A paper [[cue-sheet|cue sheet]] is a short reference containing slide numbers or titles and brief reminders. It works without internet access, a second screen, or a particular presentation application. It also creates physical privacy responsibilities: keep the page out of the audience's camera view, do not leave it unattended if it contains restricted information, and dispose of it according to the appropriate rules.

A useful paper layout can be very simple:

| Slide title | Speaking cue | Transition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Welcome | State goal; invite participation | “First, today's plan” |
| Today's plan | Explain three stages | “Let's open a browser” |
| Browser basics | Identify address bar | “Next, safe choices” |
| Safe choices | Use one fictional example | “Now, practice” |
| Next steps | Summarize and invite questions | Close and thank the audience |

There is no requirement to use software-generated printed notes if a handwritten or accessible text alternative suits the task. If notes are essential to a presenter, select a format they can navigate comfortably, including large print, a screen reader, or another preferred aid. Accessibility must include the speaker as well as the audience.

### Worked example: recovering from a presenter-screen problem

You begin a practice slideshow and discover that the projector shows the same windows as your laptop, including your notes. Stop the test immediately. Change the display configuration or stop using the separate notes window. Run a second test where a partner confirms that only the audience slide is visible. If the correct arrangement cannot be verified, continue with normal slides and a printed cue sheet. **The successful response is to adapt the workflow, not to pretend the screen is private.**

## 6. How do you review notes for accuracy, privacy, and accessibility?

Speaker notes can feel informal because the audience is not supposed to see them during a typical live slideshow. That impression can lead to careless drafting. In reality, notes may become part of an editable presentation, copied file, printed notes page, or record of collaboration. A [[privacy-review|privacy review]] and a [[content-review|content review]] should therefore treat notes as document content with potential recipients, not as invisible thoughts.

### Do not confuse hidden during delivery with secret forever

A note can be absent from the projected slide and still be available to someone who opens the editable deck. It can also appear when a file is printed with notes, when a collaborator enters the editing view, or when the wrong window is shared. Removing notes from the screen is not the same as removing them from the file. A [[pdf-export|PDF export]] may contain slides only in one workflow and notes pages in another; options and results vary. **Inspect the actual exported or printed output before distributing it.**

If a presentation contains something that should never be disclosed, do not depend on an obscure interface setting to hide it. Remove the sensitive item from the distributed copy and verify the result. Consider whether a separate confidential preparation document is appropriate under your organization's rules; that separate document still needs proper handling.

### A simple three-part review

**Accuracy:** Are names, dates, times, statistics, and descriptions correct for the intended event? Are references to “previous slide” and “next section” still true? Are illustrations clearly identified as fictional? Does the note encourage a claim that the visible slide does not justify?

**Privacy:** Do notes contain real passwords, authentication codes, private attendee details, unreleased business information, medical information, or sensitive internal discussion? Have you left editing comments that could be misunderstood or exposed? Are you sure you are not printing or exporting notes unintentionally?

**Accessibility:** Can you read and navigate your notes? Does the spoken explanation describe important visuals without relying only on color or location? Have you planned suitable pauses, captions where necessary, and an accessible audience document for information people must retain? Are any instructions dependent on a single device or participation method?

### Review with a checklist

| Check | What to verify | Example correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slide-note match | The note refers to the selected slide | Update “next chart” after moving a slide |
| Fact check | Names, amounts, and dates are confirmed | Replace a placeholder date with the verified one |
| Audience clarity | Spoken explanation matches visible message | Explain the purpose before the procedure |
| Attribution | Sources are available where recipients need them | Add appropriate credit to slide or reference document |
| Privacy | Notes contain no secrets or restricted details | Remove the confidential draft comment |
| Note length | Cues can be scanned during speaking | Split one long paragraph into brief prompts |
| Display behavior | Only intended content appears to viewers | Fix monitor or meeting-sharing selection |
| Export behavior | Distributed file contains only intended content | Inspect the output and remove notes if required |
| Accessibility | Essential visual and spoken information is usable | Add descriptive labels, captions, or written alternatives |
| Recovery | An alternate speaking aid is available | Print or prepare a safe cue sheet |

### Be careful when using collaborative presentation files

A cloud document can allow other people to view or edit the same deck. A [[collaborator|collaborator]] with permitted access to the editing interface may see notes you thought were only for you. Different permission levels can change what other people can see and do, but you should not infer privacy from the title “speaker notes.” Confirm the sharing settings and the actual file content. If a team requires separate private talking points, agree on a separate controlled process rather than putting secret instructions in a shared presentation.

When handing off a presentation to another speaker, notes may be valuable because they explain intention, evidence, and transitions. That makes it even more important that they are professional, accurate, and free of casual remarks about audience members. Write notes as if they may someday be reviewed as part of the presentation's working materials.

### Speaker notes do not replace an accessible presentation

A note saying “Describe the chart” is useful only if the presenter follows it. Someone viewing a deck without a presenter still needs meaningful content. Check headings, reading order, alternative text where needed, sufficient contrast, logical chart labels, and any accessible accompanying materials. Where a recording contains speech, provide appropriate captions or another supported access method. For real obligations in a workplace or school, follow the organization's accessibility requirements rather than relying on this introductory lesson as a compliance checklist.

### A realistic review sequence

Begin with your original practice file. Read every visible slide in order. Then read the corresponding notes and check whether the planned remarks make sense. Next, rehearse aloud and observe where you hesitate. Finally, preview the audience output and any proposed shared copy. Fix issues in that order: **content first, speaking cues second, delivery configuration third, distribution verification fourth**. Revising only the notes will not repair a slide with a wrong date, and exporting correctly will not repair a presentation whose meaning is unclear.

### Worked example: information that should be removed

A fictional workshop deck contains the note “Log in to the demonstration service using the staff password written below” followed by a sample-looking credential. Even if it is a fake value, it teaches a poor habit and may be mistaken for an actual secret. Replace it with “Use the preconfigured training account; do not display credentials. If sign-in fails, explain the prepared screenshot.” If you discover a real credential in an existing file, treat it as a security issue under the relevant organization's procedures. Do not simply assume it is safe because the note will not appear on the projector.

## 7. Guided practice: build and rehearse speaker notes

These activities use only invented names, circumstances, and schedules. You may answer on paper, in a text document, or in an available presentation application. No real account, password, external service, or live audience is required. Each activity has an expandable model answer. The answers are examples, not the only acceptable wording. If you use a slide editor, save a practice copy with an identifiable filename and verify the notes remain associated with their intended slides.

### Activity 1: Decide what belongs on the slide and what belongs in notes

You are preparing Slide 1 for a fictional **Neighborhood Learning Day**. The following draft contains six items:

1. The event title, **Neighborhood Learning Day**.
2. The fictional event time, **Saturday at 10:00 a.m.**
3. A reminder to introduce yourself and explain why the day matters.
4. The location, **Training Room B**.
5. A reminder to speak slowly and pause before starting the agenda.
6. A reminder to thank the volunteer team in your closing remarks.

Create two lists: **audience-facing slide** and **speaker notes**. Explain why each item belongs where you placed it. Then rewrite the opening note as two or three short speaking cues. Keep every detail fictional and do not add a personal telephone number or any other private contact information.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answer
**On the visible slide:** “Neighborhood Learning Day,” “Saturday, 10:00 a.m.,” and “Training Room B.” These are useful facts for the audience to locate and understand the event.

**In the notes:** “Introduce yourself and the reason for the event. Speak at a comfortable pace; pause after the welcome. Remember to thank volunteers during the final slide.” These are reminders about how to deliver the talk, not material the audience needs to read at the beginning.

The volunteer reminder might be better moved to the closing slide's notes. Speaker notes are most useful when attached to the moment at which the action should occur.
:::

### Activity 2: Change a paragraph into useful speaking cues

A draft notes paragraph says:

> I will tell everyone about the three stages of our workshop, and I should say that we will first learn what a browser is, then we will explore a few safe navigation habits, and after that everyone will practice using fictional examples, and I need to ask whether people have questions and also remember not to show any real passwords.

Rewrite the paragraph so the speaker can scan it quickly without losing its essential information. Your revised version should contain a main point, a delivery action, an interaction prompt, and a transition. Then read both versions aloud and note which is easier to use. You may use short sentences or a flat list; you do not need specialized shorthand.

:::answer Show Activity 2 model answer
**Main point:** Three stages: browser basics → safe navigation → fictional practice.

**Say:** “We'll build the skills in that order, one step at a time.”

**Do:** Point to the three stages as they appear on the slide. Use no real passwords or private accounts in the demonstration.

**Ask:** “Is there a question about today's plan?” Allow time for a response.

**Next:** “Let's begin by identifying the parts of a browser.”

The revised notes preserve the original intention but make the speaking actions easier to identify. If the audience needs an exact schedule, it should also be available to them rather than hidden only in notes.
:::

### Activity 3: Match notes to a five-slide sequence

You have a fictional five-slide workshop deck with these titles in order: **1. Welcome; 2. Today's Plan; 3. Browser Basics; 4. Safe Navigation; 5. Practice and Questions.** A collection of unsorted notes is listed below:

- **A:** Ask learners to identify the address bar in a training image, then transition to safe navigation.
- **B:** Explain that three stages will follow, and reassure beginners that no previous expertise is expected.
- **C:** Thank the audience, invite questions, and explain how they may continue practicing.
- **D:** Welcome participants, explain the session purpose, and introduce yourself.
- **E:** Give a fictional link example; remind the group to check the actual address before entering information.

Match each note to its correct slide. Then imagine moving **Safe Navigation** before **Browser Basics**. Identify one note or transition you would revise to keep the talk coherent.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model answer
The initial matching is **1 → D, 2 → B, 3 → A, 4 → E, 5 → C**.

After moving the safety slide before the browser example, a line such as “As you saw in the previous browser example” would no longer make sense. The safety slide could instead begin, “Before our demonstration, here are some safe habits to remember.” The following browser slide could begin, “Now let's use a fictional webpage to apply those habits.”

The exercise shows why speaker notes need a flow review after reordering slides. A note may remain attached to a slide while its references to neighboring material become outdated.
:::

### Activity 4: Add and verify notes in an editor

If a presentation application is available, create a small **three-slide fictional deck**: **Welcome**, **Plan**, and **Next Steps**. Use no photographs, real names, or external accounts. For each slide, add one visible title and a short audience-facing statement. Then add these notes in the application's real notes area:

1. Welcome: “Introduce the purpose and pause.”
2. Plan: “Explain the sequence; ask one check-in question.”
3. Next Steps: “Summarize the main point and invite final questions.”

Return to Slide 1 and confirm its note did not appear as ordinary slide text. Select Slide 2 and confirm its notes are different. Save the presentation, reopen it if possible, and inspect all three notes. If you cannot use a slide editor, draw three rectangles on paper, write visible slide content inside each, and write the corresponding notes on a separate sheet labeled by slide title.

Record the name of the application or paper method used, where you found the notes feature, and one thing you verified. Do not assume that the presence of a notes pane means presenter view will work in a live meeting.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answer
**PowerPoint example:** Use Normal view, select the slide, reveal the Notes pane under the canvas, and type the appropriate cue. Selecting Slide 2 shows its distinct text. Reopening the saved practice deck confirms that each slide retained its note.

**Google Slides example:** Enter each cue in the speaker notes area under the selected slide. Switching among slides shows which note belongs to which slide; the saved state must be checked.

**LibreOffice Impress example:** Use View → Notes, type into the notes box for each selected slide, then return to Normal view to confirm that the audience-visible slide remains concise.

**Paper alternative:** Draw three slides and keep a separate set of three numbered speaking prompts. The key distinction is that the audience-facing words and speaker-only reminders are separate and correctly paired.
:::

### Activity 5: Audit a screen-sharing and privacy plan

Read this fictional situation: A student has a laptop, one external monitor, and a remote-meeting application. They intend to share the entire laptop screen while reading speaker notes in a separate window. Their editable deck contains a private staff discussion, a real email address, and a reminder to show a password during a demonstration. They say, “The audience will only see the slideshow because my notes are called speaker notes.”

Identify at least five risks or unverified assumptions. Provide a safer plan that addresses the file content, the display arrangement, and the meeting software. Include an alternative if it is impossible to verify the audience's view.

:::answer Show Activity 5 model answer
The risks include: notes may be included in the editable file; collaborators or recipients may inspect them; an entire-screen share may expose notes or other windows; the laptop and monitor may be mirrored; the wrong display may be shared; the private staff discussion and email address should not be in a widely shared practice file; a password should never be displayed in a teaching demonstration; and a live presenter view may not work as expected in the chosen platform.

**Safer plan:** Remove unnecessary personal or restricted information from the distributed deck. Replace the password step with a training example that reveals no credentials. Test the physical monitor arrangement, start a rehearsal meeting, share only the intended slide output, and have a trusted test participant confirm exactly what appears when slides change. Close unrelated windows and minimize notifications. If privacy cannot be verified, use ordinary slides with a paper cue sheet or an accessible separate speaking aid instead of exposing the notes window.

The safer plan relies on **testing**, not on assumptions about labels or window locations.
:::

### Activity 6: Capstone project — prepare notes for a five-slide workshop

Prepare a fictional five-slide presentation entitled **“Everyday Computer Confidence.”** You may use a presentation editor or a paper storyboard. Your job is not to create an elaborate design. Your job is to demonstrate that you can keep audience information and speaking guidance in appropriate places.

**Required slide plan:**

1. **Welcome:** Name the fictional event and state its purpose.
2. **The Learning Plan:** Show three parts of the session.
3. **Using a Browser:** Identify an ordinary browser element and explain what it does.
4. **Practicing Safe Habits:** Give three safe, nontechnical actions.
5. **Next Steps and Questions:** Summarize the lesson and offer a fictional practice route.

For each slide, prepare **two to five short note cues**. At least once in the deck, include each of the following: a spoken opening, a brief explanation that does not simply repeat the slide, one audience check-in question, one reminder to describe a meaningful visual, one safe demonstration or fallback, one transition, and a closing statement. Use a realistic time estimate for the whole presentation, such as a **five- to seven-minute practice talk**, and time a rehearsal if you are able.

After rehearsing, perform the following review:

- **Slide separation:** Verify that notes are not accidentally visible on an audience slide.
- **Sequence:** Read the slides and notes in order and repair transitions that reference the wrong slide.
- **Accuracy:** Ensure each detail is fictional or independently verified before real-world use.
- **Accessibility:** Explain meaningful visuals verbally and make essential content available on the slide or in an audience resource.
- **Privacy:** Remove private data, credentials, and comments that should not be distributed.
- **Delivery:** Test presenter view if you have the correct equipment; otherwise prepare a reliable cue sheet.
- **Sharing:** If you create a distributable practice copy, inspect what it actually contains before sending it anywhere.

**Self-assessment rubric:** Use the table to decide which portion of your work deserves further revision. A “Needs work” finding is an opportunity to improve, not a failure.

| Criterion | Ready | Needs work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience sees the main message | Slide text is concise and understandable | Main point is hidden or unclear |
| Notes support the speaker | Notes provide explanation, action, or transition | Notes only repeat slide text |
| Notes match the sequence | Each cue belongs to the correct slide | References point to outdated positions |
| Rehearsal supports pacing | Talk allows time for essential explanations | Speaker rushes or omits main ideas |
| Accessibility is considered | Key visuals and actions have usable explanations | Instructions depend only on color, tiny text, or unexplained images |
| Privacy and distribution are checked | No secrets or inappropriate notes remain | File includes material that should not be shared |
| Backup is practical | Speaker has tested the intended view or an alternative | Entire plan assumes an untested notes window |

:::answer Show Activity 6 model answer
**Slide 1 — Welcome**

Visible: “Everyday Computer Confidence” and “Learn a few useful computer habits.” Notes: “Welcome everyone. Explain that this is a beginner session. Speak at an unhurried pace. Transition to today's plan.”

**Slide 2 — The Learning Plan**

Visible: “Recognize → Practice → Review.” Notes: “Explain the three-stage sequence. Ask whether participants have used a web browser before; do not require a public response. Reassure participants that fictional examples will be used. Next: browser basics.”

**Slide 3 — Using a Browser**

Visible: “Address bar: where a webpage address appears,” with a clearly labeled practice image if software is available. Notes: “Describe the main parts of the practice image aloud. Point out the address bar without relying on color alone. Use a fictional web address; do not sign in to a real account. If the image does not load, explain the label verbally.”

**Slide 4 — Practicing Safe Habits**

Visible: “Check the address; pause before sharing information; ask for help when uncertain.” Notes: “Give a fictional message example and ask which safe habit applies. Explain that a familiar logo does not prove a page is legitimate. Transition to what learners can practice independently.”

**Slide 5 — Next Steps and Questions**

Visible: “Practice with the fictional examples. Ask questions. Review the lesson.” Notes: “Summarize the three habits. Allow time for questions. Do not direct anyone to enter a real password as practice. Thank the audience and close.”

**Review:** The visible slides carry the main teaching information. The notes provide speaker actions, accessible explanations, and transitions. A useful rehearsal might reveal that Slide 3 needs additional time; the speaker can shorten the optional introduction rather than rushing the demonstration. The file contains no sensitive details. If presenter view is unavailable, a paper cue sheet with these five headings is a suitable fallback.

### Additional practice: choose the shortest useful note

Consider three drafts for a slide titled **Review and Questions**:

- Draft A: “Talk.”
- Draft B: “Summarize the three browser safety habits. Ask for questions. Give a fictional practice option. Thank the audience.”
- Draft C: Three full pages of background material, unrelated history, and instructions never mentioned in the presentation.

Draft B is generally the strongest prompt for a brief beginner presentation. Draft A is too vague to guide a speaker. Draft C may contain valuable material for a separate reference document but is difficult to scan during delivery. Note quality is determined by fitness for purpose, not by length.

## 8. Common misconceptions and troubleshooting

Beginners often encounter problems because they assume a note is invisible everywhere or that a feature available in one program must be available in another. Use the following corrections as a diagnostic guide before you make a major change to a file.

### Misconception: notes are automatically private

Notes may be hidden from the audience during a properly configured presentation, but people who open, edit, print, or receive the file may be able to see them. The exact exposure depends on the format, software, permissions, export choices, and display arrangement. **Write notes with realistic sharing risk in mind.**

### Misconception: the presenter must read every word

A speaking prompt is not a command to recite a script. Exact wording may be appropriate for a quotation, required statement, or accessibility need, but many presentations benefit from short cues and natural explanation. Rehearse the material until you understand it well enough to speak clearly.

### Misconception: speaker notes are part of the slide's design

Notes ordinarily live outside the audience-visible slide. Typing a reminder into a regular slide text box does not make it a speaker note. The remedy is to use the correct notes area and inspect the slide show output.

### Misconception: adding notes automatically creates a transcript

Short notes record intentions rather than every word that will be spoken. A transcript or accessible written version may need additional material, especially if the presentation must be used without a live speaker. Do not advertise cue notes as a full record of speech when they are not one.

### Misconception: speaker view will always appear on the correct monitor

A second screen, presentation software, and screen-sharing tool can interact in unexpected ways. Mirroring, extended desktop settings, application windows, and video meeting choices all matter. Test the actual audience view instead of relying on a remembered setup.

### Troubleshooting quick reference

| Symptom | Possible cause | Practical next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No notes box appears | Notes pane collapsed or different view selected | Show Notes or switch to Notes view |
| Notes appear on the projected slide | Text entered in slide canvas or incorrect window shared | Remove misplaced slide text; test the output selection |
| Notes appear under the wrong topic | Wrong slide selected; references became stale | Check slide titles and edit the associated notes |
| Presenter notes are unreadably small | Presenter font or panel settings | Increase note text size in the supported view; use a cue sheet |
| Notes do not appear in slideshow | Presenter view unsupported or not enabled | Check application/version, then use a tested alternative |
| Notes show on the audience monitor | Monitors mirrored or wrong output chosen | Stop test and correct configuration before continuing |
| Notes are missing after file conversion | Format or import/export differences | Reopen the source file and verify which data survived |
| Notes printed unexpectedly | Notes Pages or similar print setting chosen | Inspect print preview and select intended format |
| Updated notes do not persist | Save, sync, or permission problem | Check status and reopen an editable practice copy |
| A remote audience sees private content | Wrong screen or window shared | Stop sharing, remove exposure, and verify a safe setup |
| Notes are too long to scan | Paragraphs contain multiple unrelated tasks | Rewrite as a few ordered cues and rehearse |
| A transition no longer makes sense | Slides moved or removed | Read the sequence aloud and revise the wording |

> **Troubleshooting sequence:** **Stop accidental exposure → identify the display or file problem → preserve the source → make one controlled correction → verify the audience output and saved notes again.** For a real privacy or security incident, follow the affected organization's procedures rather than treating a silent slide edit as sufficient.

### Lesson summary

Speaker notes are a structured speaking aid associated with individual slides. They let you prepare explanations, transitions, demonstrations, evidence reminders, and timing prompts while keeping the visible slides focused on audience needs. The most useful notes are clear enough to scan, accurate enough to trust, and flexible enough to support natural communication. PowerPoint, Google Slides, and LibreOffice Impress provide different interfaces for creating and presenting notes, so the actual application and display setup must be tested. **Notes may be hidden during a typical slideshow but are not guaranteed to be confidential in an editable file, printout, export, or shared screen.** Protect private information, rehearse with a backup, and check the intended audience output.
