## Lesson 8: Presenting Slides

A community center has invited you to explain a fictional Saturday Digital Skills Workshop. You have already prepared six clear slides: a welcome, the goal of the workshop, a list of materials, an activity schedule, an explanation of how to get help, and a closing message. The file looks correct in the editing window. Now a projector is connected and the audience is seated. How do you make the slides appear on the correct screen, move through them without losing your place, speak clearly, and recover if something unexpected happens?

This is the work of [[presentation-delivery|presentation delivery]]. Creating a slide deck prepares the material; presenting it is the process of guiding people through that material. The audience should see the information intended for them, not your desktop notifications, private speaker notes, or a confusing series of editing windows. Good delivery also requires attention to pacing, questions, accessibility, and the possibility that the technology will not behave as expected.

This lesson teaches the everyday process of showing slides to an audience. You will learn the difference between editing and [[slide-show-mode|slide-show mode]], how to begin at the first or current slide, how to move forward and backward, what [[presenter-view|Presenter View]] can and cannot protect, and how to deliver a presentation in a room or online. Microsoft PowerPoint on Windows 11 is the principal desktop example. Google Slides and LibreOffice Impress are included so you can apply the same ideas with different software. Interface labels and feature availability can vary by edition, browser, operating system, equipment, or organizational policy.

Lesson 7 introduced [[speaker-notes|speaker notes]]. In this lesson those notes become one optional tool among several; a successful talk is not defined by whether the speaker has a second screen or a special remote. **You can practice the entire delivery sequence on one computer, on paper, or with a trusted volunteer.** All activities use invented people, invented events, and nonconfidential examples. You do not need to create an online account, contact an audience, or record your voice to finish the lesson.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 110–150 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Presentation Skills Lessons 1–7 recommended · **Practice equipment:** Paper and pencil or a presentation application; an external screen and internet access are optional · **Safety:** Use fictional examples; verify which screen or window is being shared before showing content; keep passwords, private messages, participant records, and confidential notes out of the audience output.

> **Essential idea:** **Prepare the message, test the audience view, run the slides deliberately, communicate with people rather than the screen, and maintain a simple backup plan.** The slideshow is a visual aid; the presenter is responsible for making the information understandable.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain what it means to present slides and distinguish slide-show mode from normal editing and file sharing.
2. Describe the different roles of the speaker, audience, slide deck, presenter display, and audience display.
3. Prepare a practical pre-presentation checklist for content, timing, equipment, privacy, and accessibility.
4. Start a presentation from the beginning or from a selected slide using an available application.
5. Move forward and backward, recognize build effects, pause appropriately, and exit slide-show mode.
6. Identify speaker-oriented tools such as notes, timers, slide previews, laser pointers, and annotations.
7. Explain the difference between mirrored and extended displays and recognize screen-sharing exposure risks.
8. Apply cautious procedures to in-person, online, and hybrid presentations, including audio and video playback.
9. Deliver an understandable introduction, transitions, explanation of visuals, question period, and conclusion.
10. Respond calmly to common disruptions without disclosing private data or assuming technology must work perfectly.
11. Rehearse and evaluate a complete fictional six-slide presentation using a simple improvement checklist.

## 1. What does it mean to present slides?

A [[presentation|presentation]] is a planned communication activity intended to inform, explain, teach, propose, or persuade. A [[slide-deck|slide deck]] is a file or collection of slides that supports that activity. [[presentation-delivery|Presenting]] means using the slides in sequence while helping an audience understand the intended message. A person may deliver the same deck in a small classroom, a staff meeting, a library room, a community forum, or an online call. The deck may be the same, but the delivery conditions are different.

Suppose the workshop's third slide says **Bring a device if available**. Reading those words aloud adds little information. Instead, the presenter might explain that participants can bring a laptop, tablet, or phone, that loaner equipment is limited, and that nobody will be asked to reveal private account passwords. The slide identifies the topic; the speaker communicates its meaning. A [[presenter|presenter]] is responsible for the explanation, for checking the information, and for handling questions within the presentation's purpose.

### Editing, presenting, and sharing are different actions

The same file can be used in several ways. In [[edit-mode|edit mode]], you arrange slides, modify text, inspect notes, and save changes. In [[slide-show-mode|slide-show mode]], the application displays slides for delivery, often with fewer editing controls visible. In [[sharing|sharing]], you provide other people with access to a file, a copy, an exported document, or a live screen. **Starting a slideshow does not itself grant someone file access; sharing an editable deck does not necessarily start a slideshow for them.**

| Action | Typical purpose | What the presenter should check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Edit a slide | Correct wording, design, or order | Are changes saved in the intended file? |
| Run a slideshow | Show slides in a delivery sequence | Is the intended slide visible on the audience screen? |
| View speaker notes | Read prompts for what to say | Are the notes visible only where intended? |
| Project a screen | Make computer output visible in the room | Is the correct display selected and free of private content? |
| Share a window in a meeting | Send selected application output to remote participants | Does the shared window show the correct slideshow? |
| Share a presentation file | Let other people open or modify a copy | Are notes, comments, hidden slides, and permissions appropriate? |
| Export a PDF | Create a separate document for reading or distribution | Is the exported version readable and complete? |

A teacher can show slides without sending the editable file to anyone. A student can email a deck without presenting it live. A trainer can present remotely while the actual deck remains stored on a personal computer. These are separate operations, and each requires its own privacy check.

### The speaker's screen and the audience's screen

In some rooms, a laptop is connected to a projector. In online meetings, the participant sees a shared region inside a meeting application. When using a second monitor, the speaker may have a [[presenter-display|presenter display]] with notes, a timer, and upcoming-slide previews, while viewers see an [[audience-display|audience display]] containing only the intended slide. These are different roles; not every device or application separates them automatically.

| Role or object | Main function | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speaker | Explains, paces, listens, and responds | Talking only to the screen |
| Audience | Interprets information and asks questions | Assuming every attendee can see tiny text |
| Slide deck | Supplies visual structure and reference | Treating slides as the entire speech |
| Presenter display | Supplies speaker controls and private cues | Believing it can never be shared accidentally |
| Audience display | Shows intended material to viewers | Projecting the editor or desktop instead |
| Meeting share | Transmits a selected screen, window, or other approved source | Sharing a private window or wrong monitor |
| Backup | Supplies an alternative when the primary method fails | Depending on an untested online video or device |

### The normal delivery sequence

Most short talks can be organized into five stages: prepare, start, guide, conclude, and verify. The first stage happens before the audience arrives or the meeting begins. The second begins when the speaker displays the title slide. The third is the actual navigation and communication. The fourth tells people what to remember or do. The fifth closes the slideshow, checks what remained on screen, and records what needs improvement.

**Preparation** includes checking slide content, timing, equipment, privacy, legibility, and the first slide. **Starting** includes getting the correct audience output on screen and introducing the purpose. **Guiding** includes explaining points, moving slides intentionally, pausing, and observing audience understanding. **Concluding** includes summarizing the message, asking for appropriate questions, and giving next steps. **Verification** includes ending the show, stopping the share if applicable, saving approved changes, and protecting the file afterward.

### Live presentation versus self-running presentation

A [[live-presentation|live presentation]] has a speaker who decides when to explain or advance. A [[self-running-presentation|self-running presentation]] may advance on a schedule, often at an information kiosk or unattended display. A [[recorded-presentation|recorded presentation]] combines saved slides with recorded narration or video. These methods require different preparation. Self-running slides must give viewers sufficient time to read; recorded presentations may require captions, transcripts, and editing; live presentations require attention to questions and timing.

This lesson primarily concerns **live presentation**. It introduces automatic advancement only as an optional feature, not as the default for a classroom talk. A presenter answering an audience question may need to pause for longer than an automatic timer allows. For beginners, deliberate manual navigation usually provides more control.

### Worked example: the same slides in three situations

Maya has six slides about the fictional workshop. When speaking to five colleagues around a table, she may use her laptop screen and simply advance the deck. In a classroom, she may connect the laptop to a large display, stand where she can see both the audience and projected slide, and use a wireless remote. In an online meeting, she may share a tested slideshow window and describe any important visual details aloud. The key message is identical, but the way participants see and hear it changes.

The most useful planning question is not **Which button can make the slides run?** It is **How will each person receive the message, and can I verify that the right information is reaching them?**

## 2. How do you prepare for a presentation?

A [[rehearsal|rehearsal]] is a practice run performed before the real event. Rehearsal is more than clicking through slides silently: it tests whether the explanation makes sense, whether the sequence is realistic, and whether the equipment supports the plan. A [[preflight-check|preflight check]] is a short operational review of the conditions that could disrupt the presentation. Both protect the presenter's time and the audience's ability to follow the material.

A beginner can rehearse without a projector or a paid application. Draw six numbered rectangles on paper, write one main point inside each, and practice what you would say to move between them. When software is available, use the real slide-show mode and time the talk. Where possible, repeat the rehearsal on the equipment and in the room or online platform that will be used for the event.

### Begin with the audience and purpose

Before pressing any slideshow button, decide what listeners should understand by the end. A workshop orientation might have one measurable outcome: participants know when and where to attend and what equipment is optional. A budget briefing might aim to help staff understand the recommended action. A classroom explanation might introduce three steps in a computer skill. The purpose determines what deserves emphasis and how long the speaker should stay on each slide.

Ask three questions:

1. **Who will be listening?** Are they beginners, experienced colleagues, participants from different age groups, or people joining through a phone?
2. **What must they be able to do afterward?** Register, remember safety rules, understand a proposal, or compare options?
3. **What is the time limit?** Does it include questions, demonstrations, and late-arriving attendees?

Do not assume all audience members have the same screen size, reading speed, language background, hearing ability, or familiarity with the topic. A person attending through a small phone display may not be able to read a screenshot that looks acceptable on the speaker's large monitor. An audience member relying on captions needs the speech and important visuals to be clear enough to follow.

### Build a simple run of show

A [[run-of-show|run of show]] is a plain plan for the order and approximate time of the event. It can be a table on paper or a few lines in your notes. For a six-slide workshop introduction, the plan might look like this:

| Segment | Slide | Main point | Target time | Audience action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | 1. Welcome | State the purpose and expected outcome | 30 seconds | Listen |
| Why attend | 2. Workshop goal | Describe one skill learners will practice | 40 seconds | Consider a goal |
| Preparation | 3. What to bring | Clarify that a personal device is optional | 40 seconds | Ask for assistance if needed |
| Schedule | 4. Activity plan | Explain the three-step agenda | 60 seconds | Follow the sequence |
| Support | 5. Help available | Explain where questions will be answered | 40 seconds | Note the help point |
| Closing | 6. Next steps | Repeat the date and registration route | 30 seconds | Identify one next action |

The total planned speaking time is **4 minutes**; it is a teaching example, not a universal rule for how long a slide should remain visible. Some talks take longer to explain an accessible image or invite participation. Treat a time estimate as a planning aid rather than a command to rush. If the real event allows five minutes, the extra minute could be reserved for a question or technical transition.

### Conduct a content and visual check

A slideshow is not necessarily trustworthy simply because it opens successfully. Review the slide titles, order, claims, dates, numbers, external links, sources, and any audience instructions. Confirm that the intended closing slide actually follows the main material. Use the thumbnail view to check whether duplicate or outdated slides remain. If the file contains a hidden slide, determine whether it should remain hidden during delivery and whether it could become visible in another output or shared file.

Read text at the approximate size and distance at which the audience will encounter it. A 12-point label may be legible while editing but impossible to read from the back of a room. A photo may become blurry when projected. A video may have a quiet soundtrack. A dark theme may lose contrast on a weak projector. **Test the actual output where feasible rather than assuming the design looks the same on every display.**

### Check the environment and equipment

The equipment checklist changes by setting, but the underlying questions remain stable. Is the intended file accessible? Does the display show the right image? Is the speaker audible? Can the presentation be operated without exposing other content? Does the presenter have a way to finish even if a remote, external service, or video clip fails?

| Check | What to verify | Practical fallback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| File | Correct version and location | A separate approved local copy |
| Power | Laptop and display have adequate power | Connect to power or use a printed outline |
| Projector or monitor | Correct input, resolution, and orientation | Use one screen or explain without projection |
| Navigation | Forward, backward, exit, and pointer controls work | Use keyboard or on-screen controls |
| Audio | Sound works at appropriate volume | Summarize clip content verbally |
| Video or web content | Media plays with allowed permissions and connectivity | Still image plus a prepared explanation |
| Notes | Notes are available where intended | Printed cue sheet with nonconfidential material |
| Notifications | Private pop-ups and previews are controlled | Disable notifications or use a clean presentation session |
| Accessibility | Text, speech, captions, and visual descriptions are usable | Provide an accessible handout and spoken explanation |
| Time | Estimated segments fit the schedule | Shorten examples, not essential meaning |

### A practical preflight sequence

Use the following order for a room-based presentation. The steps are deliberately plain and can be adapted to an online meeting.

1. Open the intended final file and confirm its first and last slides.
2. Save pending changes and confirm that the file is stored where expected.
3. Close or minimize unrelated private content, subject to your device and institutional policies.
4. Connect the audience display and verify the intended screen configuration.
5. Open the slideshow and check one middle slide for readability.
6. Test one forward and one backward navigation action, then return to the opening slide.
7. If using audio, play a short approved sample at a comfortable level, then stop it.
8. If using a remote, test the remote and keep keyboard controls available.
9. Confirm that speaker notes, other windows, and notifications are not visible to viewers.
10. Exit and restart from the opening slide, ready for the live introduction.

### Decide what not to show

For a fictional community workshop, it is unnecessary to open personal bank statements, participant email lists, private chat messages, or the computer's password manager. Even if the presenter expects to share only a slideshow, another display or screen-selection mistake could reveal those items. A clean [[presentation-session|presentation session]] reduces the consequences of an accidental switch. Do not enter real credentials into a live demonstration. Use screenshots only when they are properly sourced, permitted, current, and free from private information.

### Rehearse the spoken transitions

A [[transition|transition]] is the connection between two ideas. It can be verbal: “We have reviewed what to bring; next we will look at the schedule.” It can be visual: one slide replaces another. A transition effect can make the change noticeable, but a spoken connection explains why the change matters. Practice telling the audience what has just been established and what is coming next.

Rehearsal should also include deliberate pauses. After a complicated visual, stop long enough to let people inspect it. Before inviting questions, explain whether questions are welcome immediately or at the end. If a speaker must demonstrate a feature, practice returning to the exact slide afterward. More rehearsal generally improves control, but it should not lead to a memorized performance that ignores audience needs.

### Worked example: a preflight problem caught early

During rehearsal, Luis notices that the projector shows the laptop's editing interface, including the notes pane. The audience has not yet arrived. Luis stops the rehearsal, confirms how the laptop is configured, chooses the correct slideshow output, and tests the projected image again. If the equipment cannot support a separate presenter display, he uses a paper cue sheet instead. The successful decision is not to demand that Presenter View work; it is to ensure that the audience receives the intended content.

## 3. How do you start, navigate, and end a slide show?

Most presentation software provides a command to **start a slide show**. Some applications distinguish starting from the first slide and starting from the currently selected slide. These choices are not interchangeable. Starting at the first slide is appropriate for a normal opening. Starting at the current slide can be useful for rehearsal, resuming after a break, or demonstrating a particular section without forcing the audience through earlier material.

### Identify the current mode before clicking

In normal editing mode, you often see [[slide-thumbnails|slide thumbnails]], menus, a toolbar or ribbon, and the active slide in a central workspace. In [[slide-show-mode|slide-show mode]], the selected slide occupies most or all of the intended presentation area. Some applications display a small navigation toolbar when you move the mouse. Presenter-oriented screens can contain notes and slide previews. Do not interpret every large-looking slide as proof that you are in the correct mode: you may be in a reading view, full-screen editor, browser tab, or preview rather than the actual slideshow output.

A useful test is to move forward one slide, then backward, then exit. If the commands do not behave as expected, determine which window has [[keyboard-focus|keyboard focus]]. Keyboard shortcuts operate on the active application or control, not necessarily on the screen you intended. Clicking an unrelated window may cause navigation keys to act somewhere else.

### First slide versus current slide

A presentation with six slides can be started in two common ways:

- **From beginning:** The application starts at the first slide included in the show, typically slide 1. This is the normal opening for a complete talk.
- **From current slide:** The application starts at the currently selected slide. If slide 4 is selected, delivery begins around slide 4 rather than automatically returning to slide 1.

The exact key combination varies by application. In many desktop PowerPoint installations on Windows, `F5` starts from the beginning and `Shift+F5` starts from the current slide. On some laptops you may need the `Fn` key, and online or Mac versions can differ. LibreOffice Impress commonly uses `F5` and `Shift+F5` for the same two purposes. Google Slides normally uses the **Slideshow** control and its available menu; avoid assuming its editor uses the same shortcuts as PowerPoint.

### Advancing one slide at a time

A [[navigation-control|navigation control]] may be a mouse click, keyboard key, touch gesture, on-screen arrow, or remote button. When presenting manually, a deliberate press usually advances to the next step. However, a step is not always a whole new slide. If the current slide contains a staged [[animation-build|animation build]]—for example, bullets appearing one by one—the first press may reveal an item and only a later press changes slides. Do not assume one click always means one slide.

| Intent | Typical control | Important qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Advance | Right Arrow, Space, a suitable on-screen control | May trigger the next animation effect before leaving the slide |
| Go back | Left Arrow, Page Up, or a previous-slide control | May step backward through effects; behavior varies |
| Start from beginning | Application's From Beginning command | May have a Windows keyboard shortcut depending on software |
| Start from selected slide | Application's From Current Slide command | Confirm which slide is selected first |
| Jump to a particular slide | Slide overview or supported slide-number command | Not every program uses the same shortcut |
| Temporarily blank the projection | Supported black-screen or blank-screen control | Key assignments and display behavior vary |
| End the show | `Esc` in most desktop slideshow modes | Be sure the slideshow window has focus |

**Stop and think:** If you accidentally move forward, do not click frantically. Look at the screen, identify the current slide, and use the application's previous-slide control. Repeatedly pressing a button without knowing whether an animation build is active can move you even further from the intended point.

### Recover after an accidental jump

Suppose the audience asks about the schedule on slide 4 while you are speaking from slide 5. In a supported slideshow view, you may use the slide overview, an on-screen list, or a slide-number command to navigate directly to slide 4. If you do not know the relevant shortcut, use the application's visible controls instead of experimenting in front of the audience. After answering, return to the planned place and briefly reconnect the flow: “We have clarified the schedule; now we will finish with the ways to get help.”

This is another reason to know the structure of the deck. A speaker who knows the purpose of each slide can recover quickly even if the software changes the visual order unexpectedly. You do not need to memorize every key combination to communicate well; you need reliable access to a few essential commands.

### Temporarily blanking the screen

Some programs provide a shortcut or control to replace the current slide with a plain black or white screen. In supported PowerPoint and Google Slides presentation modes, `B` can display a black screen and restore the content on the next appropriate key press; LibreOffice Impress also documents black-screen controls. This can shift attention from the screen to the speaker during a discussion. **A blank screen does not stop screen sharing, mute a microphone, hide all other displays, or erase what was previously visible.** It is an attention tool, not a privacy or security boundary.

### Pointer and annotation controls

A [[laser-pointer|laser pointer]] highlights part of a slide without changing the underlying document's main text. A [[pen-tool|pen tool]] allows a presenter to mark or draw over the slide during delivery. These are useful for focusing attention on a chart or diagram, but constant pointing or circling can become distracting. When possible, use the slide's own labels and speak the important relationship aloud so people who cannot see the pointer still understand the message.

Different programs handle [[ink-annotations|ink annotations]] differently. PowerPoint may offer to retain ink markings when you end a show. Google Slides documents pen annotations as temporary during the presentation. LibreOffice provides presentation pen behavior that should be tested in the current version. **Do not assume live annotations will always disappear, save, or appear in an exported file the same way.** Verify the result before distributing anything.

### Ending the presentation correctly

The [[end-show|end show]] action exits presentation playback and typically returns the application to an editing or reading environment. Pressing `Esc` commonly ends the slide show, but it does **not** necessarily close the entire application or terminate a remote meeting's screen share. The next steps depend on the setting:

1. Thank the audience and give the intended closing instruction or resource.
2. End the slideshow using the application's control or supported keyboard command.
3. In an online meeting, separately stop screen sharing if it is no longer needed.
4. Confirm that no private application or notes are now being shown to viewers.
5. Save approved changes and close or protect files according to the setting.

A slideshow can be completed successfully even if a small technical error occurred. The goal is that people received the essential information and the presenter ended with the appropriate next action, not that every animation occurred exactly as rehearsed.

## 4. How do you use presentation controls in common applications?

PowerPoint, Google Slides, and LibreOffice Impress all support presenting slides, but they do not have identical controls or the same display features on every platform. The most reliable approach is to learn the few commands needed for the chosen setup: **start, advance, go back, jump, inspect presenter tools, blank if necessary, and exit**. Then rehearse those actions using the version actually available to you. Do not install software, change institutional settings, or purchase a subscription solely because an optional control is mentioned in this lesson.

### Microsoft PowerPoint on Windows 11

PowerPoint desktop versions generally provide a **Slide Show** ribbon tab. Start from the first slide using **From Beginning**; to rehearse a later part, first select that slide in the editor, then choose **From Current Slide**. Common desktop Windows shortcuts include `F5` to start from the first slide and `Shift+F5` to start from the selected slide. Laptop keyboards and accessibility tools may change how function keys are produced, so use the visible command if the shortcut does not work.

A basic PowerPoint rehearsal can follow this sequence:

1. Open the intended practice deck in PowerPoint.
2. Select slide 1 in the editing pane and confirm the title.
3. Choose **Slide Show**, then **From Beginning**, or use the available equivalent.
4. Check that slide 1 fills the audience presentation area.
5. Use an advance control once; if the current slide has an entrance animation, observe whether an element appears before the next slide.
6. Use a backward control and verify where it returns you.
7. Try the bottom-left slideshow controls if available, including navigation and slide overview.
8. Press `Esc` to return to the editor, then check whether the file has unsaved changes.

PowerPoint provides [[presenter-view|Presenter View]] in supported configurations. This may show the current slide, the upcoming slide, [[speaker-notes|speaker notes]], a timer, and drawing controls on the presenter's screen. The audience is intended to see the actual slides on a different display. To use it, review **Slide Show** settings and **Use Presenter View**, then check which monitor has been assigned to the slide show. Microsoft documents monitor selection and a way to switch the display roles in the Presenter View interface on supported versions. **A successful setup is determined by observing the audience screen, not merely by checking a box.**

PowerPoint's timer can help estimate how long you have been speaking. It cannot tell you whether people have understood. If a question requires an extra minute, answer it as appropriate rather than rushing the next slide simply to match the timer. Presenter View can also enlarge note text, show a slide overview, and offer a screen-darkening control in versions that support these features.

#### PowerPoint web and device differences

PowerPoint for the web offers browser-based slideshow functions, but some desktop-only features and controls may be absent or work differently. Online usage may require connectivity. On a phone or tablet, touch controls replace some keyboard shortcuts, and the interface may not display notes and controls the same way as a desktop presentation. Use the application interface you actually see and avoid promising that an exact menu or shortcut is universal.

**Official reference:** [Microsoft Support — Present your slide show](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/powerpoint/present-your-slide-show).

### Google Slides in a desktop browser

Google Slides presents a slide deck through a **Slideshow** button, usually near the upper-right area of the editor. Click it to run the deck. Google documents a dropdown next to the button for presenter-oriented options, including **Presenter view** and display settings where supported. Google Chrome is recommended for some advanced multi-monitor configurations. Depending on the browser and current interface, the presentation may occupy a browser's full-screen view or a separate slideshow window.

A typical Google Slides rehearsal is:

1. Open the practice deck in the authorized browser session.
2. Select the desired starting slide and review the visible slide title.
3. Choose **Slideshow** or its dropdown option appropriate to the intended start.
4. Advance with the Right Arrow or an on-screen navigation control.
5. Return with the Left Arrow and confirm the slide content.
6. Move the pointer toward the bottom of the slideshow to locate additional controls, when available.
7. Open **Presenter view** only if needed and supported by the screen configuration.
8. Press `Esc` to exit and return to the editing environment.

Google's documented presentation-mode shortcuts include Right Arrow for the next step, Left Arrow for the previous step, a slide number followed by Enter to jump to a specified slide, and `L` to toggle the laser pointer in supported desktop views. The official help page also documents `B` for a temporary black screen, `W` for a temporary white screen, and `Esc` to stop presenting. Keyboard layouts and browser focus matter; you do not need to use these shortcuts if visible controls are easier.

Google Slides also has a [[pen-tool|pen tool]] in supported current desktop browser presentation interfaces. It can add temporary marks during a show. Official documentation states that these annotations disappear after the slideshow ends. That is different from PowerPoint behavior, where retaining ink may be offered. Test any annotation feature before relying on it during a formal session.

Google's [[presenter-view|Presenter view]] can show speaker notes and other presenter controls. For certain multi-monitor settings, the browser may ask for permission to use displays. Read the permission prompt and confirm that the requested access is appropriate. If you have only one display, do not assume there is a private audience-versus-presenter separation; use a separate paper cue sheet or another approved approach. Some menu choices are unavailable with certain screen configurations.

**Official reference:** [Google Docs Editors Help — Present slides](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/1696787?hl=en).

### LibreOffice Impress on Windows and other desktop systems

LibreOffice Impress uses a **Slide Show** menu and supports starting from the first slide or the selected slide. In supported desktop configurations, `F5` normally starts the show from the beginning, while `Shift+F5` starts from the current slide. The menu commands can be used when a keyboard does not have convenient function keys. LibreOffice's **Slide Show Settings** also include options for slide range, display, presentation mode, navigation, and whether the [[presenter-console|Presenter Console]] is enabled.

A basic Impress rehearsal is:

1. Open a fictional practice deck in Impress.
2. Select the first slide in the editing interface.
3. Choose **Slide Show**, then **Start from First Slide**, or use the version's equivalent.
4. Use a forward control, such as Right Arrow or Space, to advance.
5. Use Left Arrow or an appropriate previous control to go backward.
6. Press `Esc` to exit.
7. Return to editing mode and select slide 3; choose **Start from Current Slide** to test a partial run.
8. Exit again and verify that the selected starting point matched your intention.

The Impress Presenter Console, when supported and correctly configured, gives the speaker a separate control view, such as the current slide, next slide, notes, and navigation tools. It may have a slide overview and note-size controls. The official shortcut documentation includes `Ctrl+1` to show the presenter console, `Ctrl+2` for notes, and `Ctrl+3` for slide overview in supported console mode. These are **not** general shortcuts to try in unrelated windows; they are context-specific. The key assignments can also differ on some platforms.

Impress offers slideshow settings for full-screen or windowed presentation and choosing the display where the audience output appears. You should not infer that selecting a display guarantees privacy. An operating system, remote meeting application, or projector may duplicate or expose more than intended. Conduct an actual audience-view test.

**Official references:** [LibreOffice Help — Slide Show Settings](https://help.libreoffice.org/latest/en-US/text/simpress/01/06080000.html) and [Presenter Console Keyboard Shortcuts](https://help.libreoffice.org/latest/en-US/text/simpress/04/presenter.html).

### Comparing the three applications

Your presentation software might use other labels, a different language, a touchscreen, a ribbon with simplified menus, or an accessibility interface. Learn the method that reliably works in **your specific environment**. Where a keyboard command conflicts with the operating system or a screen reader, use the accessible control available to you.

### Presenting with notes and timers

A presenter-oriented view may allow you to glance at the next slide or at notes without showing them to the audience. This helps with sequencing but should not become a reason to stare at the monitor continuously. Notes from Lesson 7 are most useful as cues: a reminder to define an unfamiliar word, cite a source, give a simple example, or invite questions. The timer should help you decide whether to shorten a nonessential illustration or reserve time for the conclusion.

If the speaker is using a single screen, a separate notes document, printed card, or brief outline may be safer and simpler than opening a notes window while sharing the same screen. Even with two screens, it is wise to assume the presenter view could be visible if screen-sharing or display settings are misconfigured.

### Highlighting and describing visual information

A [[laser-pointer|laser pointer]] directs attention visually, but a viewer using audio only may not know what “over here” means. Instead say, “In the top row, the third item is the help desk.” When describing a chart, identify the axis labels, units, overall pattern, and conclusion rather than pointing silently. Avoid quickly scribbling over several objects while explaining them at the same time. A concise spoken explanation should remain understandable even if the visual cue is missed.

A good presenter uses the pointer briefly and then moves it away. Repeated motion, unnecessary animations, and visual clutter can make it harder for some viewers to follow. If an annotation makes the underlying slide unreadable, clear it using the application's supported controls or move to an unmarked copy of the content.

### Media controls and animations

A slide may include audio, video, or staged animations. The speaker must know whether media plays automatically, on click, or with a separate control. A video that starts unexpectedly can interrupt speech. A silent video may convey too little information. An online video may depend on an external service that is unavailable during the talk. Whenever media is important, have a short summary ready so that its failure does not eliminate the central lesson.

Check the opening and ending of a clip, its approximate volume, whether captions or a text alternative are provided, and whether the content is lawful and appropriate to show. In an online meeting, playing sound on the presenter's device does **not** prove that remote participants can hear it: meeting software often has a separate setting for sharing system audio. Confirm with a trusted participant or test meeting rather than assuming that the audio path works.

## 5. How do you present on screens and in meetings safely?

An in-person presentation uses physical screens. A remote presentation uses a meeting or broadcasting application. A [[hybrid-presentation|hybrid presentation]] includes people in more than one setting. In each case, the presenter has to manage who can see the slides, what else might appear, and whether the audience can hear important information. This is why the selected [[display-mode|display mode]] and the meeting's sharing controls matter at least as much as the presentation file.

### Understand mirrored and extended displays

A [[mirrored-display|mirrored display]] shows essentially the same desktop output on two screens. A laptop mirrored onto a projector may show the same windows on both. This can be simple for a talk without notes, but opening a notes pane or personal message on the laptop may also make it visible on the projector.

An [[extended-display|extended display]] allows the computer to treat two screens as different workspace areas. The presenter may use a private laptop display for notes while placing the audience slideshow on the projector. This setup can be useful, but windows can be moved between screens and the wrong display can be selected. **Extended mode is a workspace arrangement, not a guarantee of confidentiality.** Test the audience screen before showing any sensitive material and keep private items closed regardless.

On Windows 11, `Windows+P` usually opens a projection-mode menu with choices such as PC screen only, Duplicate, Extend, and Second screen only. The correct choice depends on the goal and the connected hardware. Selecting **Duplicate** mirrors outputs in an ordinary supported setup; selecting **Extend** creates separate display areas. Confirm the result on the actual audience screen, because projector configuration, display adapters, and meeting software can affect what appears.

| Display arrangement | What it can help with | What must be checked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One laptop screen | Rehearsal or a small group | Notes and unrelated windows are not exposed |
| Laptop plus mirrored projector | Simple presentation with the same image on both | Private content must not appear on the laptop screen |
| Laptop plus extended projector | Separate slideshow and presenter controls | The slideshow is on the audience display, not the notes screen |
| Large classroom display | Readability for people seated at different distances | Text size, contrast, positioning, and sound |
| Remote meeting window share | Present one selected output remotely | The shared window is the correct one throughout |
| Remote meeting full-screen share | Show the desktop or a chosen monitor | All visible material on that screen is suitable to disclose |
| Hybrid room and remote meeting | Reach two audiences at once | Room projection and remote share both show intended content |

### Projecting in a classroom or meeting room

A basic projector has an input source, which may come from HDMI, USB-C, wireless casting, or another supported connection. If the projector says **No signal**, it does not mean the presentation file is missing. The display may be on the wrong input, the cable may be disconnected, or the computer may not yet recognize the external display. Adjust the physical connection and operating system settings before changing the slide file.

When the projector is connected, display the first slide and look at the actual projected image. Check that the slide fills the intended area without being stretched, cropped, or surrounded by unexpected application windows. Move to a slide with dense text and one with a visual element. If the output is unreadable, consider increasing text size or simplifying the display rather than asking the audience to interpret small information from a distance. If a person cannot see the screen well, provide the essential information verbally or through an accessible alternative.

Do not point a bright laser device toward people. Where a software pointer is available, it is generally easier to control without introducing another physical object. If using any wireless remote, confirm the connection and ensure it does not perform unexpected actions. A keyboard or on-screen navigation control should remain available as a fallback.

### Presenting in an online meeting

A [[screen-share|screen share]] sends visual output from a computer to meeting participants. A meeting application may let you select a whole screen, a particular window, a tab, or another approved content source. The choices are not interchangeable. Sharing a whole screen can expose notifications, desktop folders, and other windows that become visible. Sharing a particular window may reduce that scope, but window switching, pop-ups, and presentation software behavior can still create surprises.

A careful remote workflow is:

1. Join the meeting using an authorized account and confirm the intended audience.
2. Close confidential documents and unrelated tabs before starting a share.
3. Open and test the presentation in the intended slide-show or approved window mode.
4. Use the meeting application's sharing menu to select the intended source.
5. Confirm with a trusted participant or preview that viewers can see the correct slide, not a notes pane, browser address bar, or blank screen.
6. Check audio sharing separately if the slides include an approved clip.
7. Introduce the presentation and advance carefully, verifying that the remote view continues to update.
8. Stop screen sharing after the talk, then exit the slideshow or other presentation tools as appropriate.

### Screen sharing does not equal file sharing

When you show a slideshow in a meeting, viewers generally receive a live representation of the selected screen or window, not automatically permission to edit the presentation file. When you share an editable deck or a link, recipients may be able to inspect content beyond what was shown during the talk, depending on the permissions and file contents. **The next lesson, Exporting and Sharing Presentations, addresses document distribution in depth.** For now, recognize that ending a live share is different from removing access to a file that has already been sent.

### Check microphones, speakers, and system audio

A microphone captures the speaker's voice for remote participants. A speaker or headset plays audio from other participants and, in some cases, media. A [[system-audio-sharing|system audio sharing]] option determines whether the meeting transmits audio from the presented device or application. These pathways can differ: the audience may hear the presenter's voice but not an embedded video, or hear the video while the microphone is muted.

Before a remote talk, test the microphone and ask a permitted participant whether your voice is clear. If the presentation includes video, test whether its sound is transmitted. A headset can reduce echo in some environments. If you are presenting in a room with remote participants, use the room's appropriate microphone and speaker setup, and avoid running multiple unmuted devices nearby. If sound fails, pause and explain what has happened rather than allowing the audience to guess.

### Privacy during screen sharing

A [[privacy-check|privacy check]] includes verifying the share source and making sure the visible information is suitable for the audience. Do not rely on a presenter-only view to conceal information automatically; the meeting application might share the wrong display. A private chat window can open above the slide. A notification can contain a person's name or message preview. A browser may reveal account names and tabs. These are common reasons to use a clean session and to verify the actual shared output.

For a workshop, it is enough to demonstrate fictional names and sample registration entries. There is no instructional need to show someone's actual attendance record, email inbox, personal photo library, or password. If you inadvertently expose private data, stop the share promptly, follow the institution's applicable incident procedure, and correct the situation rather than treating it as an ordinary slide-navigation mistake.

### Captions and other accessibility supports

[[captions|Captions]] display a textual representation of speech, commonly generated live or supplied for recorded media. Some presentation applications and meeting systems offer caption controls, but availability depends on language, product, permissions, microphone conditions, and settings. Google Slides documents a presentation caption feature for supported configurations, with captions based on microphone input. It also documents limitations in accuracy and that captions are not stored by that feature. Captions should be checked for errors, and the speaker should avoid treating them as a perfect transcript.

Caption access can help people who are deaf or hard of hearing, those in a noisy environment, and people who find reading alongside speech useful. But captions alone are not an excuse to speak incoherently, omit a transcript where needed, or ignore an accessibility request. Some participants may need a downloadable accessible handout, a describable chart, an interpreter, or another reasonable accommodation. Ask the event organizer about approved accessibility arrangements before the presentation.

### Presenting without a reliable internet connection

A local presentation may be usable offline, but an online service, externally hosted video, cloud-linked document, or remote meeting depends on connectivity. A backup plan should distinguish **what you can still do offline** from **what cannot continue without the network**. You may be able to present a saved local slide file and narrate a planned web demonstration using prepared screenshots. You cannot deliver a live online meeting to distant participants without a functioning network path; the appropriate response is to follow the organizer's rescheduling or alternate communication plan.

A reasonable backup package for a small event might be a verified local copy of the approved presentation, a nonconfidential paper speaking outline, a short summary of critical multimedia, and an accessible one-page list of essential instructions. Ensure backups are appropriately secured and are not contradictory versions. A backup that contains outdated information can be worse than acknowledging that a particular item needs to be confirmed later.

### Handling a real-time technical interruption

If the screen goes black unexpectedly, the pointer disappears, or the meeting share freezes, first stop unnecessary clicking. Tell the audience what you are doing in a short neutral sentence: “The display has stopped updating. I will check the presentation connection.” Then assess the situation: is the laptop functioning, is the projector on the correct source, is the meeting share active, and can you see the correct slide locally?

You do not need to narrate every technical setting. If the problem is brief, fix it and resume with a clear transition. If recovery would take longer than the value of the affected slide, continue using the fallback explanation. Respect any organizer time limit, and do not reveal private information by opening unrelated settings or accounts while the entire screen is being shared.

## 6. How do you deliver a clear, accessible presentation?

Presentation software is a support system for communication. Knowing how to launch slides is useful, but listeners also need a message they can understand. A presentation is easier to follow when it has a clear beginning, a manageable sequence, spoken explanations of essential visuals, and a recognizable conclusion. The following practices are not rigid performance rules. They are practical methods for treating the audience as participants in the communication, not merely observers of a screen.

### Open with purpose and direction

A useful opening can be short: “Welcome to the Saturday Digital Skills Workshop orientation. In the next few minutes, I will explain what we will practice, what to bring, and how to get support.” This introduction identifies the audience's expected benefit and prepares the listener for the slide sequence. It does not require a dramatic story or elaborate visual effect.

Avoid opening by apologizing for the simplicity of the slides or by reading the title without context. Instead, state why the information matters. If the talk has a question period, mention when questions are welcome. In a meeting with remote participants, explain how they can ask a question, such as using a meeting control, the approved chat, or a moderator. Do not imply everyone must use the same form of participation.

### Speak to people, not just to the slide

The slide is a reference. Speak in connected sentences that explain relationships, definitions, and action steps. Use audience-appropriate language and briefly define unfamiliar terms. A slower pace is often helpful when explaining a new computer command, but avoid assuming a single speaking speed works for everyone. Insert pauses when listeners need time to read, compare, or complete a step.

A useful pattern for each slide is:

1. Identify the slide's main point in one sentence.
2. Explain one example or consequence.
3. Describe any essential visual evidence or on-screen instruction.
4. Check for understanding when appropriate.
5. Connect the point to the next slide.

For example, the slide **Help Is Available** might show a library desk icon, a short contact line, and an arrow to **Practice Again**. Instead of saying “Here is the help slide,” say: “If you cannot finish the practice activity, raise your hand or speak with the staff member at the help desk. You may repeat the activity; there is no penalty for needing a second attempt.” The explanation provides information the graphic alone may not convey.

### Explain visual information in words

A [[visual-description|visual description]] tells listeners what important information a chart, screenshot, photograph, map, or diagram communicates. It does not need to narrate every decorative element. If a diagram shows a three-stage process, identify the stages and their order. If a table shows a comparison, state the categories and the conclusion. If a screenshot includes a button that the audience must find, give the button's label and location in ordinary language.

| Weak explanation | More useful explanation | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Look at this chart.” | “The blue bar represents the second group and is about twice the height of the first.” | Explains the actual relationship |
| “Click over there.” | “Select the **Slideshow** button near the upper-right of the presentation editor.” | Names the control and location |
| “You can see the three steps.” | “The three steps are register, prepare a device if available, and ask for help.” | Makes the sequence available without sight |
| “The highlighted item matters.” | “The highlighted row is the final safety check before you share your screen.” | Identifies the meaning, not only the color |
| “This image proves it.” | “This image illustrates the proposed layout; it does not independently verify the underlying claim.” | Separates illustration from evidence |

Avoid distinguishing important options through color alone. Say the text label or shape as well. When using a laser pointer, name the item you are pointing at. When a slide contains a detailed table, consider highlighting the few comparisons needed for the current objective and supplying a more complete accessible copy as appropriate.

### Use questions and audience interaction deliberately

[[audience-interaction|Audience interaction]] can make a short lesson more memorable. Ask a question that advances the objective: “Which step would you take if the presentation starts on slide 4 by mistake?” Allow enough time for an answer, including people who need more processing time. For an online group, you may use the approved chat or question controls. Do not require attendees to reveal personal details to prove understanding.

A simple question protocol is:

1. Invite a question with a clear prompt.
2. Allow a reasonable pause.
3. Listen without interrupting the speaker.
4. Repeat or paraphrase the question when doing so improves access and the question is appropriate to share.
5. Answer within the scope of the presentation or state what needs verification.
6. Return the audience to the current point with a short transition.

If a question falls outside the topic, acknowledge it and offer the approved later support channel instead of abandoning the core lesson. If you do not know the answer, do not invent a confident explanation. Mark the point for verification and explain when or how an authoritative answer can be obtained, without promising an unarranged follow-up.

### Respond to timing pressure without losing meaning

A presentation may run late because participants ask questions, a demonstration needs repetition, or equipment takes time to respond. A presenter should know which examples are optional and which points are essential. In the workshop example, the date, purpose, and method of getting help are essential. A second story about why computer skills matter may be shortened if time is limited.

Avoid solving timing problems by speaking too fast, skipping a required safety note, or hiding a critical disclosure. If the remaining time cannot cover required content, notify the organizer and arrange a sensible continuation or supporting resource. Use the slide timer as a signal to make a deliberate decision, not as a reason to cut necessary information.

### Closing with a clear next step

A good conclusion summarizes what people should remember and what they should do. For the workshop, a speaker might say: “You now know when the workshop begins, what devices you may bring, and where to ask for help. Your next step is to check the posted registration instructions. Thank you; I can take one final question.” The closing slide can display the same few points in a readable format.

Do not end by silently exiting the slideshow while people still expect another explanation. Do not leave the screen on an editing window with unfinished comments or notes. After the closing message, end the show, stop a remote screen share if one is active, and confirm that the audience is no longer receiving unintended output.

### Practice an inclusive delivery style

People may engage with slides through sight, hearing, captions, assistive technology, interpreter support, a handout, or an alternative device. Keep the essential message available in more than one way when feasible. Speak clearly, describe meaningful visuals, provide enough time for actions, and explain the location of interface controls by name. Do not require eye contact from every listener or assume that someone who looks away is disengaged. Avoid rapid flashing effects, unnecessary animations, and visual surprises that can interfere with concentration or accessibility.

Accessibility is a process of meeting varied needs, not a single checkbox. A presenter may need to coordinate with an event organizer for sign language interpretation, an accessible digital copy, room seating, caption tools, or another support. Some capabilities depend on product licensing or approved organizational systems; choose workable alternatives rather than claiming that a particular tool is automatically accessible in every situation.

### A simple post-presentation review

After the audience has left or the meeting has ended, spend two minutes on a [[post-presentation-review|post-presentation review]]. Record whether the message was complete, how the display and audio worked, which slide caused confusion, whether audience questions revealed missing context, and what should be changed before presenting again. Use observations rather than unsupported judgments about participants.

| Review question | Example observation | Possible improvement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Was the opening clear? | Participants understood the purpose after the first slide | Keep the opening concise |
| Could people read the slides? | One schedule label appeared small on the projector | Enlarge or simplify the schedule |
| Was the navigation reliable? | The remote advanced two steps once | Rehearse with the keyboard as fallback |
| Did audio work? | Online viewers could not hear the embedded clip | Test the meeting's system-audio setting |
| Were visuals explained? | The presenter named all three process stages | Preserve the verbal explanation |
| Did questions fit the plan? | A registration question required one extra minute | Reserve a minute for common questions |
| Was privacy maintained? | Only the slideshow appeared in audience output | Repeat the preflight check next time |

> **Professional delivery habit:** Treat every slide as a communication decision. If a feature makes it harder for the audience to receive the central message, remove the feature or use a simpler explanation. The most effective presentation is the one that people can follow and use.

## 7. Guided practice: Present a six-slide workshop

These activities are designed for beginners and use only fictional material. You may work on paper, use an existing practice deck, or open a new presentation in an application available to you. **Do not create a real meeting invitation, upload private information, publish a file, or turn on a microphone or camera solely for the exercises.** If you are using a shared computer, follow its normal authorized-use rules. Each activity includes an expandable model response so you can compare your approach with a practical answer.

### Activity 1: Identify the presentation roles and plan the opening

**Objective:** Distinguish the editor, slide show, presenter display, audience display, and live screen share.

**Scenario:** A fictional community center wants a four-minute orientation about a Saturday Digital Skills Workshop. You have six slides: Welcome; Workshop Goal; What to Bring; Activity Schedule; Help Available; Next Steps. The audience consists of 12 new learners, including a few people who may join remotely.

**Instructions:**

1. Write a one-sentence outcome for the audience: What should everyone know or be able to do afterward?
2. Explain in your own words why opening the editor is not the same as running a slide show.
3. Identify which content belongs on the audience display and which might belong in speaker notes.
4. Write two sentences you could say before advancing from the first slide.
5. Name one access need you would consider before the audience arrives.
6. Identify one piece of private information that must not appear on any presentation screen.

**Success criteria:** Your response includes an observable audience goal, separates speaker-only cues from audience content, and acknowledges accessibility and privacy.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answer
**Audience outcome:** Participants can state the time and purpose of the workshop, understand that bringing a device is optional, and identify where to get help.

**Editor versus show:** The editor contains authoring controls, slide thumbnails, and potentially notes or comments. The slide show is an output intended to guide an audience through the material.

**Audience content:** Readable titles, three key instructions, and a contact route approved for public use. **Speaker-only content:** A brief reminder to pause after discussing the device requirement and to describe the schedule aloud.

**Possible opening:** “Welcome to the Saturday Digital Skills Workshop orientation. I will explain what we will practice, how to prepare, and where to ask for assistance.”

**Accessibility consideration:** Check that text can be read from the room and make a spoken explanation available to remote participants. **Privacy example:** Never display a private registration record or a staff member's password.

A different opening can also be correct if it clearly sets expectations and preserves privacy.
:::

### Activity 2: Practice starting, advancing, going backward, and exiting

**Objective:** Control a slideshow without becoming dependent on one input method.

**Equipment:** Optional presentation software. Paper-only learners may use six numbered index cards.

**Instructions:**

1. Open a fictional six-slide deck or arrange six numbered cards.
2. Begin with the first slide, using the application's **From Beginning** function or the first card.
3. Identify what changes between the edit interface and presentation view.
4. Advance to the second slide and then return to the first.
5. Navigate to the fourth slide using a supported overview or the numbered cards.
6. State aloud how you will return to the planned sequence after answering a question about slide 4.
7. End the slideshow and confirm that you are no longer presenting.
8. Write the name of the forward control, backward control, and exit control you used.

**Optional challenge:** If your practice deck includes an entrance animation, observe whether advancing once reveals an effect rather than immediately changing slides. Try the same exercise with the animation removed.

**Success criteria:** You can start, navigate in both directions, recover from an intentional jump, and stop without exposing unrelated content.

:::answer Show Activity 2 model answer
**A typical PowerPoint desktop answer:** Select **Slide Show → From Beginning** or use `F5` where supported. Advance with Right Arrow or Space; return with Left Arrow; use slide overview or another supported navigation tool for slide 4; press `Esc` to exit.

**A typical Google Slides answer:** Use the **Slideshow** control, navigate with arrow keys, and press `Esc`. The program documents slide-number navigation in supported presentation mode; a learner may use its visible slide list instead.

**A typical Impress answer:** Use **Slide Show → Start from First Slide** or `F5` where supported, advance and return with suitable keys, and exit with `Esc`.

**Paper version:** Present card 1, move to card 2, return to card 1, select card 4 for a question, and finish by putting the cards in order again.

**Recovery phrase:** “We returned to the schedule to clarify the timing. Now we will continue with the ways to request assistance.”

The exact controls may differ by version. A correct answer demonstrates the intended actions and verifies the visible results.
:::

### Activity 3: Test the audience view and identify privacy risks

**Objective:** Decide whether the intended material is actually reaching the audience and respond to a display mistake.

**Scenario A:** A laptop connected to a projector is using **Duplicate**. The speaker opens a speaker-notes window over the current slide. What might the audience see?

**Scenario B:** A laptop is using **Extend** and shows notes on the internal display. The projector shows the slideshow. Is the configuration automatically confidential?

**Scenario C:** During a remote meeting, the speaker selects **Share entire screen** and receives a private desktop notification. What is the risk?

**Instructions:**

1. For each scenario, describe what the audience might see.
2. Explain why merely selecting a presenter setting does not prove the audience output is correct.
3. Describe one safe correction for each scenario.
4. Create a two-column paper checklist labeled **Speaker view** and **Audience view**.
5. For each column, list the information that may appropriately appear there.
6. Write a short test request you could give a trusted participant before a live talk.

**Success criteria:** Your answers verify output rather than assuming private windows are protected.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model answer
**Scenario A:** In a normal mirrored display, the projector can duplicate the notes window. Stop the presentation setup and close the notes overlay or switch to a verified configuration before the audience begins viewing.

**Scenario B:** The intended separation is useful but not a guarantee. A window could move to the projector or a meeting application could select the wrong monitor. Inspect the actual projected image and avoid keeping sensitive information open.

**Scenario C:** The full-screen share may transmit the notification. Close confidential applications and use appropriate notification settings before sharing. If private information appears unexpectedly, stop the share and follow the organization's procedure.

**Speaker view:** Current slide, optional nonconfidential cues, timer, and approved navigation controls. **Audience view:** Intended readable slideshow content and accessible supporting information.

**Useful test request:** “Please confirm that you can see the title slide, that the full slide is visible, and that you cannot see my editing menus or speaker notes.”
:::

### Activity 4: Practice a clear spoken explanation and pointer use

**Objective:** Explain a slide to people who may not see pointer movements or read small text.

**Scenario:** Slide 4 shows a fictional workshop schedule with three steps: **Introduction at 10:00**, **Guided Practice at 10:30**, and **Questions at 11:15**. A small arrow points from each step to the next. The slide also displays a decorative icon.

**Instructions:**

1. Write a two- or three-sentence verbal explanation of the schedule.
2. Replace the vague phrase “Look at the arrow over here” with an explanation that names what changes.
3. Identify one decorative element that does not need a detailed spoken description.
4. State when you would pause to allow participants to inspect the schedule.
5. Explain how you would use a pointer sparingly if it was available.
6. Name an accessible alternative for a person who cannot read the small schedule text.
7. Explain what you would do if the pointer feature stopped working.

**Success criteria:** The schedule remains understandable without the pointer or decorative graphic.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answer
**Spoken explanation:** “The workshop begins with an introduction at 10:00. Guided practice starts at 10:30, and we will reserve a question period beginning at 11:15. If you need extra help during practice, you can ask the facilitator instead of waiting until the end.”

**Better transition phrase:** “We move from the introduction into hands-on practice, then finish with questions.”

**Decorative item:** An unrelated lightbulb icon does not require a long description unless it conveys essential meaning.

**Pause:** After naming the three times, allow participants to inspect or copy them. **Pointer:** Briefly indicate each row and then stop moving the pointer. **Alternative:** Speak the three times clearly and provide an accessible text schedule. **Pointer failure:** Continue verbally; the pointer is not needed to explain the information.
:::

### Activity 5: Deliver the six-slide capstone presentation

**Objective:** Combine slide navigation, spoken explanation, time management, audience awareness, and a controlled ending.

**Project:** Create or use the following fictional six-slide sequence. You can draw the slide headings on paper if no application is available.

| Slide | Visible content | Speaker's message | Approximate time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Welcome | Saturday Digital Skills Workshop | Welcome and outcome | 30 seconds |
| 2. Workshop Goal | Practice everyday computer tasks | Describe the benefit and one example | 40 seconds |
| 3. What to Bring | Curiosity; optional device; questions | Explain device access and safety expectations | 40 seconds |
| 4. Activity Schedule | Introduction; practice; questions | Explain order and approximate timing | 60 seconds |
| 5. Help Available | Facilitator; printed guide; repeat practice | Explain where to ask for assistance | 40 seconds |
| 6. Next Steps | Check registration; arrive on time | Summarize and close | 30 seconds |

**Instructions:**

1. Prepare six slides or six paper cards using the table as an outline. Do not insert real attendee information.
2. Write one or two speaker cues for each slide. Do not write a large paragraph on the visible slide.
3. Select the correct opening slide and begin the rehearsal from the beginning.
4. Deliver a full practice talk of approximately four minutes, using your own sentences rather than reading only the displayed bullets.
5. On slide 4, pause long enough for an audience member to follow the sequence. Then return to your prepared flow.
6. Pretend that a participant asks about the optional device requirement after slide 5. Return to the relevant information, answer, and resume.
7. Conclude with one concrete next step and end the slideshow.
8. If using an application, check the actual screen output; if working on paper, draw the speaker and audience views and explain the difference.
9. Record the actual duration, one strength, and two improvements for the next rehearsal.

**Review rubric:** Assess the following five areas on a scale from **1 = needs practice** to **3 = consistently effective**: opening and purpose, navigation, clarity and visual descriptions, accessibility and privacy, and closing and next action. The highest possible score is 15. Use the score for self-improvement, not as a claim of professional certification.

:::answer Show Activity 5 model answer
**Possible opening:** “Welcome to our Saturday Digital Skills Workshop. Today's orientation will explain what we will practice, how to prepare, and where you can get help.”

**Possible slide 3 explanation:** “You may bring a laptop or tablet if you have one, but owning a device is not required for this fictional workshop. Bring your questions. We will not ask you to reveal personal passwords during practice.”

**Possible slide 4 explanation:** “We begin with a short introduction, move into guided practice, and leave time for questions. If a task takes you longer, ask for assistance; you do not have to rush through it.”

**Return to slide 3:** “To clarify the earlier question: a personal device is optional. We will now return to the support options on slide 5.”

**Possible closing:** “The next step is to check the workshop's published registration instructions and arrive at the scheduled time. Thank you for participating.”

**Sample rehearsal reflection:** Four minutes and 25 seconds. Strong opening. Improvement 1: make the schedule easier to read. Improvement 2: pause after explaining the device requirement. This slightly exceeds the target duration but still provides a reasonable basis for revision.

**Rubric interpretation:** A self-rating is useful only if accompanied by concrete evidence. For example, “navigation = 2” could mean the presenter recovered from an accidental jump but needed extra time to find the slide overview. There is no single correct score for the hypothetical rehearsal.
:::

### Activity 6: Respond to interruptions and complete the post-show check

**Objective:** Recover safely from ordinary disruptions and end the event without leaving unintended information visible.

**Instructions:**

1. Read the five fictional disruptions below.
2. Write the first safe action you would take for each problem.
3. State a short sentence that you could say to the audience while correcting the problem.
4. Identify when you should abandon the technology-dependent step and use a prepared alternative.
5. After a fictional closing, list the actions needed to end the slideshow and, if applicable, the meeting's screen share.
6. Write two lessons learned for the next presentation.

| Disruption | Question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Projector says **No signal** | Is this a display or a slide-content problem? |
| Embedded video is silent online | Is the audio path shared with remote participants? |
| Presenter notes appear on the projector | What must stop before presenting further? |
| The slideshow advances two steps | How can you recover without frantic clicking? |
| A question takes longer than expected | Which material can be shortened without losing the essential message? |

**Success criteria:** Every response prioritizes the audience's understanding and privacy, uses a controllable recovery step, and avoids unverified assumptions.

:::answer Show Activity 6 model answer
**No signal:** Pause, verify the cable or wireless connection and projector input, and inspect the operating system display settings. Say, “The display connection needs a quick check; I will use the spoken outline if it cannot be restored.”

**Silent video online:** Test the meeting's system-audio-sharing selection if appropriate. If unresolved, explain the clip's critical message from prepared notes. Say, “The clip's audio is not reaching the meeting; here is the information it was meant to demonstrate.”

**Notes visible to audience:** Stop or blank the inappropriate output before continuing, correct the display or sharing source, and check the actual viewer view. Say, “I need to correct the display selection before we continue.” If sensitive information was exposed, follow the institution's incident procedures.

**Two-step advance:** Stop clicking, inspect the current slide, use a previous or overview control, then explain the next point. Say, “We moved ahead of the current example; I am returning to the relevant slide.”

**Long question:** Provide a concise answer, maintain required safety information, and shorten an optional anecdote if necessary. Say, “That clarifies the main question. I will move to our final instructions so we have the essential information before we finish.”

**Ending:** Give the conclusion, exit the slideshow, stop any meeting share separately, confirm the audience receives no additional desktop content, and save approved changes. **Post-show improvements:** Test online audio before the next meeting and add a visible reminder for the closing action.
:::

### What your practice should demonstrate

After completing all six activities, you should be able to distinguish the presenter and audience roles, set a reasonable objective, operate a slideshow in both directions, explain a visual without relying entirely on pointer movement, verify the intended output, respond to one unexpected problem, and end the session appropriately. Practice is successful when you can explain **why** each step matters, not only when you remember the exact location of a button.

If you are uncomfortable speaking aloud, you may write the spoken portions first, practice with a trusted person, or use another appropriate communication method. Delivery techniques should support the learning goal rather than create an unnecessary barrier for the learner.

## 8. Common misconceptions and troubleshooting

A small technical mistake during a presentation does not automatically mean the entire talk has failed. Most problems can be diagnosed by separating **content**, **application mode**, **display**, **audio**, **meeting share**, and **audience communication**. Resist the urge to change several settings at once. Identify what is wrong, test one safe corrective action, and confirm the result before continuing.

### Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
| --- | --- |
| “Once I open my deck, the audience sees only the slides.” | Editing and presenting are different modes; projector and meeting outputs must be verified. |
| “Presenter View means my notes are private in every situation.” | Incorrect display or share settings can expose notes or other content. |
| “One click always advances one complete slide.” | A click may reveal an animation step before leaving the slide. |
| “A wireless remote is required to deliver a professional talk.” | Keyboard and on-screen controls can work just as well. |
| “A black slideshow screen ends the meeting share.” | A black screen usually only changes slideshow output; sharing may continue. |
| “A successful local video test means online viewers hear it.” | Meeting system-audio settings may require a separate test. |
| “Captions make the presentation perfectly accessible.” | Captions can be imperfect and do not replace other access needs. |
| “Reading every bullet aloud is the same as explaining it.” | The speaker should add context, examples, and relationships. |
| “A pointer movement explains a chart by itself.” | Important visual meaning should be expressed verbally or in accessible text. |
| “Finishing the last slide automatically stops file sharing.” | File permissions and live meeting sharing have separate controls. |
| “I must use every special effect to keep the audience engaged.” | Clear content and intentional pacing usually matter more than effects. |
| “If the internet fails, the presenter must continue the remote meeting.” | A remote meeting needs connectivity; follow an approved alternate communication plan. |

### Troubleshooting table

| Symptom | Plausible cause | First safe response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Projector says **No signal** | Wrong input, disconnected cable, or unrecognized display | Verify connection, input source, and computer display settings |
| Audience sees the editor | Wrong window or presentation mode | Stop or correct the output; restart the intended slideshow |
| Speaker notes are projected | Mirroring or incorrect display assignment | Stop inappropriate output; verify audience and presenter screens |
| Slide appears cropped | Scaling, aspect ratio, or projection setting | Test display resolution or an appropriate fit setting |
| Text is too small | Slide designed only for a nearby editor screen | Simplify or enlarge content and supply a readable alternative |
| Slide will not advance | Application focus is elsewhere, control is disabled, or effect is waiting | Refocus the slideshow and use a visible navigation control |
| Slide advances twice | Remote bounce, repeated input, or animation behavior | Stop pressing, identify current position, then navigate deliberately |
| Slideshow starts on slide 4 | Started from current slide | Exit and choose the beginning when a full show is intended |
| Pointer is missing | Pointer tool disabled or current interface hides it | Use verbal description and supported navigation tools |
| Notes are missing | Wrong display, notes view, or slide selected | Confirm selected slide and supported presenter controls |
| Video will not play | Media link, permissions, codec, or connectivity issue | Use prepared nonvideo explanation; troubleshoot after the critical message |
| Online viewers hear no clip audio | System audio was not included in the meeting share | Test approved audio sharing or narrate the main point |
| Meeting share freezes | Network, application, or capture problem | Check the local slideshow and meeting status; use organizer fallback |
| Browser enters unexpected full screen | Browser or app shortcut was triggered | Use visible exit controls or `Esc` and restore intended mode |
| Notifications appear over the deck | Unrelated apps are active on the shared screen | Stop inappropriate sharing, control notifications, and verify output |
| Audience cannot see a detail | Poor contrast, room lighting, or small device display | Explain verbally and provide a readable alternative |
| Captions are inaccurate | Audio quality, speech recognition, language, or setting limitations | Speak clearly, check microphone and language support, and offer alternatives |
| Final slide appears but no conclusion happens | Presenter treated slide change as the closing message | State the summary and next step aloud before ending |

### Separate a content problem from a display problem

If text is incorrect on the actual slide, that is a **content problem**. Correct the source file in an authorized editor and save the intended version. If the file looks correct on the laptop but appears stretched or cut off only on the projector, that is more likely a **display problem**. Adjusting the slide text may not be the right first response. If remote viewers see the first slide while your local slideshow advances, that is likely a **sharing or capture problem**. Start by checking what source the meeting application is transmitting.

This separation prevents one mistake from producing several new ones. A beginner may be tempted to move every shape on a slide because a projector is cropping the image. That changes the document when the underlying issue may be projection configuration. Another presenter might recreate a video because remote viewers cannot hear it, even though the missing step is system-audio sharing. Diagnose the layer first.

### Recovering without escalating the problem

A calm recovery has four stages: recognize, protect, correct, and resume. **Recognize** the observable problem without guessing its cause. **Protect** the audience and any private information; stop an unintended share or display if necessary. **Correct** one likely cause and verify the output. **Resume** with a brief transition, or move to a prepared alternative if the repair is not reasonable within the available time.

Some events have technical staff or an organizer responsible for screen sharing, audio, and recordings. Do not override that person's role without authorization. If a system or accessibility issue cannot be fixed locally, ask for appropriate support and continue only within the event's permitted operating procedures.

### A final five-question delivery check

Before the next presentation, ask yourself:

1. **Purpose:** Can I state what the audience should understand or do afterward?
2. **Content:** Are the slides accurate, readable, properly sequenced, and free of inappropriate information?
3. **Output:** Have I checked the actual audience screen or meeting share, not merely my own editing window?
4. **Recovery:** Do I know how to advance, return, exit, and continue if an optional feature fails?
5. **Conclusion:** Can I end with a clear next step and stop the slideshow and any live share appropriately?

A reliable presentation does not require elaborate hardware. It requires a prepared message, a verified output, deliberate navigation, and respect for the audience.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Presentation delivery becomes easier when you can name the objects and actions involved. You do not need to memorize every menu label. Instead, learn the difference between **the deck**, **the running show**, **the speaker's controls**, **the audience output**, and **the way content is transmitted to others**. The table below summarizes terms needed for the next lesson and for practical work.

### Presentation and navigation terms

| Term | Practical meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Presentation | The planned act of communicating information | Explaining a workshop to new learners |
| Slide deck | The collection of slides supporting the talk | A six-slide orientation file |
| Edit mode | The workspace for changing slide content | Rearranging a title and checking notes |
| Slide-show mode | The interface for delivering slides | Showing the first slide on a projector |
| From Beginning | Start at the first slide in the intended show | Beginning a complete orientation |
| From Current Slide | Start at a selected slide | Rehearsing the schedule section |

### Display and presenter terms

| Term | Practical meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |

### Lesson summary

Presenting slides involves preparing the audience's experience as well as operating the software. A slide deck is created in an editing interface and delivered in a presentation interface. Starting from the beginning and from the current slide are different choices; navigation keys can advance through animation effects before changing slides; and speaker tools such as notes, timers, pointers, and annotations should support rather than distract from the message. When a projector or meeting share is involved, **verify the actual audience output**. Extended displays and Presenter View help manage separate screens, but they do not guarantee confidentiality. Test audio, captions, and media where relevant, rehearse the explanation, and maintain a simple fallback plan.

The speaker's most important responsibilities are to explain the material clearly, describe meaningful visuals, respond to questions appropriately, avoid disclosing private information, and end with a clear next action. A successful presentation is one that people can understand and use, not one that displays the greatest number of effects or shortcuts.
