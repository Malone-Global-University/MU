Beginner Practice Labs / Lab 4

## Lesson introduction

A [[presentation|presentation]] is a sequence of visual pages called [[slide|slides]] that helps a speaker explain an idea to an audience. Unlike a long document, a good short presentation uses a small amount of on-screen text, a recognizable order, readable design, and spoken explanation. The challenge is not simply placing sentences onto five pages. It is **organizing one message so the audience can follow it, saving the editable work, and checking that the slides display correctly when presented**.

This fourth Beginner Practice Lab combines skills from earlier lessons: using a folder system, entering and revising text, using a named application, saving an editable file, and testing the result. You will construct a **five-slide fictional presentation** for an imaginary event called **Community Learning Day**. You will plan the content, create title and content slides, arrange them, add optional [[speaker-notes|speaker notes]], improve readability, save and reopen the file, and run through the entire show. The scenario is invented and must be marked **Practice scenario — not a real event**.

The primary directions use **Microsoft PowerPoint on Windows 11**, but the same finished slide plan can be created using Google Slides, LibreOffice Impress, Apple Keynote, or another authorized slide editor. You do not need a paid software subscription, a printer, stock photos, online sharing, or an actual audience. If software is unavailable, a five-card paper storyboard is a valid **planning alternative**, though it does not prove the computer operations. A teacher can assess planning and software execution separately.

This lab follows [Make a Simple Budget Spreadsheet](/department/computer-science/basic-computer-skills/beginner-practice-labs/make-a-simple-budget-spreadsheet/). You can reuse the authorized practice folders established in [Create and Organize a Folder System](/department/computer-science/basic-computer-skills/beginner-practice-labs/create-and-organize-a-folder-system/). Never put real event arrangements, private names, passwords, or confidential company details into the exercise.

> **Lab objective:** Create, save, reopen, and rehearse a five-slide editable deck named `community-learning-day-practice.pptx` (or the equivalent native application file). The slides must show the exact fictional content specified below, use a consistent accessible layout, include at least two short speaker-note entries where supported, and end with a clear practice-only statement.

### Learning objectives

After completing the lab, you should be able to:

1. Explain the difference between a [[slide-deck|slide deck]], an individual slide, and a text document.
2. Identify an [[audience|audience]], an objective, a beginning-to-end message, and a suitable five-slide [[outline|outline]].
3. Create a new presentation, choose sensible [[slide-layout|slide layouts]], and fill in title and body [[placeholder|placeholders]].
4. Add, identify, move, and check slides using the [[thumbnail-pane|slide thumbnail pane]] or comparable navigator.
5. Apply a restrained [[theme|theme]] or consistent formatting, and evaluate [[contrast|contrast]], text size, reading order, and accessibility.
6. Add basic speaker notes and distinguish those notes from content displayed to the audience.
7. Save an editable original to a known destination, reopen it, and explain the difference between an original and an exported PDF.
8. Start a slide show, advance backward and forward, end the show, and rehearse without sharing unintended material.
9. Troubleshoot common slide-editing, file-location, display, and presenter-view issues without risking private files.
10. Complete six guided practice activities, an evidence-based assessment, and a 30-question knowledge check.

### What you need

- **Recommended:** A Windows 11 computer with PowerPoint or another slide-authoring application you are allowed to use; a Mac or Chromebook is also suitable with an appropriate editor.
- **Workspace:** Your own or instructor-approved folder; ideally `MGU-Practice-Files/02-Projects/Community-Event/Materials` from Lab 1, or a new `MGU-Presentation-Lab` folder that you control.
- **Material:** Only the fictional wording supplied on this page. No downloads, personal photographs, logo files, or outside research are required.
- **Time:** Work through the steps in several sessions if needed; careful saving and checking matter more than speed.
- **Optional:** A headset for private rehearsal and a second display only if you already have one and can manage its privacy settings.
- **Alternative:** Five labeled sheets of paper, a text document, or an accessible planning tool for students without an editable presentation app.

## 1. What will you create?

The deliverable is a **short, editable deck of exactly five slides**. Every slide has a particular job. Your first slide introduces the topic, the middle slides tell a small organized story, and the last slide closes it. This is a deliberately modest exercise. A five-slide presentation with clearly stated information is more successful than a twenty-slide document full of decorative effects.

### The completed slide plan

| Slide | Exact slide title | Information the audience should see | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `Community Learning Day` | `Practice scenario — not a real event` and `Five-slide orientation` | Set context and identify the exercise |
| 2 | `What We Will Practice` | `Organize basic files`; `Create simple documents`; `Ask questions and review` | State three learning goals |
| 3 | `Sample One-Hour Agenda` | `Welcome — 10 minutes`; `File practice — 20 minutes`; `Document practice — 20 minutes`; `Review — 10 minutes` | Show the fictional sequence and duration |
| 4 | `What to Bring` | `A device, if available`; `Paper for notes`; `A willingness to practice`; `Do not bring or share sensitive information` | Explain safe and simple preparation |
| 5 | `Review and Next Steps` | `Practice organizing one folder`; `Save and reopen one document`; `Review what you learned`; `Practice scenario — no event is scheduled` | Reinforce and close |

**The event is fictional.** It has no real venue, registration details, fee, host, or date. The title and wording are sample teaching material only. Keep the visible practice disclaimer on Slide 1 and Slide 5 so that exported screenshots or slides are not mistaken for an announcement.

### What the slide order communicates

Imagine that you are explaining this scenario aloud to someone who is new to computers. Slide 1 tells them what the subject is. Slide 2 answers why they are looking at it. Slide 3 describes the sequence. Slide 4 tells them how to prepare for practice. Slide 5 wraps up the message. If you reverse that order, an audience may hear next steps before knowing what the event is about.

The key skill is [[sequence|sequence]]: choosing an order that makes each new idea easier to understand because the preceding information prepared the audience for it. A good order does not need special animations. In fact, the default immediate slide change is entirely appropriate for this lab.

### Presentation, document, and handout: what is different?

| Item | Main purpose | Typical audience experience | Editable original? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Presentation deck | Support a spoken or visual sequence | One slide at a time, often full-screen | Yes, in a compatible editor |
| Word-processing document | Carry continuous detailed text | Read from top to bottom and across pages | Yes, in a compatible editor |
| PDF export | Distribute a fixed-view representation | Readers page through a saved version | Usually not the working original |
| Printed handout | Provide a physical reference | Readers can take it away or annotate it | Paper is not the editable source |
| Storyboard | Plan slides before building them | Writer sees slide ideas in sequence | Paper or digital plan only |

The [[editable-original|editable original]] is the working file you can revise in the presentation application. A [[pdf|PDF]] export is useful for reviewing or sharing a more fixed layout, but it may omit notes, embedded behavior, or editability, and slide appearance can still depend on the reader. **Never replace the editable original with only a PDF.**

### Acceptance criteria for the finished lab

To complete the software version, you must be able to demonstrate all of the following: five slides in the right order, exact fictional facts, one consistent readable visual design, at least two speaker-note entries if the application supports them, an editable file in an identified folder or authorized cloud space, a successful reopen, and one full-screen rehearsal. The final rubric separates content, file management, readability, and operational performance; no single attractive screenshot is sufficient evidence.

### Common beginner misconceptions

- Creating a presentation does not automatically make it public. Sharing requires another action or account setting.
- A new slide is not the same as a new presentation file. Adding a slide normally changes the current deck.
- Clicking outside a text placeholder does not necessarily save anything. Saving behavior depends on the application.
- Notes are not guaranteed private in all screen-sharing or export arrangements; they should never contain secrets.
- A file that looks correct in the editor may be cropped, unreadable, or out of order in slide-show mode.
- A five-slide deck does not need transitions, audio, videos, or downloaded pictures to be effective.

## 2. How do you plan the story and your workspace?

Before opening an application, define the [[message|message]] and the audience. For this lab, the audience is a fictional group of beginning computer learners. They need plain language, a comfortable pace, and reassurance that no private records are required. They do not need an elaborate sales presentation or technical vocabulary. The message is simple: **here are the learning goals, here is a sample agenda, here is what to bring, and here is what to practice afterward**.

### Step 1: Write one sentence describing the purpose

Use this model: `This presentation explains a fictional one-hour introductory computer practice session.` You can write the sentence on paper or in an ordinary text editor. It should guide what you put on the slides. If a detail does not serve that sentence, it may be unnecessary. For instance, adding an unverified venue or a made-up instructor phone number would distract from the assignment and create the risk of misleading readers.

### Step 2: Plan a beginning, middle, and ending

Break the topic into five specific functions:

1. Introduce the fictitious practice session.
2. Give exactly three simple learning goals.
3. Present four agenda blocks totaling one hour.
4. Identify simple materials and the rule against sensitive information.
5. Close with three actions and a clear practice-only reminder.

A [[storyboard|storyboard]] is a simple plan that shows each slide before the software is built. You can make five rectangles on paper and write a title and a few phrases in each. This separates thinking about the message from learning the application controls. It also makes errors easier to detect.

### Step 3: Check the agenda arithmetic

The agenda is an internally consistent example, not a real booking. Add the time blocks: **10 + 20 + 20 + 10 = 60 minutes**. The lesson tests that fact because a presentation can look professional while still being inaccurate. If you change the slide's time allocation, either update the title `Sample One-Hour Agenda` or revise the blocks to keep the total at 60 minutes.

| Agenda item | Duration | Running total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Welcome | 10 minutes | 10 minutes |
| File practice | 20 minutes | 30 minutes |
| Document practice | 20 minutes | 50 minutes |
| Review | 10 minutes | 60 minutes |

### Step 4: Choose an authorized save location

A [[storage-location|storage location]] is a device, folder, or service area that holds the file. On your own computer, you may create or reuse a folder such as `Documents/MGU-Practice-Files/02-Projects/Community-Event/Materials`. On a school or managed device, an instructor may specify a different approved location. Cloud apps often create content inside an account-controlled Drive or OneDrive area instead of a visible local `Documents` folder. Confirm which account is active, whether the material is shared, and whether you have permission to work there.

If you see two folders named `Materials`, do not assume they are the same location. Use the file manager's [[address-bar|address bar]] or [[breadcrumb|breadcrumb]] trail to find the correct parent. A clearly named file in the wrong folder can still be difficult to locate or submit.

### Step 5: Choose the naming rule

For the PowerPoint workflow, use `community-learning-day-practice.pptx` as the target filename. `.pptx` identifies a common editable PowerPoint presentation format. Other applications may use `.odp`, `.key`, or a cloud-native document that does not appear as a normal local file until you download a copy. **Do not manually append `.pptx` to a file created in another native format just to make its name look right.** Use the application’s actual Save or Export controls to select a supported format.

| File or location | What it means | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Materials` | Destination folder | Is it inside the approved workspace? |
| `community-learning-day-practice.pptx` | PowerPoint working file | Does it reopen in an editor? |
| `community-learning-day-practice.odp` | Impress or compatible working file | Does it remain editable in that app? |
| Google Slides presentation | Cloud-native file | Can you find it in the intended Drive folder? |
| `community-learning-day-practice.pdf` | Optional exported review copy | Is the editable original still retained? |
| `community-learning-day-practice-final.pptx` | Possible second named version | Is its relationship to the original understood? |

### Step 6: Check privacy before beginning

This practice topic never requires real participant names, contact details, a street address, student grades, employee records, bank information, or a real event date. Working in a cloud editor may involve autosave and synchronization with an online account. If the device is shared, verify that you are signed into the intended approved account or use a private local file if possible. A practice-only deck should remain private unless the assignment explicitly requires sharing it with an authorized instructor.

### Planning checkpoint

You are ready to start the application when you can say the audience, the purpose, the exact five slide titles, the 60-minute agenda calculation, and the save location **without guessing**. If any of those are unclear, finish the paper storyboard first. Software buttons cannot correct an unclear plan.

## 3. How do you start a presentation and add slides?

The primary route below uses PowerPoint on a Windows 11 computer. Menus may differ by version, license, language, and window size. Similar commands appear in other presentation applications; see Section 8. You can perform the actions using a mouse, keyboard, screen reader, touch controls, or other authorized access method. What matters is obtaining the intended result.

### A. Start a blank presentation

1. Open Microsoft PowerPoint using the Windows Start menu, a search result you trust, or an already approved desktop shortcut.
2. On the start screen, choose **Blank Presentation** or a comparable new blank presentation option. If a document is already open, use the application's **File → New** command.
3. Look for a large editing area containing one starter slide, a title area, and a smaller slide preview at the left or in an equivalent navigator.
4. Identify the title [[placeholder|placeholder]] and the subtitle placeholder. A placeholder is a prearranged region for content and often displays prompts such as `Click to add title`.
5. Enter `Community Learning Day` as the title. Enter `Practice scenario — not a real event` and `Five-slide orientation` as two short subtitle lines.
6. Save the new presentation immediately rather than waiting until the final slide. Saving early reduces the risk of losing your work.

The most common beginner error is to type while nothing is selected, or to type into an unintended text box. If text does not appear, click or focus the desired placeholder and try again. Do not repeatedly press keys without checking where the text is going.

### B. Save the editable file early

1. Select **File → Save As** or the Save option that allows you to choose a name and destination. Exact wording may vary.
2. If prompted to select local or online storage, choose the location you are permitted to use.
3. Navigate to the intended `Materials` folder or an approved substitute.
4. Enter `community-learning-day-practice` as the name and select the actual PowerPoint presentation type if requested. Windows or PowerPoint may add `.pptx` automatically.
5. Confirm Save and check the displayed filename in the application, then use File Explorer to verify its location when available.
6. Save again after major work milestones using the application's Save control or **Ctrl + S**, recognizing that cloud applications may autosave rather than requiring the same operation.

**Do not confuse Save As with printing or exporting.** Saving a presentation preserves its current editable content in a native or compatible file. Exporting to PDF generally produces an additional review copy. Printing produces paper or a print job. The three operations have different outcomes.

### C. Understand the normal editing view

| Region or control | Typical job | Beginner's check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slide thumbnail pane | Shows the slides and their order | Can you identify the current slide? |
| Slide canvas | Displays and edits the selected slide | Is your text in the intended place? |
| Ribbon or menu bar | Groups commands for inserting and formatting | Can you find New Slide and Layout? |
| Text placeholder | Accepts the slide's title or body information | Are you editing the correct field? |
| Notes area | Allows extra prompts for the speaker | Are notes distinct from audience-facing text? |
| Save indicator or title | Helps identify the current file | Can you state its name and storage location? |
| Slide Show or Present command | Displays slides for a viewer | Can you enter and exit safely? |

### D. Add four more slides

1. Select Slide 1 in the thumbnail pane.
2. Use **Home → New Slide** or the equivalent **New Slide** control to add Slide 2. Choose a **Title and Content** layout if it is not selected by default.
3. Type `What We Will Practice` in the slide title placeholder.
4. Repeat for Slide 3, `Sample One-Hour Agenda`; Slide 4, `What to Bring`; and Slide 5, `Review and Next Steps`.
5. Use the thumbnail pane or slide counter to count **exactly five** slides. If there are six or more, identify the extra before deleting anything.
6. If a slide has the wrong layout, select it and use **Home → Layout** to choose a compatible title-and-content design instead of deleting the whole presentation.
7. Save your work again.

The keyboard shortcut **Ctrl + M** inserts a new slide in many desktop PowerPoint configurations and in Google Slides, but command focus and software versions matter. This lesson treats the visible **New Slide** command as the primary method. In some contexts a shortcut can be intercepted or behave differently; always check the outcome rather than assuming the keystroke succeeded.

### E. Choose a simple theme

A [[theme|theme]] is an application-provided set of coordinated colors, fonts, and slide styles. Select one clean, high-contrast built-in theme if it improves readability; the default theme is acceptable. Avoid decorative photographs that compete with text, moving backgrounds, or heavily patterned borders. The assignment does not reward special effects.

For a consistent look, try to use one title style, one body-text style, and a small number of colors across all five slides. Do not change each slide's font and background independently without a reason. The [[slide-master|slide master]] or advanced theme editor is not required in this beginner lab.

### F. Recover calmly from an accidental change

If you add a slide in the wrong place, you can usually move its thumbnail to the intended position. If you change wording unintentionally, **Ctrl + Z** may undo a recent supported editing action. Undo history is application-specific; it is not a substitute for saving or version management. If you are unsure whether a slide was deleted, check the slide count and title list before taking further action.

## 4. How do you fill and organize the five slides?

You already know the titles and order. Now add the body content. Use the specified words so that you can compare your result with the answer key. You may vary simple formatting and alignment, but do not invent new event details or leave out the practice warning. The instruction to create exactly five slides is intentional: it lets learners practice both content accuracy and slide management.

### Slide 1 — title and context

**Title:** `Community Learning Day`

**Subtitle lines:**

- `Practice scenario — not a real event`
- `Five-slide orientation`

Use a title-slide layout. The main title should be the most prominent text; the subtitle should be clearly secondary but still legible. The practice disclaimer must be visible rather than hidden solely in speaker notes. If the placeholder is too small, choose a simple layout or adjust it without covering other elements.

### Slide 2 — three learning goals

**Title:** `What We Will Practice`

**Body:**

- `Organize basic files`
- `Create simple documents`
- `Ask questions and review`

Use a title-and-content layout or equivalent. Three items fit comfortably on a slide. Use the application's standard bullet option if the placeholder already formats each paragraph as a bullet. Do not type dozens of decorative punctuation marks or use long paragraphs. The slide provides an outline; your spoken explanation can add an example for each item.

### Slide 3 — the one-hour agenda

**Title:** `Sample One-Hour Agenda`

**Body:**

- `Welcome — 10 minutes`
- `File practice — 20 minutes`
- `Document practice — 20 minutes`
- `Review — 10 minutes`

Read the numbers carefully. The four parts add to **60 minutes**. You do not need to insert a table onto the slide; ordinary bullets are sufficient and often easier to read with assistive technology. If you decide to use a table, verify the headings and reading order. The lesson's Markdown table in Section 2 is a planning aid, not a requirement to create a slide table.

### Slide 4 — materials and safety

**Title:** `What to Bring`

**Body:**

- `A device, if available`
- `Paper for notes`
- `A willingness to practice`
- `Do not bring or share sensitive information`

This slide models an important lesson-design principle: a computer practice exercise should not require learners to disclose private information. Some learners will not own a personal computer or have the same accessibility tools. The wording **if available** deliberately avoids assuming everyone has a device. Make the last point easily visible and do not shrink it into tiny text.

### Slide 5 — a concise closing

**Title:** `Review and Next Steps`

**Body:**

- `Practice organizing one folder`
- `Save and reopen one document`
- `Review what you learned`
- `Practice scenario — no event is scheduled`

The closing slide turns the orientation into action. It is not a real invitation. Do not add a fictional phone number, QR code, registration link, venue, or date. Keep the closing disclaimer as visible slide text.

### Check the five-slide script before styling

| Slide | Expected body items | Critical verification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Two subtitle lines | Includes `not a real event` |
| 2 | Three learning objectives | Both files and documents are mentioned |
| 3 | Four agenda blocks | Total is exactly 60 minutes |
| 4 | Four preparation points | Device optional; no sensitive information |
| 5 | Four next steps or reminders | Includes `no event is scheduled` |

### Add short speaker notes

[[speaker-notes|Speaker notes]] are written prompts associated with a slide. They help you remember what to say without filling the audience's screen with every sentence. In PowerPoint, select a slide and locate its **Notes** area below the canvas, or use **Notes** in the interface if the pane is collapsed. Other applications have comparable presenter-note features.

For **Slide 2**, enter: `Explain that beginners will practice common tasks using fictional information.`

For **Slide 3**, enter: `Point out that 10 + 20 + 20 + 10 equals 60 minutes.`

The notes are intentionally brief. They supplement the visible slides. You may add a third note on Slide 5: `Remind viewers that the scenario is imaginary.` Notes may not remain invisible in every screen-share, recording, export, or presenter-view setup. **Never write passwords or confidential material in speaker notes.**

### Reorder a slide safely

To practice the difference between slide content and slide order, find Slide 4 in the thumbnail pane. On a copy of the exercise or with a known safe way back, move it temporarily before Slide 3, observe that the outline becomes less logical, and move it back. The final sequence must remain **1 title, 2 goals, 3 agenda, 4 preparation, 5 closing**. You may use drag and drop, a Cut/Paste workflow available in your editor, a Move Up/Down control, or an accessible slide-reordering option. Observe the results in the thumbnail pane; do not rely on a single gesture.

### Keep the message short enough to speak

A slide is not a page of a report. If you find yourself explaining every point with a paragraph on screen, consider moving the explanation to notes or spoken comments. Each slide should carry the few words the audience needs to see, while your speech supplies transitions and examples. For this lab, use the exact body wording; do not expand it into a multi-paragraph script on the slide.

## 5. How do you make the slides clear and accessible?

A presentation should be readable by people viewing a projector, laptop screen, classroom monitor, or screen-share window. It should also be usable with assistive technology where supported. [[accessibility|Accessibility]] is not an optional artistic flourish. It is part of whether the audience can receive the information accurately.

### Start with readable text and straightforward layouts

For this beginner exercise, prefer titles around **32–40 points** and body text around **24–28 points** when the slide size and available room permit. These are working suggestions, not universal legal thresholds. Use a clear sans-serif typeface and inspect the result at ordinary viewing size. If the final safety statement overflows at these sizes, adjust the layout, spacing, or wording placement rather than blindly reducing text to a tiny size.

### Use strong contrast

[[contrast|Contrast]] means that text is visually distinguishable from its background. Dark lettering on a light background, or light lettering on a dark background, is usually easier to read than pale gray text over a busy photo. Color should help organize the message, not be the only way to convey it. For example, if you use color to emphasize `Review`, include the word `Review` rather than expecting viewers to infer its meaning from a green shape.

### Give every slide a meaningful title

Slide titles help the audience understand transitions and help screen-reader users navigate presentation structure. This lab already gives each slide a unique descriptive title. Do not replace all five titles with repeated text such as `Slide` or `Information`. Keep the title in a title placeholder where practical so an application can identify it structurally as a slide title.

### Favor built-in layouts and logical reading order

A [[reading-order|reading order]] is the sequence in which assistive technology encounters slide elements. Some slide editors use the order in which text boxes and shapes were inserted, which may differ from their visible placement. Built-in title-and-content layouts reduce the risk of confusing order. When available, run the application's [[accessibility-checker|Accessibility Checker]] and review any issues; it can identify many problems but cannot replace human review.

### Images are optional, not required

No slide needs an external picture. If you add an optional meaningful visual using a source you own or are allowed to use, provide [[alt-text|alternative text]] that communicates its relevant purpose. Decorative items may be marked decorative where supported. Do not copy images from random web pages without checking usage rights, and avoid images that reveal people, locations, or sensitive details. If you cannot describe why an image improves understanding, leave it out.

### Transitions and animations should not interfere

A [[transition|transition]] changes how one slide gives way to another. An [[animation|animation]] changes how an item appears or moves within a slide. Neither is required here. Many elaborate effects can distract the audience, cause discomfort for people sensitive to motion, or make a deck behave unpredictably. Prefer an immediate or gentle transition and no unnecessary automatic advancement. You must be able to control the pace manually during rehearsal.

### Accessibility and readability audit

| Check | Passing example | Corrective action if it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title structure | Five unique visible titles | Restore titles in standard placeholders |
| Text size | All body text remains readable in slide show | Simplify content or adjust layout |
| Contrast | Wording is clear against its background | Select a less busy background and stronger colors |
| Information conveyed by text | Safety rule is written, not signaled only by red | Add a clear text label |
| Reading order | Title is read before body content | Use an accessible layout or inspect order controls |
| Visual descriptions | Every informative optional image has meaningful alt text | Add descriptive alt text or remove the image |
| Animation | Slides advance on purpose and without distracting motion | Disable automatic or excessive effects |
| Private content | No genuine personal details or secrets | Remove data and review any copies or sharing links |
| Practice disclaimer | Slide 1 and Slide 5 both mark the exercise fictional | Restore missing disclaimer wording |

### Test an actual viewing condition

In editing view, large thumbnails or a zoomed canvas can make text look more readable than it will at the intended display size. Enter Slide Show mode and inspect the whole screen. Can you read the final line on Slide 4? Does the title cover any other text? Are the bullets consistent? Does the slide order still make sense from start to finish? Return to editing view to correct issues, save, and retest. **Accessibility checking is a repeated quality-control operation, not merely a final button press.**

### A practical accessibility accommodation

If dragging thumbnails is difficult, use a keyboard, screen reader, or menu command supported by the editor. If small text is difficult to see, increase application zoom during editing, use an approved magnifier, and evaluate the final size for the audience separately. If physical speaking is difficult, a learner can demonstrate the presentation by navigating the slides and explaining their intent in writing or another accessible form. Assess the same learning objective without demanding one physical method.

## 6. How do you save, reopen, and rehearse the show?

A complete presentation lab requires verification. The slides must continue to exist after the editing window closes, remain editable, and appear in the intended order when presented. A successful save is more than seeing the words on the screen. It means that the right version of the work is stored in a location you can identify and recover.

### Save, close, and reopen the editable original

1. Finish entering content and saving the notes.
2. Select the application's Save command or verify that autosave has completed. Do not assume a cloud sync icon means a file is available offline.
3. Read the current document name. Confirm `community-learning-day-practice.pptx` or the actual native/cloud presentation title.
4. Close the presentation using the normal application controls. If asked about unsaved changes, read the prompt carefully and save the authorized work.
5. Open File Explorer, Finder, the relevant cloud drive, or the application's **Open** dialog and navigate to the original location.
6. Open the saved presentation again, preferably through the actual stored file rather than an unrelated recent-file shortcut.
7. Check all five slide titles, one body item from Slide 3, the Slide 4 safety statement, the Slide 5 disclaimer, and the two speaker notes if supported.
8. Make a small safe revision such as correcting a deliberately introduced punctuation typo; save, reopen once more if necessary, and confirm that the revised content persists.

For online editors such as Google Slides, the document may autosave in the signed-in account. Confirm the intended Drive folder, document title, account, and status; do not look for a local `.pptx` unless you deliberately downloaded or exported one. On desktop PowerPoint, a local `.pptx` is usually the editable source. PowerPoint files saved in OneDrive may use cloud synchronization and version history, depending on account configuration.

### Rehearse in slide-show mode

1. Select **Slide Show → From Beginning** in desktop PowerPoint or the corresponding Present/Slideshow command.
2. Check that Slide 1 appears full-screen with both practice lines readable.
3. Advance to Slide 2 using the keyboard Right Arrow, Space bar, click, or an accessible control supported by the app.
4. Continue to Slide 3 and read the four agenda lines. Explain aloud or in writing that the total is 60 minutes.
5. Continue to Slide 4; check that the sensitivity warning remains clearly visible.
6. Continue to Slide 5; verify the closing and practice-only statement.
7. Move back one slide and forward again to demonstrate navigation.
8. Press **Esc** or select the application's end-show command to exit, then verify you are back in the expected editing environment.

PowerPoint also commonly uses **F5** for a show from the beginning and **Shift + F5** for a show from the current slide, but laptop function-key settings and application variants can affect these shortcuts. Use the visible command when unsure. Google Slides provides a **Slideshow** command and generally uses **Esc** to exit full-screen viewing. **Do not accidentally share your entire desktop** when you only intended a private rehearsal.

### Presenter View is helpful but not guaranteed

[[presenter-view|Presenter View]] is a viewing arrangement that can display notes, timing, and a next-slide preview for the speaker while presenting audience slides separately. In PowerPoint desktop, the Slide Show controls include an option for it. Google Slides also offers a presenter view with notes. Availability and privacy behavior vary by software, browser, connected displays, and meeting platform. Do not claim that notes are hidden merely because a setting is named Presenter View. Test the exact output destination before any real screen share.

### Rehearsal versus public delivery

A [[rehearsal|rehearsal]] checks timing, legibility, sequence, and navigation. It does not require a public audience. In a real meeting, the presenter must also confirm that the correct display is being transmitted and any private notifications, tabs, or speaker-only windows are not exposed. For this lab, a local private full-screen rehearsal is sufficient. Do not send the fictional presentation to a public mailing list or upload it to a public website.

## 7. Guided practice: create, check, and present

Complete these six activities in order. They form one cumulative project rather than six unrelated tasks. The answer disclosures describe what a successful result looks like; they do not create the presentation for you. You may use a digital deck, a supervised classroom computer, or the paper alternatives described below. Do not submit any genuine personal information.

### Activity 1 — Plan the five-slide storyboard

**Goal:** Turn the fictional scenario into a clear beginning, middle, and ending before using the presentation editor.

3. Make five numbered boxes on paper or in an authorized text document.
4. Enter the exact title of each planned slide from Section 1 in its matching box.
5. Under each title, list the number of body items that slide requires: two subtitle lines, three goals, four agenda entries, four preparation points, and four closing points.
6. Calculate the total duration of the four agenda blocks.
7. Write a reminder that the presentation does not advertise a real event.
8. **Evidence:** A five-part outline with the correct order, intended audience, and arithmetic.

:::answer Show Activity 1 solution
**Expected result:** The storyboard lists `Community Learning Day`, `What We Will Practice`, `Sample One-Hour Agenda`, `What to Bring`, and `Review and Next Steps` in that order. ## 8. How do you adapt the lab and resolve problems?

The goal is not to memorize one brand's toolbar. It is to make a presentation that can be edited, found, checked, and presented. The main concepts—slides, titles, body items, order, design, saving, and rehearsal—apply across many editors. Menu names, account requirements, offline options, note support, and file formats differ. Use the version you are authorized to access rather than signing up for a paid subscription solely for the exercise.

### Equivalent workflow in other common applications

| Application | Start and organize slides | Save or store | Present and exit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PowerPoint for Windows or Mac | New presentation, New Slide, Layout, thumbnails | Save or Save As `.pptx` to selected location | Slide Show / From Beginning, then Esc |
| PowerPoint for the web | New blank presentation; choose layouts and slide list | Cloud file in an approved OneDrive location; download supported copy if needed | Slide Show; commands vary by version |
| Google Slides | Blank presentation; add slides with plus or Slide menu; reorder in left filmstrip | Automatically saves to intended Google Drive account; rename and move to correct folder | Slideshow, next/previous controls, Esc |
| LibreOffice Impress | New presentation; add slides and choose layouts in slide workspace | Save editable `.odp` or compatible supported format | Slide Show controls, exit with Esc where supported |
| Apple Keynote on Mac | New presentation from theme; add slides in navigator | Save named `.key` in chosen location | Play, navigate, exit using app controls |
| Supported mobile presentation editor | Use new-presentation and add-slide tools | Native cloud/app storage or available export | Use the app's Play/Present mode and close/exit control |

**Google Slides note:** The presentation generally exists in Google Drive, not as a local `.pptx` unless downloaded. Its menu may show **Slideshow** at the top right. In a supported computer browser, the Google presenter interface can expose separate speaker notes. The exact commands depend on the account and browser. Do not assume that simply leaving a Google Slides tab open proves the file has been placed in the correct Drive folder.

**PowerPoint for the web note:** Its feature set is not identical to desktop PowerPoint; some presenter tools and export choices depend on the app version. If you cannot find Presenter View, it is acceptable to rehearse with ordinary Slide Show mode, as long as you clearly record the limitation and do not claim a feature was tested.

**Keynote and Impress note:** A native `.key` or `.odp` file can be the valid working original if the teacher accepts the format. Changing the filename extension to `.pptx` is not a conversion. When a PowerPoint copy is needed, use a supported Export/Save As route and inspect the converted version for formatting differences before submitting it.

### Fix one issue at a time

A calm troubleshooting approach is: identify the symptom; check what is selected or open; choose the smallest reversible correction; inspect the result; save if appropriate; then attempt the task again. Avoid repeatedly clicking Delete or replacing a file just because the application looks unfamiliar.

| Problem | Likely explanation | Safe next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typing does nothing | No editable text placeholder is focused | Select the title or content placeholder before typing |
| Text appears in the wrong place | Wrong placeholder or slide selected | Undo if appropriate, choose correct slide, re-enter |
| New Slide is unavailable | App is in Slide Show, read-only mode, or restricted account | Exit show; check editing permission and view |
| Six slides appear | An extra slide was inserted | Identify the extra by title before removing it |
| Slide 4 appears before Slide 3 | Slide order changed | Reorder thumbnails or use an accessible move command |
| Bullets become tiny | Too much text or a restrictive layout | Simplify content and choose a clearer layout |
| Text disappears off-screen | Object extends beyond slide boundary | Adjust size, placement, or placeholder layout |
| Colors look washed out | Low contrast or another display appearance | Use a simpler high-contrast theme and retest |
| Notes are missing | Notes area hidden, app lacks feature, or notes saved elsewhere | Check Notes control and supported version |
| Slide Show appears on wrong display | Multi-monitor settings selected unexpected output | Stop show; review monitor/display selection privately |
| Audience can see speaker notes | Wrong window or screen is shared | Stop sharing immediately; verify correct display setup |
| File is not in Documents | It was saved to cloud or another folder | Find the named file and inspect storage location |
| Save failed | Permission, storage, or connectivity issue | Read prompt; use approved writable destination; retry |
| PDF does not allow editing | Export is not the source deck | Reopen the native presentation instead |
| Images or fonts change after export | Conversion or missing resources | Inspect exported copy and keep a compatible editable original |
| Collaboration controls are confusing | Cloud permissions and link access are separate settings | Do not share until you understand who can view or edit |

### Stop conditions and permission warnings

If the application displays a permission denial, payment request, unexpected account prompt, security warning, or invitation to open files belonging to someone else, stop that action. Seek an approved alternative editor or location. Do not turn off organizational protections, install an untrusted plugin, borrow another person's login, or accept a public sharing configuration just to complete a training assignment.

### When the app crashes or a file is missing

Do not assume the most recent version is gone or that autosave successfully stored it. First check the application's recovery or recent-files interface; then inspect the actual save location and cloud status if relevant. Prefer reopening the last confirmed saved version. If the file is an older copy, update only the harmless practice content and save again. Avoid merging unknown or unrelated presentations. If the original cannot be recovered, describe which step failed and recreate the practice deck from the storyboard; the five-slide template reduces the cost of recovering.

### What a screenshot proves—and does not prove

An image of Slide 1 can show that the title looks right at a particular moment. It cannot prove there are five slides, that the file is editable, that the complete presentation was saved, that it remains in the intended folder, or that the full-screen show works. Evidence must match the claim. For this lab, a saved editable file plus a live or supervised check is stronger than a screenshot alone.

### Accessible and offline alternatives

If there is no slide editor available, create a five-page storyboard in a word processor or on paper. The student should write the same titles, body items, order, and a spoken script for the agenda. They can still demonstrate audience awareness, sequence, safety, arithmetic, and design reasoning. Record that digital file saving, slide navigation, and actual presentation mode remain **not assessed**. Later, the same plan can be transferred to a compatible app without inventing different content. A qualified instructor can authorize equivalent assistive technology or physical methods when they measure the same skill.

### Instructor practice scenarios

**Scenario A: The polished but misleading deck.** A learner uses elegant colors but forgets the practice-only disclaimer and adds a fictitious event address. The correct decision is to restore the disclaimer and remove the misleading address; visual quality does not override accuracy.

**Scenario B: The correct deck saved in the wrong place.** A learner finishes all slides but cannot find them under the assigned folder. The correct decision is to identify the actual file location and then save or move a verified editable copy into the approved destination—not to pretend an open Recent tab confirms compliance.

**Scenario C: The edited PDF.** A learner has only an exported PDF and cannot change Slide 3's agenda. The correct decision is to locate the native working deck. Export and original have different purposes.

**Scenario D: Presenter notes revealed.** A learner shares the desktop and realizes the notes pane is visible. The correct decision is to stop screen-sharing, verify display and window selection, and restart only after confirming the audience view. The notes must never contain real secrets.

**Scenario E: The unreadable final line.** Slide 4's safety statement falls below the bottom of the screen. The learner should adjust the layout and retest in Slide Show mode rather than claim the slide is readable because the text exists in the edit canvas.

**Scenario F: Different editor, same objective.** A learner using LibreOffice Impress saves an `.odp` deck and shows all required slides. The proper assessment checks the same content and behavior. A native `.odp` file is not automatically a failure when the software choice was authorized.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The glossary below defines the main terms used in the lab. Selected words in the instructional text are marked as interactive [[vocabulary-tooltip|vocabulary tooltips]]. The full definitions remain readable here even if popovers are unavailable. Learning the vocabulary should help you explain **what** you did, **where** the file is, and **how** you checked the result—not simply recognize an application icon.

- **Presentation:** A planned sequence of slides used to communicate a message visually or with spoken explanation.
- **Slide:** One visual page in a presentation deck.
- **Slide deck:** The complete collection of slides within one presentation.
- **Slide number:** A slide's position in the presentation sequence.
- **Slide title:** Descriptive text that identifies the subject of one slide.
- **Slide layout:** An arrangement of title and content areas on a slide.
- **Placeholder:** An editable prepared area for content such as a slide title or body text.
- **Slide canvas:** Main editing area where a selected slide's content is displayed.
- **Thumbnail:** Small preview image used to identify or select a slide.
- **Thumbnail pane:** Area showing small slide previews in presentation order.
- **Slide navigator:** Control used to select and arrange slides in a deck.
- **New Slide command:** Editor control that adds another slide to the presentation.
- **Slide Sorter:** View that displays several slide previews for comparing their order.
- **Slide master:** Central design structure controlling many recurring slide elements in supporting editors.
- **Theme:** Coordinated built-in colors, fonts, and styles applied to slides.
- **Template:** Prebuilt starting document or design with reusable structure.
- **Formatting:** Changes to the appearance and presentation of content.
- **Font:** Style of lettering used to display text.
- **Font size:** Measurement of how large letters appear in a document or slide.
- **Contrast:** Visual difference between foreground elements and their background.
- **White space:** Uncrowded area that helps separate visual elements.
- **Alignment:** Position of text or objects relative to a reference edge or center.
- **Bullet:** Mark used to introduce an item in a concise list.
- **Body text:** Main explanatory words below a slide's title.
- **Text box:** Movable area designed to hold editable text.
- **Outline:** Organized plan showing topics and the order of material.
- **Storyboard:** Sketch or written plan of the content and sequence of slides.
- **Sequence:** Order in which slides or ideas are arranged.
- **Audience:** People who will view or receive a presentation.
- **Message:** Central idea or purpose a presentation should communicate.
- **Objective:** Intended result of an activity or communication.
- **Agenda:** Ordered list of topics or activities, sometimes with durations.
- **Duration:** Amount of time an activity or presentation takes.
- **Fictional scenario:** Invented situation used for safe practice rather than a real scheduled activity.
- **Practice disclaimer:** Visible statement that a scenario or communication is fictional.
- **Speaker notes:** Presenter-facing written prompts associated with particular slides.
- **Notes pane:** Editor area where speaker notes are entered or read.
- **Presenter View:** Presentation display that can show speaking controls or notes separately from an audience view.
- **Slide show:** Mode used to display slides for an audience rather than edit their content.
- **Rehearsal:** Practice run used to check order, delivery, readability, and timing.
- **Navigation:** Movement among slides or interface areas.
- **Transition:** Visual effect controlling how one slide changes to the next.
- **Animation:** Visual effect changing how an object appears or moves within a slide.
- **Automatic advance:** Feature that switches slides without a manual action, often after a set time.
- **Screen share:** Feature that broadcasts a chosen screen or application window to other people.
- **Display output:** Screen, projector, or other destination showing presentation visuals.
- **Accessibility:** Design and operation that support people with differing abilities and assistive technologies.
- **Reading order:** Sequence in which assistive technology presents slide content.
- **Accessibility Checker:** Software review tool that identifies some possible barriers to access.
- **Alternative text:** Short text description communicating the meaning of a non-text visual.
- **Screen reader:** Assistive software that announces text and supported interface structure.
- **Keyboard focus:** Interface location that receives keyboard input at a given moment.
- **Keyboard shortcut:** Key combination used to invoke an application command.
- **Undo:** Command that reverses a recent supported editing action.
- **Redo:** Command that reapplies an action after undo where supported.
- **Save:** Store the current state of an editable file at a chosen location.
- **Save As:** Command that can specify a new name, destination, or format for a saved file.
- **AutoSave:** Application behavior that automatically records changes under supported conditions.
- **Synchronization:** Coordinating data changes between device or online copies.
- **Sync status:** Indication that an online or local file is current, processing, or in error.
- **Editable original:** Primary working presentation file that can be modified in a compatible editor.
- **Export:** Produce an additional representation of a working file in another format.
- **PDF:** Portable document format commonly used to preserve a document's general page appearance.
- **PPTX:** Common PowerPoint presentation file format that supports editable slides.
- **ODP:** OpenDocument Presentation format used by LibreOffice and compatible applications.
- **Keynote file:** Native Apple Keynote presentation document, often using the .key filename suffix.
- **Cloud-native file:** Document stored as an online app's native object rather than necessarily as a local file.
- **File extension:** Suffix of a filename commonly associated with a file format.
- **Filename:** Name that identifies one stored file.
- **File path:** Sequence of folders and a name identifying a file's storage location.
- **Storage location:** Drive, folder, device, or cloud area that holds files.
- **Folder:** Container used to organize files and other folders.
- **Address bar:** Interface field showing or accepting a location or path.
- **Breadcrumb:** Series of folder labels showing the path to the current location.
- **File Explorer:** Windows program for finding and managing files and folders.
- **Finder:** macOS program for locating and managing files and folders.
- **Permission:** Rule controlling whether an account can view, create, change, or share a file.
- **Read-only:** State in which a file can normally be viewed but not modified.
- **Share link:** URL providing access to an online file under specific permissions.
- **Version:** Particular saved state of a file at a point in its change history.
- **Backup:** Separate recoverable copy retained for accidental loss or damage.
- **Recovery:** Process of restoring work from a saved, temporary, or recoverable state.
- **Print Preview:** View showing how content is expected to appear on printed pages.
- **Handout:** Printed or exported reference based on presentation slides or notes.
- **Verification:** Checking whether a performed action produced the intended result.
- **Audit:** Systematic comparison of work with stated criteria.
- **Rubric:** Set of criteria used to judge the completeness or quality of work.
- **Evidence:** Observable file, demonstration, or record supporting a claimed result.
- **Practice workspace:** Approved low-risk area used to perform learning activities safely.
- **Vocabulary tooltip:** Short popover explaining a marked term in a lesson.

## 11. Completion review and further learning

A completed Beginner Practice Lab shows **an outcome and a method for verifying it**. The outcome here is a five-slide fictional deck. The method is a repeatable workflow: plan, create, enter content, improve clarity, save, reopen, rehearse, and inspect the final output. A presentation is not complete solely because the editor shows five slide thumbnails. If the file cannot be found or the text is unreadable when shown, the work still needs attention.

### Final quality-control checklist

1. I can explain the audience and the purpose of the fictional deck.
2. I created exactly **five slides**, in the required order.
3. All five slide titles match the specified outline.
4. Slide 1 explicitly says `Practice scenario — not a real event`.
5. Slide 2 has three learning goals, not unrelated content.
6. Slide 3 lists four agenda blocks totaling exactly **60 minutes**.
7. Slide 4 states that a device is optional and sensitive information must not be brought or shared.
8. Slide 5 ends with `Practice scenario — no event is scheduled`.
9. The title, body text, and design are consistent and readable in slide-show view.
10. A meaningful image has alternative text if I added one; otherwise, I did not add unnecessary pictures.
11. The slide structure has a sensible reading order, not merely visual placement.
12. Slides 2 and 3 have safe speaker notes where the editor supports them, or I documented a limitation.
13. I saved the **editable original** in an approved named location and can reopen it.
14. I did not mistake a PDF, screenshot, or printed handout for the native working file.
15. I can start a slide show, move forward and backward, and exit it.
16. The deck contains no actual private records, contact details, account passwords, or real event claims.
17. I can identify at least one improvement made after testing the result.
18. I can explain how the same process applies to another slide-authoring application.
