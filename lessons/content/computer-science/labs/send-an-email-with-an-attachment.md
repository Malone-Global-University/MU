Beginner Practice Labs / Lab 5

## Lesson introduction

An [[email|email]] can carry a short written message, but many everyday tasks require you to send a document as well. You might submit a completed application, share a class assignment, or send an informational handout. The document is an [[attachment|attachment]]: a file associated with the message that the recipient can usually open, [[download|download]], or save, subject to their software and permissions. In this lab, you will practice both parts of the task: **writing a clear email and attaching the correct file before you send it**.

This is the fifth MGU Beginner Practice Lab. It builds on earlier exercises in creating folders, writing and saving documents, and managing files. You will create one tiny, harmless text file, compose a professional practice message, attach the file, check the recipient and attachment, and—only when authorized—send it to **your own email address** or a specifically approved instructor practice address. You will then inspect the sent message and, when available, the received copy. The exercise is designed so that no stranger, employer, business, or other outside party needs to receive anything.

The primary walkthrough uses **Gmail in a desktop web browser**, followed by equivalent **Outlook on the web** directions. Mac, mobile, and other email application alternatives appear later. The names of buttons can vary with software version and screen size. The principles stay the same: choose the authorized recipient, attach the intended file, verify it appears on the draft, and send only after reviewing the complete message.

If you lack an [[email-account|email account]], cannot use one under your institution's policies, or do not have permission to send messages, **stop after the saved draft or simulated message**. A paper or offline mockup can assess planning, but it cannot demonstrate an actual email delivery. You do not need to register a new email account for this lab, and you should not enter your password into the lesson dashboard.

> **Lab objective:** Produce a draft or authorized self-addressed email with the subject `MGU Practice Lab 5 - Attachment Test`, a short professional body, and exactly one harmless file named `mgu-practice-attachment.txt`. Verify that the chosen file is present before sending. If sending is permitted, confirm the message appears in Sent and inspect the received message when delivery succeeds.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Identify the sender, [[recipient|recipient]], subject, message body, and attached file in an email compose window.
2. Create a small text file and distinguish the document's filename from the message's subject.
3. Enter a valid, approved recipient without guessing an address or accidentally adding an unintended person.
4. Compose a courteous, concise message appropriate for a routine file transfer.
5. Use an email app's Attach command to select the correct file and identify an [[attachment-chip|attachment indicator]] in the draft.
6. Distinguish an attached copy from a [[cloud-link|cloud-sharing link]], an [[inline-image|inline image]], and text pasted into the body.
7. Use a pre-send checklist to check the recipient, subject, body, file, file type, size, and privacy.
8. Explain what Drafts, Outbox, Sent, delivery, and a received message do—and do not—prove.
9. Troubleshoot common problems such as a missing attachment, an unexpected cloud link, a large file, or a recipient typo.
10. Complete six practice activities, a practical review rubric, and a 30-question knowledge check.

### What you need

- **Recommended device:** Windows 11 computer with a current browser; a Mac, Chromebook, tablet, or smartphone may also work.
- **Account:** A personal email account you are authorized to use, such as Gmail or Outlook; no account is required for the offline planning alternative.
- **Safe destination:** Your own email address. If your instructor designates a controlled training address, use it only after confirming permission and the exact address.
- **File workspace:** An existing personal practice folder from Lab 1 or another writable folder you own. Do not use work or school confidential directories for this exercise.
- **Text editor:** Notepad on Windows, TextEdit in plain-text mode on Mac, or another program that can save a `.txt` file.
- **Content:** Only the fictitious practice statements provided below. No private details, photographs, identity documents, account numbers, or actual academic records.
- **Accessibility:** Keyboard navigation or assistive technology may be used; evaluated outcomes matter more than input method.

## 1. What does a correct practice email look like?

A successful email exercise has two deliverables. The first is the small file you create on the computer. The second is a composed message that references that file. If sending is authorized, there is a third evidence step: verifying that the message was sent and that the attachment is present in the message displayed in Sent or the recipient's Inbox.

### The model message

Use this example as the target for the exercise. **Replace the To field with your own address; do not send to the literal placeholder text.**

| Email field | Lab requirement | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| From | Your authorized practice email account | You recognize the signed-in account |
| To | Your own confirmed email address | No typo, extra recipient, or unintended autocomplete selection |
| Cc | Leave empty | No unintended visible copies |
| Bcc | Leave empty | No unintended hidden copies |
| Subject | `MGU Practice Lab 5 - Attachment Test` | Identifies the practice task |
| Body | Exact short example below | Explains the attachment politely |
| Attachment | `mgu-practice-attachment.txt` | Correct name and file visible in the draft |
| Action | Save draft or send to yourself if authorized | Do not send to a stranger or invented address |

**Sample message body:**

> Hello,
>
> I am completing a computer-skills practice exercise. I have attached a text file named `mgu-practice-attachment.txt`. This message and its attachment contain only fictional practice material.
>
> Thank you.
>
> MGU Practice Student

You may use this fictional sign-off rather than a real name. Do not invent a recipient's personal name. This exercise has no deadline, real event, payment request, or business purpose. The subject is not itself a file; it is a label that helps recipients recognize the message.

### The file being attached

The file should contain the following four lines. Its exact punctuation is not important, but its name and purpose should be clear.

```text
MGU Beginner Practice Labs
Lab 5: Email Attachment Test
This is fictional practice material.
No personal or confidential information is included.
```

You will save these lines as `mgu-practice-attachment.txt` in a known folder, then select that saved file using the email program's file chooser. If your editor adds its own file extension, make sure the end result is not accidentally `mgu-practice-attachment.txt.txt`. A true [[plain-text|plain-text]] file is appropriate here because it is small and can be opened by many programs. Do not attach a real resume, ID, photo collection, password list, or financial spreadsheet to prove the concept.

### What counts as evidence?

| Evidence | What it shows | What it cannot prove alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Saved `.txt` file in your practice folder | The file exists and can be opened | That it was attached to an email |
| Draft showing filename and recipient | The message was prepared and an attachment indicator appears | That it has been delivered |
| Message shown in Sent | The mail application has a record of sending | That the person read or opened the attachment |
| Received copy with the correct file | The self-addressed delivery was received and the file is accessible in that mailbox | That all future emails will deliver successfully |
| Written screenshot-free observation record | Learner can explain the workflow | That computer operations occurred without a demonstration |

An [[attachment-indicator|attachment indicator]] is useful evidence, but clicking or reviewing it before sending helps confirm you attached the intended file rather than another similarly named document. Some applications display attachments differently, including as thumbnails or named chips; that difference does not change the verification requirement.

### Why this lab uses a self-addressed message

Email can reach people very quickly, and some services transmit a message before you realize that the address is wrong. A [[self-send|self-addressed]] training message prevents unnecessary contact with real strangers. Enter your own address carefully, especially if the compose interface suggests contacts when you type. **Never choose an unfamiliar autocomplete suggestion just because it appears first.**

A self-test also makes it possible to inspect both the sender's view and the recipient's view, often in the same mailbox. Even if sending to yourself works, the lesson does not claim that another person's inbox will always receive your future messages: spam filters, attachment rules, address errors, and delivery delays differ among systems.

## 2. How do you prepare the file and safe workspace?

Attaching a file begins before you open an email application. If you do not know where the file is saved, it can be difficult to find the intended item in a file-selection window. The first step is to prepare a harmless file in a location you understand and can access.

### Step 1: Choose your practice folder

Open [[file-explorer|File Explorer]] on Windows by selecting its folder icon or using **Windows key + E**. Go to the folder you created for earlier MGU lessons or create an authorized folder called `MGU-Email-Lab`. If the folder is part of OneDrive or another synchronized system, check whether that is permitted; this exercise does not require cloud storage.

If you cannot find your earlier folder, use Documents or another personal location that is writable. The important skill is to **name the destination** rather than assume the file will appear automatically in a convenient place. A typical example, not a required literal path, is:

```text
Documents
  MGU-Practice-Files
    Email-Lab
      mgu-practice-attachment.txt
```

Your actual folders can differ. Do not alter folders inside Windows, Program Files, another user's home directory, a company shared drive, or an unfamiliar synchronized workspace to complete a beginner exercise.

### Step 2: Create the text file in Windows 11

1. Open **Notepad** from Start or the Windows search interface.
2. Enter the four fictional lines provided in Section 1; check spelling before saving.
3. Choose **File > Save As**, or the appropriate Save command if the file has never been saved.
4. Navigate deliberately to your chosen `Email-Lab` folder.
5. Enter the exact name `mgu-practice-attachment.txt`. When the application offers [[file-type|file-type]] or encoding options, choose plain text and UTF-8 where supported.
6. Save. If a file with that name already exists, do not overwrite an unrelated file. Inspect it, rename the new practice file, or use a new empty authorized folder; update your attachment plan consistently.
7. Return to File Explorer and confirm the saved file appears in the intended location.
8. Open the file and verify that the four lines are readable. Close it when finished; do not delete it yet.

**Notepad variations:** The Save As dialog and file-type selection may look different by Windows edition. Some configurations show or hide file extensions. If the name appears with two `.txt` suffixes, examine the full filename using File Explorer's View options or file Properties before changing it. Changing letters in a filename is not the same as changing a file's actual content format.

### Step 3: Check the file is harmless and small

Right-click the practice file and choose **Properties**, or use a comparable information command. It should be a very small [[txt-file|text document]], not a program or an archive. The exact number of bytes can vary with line endings and encoding. **Do not require an exact byte count** as proof. The accepted result is a readable plain-text file with the intended content.

| Check | Expected observation | Unsafe or incorrect alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | `mgu-practice-attachment.txt` | Similar title hiding a different file |
| Content | Four fictional lines | Real account, medical, business, or identification data |
| Location | Your approved practice folder | System, employer, or another person's private directory |
| Type | Readable text file | Executable program or unknown compressed file |
| File size | Very small | Unexpectedly large item requiring investigation |
| Access | Opens without special permissions | You cannot view the content you intend to send |

### Step 4: Keep the source file until verification finishes

When you select the local text file as a normal email attachment, the program generally uploads or incorporates a copy of that file into the outgoing message. The source file usually remains in its [[folder|folder]]. Do not delete the source before you have checked your work. In a [[cloud-storage|cloud]] sharing flow, however, the recipient may be given a [[cloud-link|link]] to the original hosted file; moving, deleting, or changing its permissions can affect access. You will compare these two approaches in Section 5.

### An accessibility alternative

Students who use speech recognition, a screen reader, switch control, or keyboard-only navigation may prepare the same text by any accessible method. A [[file-chooser|file chooser]] may announce the selected filename rather than display a mouse-friendly chip. The learner can demonstrate understanding by naming the selected file, reporting its location, and verifying it in the outgoing draft. Printed practice directions and a plain-text transcript are acceptable aids.

:::answer Show the file-preparation checkpoint
**Expected result:** The learner can identify an authorized folder, locate a readable file called `mgu-practice-attachment.txt`, state why its contents are safe, and reopen it to confirm the four fictional lines. If the text file cannot be created, describe the intended filename and path for an offline alternative and make clear that no live attachment was performed.
:::

## 3. What belongs in each email field?

A [[compose-window|compose window]] combines the addressing, descriptive text, and attached items of one outgoing message. Beginners often focus on the writing area and overlook the addressing area. In practical communication, the recipient and attachment are as important as the message body.

### The From and To fields

**From** identifies the sending account shown by your email software. Depending on your setup, you may have more than one permitted sending address or an organization-managed alias. Choose an account you are authorized to use. **To** identifies the primary recipient. For this exercise, that must be your own confirmed email address or the exact training address your instructor has explicitly approved.

An [[email-address|email address]] commonly has a local part, the `@` symbol, and a domain. Addresses often look like `name@example.com`, but `name@example.com` is an **illustration only**, not a recipient for this lab. Never submit a fictional demonstration address to test real delivery. The format alone also does not prove that an address belongs to the intended person.

### Cc, Bcc, and Reply-to

**[[cc|Cc]]** lets other people receive a visible copy of a message. **[[bcc|Bcc]]** lets additional people receive a copy without displaying their addresses to ordinary recipients. These features are not needed here; leave both fields blank. Bcc does not guarantee absolute anonymity or confidentiality, and its exact visibility in mail systems can be more complicated than a beginner needs to manage. **[[reply-to|Reply-to]]**, when present, controls where replies may be directed. Do not change advanced addressing settings for this lab.

### The subject and body

The [[subject-line|subject line]] is a short label that helps someone decide which message to open and how to find it later. Use the assigned subject exactly. Avoid an empty subject, all-uppercase alarm language, misleading urgency, or unrelated conversation threads. The [[message-body|message body]] gives the context: what is attached and why the recipient is receiving it. In real messages, an informative body can prevent confusion even if the attachment is temporarily unavailable.

Keep the writing courteous and concise. Do not write, for example, `see attached` without explaining what is attached. You do not need elaborate formatting, emojis, logos, or a professional signature. Plain readable text works well and reduces unnecessary complexity.

### Attachments versus links versus pasted text

| Method | What recipient receives | What can change later | Lab requirement? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Local file attachment | A copy incorporated into the outgoing email | Later changes to your local original usually do not update the sent copy | Yes |
| Cloud-sharing link | A way to access a hosted file, subject to permissions | Access and the hosted file may be changed or revoked | Not for the core exercise |
| Inline image | A picture displayed within the body | Display can vary by client and settings | No |
| Text pasted into body | Words inside the email itself | No separate attached document | No |
| Filename written in body | Merely a reference to the document | Does not give access to any file | No |

Many email apps label both attachments and linked cloud files as related items. Read the option you select. In Outlook, for example, you may be offered a choice between attaching a local copy and sharing a OneDrive link. Gmail may insert a Google Drive link if your attachments exceed certain limits. Both approaches can be useful, but they are not identical: **this lab requires a traditional small file attachment**.

### Privacy before convenience

An attachment can reveal more information than its [[filename|filename]] suggests. Documents may include names, author [[metadata|metadata]], comments, revision history, document properties, or data that appears hidden on the screen. Screenshots can expose tabs, contacts, notifications, or locations. Use the supplied plain-text exercise file instead of a personal document. Do not ask anyone else to send a real sensitive file so that you can practice handling attachments.

A cloud link may also accidentally grant view or editing rights to more people than intended. For real documents, review sharing scope and permissions before using a link. For this exercise, there is no need to set public access or enable outside collaboration.

### Autocomplete and unintended recipients

Some email programs use [[autocomplete|autocomplete]] and suggest saved [[contact|contacts]] as you type. The suggestion may match a familiar first name while pointing to someone else. Before sending, read the **complete email address** in the recipient field. If an unexpected address appears, remove it and re-enter the correct one. If you cannot verify the recipient, save the draft without sending. A well-written message sent to the wrong person is still an error.

## 4. How do you write the email in Gmail or Outlook?

The examples below describe currently documented desktop workflows. Buttons can move as mail services update their interfaces. If the interface differs from the screenshots in an official guide, look for the equivalent **Compose**, **New mail**, or **New message** and **Attach** commands, rather than clicking a mysterious control. Stop whenever a screen is unfamiliar enough that you cannot verify what will happen.

### Option A: Gmail on a computer

1. Open Gmail in your browser using the account you are authorized to use. Check the address bar and signed-in account before composing.
2. Select **Compose**. A new message window or pane should open.
3. In **To**, enter your own confirmed account address. If a contact suggestion appears, verify the complete address before selecting it.
4. Leave **Cc** and **Bcc** empty. Do not enter a second recipient for practice.
5. In **Subject**, type `MGU Practice Lab 5 - Attachment Test`.
6. In the body area, enter the sample message from Section 1. You may replace the generic sign-off with a neutral practice sign-off, but do not include passwords or confidential information.
7. Save the draft at this stage by leaving it open or by closing the compose pane in the manner supported by Gmail. Gmail normally saves drafts automatically, but automatic saving should not be confused with sending.
8. Reopen the draft if necessary and confirm that the To, Subject, and body fields survived before proceeding.

To attach the file, use the **paperclip Attach files** control at the bottom of the compose window. Gmail's official help instructs the user to select the file and choose **Open**. You will perform that operation in Section 5. If you are working offline or lack permission to send, stop at the saved draft with no actual Send action.

### Option B: Outlook on the web or Outlook.com

1. Open the authorized Outlook mailbox in your browser. Check that you are in the correct account rather than a work or shared mailbox.
2. Choose **New mail**, **New message**, or the equivalent new-message control.
3. Enter your own approved email address in **To** and check the full address.
4. Leave optional **Cc** and **Bcc** empty.
5. Enter the same subject and message body used for Gmail.
6. Locate **Attach** or **Attach file**. When a choice is offered, select a local-file option such as **Browse this computer**, not **OneDrive link** or another sharing-link option.
7. Before sending, check whether the file is displayed as a named attachment or as a cloud-sharing item. If it is a link, remove it and use the local-file attachment option instead.
8. If you cannot send, save the draft and describe how you would complete the remaining steps.

Outlook has several versions, including new Outlook for Windows, classic Outlook, Outlook on the web, and Outlook.com. Their ribbons and labels are not identical; the essential action remains selecting a new message, choosing a file, and deciding whether it is attached as a copy or linked from cloud storage. Follow your institution's support guidance on a managed account.

### Common navigation comparison

| Task | Gmail in desktop browser | Outlook on the web | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start new message | Compose | New mail or New message | Open empty outgoing draft |
| Choose primary recipient | To | To | Set recipient address |
| Name the message | Subject | Subject | Set searchable topic |
| Add local document | Paperclip Attach files | Attach then Browse this computer | Include file copy |
| Keep unfinished message | Drafts, usually auto-saved | Drafts, usually auto-saved | Store work not yet sent |
| Confirm attempt to send | Sent | Sent Items or Sent | Inspect outgoing copy |

### Two deliberate pauses

**Pause 1: Before adding the attachment.** Confirm that the message identifies the file correctly and is addressed only to your own verified email address. If a student is using a shared training account, follow the instructor's rules and avoid displaying the mailbox to other students.

**Pause 2: Before sending.** Confirm that the attachment is actually present and the total message is appropriate to send. Do not assume the word `attached` in the body means the document was attached.

### Do not confuse saving a draft with sending

Gmail and Outlook can preserve unfinished messages in **[[drafts-folder|Drafts]]**. A draft is editable and not yet transmitted as a new outgoing email. After selecting [[send|Send]], the compose pane may close and a message may appear in **[[sent-folder|Sent]]**. A mail system may instead queue a message in **[[outbox|Outbox]]** while offline or waiting for delivery. These statuses have different meanings; check them before claiming completion.

## 5. How do you attach and check a file?

This is the central hands-on step. The program must receive a file selection; simply mentioning a filename in the message does not attach anything. Use the **same four-line text file you already saved**. You must be able to recognize both its name and its folder location.

### Step-by-step attachment workflow

1. Open the correct draft in Gmail or Outlook. Ensure the **To**, **Subject**, and body are already filled in.
2. Locate the **Attach files** paperclip or corresponding **Attach** command. Do not choose a picture-in-body or cloud-link-only command for this core lab.
3. In the file chooser, browse to your personal `Email-Lab` folder using the sidebar, path, or navigation controls.
4. Identify `mgu-practice-attachment.txt` by its full name and current folder. If several similarly named files exist, open the intended source separately to double-check rather than guessing.
5. Select that file and choose **Open**, **Choose**, or the equivalent confirmation command.
6. Allow the attachment process to finish. Upload time may depend on connection and application; a spinner or progress symbol means it may not be ready yet.
7. Look in the compose window for the filename and its attachment chip, row, thumbnail, or other indicator.
8. Confirm that **exactly one** intended practice file is attached and that no unrelated item appears.
9. Where supported, open or preview the attached copy safely and compare its four fictional lines with the original.
10. Return to the draft and recheck the recipient and subject. You are now ready for the final review, not yet automatically authorized to send.

### The three checks that matter most

An attachment is not adequately verified by the paperclip icon alone. The three meaningful checks are: **(1) expected filename, (2) expected file and content, and (3) a completed attachment or upload operation**. In many programs, clicking a [[thumbnail|thumbnail]] or opening an attachment preview can help verify content. If the platform offers no safe preview, use the filename, size, and source file location, then verify after self-delivery if sending is permitted.

| Observation | Likely explanation | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filename appears in a chip or attachment row | File was selected for inclusion | Check exact name, content, and upload completion |
| No file is shown | Attachment may not have been selected or upload failed | Repeat Attach and choose the known file |
| A share-permission prompt appears | A cloud link may have been selected | Cancel and choose local file copy if available |
| Unexpected file name appears | Wrong file or stale recent-file suggestion | Remove it and select the intended file |
| Upload indicator still spins | File transfer is incomplete | Do not send until completion or a clear error appears |
| Message offers to grant access | File may be hosted online with permissions | Do not grant public access; review link-vs-copy choice |
| The file is blocked | Provider or institutional security rule disallows the item | Do not bypass the restriction; use approved harmless format |

### A file copy and a shared link are not the same

A traditional [[attachment-copy|attached copy]] is typically captured as part of the email that was sent. If you edit the local practice file tomorrow, the earlier attached copy will not ordinarily update itself. With a cloud link, the recipient visits a hosted item whose content and [[sharing-permission|permissions]] can change. The cloud system might notify you that the recipient needs access. For the present exercise, choose the local text file as an attachment, not a shared drive item.

**Important exception:** Some mail apps automatically convert oversized attachments to [[cloud-link|cloud links]]. Gmail documents a 25 MB [[size-limit|limit]] for standard personal-account attachments; organization-managed accounts can have different limits. Other providers and recipients set their own maximum sizes. The precise limit is not needed here, because the text file is tiny. Do not try to test size limits by attaching a huge video or compressed archive.

### What if you attached the wrong file?

Remove the incorrect item while the message is still a draft. Locate its remove button or menu, confirm the unintended file no longer appears, and attach the correct file. If you have already sent sensitive information to the wrong person, do not rely on any optional Undo Send feature as a guarantee of recall. Follow applicable organizational incident procedures promptly and ask for help if necessary. The safest prevention is checking all fields before sending.

### Why not rename a dangerous file to `.txt`?

A filename suffix is not a trustworthy security inspection. Changing `.exe` or another executable extension to `.txt` does not necessarily convert its underlying content into harmless plain text. This lab requires you to **create real plain text in a text editor**. Never disable security tools, rename a blocked file to bypass a rule, or send unknown software to complete an attachment exercise.

### A short pre-send audit

Read each question, not merely its first word: Is the recipient your own verified address? Is the subject correct? Does the body describe the attachment? Is there exactly one known and harmless file? Does the filename match? Has the attachment finished loading? Is the mail using an attached copy rather than a hosted link? Are Cc and Bcc empty? Does the message contain no unnecessary personal information? Are you authorized to send it?

:::answer Show the attachment-verification checkpoint
**Expected result:** One and only one file named `mgu-practice-attachment.txt` is present in the prepared draft. The learner can identify its original storage folder, explain that it holds four lines of fictional text, and distinguish the actual attachment from a filename typed into the body. If an app inserted a cloud link, the learner recognizes that the core attachment requirement is not yet satisfied.
:::

## 6. How do you review, send, and verify the message?

Sending an email is a real external action. You should perform it only when you know who will receive it and have permission to contact that address. For this exercise, **self-addressed sending is the preferred method**. If the recipient is not verified, do not click Send. Draft-only students can still complete most of the learning objectives and report the difference between preparation and transmission.

### Final review: the pause-before-send sequence

1. Read the full **To** address from beginning to end. If the account has multiple aliases, confirm you are sending from the intended account too.
2. Ensure the **Cc** and **Bcc** fields are empty, not simply collapsed with hidden entries.
3. Read the **Subject** and compare it with the model subject in Section 1.
4. Read the complete **Body** to confirm it is clear, polite, and accurate.
5. Confirm the attached item is `mgu-practice-attachment.txt` and that the transfer is finished.
6. Make sure the message is **not** addressed to a real employer, customer, friend, unknown contact, or public mailing list.
7. Confirm that you are authorized to send from this mailbox. If not, keep the draft and stop.
8. Only after all seven checks pass, select **Send** once. Avoid repeated clicks while the application is processing.

### After sending: examine Sent

Open **[[sent-folder|Sent]]** or **Sent Items**. Locate the message by its subject. Read the actual To field and look for the attached filename. This is an important verification step because a message may appear correct in your memory while its saved outgoing copy reveals a typo or missing file. If the app shows the item waiting in **Outbox**, do not describe it as already delivered. A message in Sent usually means the sending system recorded the attempt, not that the recipient has read it.

### If sending to yourself: check Inbox

A self-addressed message may arrive in the same mailbox, sometimes grouped into a [[conversation-thread|conversation thread]]. Look for the subject in Inbox, All Mail, or search if appropriate; arrival timing can vary. Open the message. Confirm the sender and recipient are expected and that the file is shown as an attachment. Preview or download it only if you know it is the file **you created**. Do not assume that the existence of an attachment from an unknown sender makes it safe to open.

If [[delivery|delivery]] does not appear immediately, inspect Sent and Outbox, check for error or nondelivery notices, and use the mailbox's search if available. Do not repeatedly re-send messages because of a short delay; you could create duplicate emails. When delivery remains uncertain, record the observation rather than inventing a successful outcome.

### Understanding email status

| Location or status | What it commonly indicates | Appropriate conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drafts | Message saved before transmission | Prepared but not sent |
| Outbox or queued | Message waiting to transmit or retry | Sending not yet confirmed |
| Sent or Sent Items | Mail application recorded the outgoing message | Sent record exists, delivery still not guaranteed |
| Inbox | Message appears in recipient mailbox | Recipient mailbox received a message |
| Delivery failure notice | Mail service reports that delivery did not complete | Investigate address or service issue |
| Read receipt or tracking notice | Service indicates a limited interaction signal | Not necessarily reliable proof of human reading |

### Recording evidence without exposing private information

An instructor can ask you to demonstrate the email in your own mailbox or state the exact subject and filename during a supervised session. Avoid uploading screenshots that reveal inbox content, contact lists, account addresses, notification banners, or other people's messages. For self-study, keep a plain written record such as: `Draft created; file attached and checked; self-addressed message sent; message visible in Sent; received copy confirmed.` Write only what actually happened.

### Clean up responsibly

If your instructor permits, retain the harmless practice file in your folder for later file-management labs. You can leave the test email in Sent or delete it under your own account's rules after assessment, but do not claim deletion proves it is gone from recipients, backups, or service systems. If using an institutional account, follow retention and training policies. Log out or lock a shared computer when finished.

### An honest incomplete outcome is still useful

A student who created the correct file, drafted the correct email, and verified the attachment—but was unable to send because a shared computer had no permitted account—has still demonstrated important skills. The final rubric distinguishes a **draft-stage competency** from a **fully verified send-and-receive competency**. Never fabricate a sent confirmation to make the assignment appear complete.

## 7. Guided practice: compose, attach, and verify

Each activity has a specific goal and a way to check the result. Use a real email program only when your account and location are authorized. Activities 1–5 can be completed without sending; Activity 6 is a controlled self-send when permitted. If working offline, record a mock result separately and say clearly that actual email operations were not performed.

### Activity 1: Plan the message and identify its parts

**Scenario:** A fictional learner needs to email a short practice note to themselves. They want to make sure that an email contains a recognizable purpose and does not reach anyone else.

**Your task:**

1. Open a blank paper worksheet, text document, or instructor-supplied practice template.
2. Write the labels **From**, **To**, **Subject**, **Body**, **Attachment**, and **Send status**.
3. Write `your own verified address` next to To; this is an instruction, not an address to type into a live email.
4. Write `MGU Practice Lab 5 - Attachment Test` next to Subject.
5. Write a two-sentence summary for the Body that explains the practice file and states that the material is fictional.
6. Write the required filename next to Attachment.
7. Set the initial Send status to `Not sent - planning only`.
8. Explain why the recipient must be verified before a new message is sent.

**Success test:** Another person can tell exactly which item is being attached, what the email is about, and why the destination is safe.

:::answer Show Activity 1 model answer
**From:** An account the learner is authorized to use. **To:** The learner's own confirmed address, not a placeholder or random example. **Subject:** `MGU Practice Lab 5 - Attachment Test`. **Body:** A courteous note explaining that the attached text file contains fictional practice material. **Attachment:** `mgu-practice-attachment.txt`. **Status:** `Not sent - planning only`. A clear plan prevents an incorrect or incomplete draft from becoming an unintended outgoing message.
:::

### Activity 2: Create and locate the harmless text file

**Scenario:** The email draft is ready, but you need a genuine saved document to attach. You will not use any personal document.

**Your task:**

1. Open File Explorer or the corresponding file manager.
2. Choose your personal `Email-Lab` folder and note its location.
3. Open Notepad or another plain-text editor.
4. Enter the four prescribed lines from Section 1.
5. Save the document as `mgu-practice-attachment.txt` in your selected folder.
6. Return to the file manager and identify the full filename, extension, and folder.
7. Reopen the text file to verify its content, then close it without changing the text.
8. Explain why the file should be small and free from confidential information.

**Success test:** The correct file exists, is in the documented folder, opens as readable text, and contains no real personal or business details.

:::answer Show Activity 2 verification
The student should find a readable plain-text document named `mgu-practice-attachment.txt`. The file contains an MGU training heading, the lab label, and two statements that the material is fictional and nonconfidential. The exact storage path can differ from the example. There is no requirement to upload the file anywhere outside the selected email application or show a real private file.
:::

### Activity 3: Compose a professional email and save a draft

**Scenario:** You have the source file but are not yet ready to send. Drafting first gives you time to inspect fields without transmission.

**Your task:**

1. Open the authorized mail application and check which account is signed in.
2. Start a new message rather than replying to an unrelated thread.
3. Enter **only** your own confirmed address in To. Keep Cc and Bcc empty.
4. Enter the exact required subject from Section 1.
5. Type the sample body or a faithful concise version with greeting, purpose, attachment name, and sign-off.
6. Save or allow the mail program to save the draft. Do not click Send.
7. Navigate to Drafts if your program supports it, open the draft again, and inspect its fields.
8. State the difference between a draft and a message in Sent.

**Success test:** You can show or describe a complete, unsent message draft with accurate addressing, subject, and body.

:::answer Show Activity 3 model review
The draft is addressed solely to the learner's own verified mailbox. It includes the subject `MGU Practice Lab 5 - Attachment Test` and a courteous explanation of the expected attachment. It is in Drafts or an equivalent unsent editor state. **No external sending is required for this activity**; the learner should not claim that a saved draft has been delivered.
:::

### Activity 4: Attach the file and inspect the draft

**Scenario:** An email that mentions a document is not sufficient. You must add the saved document using the Attach command.

**Your task:**

1. Open the draft from Activity 3.
2. Select the paperclip or appropriate **Attach** command.
3. Browse to the personal practice folder that contains `mgu-practice-attachment.txt`.
4. Select that file and confirm the selection.
5. Wait for the file to finish attaching or uploading.
6. Identify the attachment filename or accessible attachment indicator in the draft.
7. Compare the visible name against the file you created. Check that a second, unrelated file is not present.
8. Explain whether you attached a local copy or merely inserted a link to a cloud file.

**Success test:** The draft contains one recognized, completed attachment of the correct type. The learner can explain how it was selected and why a filename typed only in the body would fail the requirement.

:::answer Show Activity 4 model answer
The correct item appears as `mgu-practice-attachment.txt` in a file attachment chip, row, or equivalent control, with no upload still pending. The learner selected the actual saved file from a known folder. A plain statement saying `Please see mgu-practice-attachment.txt` without a file indicator is **not** enough. A hosted-link permission prompt should prompt a review of whether the wrong attachment mode was selected.
:::

### Activity 5: Catch a problem before sending

**Scenario:** A model draft contains three errors: an unknown address appears in Cc, the subject is `hello`, and the attached item is `family-records.pdf` rather than the harmless text document. The learner has not sent the draft.

**Your task:**

1. Identify each error and explain the risk it introduces.
2. Remove the unexpected Cc recipient without contacting that person.
3. Replace `hello` with the assigned descriptive subject.
4. Remove `family-records.pdf` from the draft without deleting the underlying file from its original storage location.
5. Select the correct `mgu-practice-attachment.txt` file from your personal practice folder.
6. Recheck To, Cc, Bcc, Subject, Body, and Attachment.
7. Explain why the next correct action is **not** to send until each issue has been resolved.
8. If you are working in a paper simulation, mark the corrected fields rather than entering any fictitious or real sensitive data into email software.

**Success test:** The unsafe draft becomes a safe, clearly labeled, self-addressed practice draft before any external action occurs.

:::answer Show Activity 5 corrections
**Error 1:** Unknown Cc address could send an unintended copy; remove it. **Error 2:** Subject `hello` does not describe the purpose; use `MGU Practice Lab 5 - Attachment Test`. **Error 3:** `family-records.pdf` is both the wrong file and potentially sensitive; remove it from the draft and attach `mgu-practice-attachment.txt`. The correction is made **before** sending. Removing an attachment from a draft generally does not require deleting the original stored document.
:::

### Activity 6: Perform the controlled send-and-receive test

**Scenario:** Your draft is correct, the attachment is present, and your account rules allow you to send a self-addressed practice message. You will determine what can actually be verified.

**Your task:**

1. Confirm again that the To field contains **your own verified address**, and that no other addresses are in the recipient fields.
2. Inspect the completed attachment and read the message once more.
3. Confirm you have permission to send one harmless self-test email using that account.
4. Select **Send** once. If sending is not permitted, do not click it; record `Draft only` and move to the alternative below.
5. Open Sent or Sent Items and locate the specific subject.
6. Check that the message's recipient and attached filename match your planned values.
7. If your self-addressed copy has arrived, open the received message and verify the attachment again.
8. Record the status accurately: `Draft only`, `Sent record confirmed`, `Received copy confirmed`, or `Delivery not confirmed`.
9. Do not re-send automatically to overcome a short delay or replace an uncertain result with a claim of success.

**Success test:** With permission and delivery available, the student can demonstrate the complete path from source file through prepared email to Sent and a received copy. If sending cannot be demonstrated, the status record must explain the limitation precisely.

:::answer Show Activity 6 evidence criteria
A **fully verified software demonstration** includes the correct local file, correct email fields, one actual attached copy, a Sent record, and a received self-addressed message with the expected file. A **draft-only demonstration** includes the first three elements and explicitly states that no live sending took place. A message in Sent is not proof that someone read it; an absent Inbox copy is not proof that a send action never occurred. Report only observations that can actually be checked.
:::

### Optional extension: Explain the cloud-link alternative

Do not send a second message or change sharing permissions just to complete this extension. Using an instructor-approved example, compare what would happen if a file were attached as a normal copy versus inserted from OneDrive or Google Drive as a link. Explain who might gain access, which version the recipient would see, and what could happen if the cloud item's permissions change after the email is sent.

:::answer Show the optional extension explanation
A traditional attached file is ordinarily a separate copy sent with the message. A cloud link generally points to an online item whose access depends on account settings and sharing rules. A recipient may need to request permission. A change to the online file can change what the recipient later sees, whereas changing a local source file usually does not update an earlier attached copy. Neither method should be treated as private merely because the sender intended a limited audience.
:::

After each task, write one brief observation: What did I intend to do? What did the application show? How did I confirm success? What would I change next time? An accurate report of an incomplete or blocked task is better than guessing. This habit applies to every computer skill, not just email.

## 8. How do you adapt the lab and solve common problems?

The core skill is not memorizing one brand's menu arrangement. It is safely choosing a file and communicating it to an approved recipient. Most major email programs support the basic workflow, but they differ in which commands are visible, whether they suggest recent files, and when they convert large files to shared links. On a managed account, organizational security rules may restrict attachments entirely.

### Apple Mail on Mac

1. Open **Mail** and start a new message using an account you are authorized to use.
2. Enter your own confirmed address in To; add the required subject and the same short sample body.
3. Select the **Attach** control and choose the file-attachment option, such as **Attach Files**.
4. Navigate to your known practice folder, select `mgu-practice-attachment.txt`, and confirm with the file chooser.
5. Check the selected file's name and how Mail displays it. Some images and PDFs appear inline; for this lab, use the text file.
6. Review the recipient, subject, body, and filename; send only to yourself if authorized.
7. Review Sent and the received copy if available. Apple may use Mail Drop for large attachments, but that feature is unnecessary for a very small text file.

**TextEdit note:** TextEdit on macOS can work in rich-text or plain-text mode. Create a true plain-text document when using it for this lab. If the app saves an `.rtf` file or another format instead, choose its plain-text option before preparing the practice attachment.

### Gmail or Outlook on mobile devices

A phone or tablet can usually attach a file from its supported Files, Downloads, or document-provider interface. The compose button may appear as a floating symbol; the paperclip or Attach control may appear in a top toolbar or an overflow menu. The mobile file picker may offer **Files**, **Browse**, **On My Device**, **Drive**, **iCloud Drive**, or other storage sources depending on the operating system and installed apps.

1. Prepare or locate the same safe text file in a storage location that the mobile email app can browse.
2. Start a new message and enter only your own verified address.
3. Add the subject and body before opening any file picker.
4. Choose an option that attaches the document as a file rather than inserting a hosted sharing link, if your app provides that distinction.
5. Confirm the exact filename and that the attachment has finished loading. Mobile displays sometimes hide long names; expand the item or inspect its details as needed.
6. Read the recipient and attached file once more before sending.
7. If you cannot find the correct file or cannot verify the attachment, preserve the draft and continue on a computer rather than selecting a random document.

### Chromebook and browser-only workflows

A Chromebook with Gmail can use the same browser Compose workflow. The file chooser may open the ChromeOS **Files** app with **My files**, **Downloads**, or permitted cloud locations. Select only the safe document in the known practice folder. If using a school-managed Chromebook, some storage sources or outgoing mail operations may be restricted; follow the instructor's policy instead of bypassing restrictions.

### Accessibility practices

A mouse is not required for successful email composition. [[keyboard-navigation|Keyboard-only users]] can navigate among fields and controls using **Tab**, **Shift + Tab**, arrow keys where supported, and application-specific shortcuts that have been verified. Screen-reader users should listen for the To field, Subject field, compose editor, and attachment indicator or file name before sending. The shortcut sequence differs by program; do not assume a particular key combination will always attach a file. Enlarged text, zoom controls, speech recognition, and accessible file pickers are appropriate accommodations. The instructor should evaluate whether the outgoing content is right, not how quickly or visually the learner clicks a paperclip.

### Troubleshooting table

| Problem | Likely cause | Safe next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| File is absent from the picker | Wrong folder or storage source | Open file manager; confirm location; reopen chooser |
| Wrong file was attached | Similar filenames or recent-file suggestion | Remove only the draft attachment and choose the verified text file |
| Attachment appears as a cloud link | Cloud integration or size conversion | Cancel, choose local-copy attachment, or document the restriction |
| Upload never finishes | Slow or interrupted network, provider problem | Save draft; check connection; retry only when safe |
| A recipient suggestion looks unfamiliar | Autocomplete matched wrong contact | Remove suggestion; type and verify the full address |
| The draft disappeared | Account mismatch, closing unsaved editor, sync delay | Check Drafts in correct account before re-creating |
| Send is unavailable | Offline state, authentication issue, policy, incomplete form | Do not bypass controls; use draft-only alternative |
| Message is stuck in Outbox | App is waiting for network or sending service | Do not claim Sent; check connection and support guidance |
| Message appears in Sent but not Inbox | Delay, filters, threading, or delivery issue | Search and check notifications; avoid repeat sends |
| Attached file cannot open | Unsupported format, corrupt file, security block | Inspect the original safe text file; follow service policy |
| File is unexpectedly large | Wrong item selected or unsuitable content | Remove attachment and use the tiny prescribed text file |
| Security warning appears | App flags risky file or account activity | Stop and follow trusted account or IT guidance |
| Sign-in asks for unusual verification | Account security workflow may be active or page may be fraudulent | Confirm official service and use trusted recovery/support channels |
| Shared device displays a different mailbox | Previous user may still be signed in | Do not read their messages; sign out or seek administrator help |

### If the attachment is missing after a send

Do not assume an email can be edited after transmission. A correction commonly requires a new message or a reply attaching the missing item, but this lab does **not** require a second outgoing email. If the test was sent without its attachment, record the mistake in your practice log and explain how the pre-send audit could have prevented it. In real work, follow the organization's communication policies before sending a correction. If the initial message went to an unintended recipient, prioritize incident handling and do not disclose additional information.

### Spam, phishing, and unexpected attachments

Email attachments are also a common technique in [[phishing|scams]]. A document can carry a misleading filename, an unsafe [[macro|macro]], or a prompt to install software. Treat an unexpected message differently from the known file you created yourself. Check the sender, context, and purpose; do not click unknown links or enable active content because an email urges you to act quickly. Email addresses and displayed names can be spoofed. If a purported employer or institution demands urgent action, verify using an independently known official channel, not the contact link inside the suspicious message.

The practice message should contain **no password, verification code, financial account details, national identifier, private family record, confidential work file, or sensitive screenshot**. Email is not automatically end-to-end encrypted, and some providers or administrators may retain message copies. Even self-addressed messages should be treated as real communications rather than as a private notebook.

### Check downloaded files without unnecessary exposure

When you inspect the received copy, you know its origin because you created the exact text file and sent it to yourself. In ordinary life, however, do not download and open every attachment without examining whether it was expected. Follow your organization's anti-malware and file-handling procedures. Never disable protective software or run an executable to demonstrate that you can open an attachment. If an attachment asks you to enable macros or install a program, stop and ask for guidance.

### Privacy on shared and public computers

Do not use browser remember-me options or save passwords on public training terminals without institutional approval. After a supervised lab, close drafts that are not needed, sign out of accounts as directed, and clear local temporary material when required by school policy. If a shared computer unexpectedly opens somebody else's signed-in email account, do not explore its Inbox. Stop and ask the instructor or authorized administrator to help.

### When a real send cannot be completed

A strict workplace mail policy, missing internet connection, a supervised class rule, or absence of an account can prevent a live email demonstration. The correct response is to finish the safe portions, preserve a draft if allowed, and accurately document what you could and could not verify. An instructor may then assess the recipient plan, sample message, file creation, attachment verification, and pre-send audit in a controlled environment. **Never work around a security restriction or create an unauthorized account solely to finish the exercise.**

### Instructor review suggestions

For classroom instruction, use an approved training address or instruct learners to send only to themselves. Do not collect real login credentials or require students to show private inbox contents to classmates. If a screenshot or demonstration is part of an assessment, have learners obscure unrelated messages, recipients, and personal details. Demonstrations can be conducted locally with a teacher observing the correct fields rather than collecting message copies. Separate success at drafting, attaching, sending, and receiving in the scoring rubric to reflect genuine accomplishments.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The following terms support this lab's explanations and exercises. Selected terms in the lesson open short definitions in the MGU dashboard. This visible glossary is also provided for readers using a text-only or printed version. Where controls differ by application, the concepts remain consistent.

- **Email:** Electronic message sent through an email service.
- **Email account:** Authorized mailbox identity used to send and receive messages.
- **Email address:** Address identifying a mailbox for routing email messages.
- **Sender:** Account or address shown as sending an email message.
- **Recipient:** Person or mailbox specified to receive a message.
- **From field:** Compose field identifying the account used to send the message.
- **To field:** Compose field containing the primary recipient addresses.
- **Cc:** Visible-copy recipient field in many email applications.
- **Bcc:** Blind-copy recipient field whose addresses are generally hidden from ordinary recipients.
- **Reply-to:** Address designated to receive replies where a mail system supports it.
- **Subject line:** Brief description of an email message shown in message lists.
- **Message body:** Main written content of an email.
- **Greeting:** Opening words that politely address a message recipient.
- **Sign-off:** Closing words or name used to finish a message.
- **Compose window:** Area in a mail program where an outgoing message is prepared.
- **Compose button:** Command used to start a new outgoing email.
- **New message:** Email draft created independently of an existing conversation.
- **Attachment:** File associated with an email message.
- **Attach command:** Mail control for selecting a file to include with a message.
- **Paperclip icon:** Common visual symbol for the attachment command or an attached item.
- **Attachment chip:** Named on-screen control or item representing an attached file.
- **Attachment indicator:** Visible or accessible indication that a file is included with a message.
- **File chooser:** Interface for browsing storage and selecting a file.
- **File Explorer:** Windows application for locating and managing files and folders.
- **Finder:** macOS application for browsing files and folders.
- **Folder:** Organizing container for files and other folders.
- **Filename:** Stored name used to identify a file.
- **File extension:** Suffix of a filename often associated with its format.
- **Plain text:** Unformatted characters stored without word-processing layout.
- **TXT file:** Text document commonly saved with the .txt extension.
- **Text editor:** Program used to create and modify text files.
- **Notepad:** Windows application used for text editing.
- **TextEdit:** macOS text editor that can work in plain-text or rich-text modes.
- **Save:** Store a file or draft for later use.
- **Save As:** Command used to select a name or location for saving work.
- **Draft:** Prepared email that has not yet been sent.
- **Drafts:** Mailbox location where unfinished messages can be stored.
- **Send:** Command that submits an outgoing message to the email system.
- **Outbox:** Mail area where outgoing messages may wait to be transmitted.
- **Sent:** Mailbox area holding records of outgoing messages.
- **Inbox:** Mailbox location where received email messages commonly appear.
- **Delivery:** Process by which an outgoing message reaches a destination mailbox.
- **Delivery failure:** Notification that an email could not be delivered under particular conditions.
- **Conversation thread:** Grouped series of related email messages, often including replies.
- **Autocomplete:** Suggestions that appear while a user types an address or other entry.
- **Contact:** Saved information about a person or organization, often including an email address.
- **Verification:** Checking observed evidence against an intended outcome.
- **Pre-send check:** Final review of recipient, content, attachments, and permission before sending.
- **Upload:** Transfer of data from a device to a service or other computer.
- **Upload progress:** Indicator showing whether a transfer is pending or completed.
- **Download:** Transfer of data from an online service or remote source to a device.
- **Attachment copy:** File instance included with a message instead of merely linked.
- **Cloud storage:** Data storage provided through a remote service or account.
- **Cloud-sharing link:** URL leading to a hosted file under the host service permissions.
- **Sharing permission:** Rule controlling who can access a hosted file and what they may do.
- **OneDrive:** Microsoft online storage service that can integrate with Outlook.
- **Google Drive:** Google service for storing and sharing files online.
- **Mail Drop:** Apple feature that can deliver access to larger email attachments through iCloud.
- **Inline image:** Picture displayed inside the body of a message.
- **Thumbnail:** Small visual representation of a file or image.
- **PDF:** Portable Document Format used for distributing documents with a stable page layout.
- **Attachment size limit:** Maximum file size accepted by a mail service or mailbox.
- **File type:** Kind of file as recognized from content, format, or system metadata.
- **Blocked file:** Item refused by software or policy for a security or compatibility reason.
- **Privacy:** Appropriate control over personal information and who can access it.
- **Confidential information:** Data that should be shared only with permitted parties.
- **Metadata:** Descriptive information associated with a document or file.
- **Permission:** Authorization to access, send, change, or share a resource.
- **Organizational policy:** Rules imposed by an institution or employer on approved system use.
- **Phishing:** Deceptive message designed to trick someone into disclosing information or taking unsafe actions.
- **Spoofing:** Falsifying displayed identifying information to appear to be another source.
- **Malware:** Software designed or used to cause harm or unauthorized activity.
- **Macro:** Automated document instruction that may require security scrutiny before running.
- **Spam:** Unsolicited bulk or unwanted electronic messaging.
- **Spam filter:** Tool that identifies or diverts suspected unwanted messages.
- **Account sign-in:** Process of authenticating into a permitted mailbox or service.
- **Two-factor authentication:** Security process requiring an additional verification factor for account access.
- **Screen reader:** Assistive technology that announces text and interface information.
- **Keyboard focus:** Current interface element receiving keyboard input.
- **Keyboard navigation:** Using keys to move through and operate controls.
- **Accessibility:** Design that supports people with differing abilities and assistive needs.
- **Practice scenario:** Invented situation used to learn without exposing real people or sensitive information.
- **Self-addressed email:** Message intentionally sent to the sender’s own confirmed mailbox.
- **Evidence:** Observable result used to support a claim that a task was completed.
- **Rubric:** Criteria used to evaluate quality and completeness of an exercise.
- **Recovery:** Process of restoring access or unfinished work after a failure.
- **Cloud synchronization:** Coordinating changes between connected local and online copies of data.

## 11. Completion review and further learning

### Final performance rubric

The full lab can be marked complete when the learner demonstrates the safe file, the correct draft, the verified attachment, and a sending outcome that is honestly described. **Draft-only learners should not be scored as if they delivered an email.** An instructor can mark a task as achieved, developing, or not attempted and provide opportunities for safe repetition.

| Criterion | Complete demonstration | Developing or incomplete evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Safe source document | Correct named file in approved folder; opens with expected fictional text | File cannot be located or content is unverified |
| Recipient and professional writing | Own or specifically authorized destination; correct subject and courteous body | Recipient uncertain or required fields missing |
| Attachment selection | Exactly one correct file included as an attached copy | Filename merely typed, wrong file, or cloud link instead |
| Pre-send review | Checks all fields, privacy, file, completion, and permission | Missing checks or unintended recipients remain |
| Sending status | Authorized send and Sent record demonstrated, or draft-only status accurately declared | Claims sent without evidence or ignores an Outbox state |
| Receipt status | Received message and attached file verified when the live send succeeds | Delivery unclear, not attempted, or incorrectly assumed |
| Responsible device use | No private documents exposed; account handled under local policy | Confidential material or someone else’s account accessed |
| Explanation and reflection | Can explain how to avoid and repair a missing attachment | Relies on guessing, repeated sends, or bypassing controls |

**Optional student record:** `File created: yes/no; Correct draft: yes/no; Attachment verified: yes/no; Pre-send audit: yes/no; Authorized to send: yes/no; Sent record: yes/no/not attempted; Received copy: yes/no/not observed; Notes: ...` This is a reflection format, not a transmission log collected by the lesson dashboard.

### What the lab does not require

You do not have to register an email account, purchase software, send mail to an instructor, attach real schoolwork, share your personal address in the dashboard, provide screenshots of your Inbox, change security settings, or upload the practice document to a public service. You also do not need to force a message through when it is blocked or delayed. The core competency is **safe preparation, correct attachment, careful review, and accurate verification**.

### Transfer the skill to everyday work

When you later send a genuine assignment or business document, use the same sequence: know which file is final, know its folder, open it to confirm content, enter the correct recipient, write a meaningful subject, explain the purpose, attach the intended copy or deliberately choose a permitted sharing link, and review privacy before sending. If a recipient specifically asks for a PDF rather than an editable file, use the requested approved format and confirm the export content before attaching it. Review document metadata and organizational policies when working with sensitive material.
