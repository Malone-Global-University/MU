Basic Computer Skills / Spreadsheet Skills

## Lesson 2: Rows, Columns, and Cells

Imagine a community group keeping track of supplies for an event. Someone asks, “How many notebooks are listed?” Another person answers, “Look at B3.” That brief instruction is possible because every ordinary [[cell|cell]] in a [[worksheet|worksheet]] has a recognizable location. Spreadsheet locations use letters and numbers, like addresses on a map. You do not need to understand advanced mathematics to find information in them.

In Lesson 1, you learned what a [[spreadsheet|spreadsheet]] is and why people use it. In this lesson you will read its [[grid|grid]], identify [[row|rows]] and [[column|columns]], locate cells, move around a worksheet, and select a [[range|range]] of neighboring cells. You will also learn why the letter at the top of a column is different from a label someone types into the worksheet, and why selecting a whole row requires care.

Microsoft Excel on Windows 11 is the principal desktop example; Google Sheets and LibreOffice Calc follow the same basic grid convention. Screen layouts, menus, keyboard shortcuts, and accessibility announcements can differ by application and device. Every required activity can be completed with fictional sample data on paper. Live editing is optional and should be done only in a disposable practice file.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 80–110 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lesson 1, What Is a Spreadsheet?, recommended · **Practice equipment:** Paper and pencil or an available spreadsheet application; internet not required for paper practice · **Safety:** Work with fictional data, protect real files, and practice destructive actions only in a new disposable workbook.

> **Essential idea:** A row runs **across**; a column runs **down**; a cell is where one row and one column meet. In ordinary A1 notation, the cell address places the **column letter first** and the **row number second**: `B3` means column B, row 3.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Identify the horizontal rows, vertical columns, and individual cells of a worksheet.
2. Distinguish built-in [[row-heading|row headings]] and [[column-heading|column headings]] from labels entered by a user.
3. Read and locate addresses such as `A1`, `B3`, `D5`, and `AA12` using column-first, row-second notation.
4. Explain what the [[active-cell|active cell]] is and how a selection differs from an active location.
5. Describe the purpose of the [[name-box|Name Box]] and [[formula-bar|formula bar]] where available.
6. Interpret a rectangular cell range such as `B2:D5` and identify its included corners and cells.
7. Navigate between cells using a mouse, keyboard, or accessible alternatives.
8. Distinguish selecting one cell, several adjacent cells, an entire row, and an entire column.
9. Recognize how blank cells, inserted or deleted rows, hidden rows, and merged cells can affect interpretation.
10. Use a consistent, readable grid structure to record fictional information without exposing personal data.

## 1. How is a spreadsheet grid organized?

A [[worksheet|worksheet]] is a working surface divided into cells. Think of ordinary graph paper with two special sets of identifiers: letters across the top and numbers down the side. Each intersection is a cell. These identifiers are generated by the spreadsheet application; they are not ordinarily typed into the worksheet as data.

A typical empty spreadsheet shows the column headings `A`, `B`, `C`, `D` and onward, and row headings `1`, `2`, `3`, `4` and onward. The grid can extend much farther than what fits on the screen. The precise maximum size depends on the spreadsheet program, its version, and its file format; you do not need to memorize a maximum for this course.

### First, read a fictional worksheet

The table below is a **teaching map** of a worksheet, not a live spreadsheet. The first row of the table shows the application's column letters; the first column shows its row numbers. The remaining entries represent content stored in worksheet cells.

| Worksheet row | Column A | Column B | Column C | Column D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | Item | Quantity | Unit price | Status |
| **2** | Pencils | 3 | 2.00 | Ready |
| **3** | Notebooks | 2 | 4.00 | Ready |
| **4** | Folders | 1 | 5.00 | Check |
| **5** | Tape | 2 | 1.50 | Check |

Read this map as follows:

- `A1` contains the word **Item**.
- `B2` contains the number **3**, the quantity of pencils.
- `A3` contains **Notebooks**, and `B3` contains **2**.
- `C4` contains **5.00**, the unit price for folders.
- `D5` contains **Check**, the status for tape.

A person can use these coordinates to find an entry without describing its screen position: “Read `C4`” is more reliable than “Look toward the bottom right,” because users may scroll, zoom, resize windows, or use assistive technology.

### The grid is not the same as a table in a document

A spreadsheet may *look* like a table in a letter or webpage, but the worksheet has its own address system. Its rows and columns remain addressable even when most cells are empty. An ordinary Word table does not identify its cells by A1-style worksheet coordinates. Different tools can display tabular information, but the spreadsheet grid is designed for selecting and processing individual cell locations.

A [[workbook|workbook]] in Excel is usually the overall spreadsheet file. A workbook may contain more than one worksheet, reached through [[sheet-tab|sheet tabs]] along the interface. Each sheet has its own `A1`, `B3`, and other coordinates. If the file has two sheets, `B3` in the first sheet and `B3` in the second are **different locations**, even though their addresses look the same when written without a sheet name.

> **Key distinction:** A cell address is meaningful within a particular worksheet. When more than one sheet is involved, identify which sheet you mean.

### A compact address demonstration

| Asked location | Column to find | Row to find | Content in the example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `A1` | A | 1 | Item |
| `B3` | B | 3 | 2 |
| `C5` | C | 5 | 1.50 |
| `D4` | D | 4 | Check |

Practice pointing to each named intersection on the table above. Do not count **Item** as row 1 and then start numbering the data rows at 1 again. The application's row 1 is already occupied by the headings in this example.

## 2. What do rows and columns mean?

A [[row|row]] is a horizontal line of cells: it extends from left to right. A [[column|column]] is a vertical line of cells: it extends from top to bottom. A worksheet's row and column arrangement is fixed as a grid, but the information you place in each row and column is a design decision.

### Rows run across

On the fictional supply sheet, row 3 runs across several columns and describes notebooks:

| Address | `A3` | `B3` | `C3` | `D3` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Displayed content | Notebooks | 2 | 4.00 | Ready |

The application labels this complete horizontal band with the row heading **3**. Clicking or otherwise activating a row heading can select the entire row, including cells far beyond the ones you can see. Selecting the whole row is different from selecting just `A3:D3`.

In an organized list, one row often represents a single [[record|record]]—one item, event, observation, or transaction. The example uses one row per supply item. That is a common convention, not a rule of the spreadsheet application. Some worksheets arrange information differently, such as a calendar or a financial statement. Learn to interpret the labels and intended layout rather than assuming every worksheet is a database-style list.

### Columns run down

Column B runs through all rows, and in this example it holds quantities:

| Cell in column B | Displayed content | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `B1` | Quantity | A label naming the column's contents |
| `B2` | 3 | Pencils needed |
| `B3` | 2 | Notebooks needed |
| `B4` | 1 | Folders needed |
| `B5` | 2 | Tape rolls needed |

The application identifies this vertical band with the column heading **B**. If a worksheet grows, the same column continues downward. Consistently putting quantities in column B makes the sheet easier to read and later calculate.

### Built-in headings versus entered labels

Two different things can appear to be “headings.” Confusing them is one of the most common beginner mistakes.

| Built-in grid identifier | Label entered in a cell |
| --- | --- |
| `A`, `B`, `C` at the top of the worksheet | `Item`, `Quantity`, `Unit price` in row 1 |
| `1`, `2`, `3` along the side of the worksheet | `Pencils`, `Notebooks`, `Folders` in column A |
| Supplied by the spreadsheet interface | Created, changed, or removed by the user |
| Helps identify an address such as `B3` | Explains what a data field means |

When you type **Quantity** into `B1`, the application does *not* rename column B. Column B is still B; it now has a human-readable column label inside its first cell. If you delete the text in `B1`, the built-in column letter remains.

### Row order is not the same as item identity

In our example, notebooks occupy row 3. But if you insert a new row above notebooks or reorder the records, notebooks may move to another row. A cell address describes **where the data currently is**, not a permanent identity for the item. For durable records, use meaningful labels or identifiers and check the current worksheet rather than relying on a remembered row number.

### Columns after Z

Column headings use letters that continue beyond Z. The common sequence includes `X`, `Y`, `Z`, `AA`, `AB`, `AC`, and so forth. Therefore `AA12` means column AA, row 12—not column A twice or an invalid address. The same column-first rule applies even if the column label has multiple letters.

| Column reference | What it identifies | Beginner interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `A` | First lettered column | Vertical column A |
| `Z` | Twenty-sixth lettered column | Still a column, not a row |
| `AA` | Column immediately after Z | A two-letter column identifier |
| `AB` | Column immediately after AA | Another two-letter column identifier |

> **Remember:** Horizontal = row = number; vertical = column = letter. In a cell address, the letter or letters are written **before** the row number.

## 3. How do cell addresses work?

A [[cell|cell]] is one position at the intersection of a row and a column. Its [[cell-reference|cell reference]] typically consists of a column label followed by a row number. In `C4`, `C` tells you which column, and `4` tells you which row. The order matters: `4C` is not an ordinary A1-style cell address.

### A1-style notation

The address `A1` names the cell in the first column and first row. `B2` is one column right and one row down. `D5` lies in column D and row 5. These are addresses; they are **not** necessarily the values stored in the cells.

| Cell address | Column part | Row part | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `A1` | A | 1 | Column A, row 1 |
| `B3` | B | 3 | Column B, row 3 |
| `D5` | D | 5 | Column D, row 5 |
| `AA12` | AA | 12 | Column AA, row 12 |
| `C100` | C | 100 | Column C, row 100 |

An address tells you where to look; it does not tell you whether the cell contains text, a number, a formula, an error, or nothing. `C100` may be empty in a tiny practice file, but it is still a valid location in normal spreadsheet programs.

### Active cell versus cell contents

The [[active-cell|active cell]] is the current cell to which entry or editing actions are directed. It is often outlined more clearly than neighboring cells. When a range is selected, one cell in that range is generally the active cell. The active location can change when you click or use navigation controls, even when you have not changed any actual data.

In Excel and LibreOffice Calc, the [[name-box|Name Box]] commonly displays the active cell reference. Google Sheets also has a Name Box for addresses and named ranges in its desktop interface. Interface positions can vary. A [[formula-bar|formula bar]] is a separate area that often displays the underlying content or formula of the active cell. Beginners should not confuse these two controls.

| Interface element | Typical purpose | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name Box | Shows a cell address or helps navigate to one | `B3` |
| Formula bar | Shows or edits the cell's underlying entry | `=B2*C2` in a calculated example |
| Grid cell | Shows an entered or calculated result | `6.00` |
| Sheet tab | Switches to another worksheet | Supplies or Schedule |

The visible result and the underlying content may differ. A cell may display `$6.00` because the stored number `6` has currency formatting, or because a formula calculates 6. You will study this more closely in later lessons. Here, the important distinction is that a **cell reference is a location, not a guarantee about its content**.

### A blank cell still has an address

An [[empty-cell|empty cell]] is a location with no entered content. Blank does **not** always mean zero, no, none, or not applicable. In a list, a blank status might mean “not checked yet,” “unknown,” or “accidentally omitted.” The meaning must come from the worksheet's instructions or the people managing it.

| Cell | Entry | What you can safely conclude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `B4` | `1` | A numeric entry of one in this sample |
| `D4` | `Check` | An entered instruction/status label |
| `E4` | Blank | No visible entered value; its significance is unspecified |

Similarly, the visual size of a cell does not determine its address. Making column A wider still leaves `A3` in column A, row 3. A display change is not a new coordinate.

### Cell addresses across different sheets

If a workbook includes sheets named **Supplies** and **Schedule**, each sheet has its own `A1`. A cross-sheet reference or written instruction should specify the sheet when ambiguity is possible. For example, a person might say “Open the Supplies tab and inspect B3.” Formal cross-sheet formula syntax varies and is reserved for later instruction.

## 4. How do you navigate between cells?

[[navigation|Navigation]] means moving your focus or active location around the worksheet. You may use a pointing device, keyboard, touch controls, or assistive technology. The central operation is the same: locate the intended cell and verify its address before editing it.

### Use a mouse or trackpad

In a typical desktop spreadsheet:

1. Open a new, empty practice sheet or a file you are authorized to use.
2. Find the column letters across the top and row numbers down the left.
3. Move the pointer to the intersection of column B and row 3.
4. Click the cell once. It should become the active cell, commonly outlined.
5. Check the Name Box or another displayed cell reference if available; it should indicate `B3`.
6. Look at the cell contents without typing. Selecting a cell does not require changing it.

If you double-click, begin typing, or activate an edit command, you may enter [[edit-mode|edit mode]] rather than simply select the cell. For an observation activity, single-click the cell and avoid changing its content.

### Use the keyboard

The basic keyboard actions below work in many desktop spreadsheet programs when the worksheet grid has focus. Exact behavior may depend on your selection, editing state, accessibility settings, and application.

| Action | Typical result in the grid | Check before relying on it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Right Arrow | Moves one cell to the right | Some editing contexts move a text cursor instead |
| Left Arrow | Moves one cell to the left | Confirm grid focus |
| Down Arrow | Moves one cell downward | May navigate a menu if a menu is open |
| Up Arrow | Moves one cell upward | May navigate suggestions or menus in edit mode |
| Tab | Usually moves one cell to the right | Application-specific behavior can vary |
| Enter | Usually completes entry and moves the selection, often downward | Direction can be customized |
| Escape | Often cancels an edit or closes a selection/menu | Effects vary with current state |

You can test the arrow keys on an empty sheet without typing anything. Start at `B3`: Right Arrow usually moves to `C3`, Down Arrow to `C4`, and Left Arrow to `B4`. These are *movement* actions, not calculations or data changes.

### Jump directly to an address

In desktop Excel and Calc, an available Name Box can often be used to jump to a cell by entering an address such as `H12` and confirming. Google Sheets also provides address-based navigation in its desktop interface. If a Name Box is not evident, use the application's Go To or navigation controls, or move using the keyboard. On mobile layouts, these controls may appear differently.

A safe example is to type `D5` into the Name Box of a **new blank practice worksheet**, confirm, and inspect which cell is selected. Do not type `D5` *into a grid cell* when your intention is to navigate; that would store the text instead of moving to the address.

### Scrolling versus changing the active cell

[[scrolling|Scrolling]] moves your view across or down a worksheet. Scrolling may reveal more rows without changing which cell is active. You can scroll until row 100 is visible while the active cell remains at a different address. Conversely, moving the selection with a keyboard can bring another part of the sheet into view.

| What you do | What generally changes | What may stay unchanged |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scroll downward | Which rows are visible | Active cell and cell contents |
| Click `C4` | Active cell | Existing values in other cells |
| Widen column C | Column display width | Cell addresses and underlying data |
| Switch worksheet tabs | Active worksheet and visible grid | Other worksheets' stored contents |

### Accessibility and confirmation

When using a [[screen-reader|screen reader]], the application can announce cell addresses and contents as you navigate. Microsoft provides screen-reader procedures for Excel; Google provides specific help for Sheets. Keyboard-only users should ensure the focus is in the grid before using arrow keys. Screen magnification or higher zoom can make content easier to see without changing actual row and column addresses.

A reliable habit is to say to yourself, “I am in column C, row 4; this is the price for folders,” **before** making an edit. That short location-and-meaning check prevents many accidental changes.

## 5. How do you select cells and ranges?

A [[selection|selection]] identifies the cells an operation would affect. Selecting one cell is a small target. Selecting a row, column, or block of cells can affect many locations if you later delete, paste, format, or overwrite data. Before using a command, verify the size of the selection.

### One cell

A one-cell selection identifies a single address, such as `B3`. It can be made by clicking that cell, navigating to it, or entering its location through a supported navigation control.

### A rectangular range

A [[range|range]] is a group of cells. In A1 notation, `B2:D5` normally names a rectangle whose top-left corner is `B2` and whose bottom-right corner is `D5`. The colon means **through**, including both endpoints and the cells between them. This range contains three columns—B, C, and D—and four rows—2, 3, 4, and 5—for **12 cells** total.

| Row in range | Column B | Column C | Column D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | `B2` | `C2` | `D2` |
| 3 | `B3` | `C3` | `D3` |
| 4 | `B4` | `C4` | `D4` |
| 5 | `B5` | `C5` | `D5` |

In many desktop applications, you can select adjacent cells by clicking the first cell and dragging to the last, or by clicking a starting cell and extending the selection with Shift plus arrow keys. Keyboard behavior depends on focus and editing state. On touchscreens, use the application's selection handles or touch controls.

### Compare common selection targets

| Target | Meaning | Scope of the selection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `B3` | One cell | Exactly one location |
| `B2:B5` | Vertical range | Four cells in column B |
| `A3:D3` | Horizontal range | Four cells in row 3 |
| `B2:D5` | Rectangular range | Twelve cells |
| Entire column B | Column-heading selection | May include many cells beyond the visible data |
| Entire row 3 | Row-heading selection | May include many cells beyond visible data |

A full-column selection and a short range such as `B1:B5` are **not** equivalent. The first can cover a very large number of cells, even though you can see only a few rows. The same distinction applies to an entire row compared with `A3:D3`.

### The active cell within a selection

If you select `B2:D5`, the rectangle is selected as a group, but the application still typically has an [[active-cell|active cell]] within that group. Entering data without checking the selected target can overwrite the active cell or—depending on the operation—apply a change to the selected group. This is why a beginner should practice selection separately from entering data.

### Whole row and whole column selection

In many desktop apps, selecting the built-in row number, such as **3** at the left, selects row 3. Selecting the built-in column letter, such as **B** above the grid, selects column B. Use caution: clicking the contents of `B1` selects that one cell; clicking the column heading **B** selects the column. Their appearance may be close, but their meanings differ.

Some keyboard shortcuts can select rows or columns, but details differ among products, platforms, and editing contexts. The simplest beginner method is to identify the built-in heading and visually confirm the highlighted region without entering a destructive command.

### What a selection does not mean

Selection is not automatically deletion, copying, or moving. Selecting `B2:D5` usually does not change underlying data. But **later actions**—typing, pasting, clearing, merging, sorting, or formatting—can change the selected region. A selection is therefore a decision about the possible scope of the *next* operation.

> **Safety check:** Before pressing Delete, pasting, or changing a large region, ask: “Did I select only the cells I intend to change?” If unsure, press Escape when appropriate and select the exact target again.

## 6. What makes a grid reliable and readable?

The grid itself provides addresses, but people must give those addresses useful meaning. A spreadsheet with inconsistent headings, mixed units, ambiguous blanks, or accidental merged cells is harder to understand and easier to misuse. This section introduces practical conventions that support future lessons on formatting, formulas, sorting, and error checking.

### Begin with meaningful labels

In a simple list, row 1 often serves as a [[header-row|header row]], holding user-entered labels such as **Item**, **Quantity**, **Unit price**, and **Status**. A good header names what the column represents. “Quantity” is clearer than “Q”; “Unit price (USD)” is clearer than “Money” if currency and meaning matter.

For a list of similar items, a reliable beginner structure has one item per data row, one main category per column, and consistent meanings for values. That helps a human read the sheet and makes later sorting or calculation more predictable.

| Design choice | Better beginner approach | Problem it helps prevent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Column labels | Name each column clearly | Readers guessing what a number means |
| Row organization | Keep one record per row where appropriate | Unrelated details mixed together |
| Units | Specify dollars, kilograms, hours, etc. | Comparing unlike quantities |
| Missing information | Decide what blanks mean | Assuming unknown means zero |
| Data types | Keep quantities numeric in quantity fields | Text appearing where calculations expect numbers |
| Editing | Check location before making changes | Replacing the wrong cell |

### Distinguish blank cells from hidden information

A cell may appear empty because it has no entry, because content is hidden by formatting or display limitations, or because you are viewing only part of the sheet. A [[hidden-row|hidden row]] or [[hidden-column|hidden column]] can keep information out of view without deleting it. A [[filter|filter]] can also temporarily hide records. Later lessons explain these tools in depth. For now, never assume that what is currently visible represents the entire stored dataset.

A column that looks empty on the screen may also have information farther down. Before deleting a whole column, verify whether other people have used it elsewhere in the sheet.

### Inserting and deleting are structural changes

[[insert-row|Inserting a row]] creates an additional row at a location in the grid, shifting existing content to make room. [[delete-row|Deleting a row]] removes a worksheet row and shifts remaining rows. Similar operations exist for columns. Deleting an entire row or column differs from clearing one cell's contents, and may also affect formulas and references in the workbook. Exact effects depend on the operation and the application.

Suppose **Notebooks** is in `A3`. If you insert a worksheet row immediately above row 3, the notebook entry will usually move to row 4. A remembered instruction to “check `A3` for notebooks” is now outdated. This is why a cell address describes a location, not permanent identity.

### Resizing, wrapping, and merging are different

[[column-width|Column width]] changes how much horizontal room a column occupies on screen. [[row-height|Row height]] changes vertical display space. [[wrap-text|Wrapping text]] allows a long entry to occupy multiple lines within its cell. These presentation adjustments ordinarily do not change the cell's address.

[[merge-cells|Merging cells]] combines a selected block into one displayed cell area. Merging can complicate selection, navigation, copying, sorting, and assistive reading; depending on the application and action, some existing values can be lost. Avoid merging a beginner's data table just to make it look attractive. For a title, a separate title row or simpler formatting is usually preferable.

### Freeze is not delete, and hide is not remove

[[freeze-panes|Freezing]] a header row or column keeps it visible while you scroll; it does not move, duplicate, or delete the underlying data. Hiding a row or column conceals it from the current view but does not normally delete it. Understanding the difference is vital when a worksheet appears to skip row numbers or column letters.

| Action | What it usually changes | Does it normally delete cell data? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resize a column | Display width | No |
| Wrap text | Display arrangement | No |
| Freeze header row | Scrolling behavior | No |
| Hide a row | Visibility | No |
| Clear selected contents | Stored entries in selected cells | Yes, for affected entries |
| Delete an entire row | Worksheet structure and positions | Yes, for that row's contents |
| Merge cells | Cell structure/display | May remove entries in some cells; avoid on populated data |

### Privacy, sharing, and mistake recovery

Practice with **fictional** records and do not enter actual names, addresses, passwords, or financial details unless you are authorized and the task requires them. Before editing a real shared workbook, confirm whose information you are viewing, what changes you are allowed to make, and whether collaborators are working on it.

An [[undo|Undo]] command may reverse recent changes, but available history differs by application and session. Version history, backups, and saved copies can help with recovery, yet none should be assumed to exist. For learning, work in a separate practice document rather than depending on recovery from an error in an important file.

> **Reliable operating habit:** **Locate → Confirm → Select → Act → Verify.** Know the address, confirm what belongs there, verify the selected scope, perform only the intended action, and inspect the result.

## 7. Guided practice: Locate and select information

All entries below are fictional. Complete the exercises on paper, in a notebook, or in a new blank spreadsheet. You do not need to create an account, upload a file, enter real contact details, or use formula functions. If you use software, do not perform the optional structural-change exercise in a file that matters.

### Activity 1: Point to rows, columns, and cells (about 8–10 minutes)

Use the teaching grid below. Read the row numbers on the left and the column letters across the top. Then answer the questions before revealing the responses.

| Row | A | B | C | D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Product | Units | Shelf | Checked |
| 2 | Paper | 4 | A | Yes |
| 3 | Tape | 2 | B | No |
| 4 | Labels | 6 | A | Yes |
| 5 | Markers | 3 | C | No |

1. Which direction does row 4 run?
2. Which direction does column C run?
3. What is written in `A3`?
4. What is written in `B4`?
5. What is written in `D5`?
6. Does the word **Product** rename column A, or is it a value in `A1`?

:::answer Show Activity 1 answers
1. Row 4 runs horizontally from left to right.
2. Column C runs vertically from top to bottom.
3. `A3` contains **Tape**.
4. `B4` contains **6**.
5. `D5` contains **No**.
6. **Product** is the text in cell `A1`; the built-in column is still A.

:::

### Activity 2: Decode and create addresses (about 10 minutes)

Write the column and row separately for each address. Then write an address for each verbal description.

| Prompt | Your response |
| --- | --- |
| `C7` | Column ____; row ____ |
| `A12` | Column ____; row ____ |
| `AA4` | Column ____; row ____ |
| `D21` | Column ____; row ____ |
| Column B, row 9 | ____ |
| Column E, row 3 | ____ |
| Column AB, row 15 | ____ |

:::answer Show Activity 2 answers
1. `C7` means column C, row 7.
2. `A12` means column A, row 12.
3. `AA4` means column AA, row 4.
4. `D21` means column D, row 21.
5. Column B, row 9 is `B9`.
6. Column E, row 3 is `E3`.
7. Column AB, row 15 is `AB15`.

:::

### Activity 3: Trace a navigation path (about 10 minutes)

On a paper grid or a **new blank practice worksheet**, start at `B3`. Imagine that the worksheet has focus and that each arrow key moves exactly one cell. Trace this sequence: **Right, Right, Down, Left, Down, Left**. Write the address after every move and identify the final address.

| Step | Movement | Address afterward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start | None | `B3` |
| 1 | Right | ____ |
| 2 | Right | ____ |
| 3 | Down | ____ |
| 4 | Left | ____ |
| 5 | Down | ____ |
| 6 | Left | ____ |

:::answer Show Activity 3 navigation trace
1. Right from `B3` gives `C3`.
2. Right from `C3` gives `D3`.
3. Down from `D3` gives `D4`.
4. Left from `D4` gives `C4`.
5. Down from `C4` gives `C5`.
6. Left from `C5` gives `B5`, the final address.

## 8. Common misconceptions

### “A row goes up and down.”

No. A row runs **left to right**. A column runs **top to bottom**. If you are unsure, read the built-in headings: row numbers appear along the side; column letters appear across the top.

### “B3 means row B and column 3.”

In the usual A1 reference style, **B is the column and 3 is the row**. The letter comes first. Different reference systems exist, but this beginner lesson uses the common A1 notation.

### “A label typed into B1 changes the name of column B.”

No. If you enter **Quantity** into `B1`, that word becomes cell data. The application still identifies the column as B.

### “Row 1 is always the first data record.”

Not necessarily. A well-organized list frequently places column labels in row 1 and starts records in row 2. Other worksheets may use several title or instruction rows. Read the layout rather than assuming.

### “If I scroll to row 100, row 100 becomes the active row.”

Scrolling changes the view. It does not necessarily change the active-cell address. Check the selection indicator when you intend to edit.

### “A selected cell must already contain data.”

An empty cell still has an address and can be selected. Its emptiness does not remove it from the grid.

### “Selecting B2:D5 means selecting only the two corner cells.”

No. The colon specifies the inclusive rectangle from `B2` through `D5`. It includes all twelve cells inside the rectangle.

### “Clicking B at the top is the same as clicking B1.”

No. Clicking the built-in column heading B typically selects the entire column, while clicking the grid cell `B1` selects only that cell.

### “Hiding a row permanently deletes its information.”

Hiding changes what is visible, not normally what is stored. A filtered view may also conceal records. To know whether data was removed, inspect the actual file and relevant controls.

### “Inserting a row never affects addresses.”

Inserting a row can move existing content to new row positions. An item previously in `A3` can appear in `A4` afterward. Check references after structural changes.

### “A merged cell is always harmless.”

Merging can interfere with data operations and may discard values in some applications or circumstances. Avoid merging populated data cells in a simple record list.

### “A blank quantity is the same thing as zero.”

An unfilled quantity might mean unknown, pending, not applicable, or forgotten. Do not turn missing information into a confirmed zero without evidence.

### “The sheet on my screen contains all the data there is.”

Data may extend beyond the visible window or lie on other worksheet tabs. Hidden rows, hidden columns, and filters may conceal additional entries.

### “I can experiment safely by deleting rows in an important workbook.”

Do not experiment destructively with real records. Use a new practice file; verify selection scope; check permissions and recovery options before changing valuable data.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The expressions marked with dotted-underlined tooltips in the lesson can display short definitions in the MGU dashboard. The definitions below are available for readers whose device or viewing mode does not show tooltips. The final `:::terms` block supplies the machine-readable tooltip definitions.

### Grid coordinates and structure

- **Spreadsheet:** Digital document for organizing and calculating information in a grid.
- **Worksheet:** One sheet or grid within a spreadsheet document.
- **Workbook:** The overall Excel spreadsheet file that can contain multiple worksheets.
- **Grid:** The intersecting layout of rows and columns.
- **Row:** A horizontal line of cells, usually identified by a number.
- **Column:** A vertical line of cells, usually identified by a letter or letters.
- **Cell:** A single location at a row-column intersection.
- **Row heading:** A built-in number identifying an entire worksheet row.
- **Column heading:** A built-in letter label identifying an entire worksheet column.
- **Cell reference:** A cell's address, such as `C4`, in the common A1 system.
- **A1 notation:** Cell reference style using column letters followed by row numbers.
- **Multiple-letter column:** A column label such as `AA` or `AB`, extending beyond Z.
- **Header row:** A user-entered row of labels identifying the meaning of data columns.
- **Record:** A represented item or event, often occupying one row in a consistent data list.
- **Field:** One category of information, often represented by a data column.
- **Sheet tab:** A control used to select which worksheet is visible.

### Selecting and moving

- **Active cell:** The cell currently designated for entry or editing action.
- **Selection:** A cell or group of cells targeted for an operation.
- **Range:** A group of cell addresses, often written using two corners joined by a colon.
- **Adjacent cells:** Cells next to one another in the worksheet grid.
- **Name Box:** An interface area used to identify or navigate to an address or named range.
- **Formula bar:** Interface area where underlying cell contents or formulas can be viewed or edited.
- **Navigation:** Moving between cells, worksheets, and interface regions.
- **Scrolling:** Moving the displayed view without necessarily changing the active cell.
- **Grid focus:** A state in which keyboard commands act on the worksheet rather than another control.
- **Edit mode:** A state in which typing changes the content of a cell.
- **Keyboard shortcut:** A key combination that triggers an application action.
- **Screen reader:** Assistive software that announces interface controls and text.
- **Zoom:** A change to display scale that does not change a cell's address.

### Layout, visibility, and structural changes

- **Empty cell:** A grid location with no entered value.
- **Column width:** The horizontal display size of a worksheet column.
- **Row height:** The vertical display size of a worksheet row.
- **Wrap text:** Displaying a longer entry on multiple lines within a cell.
- **Merged cells:** A combined displayed cell area formed from multiple cells.
- **Hidden row:** A row concealed from view without normally being removed.
- **Hidden column:** A column concealed from view without normally being removed.
- **Freeze panes:** Keeping designated rows or columns visible while scrolling.
- **Filter:** Showing records matching conditions while concealing others in the view.
- **Insert row:** Add a worksheet row and shift existing rows as the application specifies.
- **Delete row:** Remove a worksheet row and shift remaining rows.
- **Insert column:** Add a worksheet column and shift existing columns.
- **Delete column:** Remove a worksheet column and shift remaining columns.
- **Clear contents:** Remove data from selected cells without necessarily removing the worksheet cells themselves.
- **Undo:** Reverse a recent action when supported and available.
- **Backup:** An additional retained copy intended for recovery.
- **Version history:** Previous recorded document states, if supported and available.
- **Sharing permission:** A rule governing who can view or change a file.

### Content and consistency

- **Data:** Recorded information used within a worksheet.
- **Text entry:** Words or labels stored in a cell.
- **Numeric entry:** A number stored for potential calculation.
- **Formula:** An expression that calculates a result, often using cell references.
- **Formatting:** Changing the appearance of cells or their displayed values.
- **Data consistency:** Using the same meanings and value types for comparable entries.
- **Missing value:** Information that has not been recorded or cannot be determined from the current cell.
- **Data validation:** Rules designed to restrict or check values entered in cells.
