## Lesson 7: User Accounts and Passwords

A computer can be used by one person, shared by a household, or managed by a school or workplace. In each setting, the device or website needs a way to recognize the person using it and decide what that person is allowed to do. A user account provides that identity and its associated settings. Passwords, passkeys, and other sign-in methods help protect it. This lesson explains these ideas in plain language and teaches everyday habits for using accounts safely—without asking you to reveal, create, or enter a real password during practice.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 70–90 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–6 recommended · **Practice equipment:** Any desktop, laptop, tablet, or smartphone (optional; all practice can be completed on paper)

> **Essential idea:** A [[user-account|user account]] identifies a person or an authorized user of a computer or online service. [[authentication|Authentication]] checks that the person signing in is entitled to use the account. A strong, unique [[password|password]] or supported [[passkey|passkey]] helps protect access; [[multi-factor-authentication|multifactor authentication (MFA)]] can add a further safeguard. **Never share real passwords, verification codes, or recovery codes in a lesson, message, or unfamiliar form.**

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish a computer/device account from an account used on a website or in an app.
2. Describe the basic sign-in process and the difference between a username, password, PIN, and profile.
3. Explain why long, unique passwords and reputable password managers are recommended.
4. Recognize common forms of multifactor authentication and the basic idea of a passkey.
5. Choose when to lock a device, sign out of an account, or use a separate user account.
6. Identify a safe recovery route and recognize attempts to steal credentials or verification codes.
7. Apply basic account-security habits to realistic, fictional situations without exposing personal information.

## 1. What is a user account?

A [[user-account|user account]] is a record that connects a person or another authorized user to a computer, application, or online service. It may contain a username, sign-in methods, permissions, preferences, and connections to that person's files or information. An account does not have to display all these details at once. Its purpose is to help a system provide the right environment and access to the right person.

Imagine two people using a household laptop. Each person might have a separate sign-in name, wallpaper, saved documents, and browser settings. A separate account helps keep their activities organized and limits accidental access to one another's material. This separation is useful, but it is not automatically a guarantee of privacy against the device's owner, administrator, malware, or someone with unrestricted physical access.

### Device accounts and online accounts

A **device account** is used to access a computer, tablet, or phone. On a Windows laptop, for example, the sign-in area may ask which user wishes to open their desktop. A **service account** is used to access an email service, a shopping website, an online classroom, or another app. These account types can be related, but they are not identical.

Some devices permit a locally managed account that works without connecting to a particular company's online account. Other setups use an online identity to sync settings, files, or purchases across devices. When you sign in to an app on a computer, you may be signing into the app's service rather than changing the main user of the computer. A single device can therefore have one signed-in computer user and several different website or app accounts.

For example, a learner might unlock a laptop, open a browser, and sign in to an online learning portal. These are potentially **two separate sign-in events**. Closing the browser does not necessarily sign the learner out of the portal, and signing out of the portal does not necessarily lock the laptop.

### Username, email address, profile, and account

A [[username|username]] is a name or identifier used to select an account. Some services use an email address as the username; others assign an account name or employee or student identifier. A [[profile|profile]] is the collection of settings and information associated with a user, such as display name, preferred language, or saved preferences. The username identifies the account; the profile describes aspects of that user's experience.

Do not assume the username is private. An email address used as a username may be publicly known, so the system should still require an appropriate sign-in method. Likewise, a visible profile name does not by itself prove who controls the account. Two people can have the same display name on different services.

### Accounts and permissions are different questions

[[authentication|Authentication]] asks, “Can the system verify that this is the right user?” [[authorization|Authorization]] asks, “What is this user allowed to do?” The second question is enforced through [[access-control|access control]] and account [[permissions|permissions]]. Being able to sign in to a device does not mean you may install any software, view every file, or change settings belonging to another person.

An [[administrator-account|administrator account]] may have permission to install applications, manage certain system settings, or add other users. A [[standard-account|standard account]] typically has fewer privileges. Schools and workplaces often restrict changes to protect equipment and other users. For everyday tasks, a standard account is commonly a safer default because unnecessary administrative access increases the consequences of mistakes or malicious software.

*Common account and permission types*

| Type | What it usually gives access to | Beginner example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Device user account | A device session and its allowed files and settings | Signing in to a family laptop |
| Online service account | A particular website or service | Accessing an email inbox |
| Standard device account | Everyday tasks with restricted system changes | Typing a document without installing software |
| Administrator account | Broader device-management permissions | Adding a user or changing protected settings |
| Guest or temporary access | Limited use, where the device supports it | A visitor using an approved guest session |

The names and capabilities of these account types vary. Not every device has a guest mode, and work or school devices may be managed under additional policies. When in doubt, ask an authorized administrator rather than trying to bypass a restriction.

> **Remember:** Your computer account, browser profile, and online classroom account may be three different things. Learn which one you are entering or leaving before typing credentials or sharing a device.

## 2. How do you sign in to an account?

[[sign-in|Signing in]] (also called **logging in**) is the process of proving that you are permitted to use a particular account. A sign-in screen may ask for an account name and a password, or it may offer a supported PIN, passkey, security key, or biometric method. Exact options depend on the computer, website, organization, and how the account was configured.

### The ordinary sign-in sequence

A safe beginner sequence for a website or app is:

1. Open the correct app or go to the official site using a trusted bookmark or an address you already know. Avoid arriving through a suspicious message's urgent sign-in link.
2. Check which service and account you are signing into. Confirm you are not on another person's account or a look-alike page.
3. Enter the requested [[username|username]] or email identifier, if required.
4. Use the approved [[credential|credential]]—for example, a password, passkey, or other sign-in method.
5. Complete any additional verification only when **you initiated** the sign-in and the request matches the service and action you intended.
6. Confirm that you reached the expected account. Do not continue if a page unexpectedly asks for unrelated personal or payment information.

This is a general process, not a claim that every website has the same screens. Some services remember the username; others present a choice of linked accounts. A workplace may have a central sign-in page that authenticates users for several approved applications.

### Why passwords appear as dots

Many sign-in fields hide typed password characters behind dots or asterisks to reduce casual observation. The hidden characters still exist as what you entered; the dots are a display feature, not a different password. Some pages have a Show password control that can help check a typing error when nobody else can see the screen. Avoid exposing the password in public, in screenshots, during screen sharing, or where someone is looking over your shoulder.

A keyboard may enter an unexpected character if Caps Lock is on or the keyboard layout is different. On a mobile device, the on-screen keyboard and automatic capitalization may behave differently. If a sign-in fails, inspect the account name and typing carefully rather than repeatedly trying random variations.

### Password, PIN, and biometrics

A [[password|password]] is a secret used for account access. A [[pin|personal identification number (PIN)]] is often a shorter secret used to unlock a particular device or activate a protected sign-in method. A device PIN is **not always sent to the same online service as an account password**. In many modern configurations, a PIN unlocks a local device or cryptographic key while the underlying online account is protected by other mechanisms. Do not assume that a short device PIN is suitable as a password on an unrelated website.

[[biometric-authentication|Biometric authentication]] uses a physical characteristic, such as a fingerprint or face scan, as part of a device or sign-in process. This can be convenient but is not infallible. Devices typically provide a fallback method. A fingerprint scan used to unlock a phone should not be described as a replacement for every password on every site; the phone or app decides what the scan authorizes.

### One account, more than one sign-in method

An account may support several ways to sign in. You might be able to use a password with multifactor authentication, a passkey, a hardware security key, or a managed school sign-in method. Some options are set up by the account holder; others are controlled by an organization. Only add methods using an authentic settings page or a verified enrollment process.

### Authentication versus access

A signed-in user may see a document but not be allowed to edit it. A student may be able to view course materials but not change a teacher's grades. This is an example of [[authorization|authorization]]. Signing in establishes an identity, while the service or device decides what that identity can do. The MGU Encyclopedia explains the distinction in [Authentication Systems](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/authentication-systems) and [Access Control](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/access-control).

> **Quick identification:** “Who are you?” is an identity question. “Can you change this setting?” is a permission question. They are connected, but they are not the same.

## 3. How do you create and manage strong passwords?

A [[password|password]] is a secret that protects an account when the account uses password-based sign-in. Good password practices reduce the chance that someone can guess a password or reuse one stolen from another service. They cannot prevent every attack: people can still be tricked into giving a password to a fake site, or a compromised device can be unsafe. Account protection therefore needs several habits working together.

### Length and uniqueness matter most

For accounts that require a password, use a **long, unique** password. When you must invent and remember a password yourself, a length of **at least 15 characters** is a useful modern target, in line with NIST's public guidance for passwords. Some services have different technical requirements; follow the service's actual limits while favoring more length and uniqueness wherever possible.

A [[passphrase|passphrase]] is a password made from multiple words. Unrelated words can be easier to remember than a jumble of short, predictable substitutions. A password generated by a reputable manager may instead be a long random sequence of letters, digits, and other characters. Either approach can be suitable when sufficiently long, unique, and protected.

Do not use personal facts such as your birthday, street name, pet's name, or your school's name in a password. Avoid widely used phrases, predictable keyboard patterns, obvious substitutions, or a password that differs from another by a single number. A long password based on a famous quote or common phrase can still be predictable.

**Illustrative example only:** `Copper-Lantern-Orbit-River-58` is a made-up classroom example of a long multiword string. **Do not use that exact string for a real account.** Examples printed in lessons are public; they are not secrets. Never submit a genuine password to an online “strength test” merely to see whether it is strong.

### Why every account needs a different secret

Suppose someone uses the same password on a small shopping website and on their main email account. If the shopping site suffers a data breach, someone who obtains the password may try it against the email service. This is called [[credential-stuffing|credential stuffing]] when stolen username-password combinations are tested across services. A unique password for each service limits this kind of spread.

Your main email account deserves particular care because password-reset messages for other accounts may arrive there. Protect it with a unique, strong sign-in method and the strongest practical MFA or passkey option offered. Keep its recovery methods current and protected too.

### Password managers

A [[password-manager|password manager]] is software that can generate and securely store unique passwords, then help fill them into the appropriate sign-in fields. A password manager reduces the number of separate secrets you have to memorize. Some are built into a browser or operating system; others are independent applications. Their storage, synchronization, recovery, and sharing options differ.

A practical password-manager routine is to choose a trustworthy manager, understand how it is secured and recovered, enable MFA for its account when available, and let it generate separate passwords for new accounts. Do not copy an entire password vault into an ordinary document, public note, or email. Be mindful of shared computers: a manager left unlocked or signed in can expose many accounts at once.

The password used to unlock a manager, if one is required, deserves special protection. A manager does not mean you should ignore phishing warnings or approve unfamiliar sign-in requests. An unusual request for the manager's master password or a recovery phrase is a reason to stop and verify the situation.

### Should you change passwords every month?

Not simply because a calendar date has passed. Modern guidance favors changing a password when there is evidence it may be compromised, reused, disclosed, or known to someone who should not have it. An employer or service may impose its own policy, which users must follow. Frequent forced changes without a security reason can encourage predictable variations and poor habits.

Change a password promptly through the legitimate service if you suspect it was stolen, entered into a fake site, exposed in a relevant breach, or shared improperly. If it was reused elsewhere, change those other accounts to different unique passwords as well. Do not change passwords by following a link from an unexpected warning message; navigate to the service yourself.

*Password and account habits*

| Habit | Better choice | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Choosing a password | Use a long, unique password | Reduces guessing and reuse risk |
| Keeping multiple passwords | Use a reputable password manager | Reduces memorization and reuse pressure |
| Recording a classroom example | Use a fictional string only | Prevents exposure of real secrets |
| Receiving an unexpected reset email | Visit the service directly before acting | Avoids suspicious links |
| Suspecting a compromised password | Replace it using the official service | Limits continued misuse |
| Protecting your main email account | Use strong authentication and updated recovery options | Email may unlock access to other accounts |

> **Practical principle:** Password strength is about more than whether a password contains a special symbol. Longer and unique credentials, protected storage, and resistance to deception are the core priorities. For more information, see the [NIST guide to creating a good password](https://www.nist.gov/cybersecurity-and-privacy/how-do-i-create-good-password).

## 4. What are MFA and passkeys?

A password can be stolen even when it is long. [[multi-factor-authentication|Multifactor authentication (MFA)]] adds a second, independent category of verification to protect the account. A system might ask for something you **know**, something you **have**, or something you **are**, depending on its design. Not every combination is truly multifactor; two passwords are still two knowledge secrets rather than two independent factor types.

### Common verification methods

- **Something you know:** A password or other secret.
- **Something you have:** An approved authenticator app on a phone, a registered hardware security key, or another bound device.
- **Something you are:** A biometric characteristic, used within an appropriate authentication system.

An [[authenticator-app|authenticator app]] may generate time-limited codes or ask you to approve a prompt. Some services send one-time codes by text message or email. SMS codes are usually better than having no additional protection at all, but they can be intercepted or abused and are less resistant to phishing than modern passkeys or security-key-based methods. The best available option depends on the service and the devices you can reliably use.

A [[security-key|security key]] is a physical device used for supported authentication. It is not an ordinary USB flash drive containing a text file of passwords. Certain hardware security keys and standards-based passkeys can prevent a credential from being used at a fake website because the authentication is bound to the legitimate site.

### What is a passkey?

A [[passkey|passkey]] is a passwordless sign-in method using cryptographic keys. In a typical setup, the service stores a public key, while the private key stays protected by your device, authenticator, or a properly secured sync system. You may authorize sign-in by unlocking your device with a PIN, fingerprint, face recognition, or another approved method. Because the key is tied to the service's real identity, passkeys offer strong protection against many common phishing attacks.

Passkeys are not supported everywhere, and their recovery or transfer process varies. A passkey may be stored on one device, a hardware key, or in a synchronized account. Before depending on one, understand what happens if you lose or replace your device. Some accounts let you keep another recovery or sign-in method as a backup.

### When a code or approval arrives unexpectedly

Suppose your phone displays a sign-in approval request while you are **not** trying to sign in. Do not approve it. Someone may know your password or be attempting to trick you. Likewise, do not read a one-time code to a caller or send it in a chat. A verification code is a temporary secret even if the caller claims to be from technical support, the bank, or the university.

An attacker may repeatedly send approval prompts hoping that you tap **Approve** just to stop the notifications; this is sometimes called [[mfa-fatigue|MFA fatigue]]. Do not accept unfamiliar requests. Use the account's official security tools or your organization's verified support channel to investigate.

### Comparing account sign-in methods

| Method | Basic idea | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Password alone | Proves knowledge of a secret | Can be guessed, stolen, or phished |
| Password plus authenticator | Adds a separate approval or code | Never approve requests you did not initiate |
| SMS code as added factor | Sends a code to a registered phone number | Less resistant to some attacks than passkeys or security keys |
| Passkey | Uses cryptographic keys bound to the service | Plan device loss and recovery in advance |
| Supported security key | Uses a registered physical authenticator | Keep a safe backup or recovery option if available |

Not every device unlock method or sign-in prompt has the same security strength. A fingerprint that merely unlocks a local screen is not, by itself, proof that every website has enabled MFA. Learn what protection the **specific account** is using.

> **Remember:** A login code or approval should correspond to a sign-in or change **you initiated**. If the request is unexpected, deny it and check the account through the official app or site. NIST's [authentication guidance](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html) distinguishes these methods and their protections.

## 5. How do you use accounts safely on shared devices?

An account can remain open after you finish using an app. This is convenient on a private device but creates risk when a laptop, classroom computer, library terminal, or family tablet is shared. The safest choice depends on the setting: lock the device when stepping away briefly, sign out of a service after using it on a shared device, and use separate device accounts where available and appropriate.

### Lock, sign out, switch user, and shut down

**Locking** a device leaves the current user session in place but usually requires authentication before someone can continue. It is useful when you step away briefly from a computer you are authorized to use. [[sign-out|Signing out]] of a website or app ends its authenticated session when the service implements sign-out correctly. **Switch user** allows another device account to open while the first user's session may remain active. **Shutting down** turns the device off, but it should not be treated as a substitute for changing a compromised password or removing an unauthorized account session.

On many computers, pressing a lock control is not the same as signing out of a website. Closing a browser tab or window may leave a session available for later use. Even a browser's private mode does not make you invisible to a network administrator or guarantee that your activity is erased from the website's own records.

*Choosing the right account action*

| Situation | Preferred action | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stepping away from your own computer | Lock the screen | Reduces immediate access by others |
| Finishing email on a public computer | Sign out of the email service | Ends access to that account session |
| Passing a shared laptop to another household member | Use separate device accounts or approved user switching | Helps keep work and settings separate |
| Leaving a school computer at the end of a session | Follow school sign-out instructions | Reduces account exposure and preserves local policies |
| Suspecting another person knows your password | Secure the account through its official security settings | Locking alone does not remove stolen credentials |

### Windows, Mac, Chromebook, and mobile devices

On Windows, user accounts can be local or connected to a Microsoft account, depending on setup. The Start menu and account options often provide lock, sign-out, or switch-user functions, but exact wording can vary. On macOS, Lock Screen and Log Out are distinct actions; Mac user accounts can have different permissions. Chromebooks use Google-related sign-in in many setups and may offer a guest experience where permitted. On phones and tablets, a screen lock is common, while separate multiuser profiles are not available on every device model or platform.

Do not add a new account, change an administrator, enable a guest mode, or remove another person's account without permission. On workplace or school equipment, ask the designated administrator if the right control is unavailable. The goal of this lesson is understanding appropriate behavior, not changing organization policies.

### Saving passwords on a shared computer

Many browsers ask whether they should remember a password. On a computer you do not personally control, choose **not to save** it. Likewise, avoid selecting “Keep me signed in” on shared devices. If using a public computer to check an account is unavoidable, sign out when finished, close the browser, and follow posted device-use procedures. Do not rely on deleting browser history as proof that your account is secure.

If the browser has a private or incognito mode, it may reduce local retention of certain browsing information after all private windows close, but it is not a security boundary against someone viewing your screen, malware, or the service itself. Logging out remains important. A trusted personal device is preferable for high-risk tasks such as managing financial accounts, if one is available.

### Household and school situations

In a household, separate accounts can reduce accidental file changes and provide age-appropriate or role-appropriate access where supported. Shared device use should be governed by clear rules about who may install programs, access files, or use another person's account. Children should not be directed to bypass parental or administrator controls. Similarly, borrowing a school or workplace login is not a substitute for requesting proper access.

Remember that **a separate browser profile is not the same as a separate protected operating-system account**. Profiles may organize bookmarks and history, but other device users with sufficient permissions might still access the data. When privacy or separation matters, rely on the account and access controls actually provided by the device and organization.

> **Practical caution:** Use only accounts that belong to you or that you are specifically authorized to use. Do not photograph someone else's sign-in screen, share their password, or inspect their files for practice.

## 6. What if you forget your password or see a warning?

Forgetting a password can be frustrating, but it does not mean you should abandon the account or follow an unexpected message's instructions. Legitimate services often offer [[account-recovery|account recovery]] tools, such as a verified reset process, a known recovery email address, a registered phone, a recovery code, or a supported alternate authenticator. The available methods differ greatly by provider, and some accounts may not be recoverable without a previously established method.

### A safe recovery sequence

1. Go directly to the legitimate website or app through a trusted bookmark or a known address; do not start from an unexpected email or search advertisement.
2. Locate the service's normal **Forgot password**, **Can't sign in**, or account-help option, if available.
3. Follow only the recovery steps presented within the verified service or by your organization's trusted support department.
4. Verify that any message or code corresponds to **your own request**. Do not send the code or a recovery link to another person.
5. When the account is recovered, review the security settings, registered recovery methods, and active sessions if these controls are offered.
6. If the account is a school or work account, use the institution's established recovery process instead of improvising around its controls.

A [[recovery-email|recovery email]] is an address used to help regain access. It must itself be protected. A [[recovery-code|recovery code]] is a backup credential intended for account recovery, often provided once during setup. Treat recovery codes like sensitive keys: store them privately in an approved secure location, do not use them as ordinary notes or classroom examples, and never give them to a stranger. Some people use a password manager for supported recovery data; others securely store printed codes offline. Use a method appropriate to the service and your circumstances.

### Warning signs of credential theft

[[phishing|Phishing]] is an attempt to deceive someone into revealing credentials, clicking a harmful link, or approving an unauthorized action. Phishing may arrive by email, text, voice call, social message, search result, or a fake website. It sometimes looks official, includes a familiar logo, or claims there is an urgent problem with your account.

Common warning signs include an unexpected request for a password or verification code, a demand for immediate action, a sign-in link with an unfamiliar domain, threats of instant suspension, or a caller insisting that you approve a login they initiated. However, a professional-looking message can still be fraudulent, and a message without spelling errors can still be a scam.

**Do not enter credentials through a link in a suspicious message.** Instead, open the organization's known site or app independently and review notices there. If you must contact support, use the contact information from that known site, an official account statement, or a verified organizational directory—not the phone number supplied in the suspicious message.

### What to do if you entered a password into a fake page

If you believe a password or other sign-in secret was exposed, act promptly but deliberately. Use a **trusted device and the legitimate account site** to change the affected password to a new unique one. If the password was reused, replace it on those other accounts too. Enable or strengthen MFA where offered, examine recovery information for unauthorized changes, and use an available **sign out of other sessions** feature if appropriate. Report suspicious account activity to the official provider or your organization's IT or security staff.

If you disclosed a code, approved an unfamiliar sign-in, or lost a device that held passkeys, tell the provider or trusted administrator what happened. A password reset alone may not reverse all account changes or active sessions. Some services provide security alerts, devices lists, or recent sign-in history—inspect them through the real site, not an unsolicited warning. For financial or other sensitive accounts, follow the provider's official incident and fraud-reporting process.

### If you lose a phone or other authenticator

Before a device is lost, review the recovery methods offered by your important accounts. If a registered phone, hardware key, or passkey-containing device is lost, use legitimate recovery options and, where offered, remove or revoke the lost authenticator after regaining access. A service may have extra verification steps or delays to prevent account theft. Never pay an unknown person or disclose a password to someone promising to bypass the recovery process.

### Preventing accidental lockout

Keep recovery contact information current; record backup codes in a protected way if the service supplies them; and understand how your password manager or passkeys can be recovered if you change devices. You do not need to test real recovery by purposely locking yourself out. It is safer to inspect available options while already signed in, without changing anything during this beginner lesson.

> **Safety rule:** Technical support should not need you to read out a one-time code, disclose your password, or approve a sign-in you did not start. If someone requests these things, stop and independently verify the situation.

## 7. Guided practice: Protect user accounts

These activities are designed for a Windows computer, Mac, Chromebook, tablet, phone, or paper worksheet. **No activity requires a real username, password, PIN, passkey, verification code, account creation, reset, sign-out, setting change, or disclosure of private information.** Use invented examples. If you are on a shared or managed computer, observe without altering the account.

### Activity 1: Identify the account types (about 5–7 minutes)

For each example, decide whether it describes a device account, an online service account, or a profile or setting associated with an account. Some situations involve more than one layer.

1. A student chooses a user at a laptop's sign-in screen.
2. A person signs into a webmail website after opening a browser.
3. A household member changes the wallpaper shown after signing into their laptop account.
4. A user opens the same online classroom from both a phone and a computer.
5. A person chooses a different browser profile to see another set of bookmarks.

:::answer Show Activity 1 answers
1. **Device account.** The laptop is selecting a user of the computer.
2. **Online service account.** Signing into webmail authenticates access to that service.
3. **Profile or setting.** Wallpaper is usually part of the user's device environment.
4. **Online service account.** The service can be accessed from more than one device; each device may also have its own account.
5. **Browser profile.** It organizes browser information; it does not automatically replace a protected device account.
:::

### Activity 2: Follow a fictional sign-in (about 5 minutes)

Imagine opening an online learning website from a trusted bookmark. The sign-in page asks for the account name `student-example`, a password, and then an authenticator approval. Do not enter any real details.

1. What is the purpose of `student-example`?
2. What would the password help verify?
3. Why might the system require another approval?
4. If the approval appears when the fictional student is **not** signing in, what should they do?
5. Why is the website's actual address more important than a familiar-looking logo?

:::answer Show Activity 2 answers
1. It is the fictional username or account identifier.
2. It is one method of authenticating the account holder.
3. The additional step may provide multifactor protection.
4. Deny or ignore the unexpected approval and check the account through the official service.
5. A fake website may copy a genuine logo. A verified destination helps distinguish the real service from an imitation.
:::

### Activity 3: Compare password choices (about 5–7 minutes)

Read the following **public, fictional** examples. Do not use any of them as a real password. Decide which principles they illustrate rather than trying to calculate a numerical security score.

| Fictional example | What to notice |
| --- | --- |
| `password123` | A very common, predictable pattern |
| `BlueCar2026` | Shorter and built from familiar words and a year |
| `Marble-Echo-Canyon-Teapot-47` | Longer, multiword classroom example; still public and unsuitable for real use |
| One reused secret for five accounts | A single exposure could affect all five accounts |
| One long manager-generated secret per account | Different strong credentials reduce password reuse |

Answer these questions:

1. Why is adding a year to a common word often not enough?
2. Why should the long classroom passphrase still **not** be used for a real account?
3. Which habit limits damage if one website's password data is exposed?
4. What is a practical benefit of a password manager?

:::answer Show Activity 3 answers
1. Predictable words, patterns, and years are easier to guess than unique random values.
2. It appears publicly in the lesson, so it is no longer private.
3. Use a different unique secret for every account.
4. It helps generate, store, and fill separate long passwords without memorizing them all.
:::

## 8. Common misconceptions

### “My computer account and my email account are always the same.”

Not necessarily. A device may use a local account or an online identity, and your email may use an entirely separate service. Some sign-in systems are connected, but you should identify which account each screen is asking for.

### “A username is secret, so I do not need a password.”

No. Usernames and email addresses are often known to other people. Proper authentication still matters. A display name is not proof of ownership.

### “A short password is safe if it has a symbol.”

A symbol does not automatically compensate for a short, predictable password. Length, uniqueness, and resistance to common patterns are more useful principles. Use a reputable manager or a long unique passphrase where appropriate.

### “Using the same strong password everywhere is efficient and safe.”

It is efficient only until one service exposes the secret. Password reuse allows a breach at one service to threaten other accounts. Use different passwords.

### “Two passwords count as two-factor authentication.”

Not automatically. Two secrets that are both things you know are still from the same category. Proper MFA combines independent factors, as implemented by the service.

### “Every text-message verification code is completely secure.”

Text codes can add protection but are not foolproof. They can be phished, intercepted, or abused. Prefer supported phishing-resistant methods, such as passkeys or compatible security keys, when practical.

### “Closing the browser signs me out of my account.”

Not always. Many websites preserve sign-in sessions. On a shared computer, use the service's sign-out control and follow device procedures.

### “Incognito mode makes me invisible.”

No. Private browsing mainly affects certain information stored locally by the browser. It does not automatically hide activity from websites, networks, administrators, or malicious software, and it does not replace signing out.

### “Technical support needs my verification code to fix my account.”

A caller or message requesting that you hand over a one-time code or approve an unexpected sign-in is a major warning sign. Use the official support channel independently.

### “I must change every password every month even if nothing happened.”

Not generally. Modern security guidance discourages arbitrary forced password changes without evidence of compromise. Change a password when it may have been exposed or when a legitimate policy requires it, and do not reuse earlier ones.

### “Passkeys mean that I can never lose access.”

No. Passkeys improve resistance to phishing, but accounts still need appropriate recovery planning. Losing an authenticator or access to a synchronized account can create recovery challenges.

### “An administrator account is better for every task.”

No. Broader privileges are not automatically helpful for daily work. Limiting permissions reduces potential damage. Use your assigned account and seek authorized assistance for restricted tasks.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Terms marked with a dotted underline earlier in the lesson can be selected to display a brief explanation. Where a confirmed MGU Encyclopedia article is relevant, the tooltip also offers a deeper-reading link. These short definitions are restated here for review.

- **User account:** A record associating a person or authorized user with a computer, app, or service.
- **Username:** An identifier, such as a name or email address, used to select an account.
- **Profile:** Preferences and information associated with a user or account.
- **Sign-in:** The process of entering or confirming an approved method to access an account.
- **Authentication:** Verifying that a claimant is entitled to use an identity. [Authentication Systems](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/authentication-systems).
- **Authorization:** Deciding which actions an authenticated user is permitted to perform. [Access Control](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/access-control).
- **Access control:** Rules and mechanisms that regulate which users can reach particular resources. [Access Control](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/access-control).
- **Permissions:** Allowed actions, such as viewing, editing, or managing a file or device setting.
- **Administrator account:** An account with broader device-management permissions than a standard account.
- **Standard account:** An account normally intended for everyday use with restricted administrative privileges.
- **Credential:** Evidence used in sign-in, such as a password or another recognized authenticator.
- **Password:** A secret string used to authenticate access to an account.
- **PIN:** A personal identification number often used to unlock or activate a device-based sign-in method.
- **Biometric authentication:** Recognition of a physical characteristic as part of an authentication process. [Authentication Systems](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/authentication-systems).
- **Passphrase:** A long password formed from multiple words or other memorable elements.
- **Password manager:** Software that generates, stores, and may fill credentials securely.
- **Credential stuffing:** Trying stolen account credentials against other services, often exploiting password reuse.
- **Multifactor authentication (MFA):** Sign-in that combines independent authentication factor categories. [Authentication Systems](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/authentication-systems).
- **Authenticator app:** An application that provides time-limited codes or supported authentication approvals.
- **Security key:** A physical authenticator registered for use with supported accounts.
- **Passkey:** A cryptographic, site-bound sign-in method that does not require typing a reusable website password.
- **MFA fatigue:** Repeated unexpected authentication prompts intended to pressure someone into accepting one.
- **Sign-out:** An action intended to end a service or device account session.
- **Account recovery:** A provider's process for restoring access when ordinary sign-in is unavailable.
- **Recovery email:** A contact address used by a service to assist with account recovery.
- **Recovery code:** A protected backup credential provided to support account recovery.
- **Phishing:** Deceptive attempts to obtain credentials or cause unsafe account actions. [Computer Security](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/computer-security).
