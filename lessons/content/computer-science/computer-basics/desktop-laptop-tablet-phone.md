## Lesson 3: Desktop, Laptop, Tablet, Phone

Computers come in different shapes because people use them in different places and for different purposes. A desktop may stay on an office desk, a laptop may travel to a classroom, a tablet may be used while standing, and a smartphone may fit in a pocket. All four are computers, but their screens, controls, power sources, accessories, and software can differ. This lesson will help you recognize each type and choose an appropriate device for an everyday task.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 60–85 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lessons 1 and 2 recommended · **Practice equipment:** Any desktop, laptop, tablet, or smartphone (optional; no purchases, downloads, or account sign-ins required)

> **Essential idea:** A desktop, laptop, tablet, and smartphone can all receive information, run programs, display results, and save files. The main practical differences are **portability, screen size, input methods, power arrangements, available connections, and software compatibility**. No one type is best for every task.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Recognize a desktop, laptop, tablet, and smartphone by their typical physical features.
1. Describe the main strengths and limitations of each type without assuming that every model works the same way.
1. Explain how keyboards, pointing devices, touchscreens, cameras, and microphones affect how you use a computer.
1. Distinguish the device itself from the operating system, apps, internet connection, and mobile phone service.
1. Compare devices for typing, studying, video calls, reading, travel, and other everyday activities.
1. Identify basic charging, connection, privacy, and physical-care considerations across devices.
1. Recommend a suitable device for a particular task and explain the trade-offs.

## 1. What do the four device types have in common?

A [[computer|computer]] is a programmable machine that works with information according to instructions. **Desktop computers, laptops, tablets, and smartphones** all belong to the broad family of personal computing devices. You can use them to write, calculate, display pictures, play sound, communicate, and save information. Their hardware arrangements differ, but the fundamental idea from Lesson 1 remains the same: **input, processing, output, and storage** work together.

For example, you can prepare a short message on any of these devices:

1. **Input:** You type using a physical or on-screen keyboard, or you dictate using a microphone.
1. **Processing:** The computer and its app handle the text you enter.
1. **Output:** The words appear on the screen.
1. **Storage:** The message may be saved as a draft or file, depending on the app and its settings.

The important difference is **how you interact with the machine**, not whether the machine counts as a computer.

### Four types at a glance

| Type | Typical form | Common input method | Typical power arrangement | Everyday example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Desktop** | Computer stationed at a desk, often with separate display and accessories | Physical keyboard and mouse | Usually connected to a wall outlet | Writing reports at a fixed workstation |
| **Laptop** | Portable computer with folding screen and built-in keyboard | Physical keyboard and touchpad | Rechargeable battery and power adapter | Studying or working in different locations |
| **Tablet** | Thin, portable computer built around a touch display | Touchscreen; optional stylus or keyboard | Rechargeable battery | Reading, drawing, completing forms, viewing lessons |
| **Smartphone** | Pocket-sized mobile computer with telephone capabilities | Touchscreen, microphone, and other sensors | Rechargeable battery | Calls, messages, navigation, photos, quick online tasks |

These are **typical characteristics**, not rigid rules. A desktop can have a touchscreen; a tablet can use a keyboard; a laptop can turn into a tablet-style device; and a smartphone can connect to a larger display or external keyboard. The boundaries between categories can overlap.

### Hardware, software, and the connection are different things

The physical device is [[hardware|hardware]], and the screen and speakers are examples of [[output-device|output hardware]]. The [[operating-system|operating system]] and [[app|applications (apps)]] are [[software|software]]. The [[internet|internet]] is a network of interconnected networks, not a computer type. A device may work without an internet connection for tasks supported by its installed software and saved files.

For example, a smartphone can be used as an offline calculator. A desktop can use the internet when properly connected. Having a phone shape does not make internet service automatic; having a desktop shape does not prevent internet access.

> **Remember:** A smartphone is a computer, even though we usually call it a phone. A tablet is also a computer, even if it has no physical keyboard attached.

### Why do the types exist?

Different activities create different practical needs. A large screen may help with a complicated spreadsheet; a small device may be easier to carry; a battery helps when you cannot stay beside an outlet. Manufacturers make trade-offs among size, comfort, durability, performance, battery life, and cost. The best category depends on what you need to accomplish.

## 2. What is a desktop computer?

A [[desktop|desktop computer]] is a personal computer intended mainly for use in one established location, such as a desk, home study area, reception counter, or office workstation. Its components may be arranged in a separate case or combined with a screen. Desktops are often used for long working sessions because a full-size screen, keyboard, and pointing device can provide a comfortable workspace.

### Recognizing a desktop

A common desktop arrangement includes:

- A **computer case** (sometimes called a tower or system unit), containing important processing and storage components.
- A separate **monitor** that displays the computer's output.
- A physical **keyboard** and **mouse** for entering information and selecting items.
- A **power connection**, plus any needed display, accessory, audio, and network connections.

However, not every desktop has a visible tower. An [[all-in-one|all-in-one computer]] places many internal computer components in the same enclosure as the monitor. A small desktop computer might use a compact box. A monitor by itself is not necessarily the entire computer.

### When a desktop is useful

Consider a student preparing a long research report. A large display can show reading material beside a document. A full-size keyboard makes extensive typing more convenient. Attached [[peripheral|peripheral devices]] such as a printer, external drive, or second display may also be useful when supported by the system.

Desktops can be suitable for office administration, regular study sessions, detailed spreadsheets, video editing, specialized work, and shared workstations. **Many**, but not all, allow relatively convenient replacement of accessories and certain internal components.

### Important limitations

A desktop is usually less convenient to carry between locations. Most need an electrical outlet and do not have built-in batteries for portable operation. Separate components require suitable connections and space. Some small and all-in-one designs are harder to expand or repair than a conventional desktop tower.

A desktop is not automatically faster or more powerful than every laptop. Performance depends on the particular model, components, and software.

### Safe observation: Find the computer and the monitor

If you can see a desktop computer, identify where its processing hardware is likely housed. Is there a separate case, an all-in-one display, or a small box? Locate the screen, keyboard, and mouse. Do not disconnect cables or open any equipment for this exercise.

> **Example:** “The monitor shows my document, but the separate computer case contains the major processing components. I use the keyboard to type and the mouse to select items.”

## 3. What is a laptop computer?

A [[laptop|laptop computer]] is a portable personal computer that usually combines a screen, physical keyboard, touchpad, processor, memory, storage, speakers, and rechargeable battery into a folding device. It is commonly called a **notebook computer**. Many laptops support much of the same everyday work as desktops while being easier to move.

### Recognizing a laptop

Most laptops have a **hinged display** above a built-in keyboard. Below the keyboard is often a [[touchpad|touchpad]], which serves as a pointing device. The body may also contain a camera, microphone, speakers, power connector, and [[port|ports]] for compatible accessories.

A laptop can be opened to view its screen and closed for transport. Closing the lid does not always mean the computer has shut down; it might sleep, remain active, or perform another configured action. You will learn more about power states in the next lesson.

### When a laptop is useful

A laptop is often a good choice for someone who must **type substantial amounts of text** in more than one location. A learner might take notes in class, move to a library, and finish an assignment at home. A worker may attend a meeting, present a document, and later use an external monitor at a desk.

Laptop computers commonly support full-featured productivity software, web browsers, video calls, and file management. The exact applications available depend on the laptop's operating system, hardware, and licensing or installation requirements.

### Battery and charging basics

A laptop has a [[battery|rechargeable battery]] so it can operate away from an outlet for a limited time. The amount of time varies with device age, battery health, screen brightness, applications, workload, and other conditions. Some computers have power-saving features that reduce consumption.

Use the power adapter or charger specified as compatible with the device. Do not force a plug into the wrong socket. Before moving the laptop, secure or remove attached cables and accessories appropriately. Avoid blocking air vents; some laptops require space for airflow to manage heat.

### Laptop, notebook, and Chromebook

**Laptop** and **notebook** usually mean the same broad physical type. A **Chromebook** is a laptop or related device that runs ChromeOS; the name identifies a software/platform family rather than a completely separate physical category. Windows laptops and MacBooks are also laptops, but they use different operating systems. The available apps, controls, and file workflows can therefore differ.

### Laptops with tablet-like features

A [[convertible|convertible (2-in-1)]] is a device whose form can shift between laptop-style and tablet-style use. Some have a screen that folds around; others have a detachable keyboard. These devices blur the category boundary. A touchscreen laptop remains a laptop even though its screen accepts taps.

> **Practical distinction:** A laptop is generally easier to transport than a desktop and more comfortable for long physical-keyboard typing than a tablet without an attached keyboard. Neither advantage is universal for every user or model.

## 4. What is a tablet computer?

A [[tablet|tablet computer]] is a portable computer designed primarily around a touchscreen. Many tablets look like a thin, flat screen with a front-facing camera and few physical controls. The entire device is held, propped on a stand, or placed on a surface. Most use a rechargeable battery.

### How you control a tablet

A tablet commonly uses a [[touchscreen|touchscreen]] for both input and output. You may tap an icon to open an app, swipe to move through content, or use an on-screen keyboard to enter text. Some models support a [[stylus|stylus]] for writing or drawing. Compatible physical keyboards and pointing devices may also be available, but they are not necessary for the basic definition of a tablet.

The shape is useful for many activities where people interact directly with content instead of typing long passages. A learner might hold a tablet like a book while reading, mark up a document using a stylus, or complete a form while away from a desk.

### When a tablet is useful

Tablets are often suitable for reading digital textbooks, watching instructional videos, using educational apps, showing photos, signing or annotating forms, drawing, and conducting portable presentations. Many also support video calls, web browsing, and document editing.

Because the screen is larger than that of many smartphones, content may be easier to view. Because the device often lacks a permanently attached keyboard, it may be lighter or easier to position than a conventional laptop. Actual weights, sizes, and accessories vary.

### Important limitations

Long-form typing can feel slower on a glass screen because the on-screen keyboard occupies part of the display and does not provide the same physical feedback as a standard keyboard. A compatible external keyboard can help. Complex desktop applications may be unavailable, adapted for touch, or missing some features on a particular tablet operating system. Some tablets offer extensive multitasking and external-display support; others are more limited.

**Do not assume that an app with the same name has identical capabilities** on a phone, tablet, and computer. Features can vary by platform, version, and subscription.

### Does a tablet need mobile data?

No. Many tablets use [[wifi|Wi-Fi]] to access local networks and the internet, and some models can also connect to a cellular network through a supported data plan. A Wi-Fi-only tablet can still read saved materials and run suitable offline apps without cellular service. Having a tablet does not automatically mean you have an internet connection.

> **Example:** “A tablet is helpful for reading a digital textbook on the sofa. I can tap links, zoom into illustrations, and use a compatible stylus to write notes. I might attach a keyboard when I need to type a long response.”

## 5. What is a smartphone?

A [[smartphone|smartphone]] is a portable computer that also provides mobile telephone functions. It typically has a touch display, rechargeable battery, microphones, speakers, cameras, wireless networking capabilities, and a mobile operating system. It is small enough to carry in a pocket or bag and is designed for frequent use throughout the day.

### A phone is more than a telephone

A smartphone can make calls and send messages, but it can also open web pages, take and organize photos, provide navigation, show a calendar, use a calculator, and run many other applications. Many tasks do not involve a telephone call at all.

Smartphones commonly include sensors and components that support features such as orientation detection, location-based services, photography, and secure device unlocking. The exact capabilities vary by model and settings; no single feature should be assumed to exist or be enabled on every phone.

### Touch, voice, and small-screen interaction

The main [[input-device|input device]] is usually the touchscreen, although voice input, accessibility controls, and compatible external accessories may also be available. On a small screen, controls and documents can be harder to view than on a laptop or desktop. A phone is convenient for **short, immediate tasks**, but reviewing a large spreadsheet or formatting a long report can require more scrolling and precision.

For example, a phone is useful for checking an appointment time while traveling. It may be less convenient for carefully comparing several lengthy documents side by side.

### Cellular service, Wi-Fi, and the internet

A smartphone may connect to the internet using a compatible **cellular data service** or Wi-Fi. These are different ways of obtaining network access, and a service plan may involve data limits or charges. Voice calls and texts depend on the device, provider, coverage, account status, and supported calling or messaging method. Some messaging and calling apps instead use an internet connection.

Without cellular service, a compatible smartphone may still connect to Wi-Fi, use offline apps, take photos, and play previously downloaded media. Some emergency calling capabilities vary by region and device, so do not rely on an assumed capability without checking official instructions.

A phone's **mobile operating system** may be Android or iOS, among other platforms. A computer's operating system matters because apps and settings are not necessarily interchangeable across devices.

### Privacy and attention

Because phones are easily carried and frequently connected, pay attention to where you place yours, what appears on its lock screen, and which apps have permission to use the camera, microphone, or location. Install apps from trusted sources, keep supported devices updated, and use an appropriate screen lock. A device's small size does not mean the information it contains is unimportant.

> **Remember:** “Smartphone” describes a computer category with phone capabilities; “cellular plan” describes a service. The physical device and the service are not the same thing.

## 6. How do you compare and choose a device?

Choosing a device is easier when you begin with the **task**, rather than a brand name or a claim that one category is always better. Ask what you want to do, where you will do it, and which input method feels comfortable.

### A practical comparison

| Consideration | Desktop | Laptop | Tablet | Smartphone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ease of carrying** | Usually stays in one place | Portable; fits many bags | Very portable; often hand-held | Pocket-sized or close to it |
| **Long typing sessions** | Often comfortable with a full-size keyboard | Often comfortable with built-in keyboard | Usually better with an optional keyboard | Possible, but small screen and keys may slow typing |
| **Reading or viewing** | Often large display | Medium-to-large display | Touch-friendly portable display | Convenient for quick reading, smaller viewing area |
| **Pointing or touch** | Often mouse and keyboard; touch on some models | Touchpad and keyboard; touch on some models | Touch usually central; stylus on some models | Touch usually central; voice on many models |
| **Power** | Commonly needs continuous outlet power | Battery or compatible charger | Battery or compatible charger | Battery or compatible charger |
| **External accessories** | Often several connection options | Often some ports and wireless options | Depends on model and adapters | Depends on model and adapters |
| **Software availability** | Depends on operating system and specifications | Depends on operating system and specifications | Depends on mobile/desktop platform and app support | Depends on mobile platform and app support |
| **Common strength** | Stable, comfortable work area | Work across different locations | Direct touch interaction and portable reading | Immediate access while moving |

This table compares **common patterns**, not guaranteed technical specifications. A high-end tablet may perform demanding work; a small laptop may have fewer ports than a large tablet; and phones may support external monitors or keyboards. Always examine the actual device and the requirements of the software you intend to use.

### Match the device to the activity

**Scenario A: Write a ten-page report.** A desktop or laptop with a physical keyboard and a comfortable screen may make the work easier. A tablet or phone may still be capable, particularly with accessories, but long typing and formatting are likely to require more effort on a small touch interface.

**Scenario B: Read an illustrated textbook while traveling.** A tablet may provide a useful balance of screen space and portability. A laptop can also work, while a phone is convenient for reading shorter sections.

**Scenario C: Take a photo and send it to a family member.** A smartphone is often the most convenient because it generally includes a camera and messaging apps. A tablet can also take pictures and send them if its hardware and software support the task.

**Scenario D: Work with a large spreadsheet all afternoon.** A desktop or laptop with a keyboard, pointing device, and sufficiently large screen may be easier to use. Software features, memory, and display arrangement also matter.

**Scenario E: Complete a short online form.** Any of the four may work. A phone is convenient for a short form, but a longer form with attachments or detailed text may be easier on a laptop or desktop.

**Scenario F: Join a video appointment.** A laptop, tablet, or smartphone with a suitable camera, microphone, speaker, app, and network connection may work. A desktop may also work if it has appropriate built-in or attached equipment. Choose a quiet location and confirm the microphone and camera you intend to use.

**Scenario G: Share a device in a fixed location.** A desktop is often practical for a reception counter or shared study station, but shared use requires attention to user accounts, privacy, and sign-out procedures regardless of device type.

### A five-question decision check

Before choosing a device for a task, answer these questions:

1. **Location:** Will I work mainly at one desk or move between places?
1. **Input:** Will I type many pages, tap simple controls, draw, or speak?
1. **Display:** Do I need to see large documents or several items at once?
1. **Software:** Does the specific application run on the device's operating system and offer the features I need?
1. **Connections:** Will I need Wi-Fi, cellular data, a printer, an external monitor, or other accessories?

There may be several suitable answers. You do not have to purchase the newest or most expensive device to accomplish ordinary computer tasks.

### Working across two devices

You might start a document on a desktop and read it later on a phone. You may send a photograph from a smartphone to a laptop, or use a tablet to review information created on another device. Devices can exchange information through compatible cables, local sharing methods, network services, or cloud-based apps. These methods depend on settings, compatibility, and permissions.

**Do not assume that a file automatically appears on every device.** Some apps [[sync|synchronize]] through an account, some require an explicit transfer, and others keep files only on the original device. Before relying on a file during travel or an appointment, check that the correct version is saved and accessible where you plan to use it.

### Accessibility and comfort

[[accessibility|Accessibility]] matters because different devices support different ways to interact. Examples include larger text, screen magnification, voice input, screen readers, captions, alternative pointing devices, and external keyboards. Available settings vary, but the purpose is the same: make the computer easier to use for the person and the task.

Choose an arrangement that supports comfort and readability. On a desktop or laptop, the display and keyboard can often be positioned for prolonged work. On a tablet or phone, a stand, larger text, or external keyboard may reduce the need to hold the device for long periods. Accessibility needs are valid design considerations, not an exception to ordinary computer use.

### Before you connect or carry a device

- Use a charger and cable that are appropriate for that model and its electrical requirements.
- Keep liquids away from electronics and protect devices from severe heat, moisture, and physical impact.
- Do not force unfamiliar connectors or remove storage devices while files are being written.
- Lock your screen when leaving a device unattended, especially in shared or public settings.
- Avoid entering passwords or sensitive account information in practice exercises.
- Carry laptops with the lid secured and cables managed; protect tablet and phone screens during transport.

> **Decision rule:** Start with the task, then choose the device whose screen, controls, software, portability, and connections best support it.

## 7. Guided practice: Compare everyday devices

You may complete these activities using any device available to you, photographs of devices you are permitted to examine, or written examples. You do not need to open a computer case, change settings, install apps, buy accessories, or sign in to any account. Each activity is intended to be completed independently.

### Activity 1: Identify the computer type (about 5 minutes)

Look at one computing device you can safely observe. Record your answers on paper or in a text document:

1. Is it mainly a desktop, laptop, tablet, or smartphone?
1. Does it have a separate monitor, folding screen, flat touch display, or small pocket-sized display?
1. How does the user enter information: keyboard, mouse, touchpad, touchscreen, microphone, or another method?
1. Does it normally remain plugged in or use a battery while operating?
1. Name one activity the device is well suited to perform.

**Example response:** “This is a laptop. It has a folding display, built-in keyboard, and touchpad. It can run on battery power. It is useful for typing class assignments in different locations.”

### Activity 2: Device choice challenge (about 5–10 minutes)

Choose a device for each situation. More than one answer may be reasonable if you explain your choice.

1. Someone writes and edits reports for several hours at the same desk.
1. Someone needs to photograph a street sign and send the image while away from home.
1. Someone reads digital magazines on a train and prefers tapping the screen.
1. Someone regularly types notes in different classrooms.
1. Someone uses a calculator to check a grocery total while standing in a store.

:::answer Show sample answers to Activity 2
1. **Desktop or laptop:** A keyboard and comfortable display support longer work sessions.
1. **Smartphone:** Its portability, camera, and communication features suit the situation; a connected tablet may also work.
1. **Tablet or smartphone:** Both can support portable, touch-based reading; the tablet may provide a larger display.
1. **Laptop:** The integrated keyboard and portability are useful, though a tablet with a keyboard may also be suitable.
1. **Smartphone:** It is easy to carry and usually has a calculator app; another available device could also calculate.

These answers illustrate the reasoning, not exclusive rules. The application, accessibility requirements, and available equipment may change the best option.
:::

### Activity 3: Device, software, or service? (about 5 minutes)

For each item, decide whether it is a **physical device or accessory**, **software**, or a **network/service**. Then check your responses.

1. A laptop computer
1. A word-processing application
1. A touchscreen
1. A cellular data plan
1. An operating system
1. A Wi-Fi network
1. A physical keyboard
1. A photo-editing app

:::answer Show Activity 3 answers
1. **Physical device:** A laptop is computer hardware.
1. **Software:** A word processor is an application.
1. **Physical device/component:** A touchscreen is hardware providing input and output.
1. **Network/service:** A cellular data plan provides access under a provider's terms; it is not the phone itself.
1. **Software:** An operating system manages hardware and supports applications.
1. **Network:** A Wi-Fi network is a local wireless network, not a computer model.
1. **Physical device/accessory:** A keyboard is an input device.
1. **Software:** A photo editor is an application.
:::

### Activity 4: Compare two devices you know (about 10 minutes)

Choose two different device types, such as a desktop and phone, or a laptop and tablet. Complete this table in your notes. If only one device is available, use what you learned in the lesson for the second column.

Finish with this statement: “For **[task]**, I would choose **[device]** because **[two practical reasons]**.”

### Activity 5: Observe an everyday workflow (optional)

Imagine preparing for an online appointment. Identify the equipment and conditions needed if you join from a desktop, laptop, tablet, or phone. Consider the screen, microphone, camera, speakers or headphones, internet connection, battery or outlet, privacy of your surroundings, and application requirements.

**Discussion question:** Which requirements remain the same across devices, and which change depending on the hardware arrangement?

> **Practice safety:** Use fictional examples and ordinary, nonsensitive information. Do not enter passwords, disclose private records, take photographs of others, or change security settings for these activities.

## 8. Common misconceptions

### “A smartphone is not a real computer.”

False. Smartphones process data using programmable hardware and software. Their small size and telephone functions do not exclude them from being computers.

### “A tablet is just a big phone.”

Not necessarily. Tablets and smartphones share touch-based design features, but tablets often provide different screen sizes, accessory support, software layouts, and optional cellular capabilities. Some tablets do not have ordinary cellular calling features.

### “Every desktop is faster than every laptop.”

False. Performance depends on the actual processor, memory, storage, cooling, software, and workload. Computer categories describe form and typical use, not a guaranteed ranking of speed.

### “A laptop must stay plugged in to work.”

False for a typical laptop with a functioning charged battery. Most can operate for a period without an outlet, though battery runtime varies. A failed or absent battery may change this behavior.

### “All devices run the same apps because they are all computers.”

False. Software [[compatibility|compatibility]] and features depend on the operating system, hardware capabilities, app version, licensing, and sometimes region. A familiar app name does not guarantee identical features.

### “A tablet cannot use a keyboard.”

False. Many tablets can connect to compatible physical keyboards. Availability, connection type, and supported features vary by model.

### “A phone needs cellular service to do anything.”

False. A phone may still run offline applications, take photographs, and use Wi-Fi when available. Specific services such as provider-based calls and mobile data require the relevant capabilities and arrangements.

### “A bigger screen automatically makes a device better.”

False. A larger screen may help with reading and multitasking, but it can make the device heavier or less portable. The most suitable size depends on the job and the user's needs.

### “Closing a laptop means it has been safely shut down.”

Not always. Depending on settings, closing the lid may cause sleep, leave the machine running, or take another action. You will study startup, shutdown, and sleep in the next lesson.

### “Files automatically follow me to every computer I use.”

Not always. Some apps synchronize files after an account is configured and an appropriate connection is available; others store information only on the device. Confirm your saving and transfer method rather than assuming.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Words with dotted underlines earlier in this lesson can be selected to open a brief definition. When a suitable, verified MGU Encyclopedia entry is available, its tooltip also provides a link for deeper reading. These explanations are collected below for review.

- **Computer:** A programmable machine or system that receives and processes information according to instructions. [Definition of a Computer](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/computational-foundations/definition-of-a-computer).
- **Desktop computer:** A personal computer intended mainly for use in a fixed location, often with a separate monitor, keyboard, and mouse.
- **All-in-one computer:** A desktop arrangement that combines major computer components and the display in one enclosure.
- **Laptop computer:** A portable personal computer that normally includes a folding screen and physical keyboard.
- **Touchpad:** A touch-sensitive pointing surface commonly found on laptops. [Input Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/input-devices).
- **Convertible (2-in-1):** A computing device that can change between laptop-style and tablet-style use.
- **Tablet computer:** A portable computer designed primarily around a touchscreen.
- **Touchscreen:** Hardware that displays visual information and detects user touch. [Input Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/input-devices) and [Output Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/output-devices).
- **Stylus:** A compatible pen-like tool used to point, write, or draw on a supported screen.
- **Smartphone:** A pocket-sized computer with mobile telephone functionality and a touch-oriented interface.
- **Hardware:** The physical parts of a computer and its accessories.
- **Software:** Programs and instructions that run on computer hardware. [Operating Systems](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/software-systems/operating-systems) discusses foundational system software.
- **Operating system:** Core software that manages a computer's resources and helps applications run. [Operating Systems](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/software-systems/operating-systems).
- **Application (app):** A program used for a task such as writing, messaging, or viewing photographs.
- **Battery:** A source of stored electrical energy that allows a portable device to operate away from an outlet.
- **Port:** A physical connection point for a compatible cable or accessory. [Peripheral Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/peripheral-devices).
- **Wi-Fi:** Wireless networking technology used to connect suitable devices to a local network.
- **Cellular data:** Network access offered through a mobile service provider and compatible device or plan.
- **Internet:** A worldwide system of interconnected networks used to exchange data. [Computer Networking](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/networking-systems/computer-networking).
- **Input device:** Hardware used to provide information or commands to a computer. [Input Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/input-devices).
- **Output device:** Hardware that presents information or other results from a computer. [Output Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/output-devices).
- **Peripheral:** An attached or connected component that extends a computer's capabilities. [Peripheral Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/peripheral-devices).
- **Compatibility:** The ability of particular devices, software, files, or accessories to work together as required.
- **Synchronization (sync):** A process that keeps selected information consistent between devices or services when configured and functioning.
- **Accessibility:** Features and practices that make computer systems more usable for people with differing needs. [Human-Computer Interaction](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/human-interaction-systems/human-computer-interaction).
