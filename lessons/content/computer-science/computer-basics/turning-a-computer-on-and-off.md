## Lesson 4: Turning a Computer On and Off

Every computer needs a way to start operating, pause its work, and stop safely. On a desktop, you may press a physical power button and later choose **Shut down** from a menu. On a laptop, closing the lid may only put the computer to sleep. On a phone, briefly pressing the side button often turns the *screen* off without switching off the entire device. These actions look similar, but they are not the same. Learning the difference helps you protect your work, avoid unnecessary problems, and use your device confidently.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 60–80 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–3 recommended · **Practice equipment:** Any desktop, laptop, tablet, or smartphone (optional; observation-only alternatives are provided)

> **Essential idea:** **Turning a computer on** starts its operating process. **Shutting it down** asks the system to close its work and power off normally. **Sleep**, **screen off**, **lock**, **sign out**, and **restart** are different actions. Before shutting down or restarting, save your work and allow the device time to finish.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Locate and recognize common power buttons without opening or disassembling equipment.
1. Describe what generally happens when a computer starts up.
1. Follow basic startup and normal shutdown procedures on a Windows PC, Mac, or Chromebook.
1. Explain how turning a tablet or phone screen off differs from powering the device off.
1. Distinguish shutdown, restart, sleep, hibernate, lock, and sign-out actions.
1. Save work and respond appropriately to update or confirmation messages before shutting down.
1. Identify safe first steps when a computer appears not to start or respond.

## 1. What does it mean to turn a computer on or off?

A computer depends on electrical [[power|power]] from a wall connection, an approved power adapter, or a charged [[battery|battery]]. But receiving electricity and being ready to use are not exactly the same. The device must also initialize its components and start the software that makes it usable.

### Starting up: From the power button to a usable screen

When you press a computer's [[power-button|power button]], it can begin a [[startup|startup]] process. This process is also called **booting** or a [[boot|boot]]. In simplified form, several things happen:

1. **Power becomes available to the components.** The computer's power-management hardware supplies the electricity needed to operate.
1. **Basic hardware checks and initialization occur.** Low-level [[firmware|firmware]] prepares important parts of the device.
1. **The operating system starts.** The [[operating-system|operating system]]—such as Windows, macOS, ChromeOS, Android, or iOS—prepares the environment used to run programs.
1. **A sign-in, lock, or home screen appears.** You may be asked to select a user account and enter an approved password, PIN, or other authentication method.
1. **You can begin normal work.** Apps become available after the device has finished enough of its startup process.

The details differ among devices. You may see a manufacturer logo, a spinning symbol, a progress bar, or a message such as **Please wait**. Do not assume the device is broken just because a screen does not appear instantly.

### Turning off: More than making the display dark

A normal [[shutdown|shutdown]] is a software-controlled process that lets the computer stop running programs, complete important operations, and turn itself off. When you choose **Shut down**, the system may close open apps, finish writing information to storage, and stop services before power-down.

A dark screen does **not** prove that the computer is fully off. The monitor may be off while a separate desktop computer is running; a laptop may be sleeping; and a phone may be locked with its screen dark. Some devices also show a clock or notifications even while the main screen is inactive.

### A simple everyday example

Suppose you write a short note on a laptop:

1. You press the power button and wait for the sign-in screen.
1. You open a notes application and type the note.
1. You save the note so it can be retrieved later.
1. You select the operating system's **Shut down** command.
1. You let the computer complete its shutdown before putting it away.

After the computer is turned on again, the saved note should still be available from the location where it was stored. Unsaved changes may not be preserved unless the application offers automatic saving or recovery.

> **Remember:** Starting a computer and waking a sleeping computer can produce similar screens, but a full startup and a wake-up are different processes. Likewise, a blank screen and a completed shutdown are not interchangeable.

### Why does startup sometimes take longer?

A device may need more time if it is installing updates, starting services, checking storage, recovering from an interruption, or simply running on older or slower hardware. Mobile devices also vary in the time needed to restart. Watch for on-screen messages rather than repeatedly pressing the power button.

## 2. How should you prepare to start a computer?

Before touching the power button, take a moment to identify the device and check its surroundings. This is especially useful when using equipment in a classroom, office, library, or shared household.

### Step 1: Identify the device and its power source

- **Desktop computer:** Usually uses a power cord connected to an appropriate outlet or power distribution device. The monitor may have a separate power button and power cord.
- **Laptop:** Usually can operate from a charged battery or an approved charger. A low or depleted battery may prevent startup until the device has charged.
- **Tablet or smartphone:** Usually uses a built-in rechargeable battery. The charging port and the power or side button are separate controls on many devices.
- **All-in-one desktop:** May look like a monitor but includes the computer inside its enclosure. Check its designated power button rather than assuming a separate tower is present.

Never dismantle equipment, touch exposed wiring, or open an electrical power supply to locate its button. Use ordinary visible controls only.

### Step 2: Check the immediate area

Look for obvious hazards: liquid near the device, a frayed cable, unusual heat, smoke, a damaged battery, or a blocked ventilation area. If any of these are present, do not attempt to power on the equipment. Seek help from a responsible adult, supervisor, facility support staff, or qualified technician as appropriate.

A laptop should rest on a stable surface that does not obstruct its ventilation. Do not cover a running computer with bedding or fabric. For a desktop, keep cords arranged so that they are not pulled or tripped over.

### Step 3: Recognize the power symbol and its limits

Many power buttons display a circle with a short vertical line at the top. The symbol is common but not universal. A button may be on the front of a tower, a laptop's keyboard area, the rear of an all-in-one computer, or the edge of a phone or tablet.

**A quick press** is often enough to start an ordinary desktop or laptop. On some phones and tablets, turning on a fully powered-off device requires holding its designated button until a logo appears. Power-button actions are configurable on some computers, so they do not always behave the same way once the device is running.

### Step 4: Notice any existing activity

If lights are on, fans are running, a screen is displaying an application, or the device responds when you move the mouse or touchpad, it may already be on. Avoid pressing the power button again without checking. The screen may simply be asleep.

*What to check before beginning*

| Observation | Possible meaning | Safe first response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dark screen, but keyboard lights or fans are active | Computer may be running with the display off or sleeping | Gently move the mouse or use a normal wake control |
| Laptop shows a low-battery indicator | Battery may need charging | Connect its approved charger if authorized |
| Monitor is dark but desktop tower has lights | Display may be off while the computer is on | Check the monitor's ordinary power control |
| A startup or update progress message is visible | The device is working on a process | Allow it to continue; follow trusted on-screen instructions |
| Damaged cable, liquid, smoke, or abnormal heat | Possible safety hazard | Stop and seek appropriate assistance |

### One important distinction: The computer and the monitor

With a traditional desktop, the computer and the [[monitor|monitor]] may have separate power controls. Turning on only the monitor does not necessarily start the computer. Turning off only the monitor does not shut down the computer or guarantee that your work has been saved.

> **Practice caution:** For a first observation, you can identify the power button without pressing it. Do not operate shared or managed devices without permission.

## 3. How do you start a desktop or laptop?

Windows PCs, Macs, and Chromebooks all have a normal startup sequence. The exact appearance of the login screen, button, and prompts depends on the manufacturer, operating system version, and device configuration.

### A. Starting a Windows desktop or laptop

1. Confirm that the device has an appropriate power source and that the display is connected or built in.
1. Locate the computer's **Power** button. On a tower, look on its case; on a laptop, look for its designated button.
1. Press the button normally once. Avoid repeatedly tapping it.
1. Watch for a manufacturer logo, Windows startup screen, or sign-in screen.
1. If prompted, select your user account and sign in using the authorized method. A PIN, password, fingerprint, or other method may be offered.
1. After sign-in, wait until the desktop or other normal interface is ready before opening your application.

Some Windows computers resume from sleep when you open the lid or press a key; this is not the same as starting from a full shutdown. If the screen remains black, review Section 6 before attempting more actions.

### B. Starting a Mac desktop or laptop

1. Check that the Mac has a charged battery or appropriate electrical power.
1. Locate its designated **Power** button or Touch ID/power control where applicable.
1. Start the Mac using its normal control. Certain Mac notebooks may start automatically when the lid is opened or power is connected, depending on model and settings.
1. Wait for the Apple logo, progress indicator, or login window.
1. Sign in using the authorized account and credentials if requested.
1. Wait for the Finder and desktop environment, or another configured work environment, to become usable.

A Mac can sometimes be sleeping with a dark screen. Try its normal wake action before concluding that it has been shut down.

### C. Starting a Chromebook

1. Confirm that the Chromebook has battery power or an approved charging connection.
1. Open the lid. Some Chromebooks start or wake at this point; others require their **Power** button.
1. If necessary, press the designated power button once.
1. Wait for the ChromeOS sign-in or lock screen.
1. Use the account and authentication method assigned to you. On a school or work device, follow the organization's instructions.
1. Allow the desktop and apps to become ready.

If the device is already signed in and merely asleep, opening the lid, pressing a key, or using the touchpad may wake it without a full startup.

### What does the sign-in screen mean?

A [[sign-in|sign-in]] screen helps the operating system determine which person's files, settings, and permissions should be used. It is **not** the power switch. Signing in does not necessarily connect you to the internet, and signing out does not necessarily turn off the device. Detailed account security is addressed in Lesson 7.

### What if you cannot sign in?

Read the message carefully. Check whether you selected the correct user account, whether Caps Lock is on, and whether a supported alternate sign-in method is offered. Do **not** share your password or repeatedly guess someone else's credentials. For an unfamiliar school, library, or work device, ask authorized support staff for help.

> **Checkpoint:** You should be able to explain the basic sequence without memorizing every screen: **identify the correct device → provide power → start once → wait → sign in if asked → use the computer**.

## 4. How do you shut down a computer safely?

A safe shutdown begins while the device is still responding normally. The most reliable beginner habit is **save, review, shut down, and wait**. The computer's operating system provides a normal command for turning off, and that command is preferable to abruptly disconnecting power.

### Before you select Shut down

1. Save any open documents, spreadsheets, images, or notes in their intended locations.
1. Check that file transfers, downloads, uploads, printing, or other important tasks have completed when practical.
1. Close applications if appropriate. Some systems can close them during shutdown, but an open app may display a warning about unsaved changes.
1. If the device belongs to a school or employer, follow its shutdown, update, and security policies.
1. Find the operating system's **Shut down** control rather than pulling out a power cable.

Some applications save automatically, but **do not assume every application does**. Confirm that important work is saved before beginning.

### A. Normal shutdown on Windows 11

1. Select **Start** on the taskbar.
1. Select the **Power** icon in the Start menu.
1. Choose **Shut down**.
1. If Windows warns that an application has unsaved work, review the warning and take the needed action. You may need to cancel the shutdown and save your document first.
1. Wait while Windows closes and the device powers down.

In some situations, Windows offers an option involving an update, such as **Update and shut down**. Updates can require extra time and may change the sequence. Do not disconnect power or repeatedly press buttons during a normal update. On managed equipment, follow local guidance.

**Windows example:** You have finished a typing assignment and saved it. You select **Start → Power → Shut down**. If a prompt asks whether to save a second unsaved note, you save it or consciously decide whether to discard it before continuing.

### B. Normal shutdown on a Mac

1. Save your work in the applications you were using.
1. Choose the **Apple menu** in the menu bar.
1. Select **Shut Down**.
1. Respond to any confirmation or unsaved-work prompts.
1. Allow the Mac to complete shutdown before disconnecting equipment or packing it away.

Some Mac shutdown dialogs offer to reopen windows when you next sign in. That preference is separate from the central safety rule: ensure your important changes are saved.

### C. Normal shutdown on a Chromebook

1. Save or confirm the saved status of your work. This matters even when an app usually synchronizes automatically.
1. At the bottom-right of the screen, select the **time** to open Quick Settings.
1. Select the **Power** control, then choose **Shut down**, if that option is presented.
1. Wait for the Chromebook to finish powering off.

Google also documents device-dependent physical-button methods, but the visible operating-system menu is a useful first choice for beginners. Managed Chromebooks may have restrictions or different controls.

### Is it safe to close the laptop lid instead?

Closing a laptop lid often causes the device to **sleep**, not shut down. The exact action depends on the model and its settings. A laptop may remain active even with its lid closed. If your goal is a full shutdown, use the operating system's shutdown command and confirm that the process completes.

### Why wait after choosing Shut down?

The computer may still be completing storage operations, closing apps, or processing an update after the command is selected. A device that immediately goes dark may finish quickly, but a device displaying **Shutting down**, **Installing updates**, or an active progress indicator needs time. Do not remove power merely because the process seems slower than usual. If it remains unresponsive for an unusually long period, consult authorized help rather than improvising repair steps.

### Normal shutdown compared with pulling the plug

A normal shutdown gives software a chance to finish properly. **Unplugging a desktop, removing power, or forcing a computer off** can interrupt unsaved work and ongoing writing to storage. It may cause data loss or other problems. An emergency involving smoke or electrical danger is different: prioritize personal safety and follow local emergency procedures.

> **Golden rule:** **Save first. Use the normal Shut down command. Wait for completion.**

## 5. How are sleep, restart, lock, and sign out different?

Computers offer several ways to pause activity, protect access, or begin again. These actions are useful, but they solve different problems. Understanding the differences is more important than memorizing a particular icon.

### Sleep: A shorter pause

[[sleep|Sleep mode]] reduces power use while allowing the computer to return to work relatively quickly. On many laptops, it occurs when you close the lid or leave the computer idle. The current session may remain available when you wake it, but it is still wise to save important work because unexpected power loss or software problems can occur.

To [[wake|wake]] a sleeping computer, you may open a laptop lid, press an ordinary key, move the mouse, use a touchpad, or briefly press a designated power button. The correct method varies. You may see a lock screen and need to sign in again.

### Restart: Turn off and start again

A [[restart|restart]] (sometimes called a **reboot**) directs the device to end the current operating session and start a new one. This can help after certain updates or when software is not behaving correctly. A restart is **not** the same as merely turning the screen off, and it should not be used as a substitute for saving work.

On many systems, **Restart** is available near **Shut down** in the power menu. Save your files first, select the normal Restart command, and allow the system to complete its cycle without repeatedly pressing buttons.

### Lock: Protect your active session

[[lock|Locking]] a device hides access to the current session until the authorized person unlocks it. The computer usually stays on, and apps may continue running. Locking is useful when stepping away from an office or shared computer, but locking **does not** close programs or complete a shutdown.

An example is a Windows PC locked with **Windows key + L**. Other systems have their own lock commands. A phone often locks when you briefly press its side button.

### Sign out: Leave a user account session

[[sign-out|Signing out]] ends or leaves your active user session without necessarily switching off the hardware. It is useful on a shared computer when another person needs to sign in. Before signing out, save work and respond to prompts. On shared computers, signing out helps prevent other users from accessing your open session, though it is not a substitute for strong account security.

### Hibernate: Save the session to persistent storage

[[hibernate|Hibernate]] is a power-saving state available on certain computers, notably some Windows PCs. It preserves the session in persistent storage so the device can use very little or no power while off and later resume. It differs from ordinary sleep and is not offered on every device or configuration. Beginners need only recognize it as another possible power-menu option.

### Screen off: A dark display, not necessarily an off computer

The screen can turn off because of inactivity, a monitor's power button, brightness settings, or a quick press of a phone's side button. The rest of the computer may continue operating. For example, a desktop might still be printing or downloading while its monitor is dark.

*Compare common power and access actions*

| Action | What normally happens | Why you might use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shut down | Operating session ends; device powers off normally | Finishing use, transporting a device, or turning off fully |
| Restart | Session ends, then the device starts again | Applying certain changes or responding to a temporary software problem |
| Sleep | Device uses less power and can usually resume quickly | Taking a short break |
| Hibernate | Supported session is preserved to storage for low-power resumption | Leaving a compatible computer unused longer |
| Lock | Current session remains but access requires unlocking | Leaving your device unattended briefly |
| Sign out | Your user session ends or exits | Allowing another user to sign in or ending your session |
| Turn display off | Display becomes dark; device may still be active | Saving display power or stopping visible output |

These descriptions are **typical behavior**. Device settings and organizational policies may change the result. A computer may also perform background tasks while in certain low-power states.

### Choosing the right action for the situation

- **You are getting a drink and will return shortly:** Lock the device, or use an appropriate sleep/lock method.
- **You are leaving a shared computer:** Save your work and sign out according to the facility's rules; shut down only if instructed.
- **You are finished with your personal laptop for the day:** Save your work and choose an appropriate shutdown or sleep option based on how and when you will use it next.
- **An update requires restarting:** Save your files and select the normal Restart or update command.
- **You only pressed the phone's side button briefly:** Expect a locked or darkened screen in many models, not a complete shutdown.

> **Think about it:** Why might locking a computer be appropriate at a public workstation for a short absence, while signing out would be better when you are finished? What security and convenience trade-offs are involved?

## 6. What about tablets, phones, and common power problems?

Tablets and smartphones are computers, but they often keep running while the display is off. Their power controls may use a combination of physical buttons and touch controls. **Do not assume that one brand's button sequence works on every device.**

### A. Turning an iPhone on and off

**To turn on an iPhone that is fully powered off:** Press and hold the designated side button until the Apple logo appears, then allow it to start.

**To turn off an iPhone through its settings:**

1. Open **Settings**.
1. Select **General**.
1. Scroll to **Shut Down**.
1. Move the on-screen power-off slider as instructed.
1. Wait for the device to shut down.

Apple also provides button-based methods, which vary depending on whether the iPhone has a Home button or Face ID. On models without a Home button, a power-off slider commonly appears after holding the side button and a volume button together. On models with a Home button, holding the side button may show the slider. Follow the instructions specific to the model rather than guessing.

A **brief tap of the side button** typically locks the screen instead of turning off the phone completely.

### B. Turning an iPad on and off

**To turn on an iPad that is fully powered off:** Press and hold its designated top button until the Apple logo appears.

**To turn off an iPad through its settings:** Open **Settings → General → Shut Down**, and use the on-screen slider. Button combinations vary between iPad models with and without a Home button. Some iPads use the top button plus a volume button to display the power-off slider.

When you briefly press the top button to make the screen dark, the iPad normally remains on and can be awakened or unlocked later.

### C. Turning an Android phone or tablet on and off

Most Android devices can be turned on by holding the device's designated power button until a logo appears. To turn off, open the model's **power menu** and select **Power off**. Depending on the manufacturer and configuration, the power menu may appear after holding the power button, pressing the power and volume-up buttons together, or selecting a power control from Quick Settings.

For example, Google documents a power-plus-volume-up method on newer Pixel phones. This is a **model-specific example**, not a universal instruction for all Android phones. Use your device's own instructions when the buttons behave differently.

A quick press of the power button usually darkens or locks the screen; it does not ordinarily shut down the Android device.

### D. What if a device appears not to turn on?

A black screen does not by itself identify the problem. Start with simple, non-invasive checks:

1. **Check whether it might be asleep.** Try a normal wake action such as moving a mouse, opening a laptop lid, or briefly pressing the screen/wake button.
1. **Check the obvious power source.** For an authorized device, confirm that its charger is connected correctly or that its battery is charged. Avoid handling damaged equipment.
1. **For a desktop, distinguish the monitor from the computer.** Make sure the correct screen is on and the system unit is operating. Do not disconnect cables without authorization.
1. **Look for messages.** A low-battery symbol, update indicator, or sign-in prompt gives more information than a blank screen alone.
1. **Allow time.** A device that has been fully discharged or is completing an update may not respond immediately.
1. **Ask for help if the basic checks do not resolve the issue.** A responsible support person can review manufacturer-specific instructions.

**Do not** open a device, remove a built-in battery, repeatedly force a shutdown, or experiment with unfamiliar recovery menus as part of this lesson.

### E. What if the computer freezes?

A [[freeze|freeze]] occurs when the device or an application stops responding normally. First, give the device a little time: a temporary delay is not necessarily a complete freeze. If only one application is affected and you know a normal way to close it, use the operating system's ordinary controls. If other parts still respond, save any available work.

When a device is entirely unresponsive, manufacturer-specific **force restart** or **forced shutdown** procedures sometimes exist. These are recovery methods, not everyday shutdown methods. They can cause the loss of unsaved data or interrupt important operations. Do not use a long power-button hold or button combination casually; follow the device manufacturer's instructions or ask authorized support staff when normal controls fail.

### F. Charging, updates, and unusual warnings

- **Low battery:** Connect the device to an appropriate, undamaged charger, if authorized. A critically discharged device may need some time before it starts.
- **System update:** Read the message. If the device is updating, allow it to continue and maintain a suitable power supply when instructed.
- **Overheating warning:** Stop intensive use and follow the device's safety instructions. Do not cover vents or attempt internal repairs.
- **Unfamiliar security or recovery screen:** Do not select options such as reset, erase, or factory restore without understanding the consequences and having proper authorization. These can delete data.
- **Shared or managed device:** Consult the responsible support person before changing power settings or attempting recovery.

> **Safety priority:** A device with smoke, leaking battery material, significant swelling, damaged wiring, or abnormal heat is not a practice exercise. Stop using it, avoid handling the hazard, and obtain qualified assistance.

## 8. Common misconceptions

### “If the screen is black, the computer is off.”

Not necessarily. A display may be asleep, turned off independently, locked, disconnected, or dark while the rest of the device continues to operate. Check for normal signs of activity and try an appropriate wake action.

### “Closing a laptop always shuts it down.”

Not necessarily. Closing the lid often places a laptop in sleep mode. Some computers have configurable lid-close actions. If you require a full shutdown, use the normal shutdown command.

### “Signing out and shutting down mean the same thing.”

No. Signing out normally ends a user session while leaving the computer available for another user. Shutdown normally ends operation and powers the device down.

### “It is always safe to pull the plug.”

No. Abrupt power loss can interrupt unsaved work or storage operations. A normal shutdown is the standard method. Genuine electrical hazards are exceptional situations requiring a safety-first response.

### “Sleep preserves every unsaved change forever.”

No. Sleep is convenient, but the session can still be disrupted by battery depletion, hardware problems, updates, or other faults. Save important work rather than depending entirely on a sleep state.

### “Restart means that my files will be deleted.”

A normal restart is not designed to erase properly saved personal files. However, **unsaved work can be lost**, and some apps may close or resume differently. Restart is also not the same as a factory reset or erase operation.

### “Pressing a phone's side button once shuts the phone down.”

On many modern phones, a brief press turns off or locks the screen while the device remains powered on. Full power-off uses a separate menu, button combination, or slider.

### “If the computer seems slow, I should keep pressing Power.”

Repeated power-button presses can interrupt startup or trigger unintended behavior. Check for a progress or update message and allow time. Escalate persistent problems rather than forcing a shutdown casually.

### “Every computer offers Hibernate.”

No. Hibernate availability depends on hardware, operating system, configuration, and policy. You need to recognize what it means, not assume you will find it on every device.

### “I must know complex recovery procedures to use a computer.”

No. A beginner's core responsibilities are to use ordinary controls, save work, recognize common states, and request help when normal operation fails. Advanced recovery can be left to appropriate technical support.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Words marked with a dotted underline earlier in the lesson can be selected to open definitions. When an appropriate MGU Encyclopedia article is available, the tooltip also provides a link for deeper reading.

- **Power:** Electrical energy that allows a device to operate. [Power and Thermal Systems](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/power-and-thermal-systems).
- **Battery:** A component that stores electrical energy for use when external power is not available.
- **Power button:** A physical control that starts a device or triggers another configured power action.
- **Startup / boot:** The process of initializing a device and loading the software needed for normal use. [Firmware](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/software-systems/firmware).
- **Firmware:** Low-level software involved in starting, controlling, and initializing hardware. [Firmware](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/software-systems/firmware).
- **Operating system:** Software that manages hardware and provides the environment in which applications run. [Operating Systems](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/software-systems/operating-systems).
- **Shutdown:** A normal system-controlled process for ending activity and powering a device off.
- **Sleep:** A reduced-power state from which a device can usually resume quickly.
- **Wake:** To bring a sleeping or inactive device back to normal interaction.
- **Restart / reboot:** To end the current running session and start the device again.
- **Lock:** To protect access to an active session without necessarily shutting down the device.
- **Sign in:** To identify or authenticate yourself to an account so you can use its permitted resources.
- **Sign out:** To end or leave an active user account session.
- **Hibernate:** A supported low-power state that preserves a session in persistent storage for later resumption.
- **Monitor:** A display that shows visual output from a computer. [Output Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/output-devices).
- **Freeze:** A condition in which an application or device stops responding normally.
- **Update:** A change to installed software or system components, sometimes requiring a restart.
- **Save:** To preserve document or application information to an intended storage location. [Storage Hardware](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/storage-hardware).
- **Unsaved changes:** Work not yet preserved in the intended location, which may be lost when an app or device closes unexpectedly.
- **Force restart:** A recovery action used when ordinary controls fail, with possible loss of unsaved work; not an everyday shutdown technique.
