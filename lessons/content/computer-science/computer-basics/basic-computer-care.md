## Lesson 8: Basic Computer Care

A computer can last longer and work more reliably when its owner practices simple, consistent care. You do not need to repair electronics, purchase special equipment, or understand advanced computer engineering to develop good habits. Everyday care means keeping a device reasonably clean and dry, using it in a suitable location, protecting it from physical damage, installing trusted software updates, and making sure important information can be recovered. It also means knowing when to stop and ask for qualified help.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 70–90 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–7 recommended · **Practice equipment:** A desktop, laptop, tablet, or smartphone (optional); a clean, dry, soft lint-free cloth (optional); paper or a notes app for planning

> **Essential idea:** Good computer care combines **physical care**, **safe power and temperature management**, **software maintenance**, **data protection**, and **security habits**. A useful maintenance routine is usually simple: protect the device, allow trusted updates, save and back up important files, respond to warning signs, and seek qualified assistance when the problem is beyond routine care.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Identify everyday practices that protect computers and mobile devices from common damage.
2. Describe a safe, beginner-level approach to cleaning an external screen or case.
3. Explain why ventilation, sensible charging, and careful cable handling matter.
4. Recognize trusted operating-system and application update procedures.
5. Distinguish saving, synchronizing, and backing up important files.
6. Identify security and hardware warning signs that require caution or assistance.
7. Create a practical maintenance checklist appropriate to a device you use.
8. Know when **not** to attempt a repair, cleanup, reset, or unfamiliar system change.

## 1. What is basic computer care?

[[computer-care|Basic computer care]] is a collection of ordinary habits that reduce avoidable damage, data loss, confusion, and security risk. A computer can be well cared for without looking new. A worn keyboard or an older laptop is not necessarily poorly maintained. The real questions are whether the equipment can be used safely, whether its information is protected, and whether its software is receiving the maintenance it needs.

The word **maintenance** can sound technical, but many maintenance tasks are familiar. You keep drinks away from a laptop, carry a tablet in a protective case, leave space around a computer's vents, install a legitimate security update, and preserve a second copy of an important document. These activities protect different parts of the same computing experience.

### Five areas of everyday care

A good beginner routine organizes care by purpose. It does not require a different expensive product for every category.

| Area | Everyday purpose | Example of a safe habit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical care | Reduce damage, dust, moisture, and accidental drops | Place a laptop on a stable, dry surface |
| Power and temperature | Allow safe charging and proper release of heat | Keep ventilation openings unobstructed |
| Software upkeep | Receive supported fixes and reduce known software risks | Check the device's official update settings |
| Information protection | Preserve work and plan for recovery | Keep a separate backup of important files |
| Security | Reduce unauthorized access and harmful software | Lock the device and distrust unexpected downloads |

These areas overlap. For instance, an overheated computer may slow down or shut down unexpectedly. If an unsaved document is lost at that moment, physical conditions have affected your information. A supported software update may correct a problem that makes the device less reliable. A backup can help you recover if a storage device fails, but it cannot prevent the physical failure itself.

### Care is different from repair

[[preventive-maintenance|Preventive maintenance]] means actions intended to reduce problems before they happen. [[repair|Repair]] means diagnosing and correcting an existing fault, sometimes by replacing components. Looking for visible damage on a cable is a beginner care activity; taking apart a charger or opening a battery pack is not. Checking official update settings is routine; installing unknown repair tools that request administrator access is not.

It is appropriate to observe, learn, and stop when a task involves an electrical hazard, exposed wiring, swollen battery, liquid inside electronics, or an unfamiliar repair procedure. Professional or manufacturer support is the right next step in those cases. School and workplace devices may also require permission before any update, cleaning, or configuration change.

### The safest order of decisions

Before undertaking an unfamiliar care task, ask yourself four questions:

1. **Is the device physically safe?** If there is smoke, sparking, unusual heat, liquid damage, or battery swelling, stop using it and follow the safety guidance in Section 6.
2. **Does the task affect files or settings?** If so, check whether your important work is saved and backed up first.
3. **Is the instruction from a reliable source?** Prefer the device manufacturer, its operating-system provider, or an authorized organization.
4. **Do I understand and have permission to perform the step?** If not, stop and ask an authorized person for help.

> **Remember:** Good care does not mean changing everything you can find in Settings. Many of the most useful actions are simply checking, protecting, and leaving unfamiliar controls alone.

### Why computer care belongs in basic computer skills

Earlier lessons explained what computers do, how their components work, how to use input devices and screens, and how to protect user accounts. This final lesson connects those skills. A user who knows how to save a document can protect it. A user who recognizes an operating system can locate the right update settings. A user who understands passwords can be more cautious about a suspicious maintenance warning that asks for credentials.

Your goal is independence at the beginner level, not technical specialization. Once you can distinguish a harmless smudge from a serious warning, find legitimate settings, and plan a backup, you have a practical foundation for more advanced study.

## 2. How do you clean and protect a device?

Dust, fingerprints, crumbs, and ordinary handling can affect a device's appearance and, in some cases, how comfortably it can be used. The safest starting point is **gentle external cleaning**, guided by the instructions for your exact device. Different screens, coatings, keyboards, fabrics, and materials may need different methods. A cleaning method suitable for one product may damage another.

### Prepare before cleaning

Use this general sequence for the outside of a personal computer or mobile device:

1. **Save your work.** Close documents or apps when appropriate so cleaning will not cause accidental typing or taps.
2. **Shut down or power off when the manufacturer directs.** Unplug external power and accessories if appropriate and safe to do so. Do not attempt to unplug visibly damaged or sparking equipment; seek qualified assistance.
3. **Read the device-specific cleaning instructions.** Check which materials and solutions are permitted for its screen and enclosure.
4. **Start with a clean, soft, lint-free cloth.** A dry cloth is often enough for fingerprints or light dust.
5. **If the manufacturer permits moisture, use only its specified method.** The cloth should not drip. Never pour or spray liquid onto the device itself.
6. **Keep moisture away from openings.** Do not push objects, cleaning fluid, or debris into ports, speaker holes, seams, or ventilation openings.
7. **Allow the device to dry as instructed before reconnecting power.** Do not resume use if liquid may have entered the equipment.

This is a teaching sequence, not a universal recipe for every screen. For example, Apple advises using a soft, lint-free cloth, disconnecting external power, avoiding direct sprays, and keeping moisture out of openings. Microsoft's Surface guidance likewise calls for gentle methods and emphasizes device-specific care. Always prioritize the product's current instructions.

### Cleaning the screen

A [[display|display]] may have a coating that reacts poorly to harsh cleaners or abrasive wiping. A clean, dry microfiber cloth is a reasonable first choice for ordinary surface fingerprints if the manufacturer permits it. Wipe gently, without pressing hard or scratching at spots with a fingernail. A tissue or rough paper towel can be more abrasive than it appears.

For stubborn smudges, **do not guess which cleaner to use**. Some manufacturers allow specific alcohol-based wipes or a lightly dampened cloth on some surfaces; others restrict what can touch the screen. A product labeled “glass cleaner” is not automatically safe for an electronic display. Cleaning agents can damage coatings even if they do not immediately cause a visible problem.

### Cleaning the keyboard, mouse, or touchpad

A [[keyboard|keyboard]] can collect crumbs and dust between keys. A [[mouse|mouse]] or [[touchpad|touchpad]] can collect oils from hands. Beginners should limit themselves to approved **external** cleaning. Power down or disconnect the accessory when instructed, wipe exposed surfaces gently, and avoid saturating gaps around keys or buttons.

Do not pry off keys, insert sharp objects, spray liquid between them, or flood a keyboard with cleaner. Compressed air is not universally appropriate: some manufacturers specify its use for particular keyboard designs, while others caution against blowing debris into small device openings. If a key is stuck, first check the manufacturer's guidance and ask for assistance rather than forcing it.

### What to use and what to avoid

| Situation | Safer beginner approach | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dust or fingerprints on a permitted surface | Soft, clean, lint-free cloth | Abrasive scrubbing or scratching |
| Screen smudge that remains | Follow model-specific cleaning guidance | Assuming all household glass cleaners are safe |
| Dust around keys | Gentle external cleaning | Liquids poured into keys or removing keys without guidance |
| Dirty ports or slots | Stop and consult manufacturer instructions | Metal tools, liquids, or forced objects inside openings |
| Spilled drink near electronics | Move away from the hazard and assess safely | Continuing to use or charge a wet device |
| Unknown cleaning chemical | Do not apply it until compatibility is confirmed | Mixing products or spraying directly onto electronics |

### Protect equipment during ordinary use

A stable desk or table reduces the chance of accidental falls. Avoid setting a laptop on the edge of a counter, carrying an open laptop by its display, or leaving a phone where someone will sit on it. Use a protective sleeve or case when transporting a device, but make sure the case does not block intended ventilation or interfere with charging.

Keep drinks and food away from openings and electrical connections. Route cables so people will not trip or pull equipment off a surface. Protect devices from rain, condensation, extreme heat, prolonged direct sunlight, and freezing conditions beyond the manufacturer's specifications. Some phones are advertised as water-resistant, but resistance varies by model and can decline through wear or damage. It does **not** mean a device is waterproof or safe to charge while wet.

### Shared equipment and accessibility

A school keyboard, library computer, or shared workplace device may have a designated cleaning procedure. Follow that procedure rather than using your own chemicals or unplugging equipment. Learners using assistive switches, adapted keyboards, hearing devices, or tactile markers should avoid removing accessibility equipment during cleaning. Ask the responsible technician or accessibility coordinator for an approved method.

> **Practical caution:** Never open a computer case, power adapter, battery housing, or display assembly as part of this lesson. Nothing in the practice activities requires tools or internal cleaning.

## 3. How do you manage power, batteries, and heat?

Every computer needs electrical power and a way to manage heat. Desktops commonly use a power cord and an internal power supply. Laptops, tablets, and smartphones usually include rechargeable batteries and charging circuits. Heat is produced during normal operation, so a warm device is not always defective. However, **excessive heat or a damaged battery can be dangerous**.

### Why ventilation matters

[[ventilation|Ventilation]] allows air to circulate around the equipment. Some computers include fans that move air through vents; other devices distribute heat through their body without a fan. If a fan-cooled laptop is placed on a blanket or cushion, fabric may cover an intake or exhaust opening. Restricted airflow can increase temperature, cause the computer to slow down, or trigger automatic shutdown.

For regular use, place a laptop on a stable surface that permits airflow around the openings specified by its manufacturer. Keep books, clothing, dust piles, and bags from blocking vents. Do not place powered devices under pillows or bedding. A desktop tower also needs clearance around its vents, even if it normally remains in one location.

A fan does not need to be completely silent. A noticeable increase in fan noise while running an intensive application may be normal. But persistent unusual noise, repeated temperature warnings, burning smells, or unexpected shutdowns deserve attention. Avoid diagnosing a fan by inserting a tool into the vent or opening the machine.

### Chargers and cables

A [[charger|charger]] or power adapter should be appropriate for the device's connector, electrical requirements, and manufacturer's safety guidance. A plug fitting into a port does not by itself guarantee that the combination is suitable. Some USB-C ports and power adapters support different charging capabilities. Use compatible, reputable equipment and follow the maker's requirements.

Before use, visually check accessible cords for cracked insulation, bent or exposed metal, fraying, discoloration, or obvious heat damage. Keep cords from being pinched under furniture or pulled sharply around corners. Never cover a charger that requires ventilation, stack heat-producing adapters under soft materials, or overload a power outlet or extension device. If power equipment appears damaged, **do not continue using or handling the damaged connection**; ask a qualified person for assistance.

### Battery basics

A [[battery|rechargeable battery]] gradually changes with use and age. Its capacity may decrease over time, and heavy workloads or high temperatures may affect battery runtime. Battery management software may adjust charging behavior to protect the device. There is no single percentage rule that every learner must follow for every phone and laptop; manufacturer recommendations and available battery-health settings differ.

It is sensible to avoid unnecessarily extreme temperatures, use approved charging equipment, and let built-in battery protections work as designed. If you will store a device unused for an extended period, consult the manufacturer's long-term storage guidance rather than assuming it should remain plugged in or completely drained.

### Heat and battery warning signs

| What you notice | What it might indicate | Appropriate beginner response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Device feels mildly warm during a task | Normal operating heat in some situations | Use it in an approved, ventilated location |
| Air vent is covered by a blanket | Restricted airflow | Stop the activity and clear the obstruction if safe |
| Battery appears swollen or pushes the case apart | Possible battery damage | Stop using and charging; seek qualified service |
| Smoke, sparks, or a burning smell | Possible electrical or fire hazard | Move away, do not touch damaged equipment, and obtain emergency help as appropriate |
| Charger or cable has visible damage | Possible electrical hazard | Do not use it; arrange approved replacement or inspection |
| Repeated thermal shutdowns | Cooling, environmental, or hardware problem | Stop demanding tasks and consult authorized support |

**A swollen battery is never a beginner repair task.** Do not press it flat, puncture it, bend it, remove it without appropriate expertise, or continue charging it to see whether the problem disappears. Follow the manufacturer's safety and service guidance.

### If water or another liquid reaches the device

Stop using the affected equipment and disconnect it from power **only if it can be done safely without touching wet or damaged electrical components**. Do not charge it again merely because the outside looks dry. Do not use a hair dryer, oven, direct heat source, or a container of rice as an improvised repair. Follow product-specific liquid-exposure instructions and seek professional advice when liquid may have entered the device.

Apple's iPhone handling guidance, for example, says not to charge a device exposed to liquid until it is completely dry and gives model-specific procedures. Other devices have different requirements. A person's safety takes priority over saving the device.

### What about surge protection?

A [[surge-protector|surge protector]] is designed to reduce some electrical surges, subject to its ratings and limitations. It does not guarantee protection from every electrical event and is not a substitute for safe wiring or suitable equipment. Avoid treating extension cords or power strips as an unlimited supply of outlets. For significant electrical questions, consult a qualified professional.

## 4. How do you keep software up to date?

Physical cleaning helps the outside of the computer; [[software-update|software updates]] help maintain programs and the computer's operating environment. Updates may correct bugs, improve reliability, support new capabilities, and repair security weaknesses. A [[security-patch|security patch]] is an update intended to address a security vulnerability. Updates cannot guarantee that a device will never have a problem, but delaying important supported fixes can leave known risks unresolved.

### Where updates should come from

Begin with the official update area built into your device. A computer's [[operating-system|operating system]] typically has a Settings location for viewing or installing updates. Applications may update through a trusted app store or their own legitimate update mechanisms. Use established official sources rather than a pop-up advertisement that says your computer is infected or that you must install a “miracle updater.”

Do not download random driver packages, firmware files, or “PC optimizer” programs simply because a web advertisement promises faster performance. Drivers and [[firmware|firmware]] can be important, but an incorrect or malicious update can cause significant problems. Normally allow the supported system and hardware vendor to provide appropriate updates automatically.

### Locate the update area on common devices

Menu names can change with device model, software version, and organizational policies. The following are common starting points, **not a guarantee that every screen will be identical**.

| Device type | Common place to inspect updates | Helpful reminder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Windows 11 PC | Settings, then Windows Update | Save work before an update that requires restarting |
| Mac | System Settings, General, then Software Update | Install compatible updates provided for that Mac |
| Chromebook | Settings, About ChromeOS, then Check for updates | Some older models eventually reach an update end date |
| iPhone or iPad | Settings, General, then Software Update | Use an appropriate power and network connection |
| Android phone or tablet | Settings, then the system or software update area | The exact location varies by manufacturer |

This table is only a navigation aid. It does not require the learner to install anything during this lesson. On a shared or managed device, administrators may handle updates and restrict the controls you can see.

### A sensible update routine

1. **Open Settings by a familiar, trusted route.** Do not follow an unsolicited update link from an email or advertisement.
2. **Read the update notice.** Confirm which operating system or app is being updated and whether a restart or extra storage may be needed.
3. **Save work and plan for interruption.** A system may restart or remain temporarily unavailable while it finishes installing a legitimate update.
4. **Use an appropriate power supply and network.** Follow the device maker's instructions, especially for larger operating-system updates.
5. **Install supported updates or allow authorized automatic updates.** If an update is managed by your school or employer, follow its process.
6. **Confirm completion.** Review the normal update area and any restart notice rather than trusting a random page that claims the work is finished.

Never forcibly power off a device during a normal firmware or operating-system update unless manufacturer support specifically instructs you or a physical safety emergency requires it. Interrupting certain updates can damage the software installation or prevent normal startup.

### Updates, upgrades, and support dates

An **update** commonly improves an existing system or application. An [[upgrade|upgrade]] may move to a new major release, introduce broader changes, or require different hardware. Not every computer can run every newest release. An [[end-of-support|end-of-support]] date means a company no longer provides some or all routine support and fixes for that version or product under normal terms. A device may still power on after its support ends, but that does not mean it remains suitable for sensitive online activities.

As of this lesson's preparation, **standard support for Windows 10 ended on October 14, 2025**, although certain paid or specialized arrangements may provide extended security coverage. An older Chromebook may also have a model-specific final update date. Do not infer a support date solely from how old a machine looks. Check the manufacturer's official support information or consult your organization.

### Apps, browsers, and protective software

A web browser, email app, and document editor may receive separate updates from the operating system. Keep reputable applications current using their approved channels. If the device includes built-in security protections, leave them enabled unless an authorized administrator directs otherwise. [[antivirus|Antivirus or endpoint protection]] can help identify some threats, but it is not a substitute for careful choices or backups.

A program that is unfamiliar is not automatically malicious, and a warning message is not automatically real. Avoid downloading a second security product in response to a frightening webpage. Use the actual operating system's security area or an approved support contact to evaluate concerns.

> **Remember:** The most useful beginner software-maintenance habit is to know **where official updates live** and to avoid installing unknown software in the name of maintenance.

## 5. How do you protect files and manage storage?

An important part of computer care is protecting the information that makes the device useful. Documents, family photographs, class assignments, financial records, and personal projects may be much harder to replace than the computer itself. The concepts of [[save|saving]], [[sync|synchronization]], and [[backup|backup]] are related, but they are not identical.

### Saving is the first step

To **save** means to preserve work in a location selected by an app or user. For example, saving a letter puts a file in a folder, on a device, or in an approved online location. You should learn whether the app saves automatically, shows a Save button, or requires you to name a document. A file appearing on the screen does not always prove that it was saved. Close and reopen a harmless practice document, if appropriate, to learn the difference.

Use descriptive names such as `reading-notes-november.md` or `family-budget-draft.xlsx` rather than a series of indistinguishable files named “new” or “final.” Choose locations where you will be able to find the work later. For shared devices, make sure you save only in your authorized account or folder.

### Storage is not the same as a backup

[[storage|Storage]] refers to the place where saved information resides. A computer's internal SSD, a phone's flash storage, and an external drive can all hold files. But a file stored only on the laptop is still vulnerable if that laptop is lost, damaged, stolen, or becomes unreadable. Even when a drive normally keeps files without power, it can fail.

A **backup** is an extra copy kept so information can be recovered after an unwanted loss or change. Good backups are separate enough that a single accident is less likely to destroy both the original and its backup. A trusted external drive, a suitably protected cloud-backup service, or another approved system may serve that purpose. Sensitive information should be protected by appropriate account security and, where suitable, encryption.

### Synchronization and backups are different

[[sync|Synchronization]] keeps selected data consistent across devices or locations. This can be convenient: change a document on one device, and the change may appear on another. But synchronization can also carry accidental deletion or unwanted changes to other devices. Some sync services have version history or recovery bins; others differ. **Do not assume that automatic sync means every deleted or corrupted file can always be restored.** Check the specific service's recovery and retention features.

| Action | Main purpose | What to understand |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Save a file | Keep the current work | It may exist in only one location |
| Synchronize files | Keep selected copies in agreement | Changes and deletions may spread |
| Back up files | Maintain recoverable extra copies | A usable backup should be recoverable after loss |
| Restore a file | Recover saved information from a backup or other recovery source | Recovery may require the correct account, device, or permissions |

### A basic backup plan

A beginner's plan can begin with a small number of important files and simple questions:

1. **What matters?** Identify schoolwork, important documents, photographs, and other files that would be difficult to replace.
2. **Where is the original?** Know whether each file is on the device, in an online service, or somewhere else.
3. **Where is the extra copy?** Choose an approved location sufficiently separate from the original.
4. **How often does it need updating?** Match backup frequency to how often the information changes.
5. **How would you recover it?** Know what account, password, recovery key, external device, or support person would be required.
6. **How can you verify it?** When appropriate, confirm that a harmless test file can be found and opened from the backup without overwriting the original.

More advanced guidance sometimes uses a **3-2-1 approach**: maintain three copies of important data, across two different types of storage, with one copy off-site. This is a useful resilience model rather than a promise that a specific set of three copies can never fail. A practical initial goal for a beginner is to ensure there is at least one reliable, separate recovery copy of irreplaceable information, then improve the plan as circumstances allow.

### A safe example: preserving a practice document

Suppose you wrote `computer-care-practice.txt`. After saving it, confirm that it appears where you expect. If you already have an approved backup location, copy the harmless file there using the normal method. Finally, open the extra copy to check that it contains the intended text. Do not move or delete the only original and do not change backup settings for other people's data.

An online service may copy a file automatically, but you should still confirm that the intended folder is included and that the sync or backup status is normal. An icon showing a cloud does not, by itself, describe the service's complete recovery capability.

### Free space, clutter, and careful deletion

A nearly full storage device may prevent downloads, updates, or new file saves. You can usually inspect available capacity through a device's Storage or About settings. If storage is limited, consider whether **your own known, unneeded files** can be safely removed, or whether an approved storage option is needed. The operating system may offer built-in recommendations for temporary files, but review the descriptions before accepting them.

Do **not** delete files from unfamiliar system folders, remove recovery partitions, erase an entire drive, or install aggressive “cleaner” programs to make the computer faster. Do not empty the recycle bin or recently deleted area without checking that it does not contain anything you need. Uninstalling unfamiliar software can also break a system, particularly if the device is managed by an employer or school.

### Before replacing, selling, or recycling a device

The ordinary Delete command may not fully remove sensitive information from storage. If you are authorized to sell, donate, or recycle a device, first preserve important files and follow the manufacturer's **device-specific secure erasure or reset process**. Remove accounts, trusted accessories, and SIM or removable storage when appropriate under official instructions. An organization's technology administrator should handle an organizational device. Electronics should be taken to a reputable collection or recycling channel rather than treated as ordinary household trash where prohibited or inappropriate.

> **Practical caution:** In this course, you will **plan** a backup and examine harmless examples. You are not expected to erase drives, reset devices, buy storage products, or modify someone else's files.

## 6. When should you stop and ask for help?

Not every unusual computer behavior means a serious problem. A full battery icon, a temporarily slow webpage, or a fan that speeds up during a demanding task may be ordinary. Other signs are important because they involve **personal safety**, **information loss**, or **possible unauthorized activity**. The beginner's responsibility is to recognize the difference and choose a safe next action—not to fix every problem alone.

### Physical emergencies take priority

If you see smoke, fire, sparks, exposed electrical wiring, significant battery swelling, or a device that appears dangerously hot, stop using it. Move yourself and others away from the hazard. Do not handle damaged electrical equipment, continue charging, puncture the battery, or try an improvised repair. Contact emergency services when circumstances warrant and use qualified repair or disposal resources afterward. If liquid is involved, do not touch wet electrical equipment or attempt to unplug it in unsafe conditions.

A merely dirty screen can wait; electrical safety cannot. The same applies to a cable that has melted or a power adapter that smells burnt. These are not ordinary cleaning problems.

### Data or performance problems

A document may disappear from view because it was closed, hidden, moved, or not saved. Repeated crashes, strange clicking from a mechanical drive, frequent unexplained restarts, or storage warnings may indicate a problem that needs attention. If the device is safe and still working, save important work and check the status of your existing backups before pursuing unfamiliar fixes. When a problem is persistent, contact the device maker or an approved support person.

Do not run random “deep repair” commands from a website, download files advertised as universal fixes, or reset the device without considering the effect on stored information. A factory reset can erase personal files and applications. The appropriate recovery method depends on the situation, hardware, software, and available backups.

### Suspicious pop-ups and security warnings

[[malware|Malware]] is harmful software. [[phishing|Phishing]] involves attempts to deceive people into taking unsafe actions, often including entering credentials or downloading unwanted software. A website may display a dramatic message such as “Your computer is infected—call this number immediately.” The appearance of such a message **inside a webpage** does not make it an authentic operating-system warning.

A safer response is to avoid clicking the warning, avoid calling its advertised telephone number, and avoid downloading its proposed tool. If possible, close the suspicious tab using normal controls and check your device's legitimate security status through its usual Settings or approved security application. If you entered a password or other sensitive information into a suspicious page, use the real service's recovery or security controls and request trusted assistance promptly.

### Choose an appropriate response

| Warning or situation | First priority | Do not do this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visible smoke or battery swelling | Personal safety and qualified help | Keep charging or open the battery |
| Liquid entered an electronic device | Stop use and follow official liquid-damage guidance | Immediately reconnect power or apply heat |
| Suspicious browser popup demands payment | Avoid interacting and verify through trusted channels | Call the popup's number or install its tool |
| Device storage almost full | Inspect familiar storage settings and protect files | Delete unknown system files |
| Update requests a restart | Save work and use the official update process | Force shutdown during installation without cause |
| Important file seems missing | Check ordinary folders and recovery options | Factory-reset the computer as a first step |
| Work or school device requests admin approval | Contact authorized support | Circumvent the restriction |

### A practical decision tree

**Step 1 — Safety:** Is there any immediate physical hazard? If yes, stop and seek appropriate help. If no, continue.

**Step 2 — Information:** Might the next action destroy a file, change settings, or disconnect an account? If yes, preserve work and verify the recovery plan first.

**Step 3 — Source:** Is the suggested action from the operating system, device maker, authorized administrator, or another trusted source? If not, do not follow it merely because it appears urgent.

**Step 4 — Scope:** Can you safely perform the step without disassembly, special tools, unknown software, or elevated permissions? If not, refer the problem to qualified support.

This sequence is a simple form of [[risk-assessment|risk assessment]]: considering possible consequences before acting. It does not require technical expertise. Often the best beginner decision is to document what you saw, avoid further changes, and ask for help.

### Asking for help effectively

You can help a support person by reporting the device type, the task you were attempting, when the problem began, and the exact wording of an error message **without sharing sensitive data**. A screenshot may help in ordinary circumstances, but inspect it first for names, email addresses, account numbers, messages, verification codes, or other personal information. If the device is physically unsafe, do not handle it merely to obtain a screenshot or identifier.

> **Remember:** A confident computer user knows both what to do and when to stop. Stopping an unsafe action is a successful maintenance decision.

## 7. Guided practice: Build a computer-care routine

These exercises can be completed with any computer, tablet, or smartphone, or entirely on paper. No exercise requires disassembly, account credentials, file deletion, system resets, purchases, or installing an update. If the equipment belongs to a school, library, workplace, or another person, follow its policies and ask permission before changing anything.

### Activity 1: Observe the device and its environment (about 5–7 minutes)

1. Identify the device type: desktop, laptop, tablet, smartphone, or another computer.
2. Observe where it sits or is carried. Is the location stable, dry, and protected from accidental falls?
3. Without moving heavy equipment, look for accessible ventilation openings or signs of blocked airflow.
4. Look at visible cables from a safe distance. Do not unplug them or handle anything damaged.
5. Record two existing good habits and one improvement you could make safely.

*Example observation worksheet*

| Item | Your observation | Possible safe improvement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Laptop location | On a stable desk | Keep drinks farther away |
| Airflow | Rear vents appear clear | Avoid placing it on a blanket |
| Cable placement | Charging lead crosses walkway | Ask permission to route it more safely |
| Your device | ________ | ________ |

> **Example response:** “My laptop is on a desk, but my water glass is next to the keyboard. I can move the drink away without altering the computer.”

### Activity 2: Decide whether a cleaning action is appropriate (about 5 minutes)

For each scenario, say **safe beginner action**, **check manufacturer guidance first**, or **stop and seek qualified help**.

### Activity 5: Respond to an unexpected warning (about 5–7 minutes)

Read each fictional situation and choose the safest first step.

1. A browser tab announces that your device is infected and tells you to call a number.
2. A laptop on a bed becomes unusually hot, but there is no smoke or visible damage.
3. A shared computer says software updates are managed by an administrator.
4. You cannot find a document and a stranger online suggests a factory reset.
5. A phone with visible battery swelling will no longer hold a charge.

:::answer Show Activity 5 answers
1. Do not trust or interact with the popup. Close the suspicious page normally if possible and verify through the real security controls or trusted support.
2. Stop the demanding activity, put the device in a manufacturer-approved, ventilated location if safe, and seek help if overheating persists. Do not continue if there is a physical hazard.
3. Follow the organization's process and contact authorized support rather than trying to bypass its settings.
4. Check ordinary save locations, recent files, and available backups. A factory reset is not an appropriate first response to a missing document.
5. Stop using and charging it; seek qualified service. Do not puncture, press, or repair the battery yourself.
:::

- **After important work:** Save the document and confirm that a suitable backup process protects it.

## 8. Common misconceptions

### “If a computer turns on, it does not need any care.”

A working computer may still need secure backups, supported updates, appropriate airflow, and protection from spills or theft. Problems can develop without warning. Basic preventive habits reduce avoidable risks even when nothing looks wrong.

### “Any cleaner that works on glass is safe for a screen.”

Not necessarily. Displays may have delicate coatings, and electronics have openings that must remain dry. The safest cleaning approach depends on the manufacturer and specific surface. A familiar household cleaner is not automatically compatible.

### “A warm laptop must be broken.”

Not always. Computers generate heat, and a warm surface may be expected during some tasks. The warning signs are excessive or rapidly increasing heat, smoke, burning smells, swollen batteries, repeated shutdowns, and other signs described by the manufacturer.

### “I should open my computer and remove dust to complete basic maintenance.”

No. This lesson requires no disassembly. Internal cleaning and battery work involve device-specific risks and may require qualified service. External observation and approved cleaning are enough for beginner care.

### “An update popup on a webpage is the same as an official operating-system update.”

No. A webpage can imitate an urgent system alert. Use the device's trusted update settings or legitimate vendor channels. A sudden browser message demanding a phone call, payment, or unknown download is not reliable proof of infection.

### “Security software makes backups unnecessary.”

No. Protective software may reduce some threats, but files can still be lost through hardware failure, accidental deletion, theft, damage, or other events. Backups address recovery needs that antivirus software does not.

### “Cloud synchronization always protects a file from deletion.”

Not necessarily. A sync service may propagate a deletion or unwanted change to other locations. Check its history, retention, and recovery features. A separate, verifiable backup is a stronger recovery plan.

### “I can make a computer faster by deleting unfamiliar folders.”

Deleting unknown system files or applications can cause serious problems. Inspect available storage, remove only files you clearly own and no longer need, and use approved built-in guidance. Ask for help before taking irreversible action.

### “A battery must always be fully drained before charging.”

This is not a universal rule for modern rechargeable devices. Charging and battery-health recommendations differ by product. Follow the manufacturer's current guidance rather than applying a rigid percentage rule to every laptop and phone.

### “If a device is water-resistant, it can safely be charged while wet.”

No. Water-resistance ratings have limits. Charging a wet device can be unsafe or damaging. Stop, follow the device's liquid-exposure instructions, and allow professional assessment when required.

### “A factory reset is a routine cleanup step.”

No. Resets may remove files, apps, settings, or access to accounts. They are not a substitute for ordinary cleaning or storage review. Confirm your recovery plan and use authorized, model-specific instructions before any reset or secure erase.

### “An old device that still works must still receive security support.”

Not necessarily. Manufacturers eventually end normal updates for products or software releases. A device can function after support ends while becoming more exposed to newly discovered security flaws. Verify the support status using official sources.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Words with a dotted underline earlier in the lesson open brief definitions. When a verified MGU Encyclopedia article exists, a tooltip also provides a link to further reading. The important concepts are summarized here.

- **Computer care:** Practical habits that protect physical equipment, software, information, and safe operation.
- **Preventive maintenance:** Steps taken to lower the chance of future problems rather than repairing an existing fault.
- **Repair:** Diagnosing and correcting a fault, which may require specialist knowledge or replacement parts.
- **Display:** Screen hardware that shows information from a computer. [Output Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/output-devices).
- **Keyboard:** Input hardware used to enter characters and commands. [Input Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/input-devices).
- **Mouse:** A pointing input device used to select and control items on screen. [Input Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/input-devices).
- **Touchpad:** A touch-sensitive pointing surface, commonly found on laptops.
- **Ventilation:** The movement of air around or through a device to help release heat.
- **Charger:** A compatible power accessory used to supply electrical power for charging a device.
- **Rechargeable battery:** A component that stores electrical energy for portable operation.
- **Surge protector:** Equipment designed to limit some electrical surges within its specified capabilities.
- **Software update:** A change supplied to software to address defects, security concerns, compatibility, or features.
- **Security patch:** A software correction that addresses a known or suspected security weakness. [Computer Security](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/computer-security).
- **Operating system:** Core software that manages the device and supports its applications. [Operating Systems](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/software-systems/operating-systems).
- **Firmware:** Low-level software associated with the operation or control of hardware. [Firmware](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/software-systems/firmware).
- **Upgrade:** A move to a different major release or capability level, possibly involving new requirements.
- **End of support:** The point after which a product or software version no longer receives certain normal support or updates.
- **Antivirus or endpoint protection:** Software designed to detect, block, or help respond to some harmful activity. [Computer Security](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/computer-security).
- **Save:** Preserve information in a file or another selected location.
- **Storage:** Hardware or service capacity used to retain information beyond an immediate task. [Storage Hardware](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/storage-hardware).
- **Synchronization (sync):** Keeping selected data consistent between devices, accounts, or locations.
- **Backup:** An extra recoverable copy of data kept to protect against the loss of the original.
- **Restore:** Recover data from a backup or another supported recovery source.
- **Malware:** Software designed to perform harmful or unauthorized actions. [Malware and Threats](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/malware-and-threats).
- **Phishing:** A deceptive attempt to trick people into revealing information or taking unsafe actions. [Malware and Threats](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/security-systems/malware-and-threats).
- **Risk assessment:** Considering what could go wrong and how serious the consequences may be before acting.
- **Factory reset:** A device or software reset toward an initial configuration, which may delete personal files and settings.
- **Secure erase:** A device-appropriate process intended to remove data before transfer or disposal, subject to the technology and method used.
