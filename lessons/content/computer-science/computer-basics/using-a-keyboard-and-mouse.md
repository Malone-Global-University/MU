## Lesson 5: Using a Keyboard and Mouse

A keyboard and mouse are two of the most common tools for operating a desktop or laptop computer. You use a keyboard to enter words, numbers, and commands. You use a mouse—or a laptop touchpad—to point at things on the screen, choose them, and move through pages. This lesson explains the parts of these devices, teaches common actions step by step, and provides safe practice activities. You do not need to type quickly or already know any keyboard shortcuts.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 65–85 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–4 recommended · **Practice equipment:** A computer with a keyboard and mouse or touchpad, if available (paper-based alternatives included)

> **Essential idea:** A [[keyboard|keyboard]] sends information and commands to a computer through keys. A [[mouse|mouse]] or [[touchpad|touchpad]] controls an on-screen [[pointer|pointer]]. Typing, clicking, scrolling, selecting, and using simple shortcuts are separate skills that become easier with calm, repeated practice. What happens depends on which application or control is active.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Identify the main groups of keys on a typical keyboard and explain their functions.
2. Enter and correct a short piece of text using letters, numbers, punctuation, Space, Enter, Backspace, and Shift.
3. Explain the difference between the mouse pointer and the text insertion point.
4. Demonstrate or describe a single click, double-click, secondary click, scrolling, and dragging.
5. Use a keyboard and pointing device together to select, edit, and review harmless practice text.
6. Recognize several basic keyboard shortcuts and important Windows, Mac, and Chromebook differences.
7. Describe accessibility options and safe, comfortable input habits.

## 1. How do a keyboard and mouse help you use a computer?

A keyboard and mouse are [[input-device|input devices]]. They allow people to tell a computer what to do or give it information to process. A keyboard sends signals when keys are pressed. A mouse detects movement and button actions. The computer's software interprets those signals and responds, usually by updating something on the display. The screen presents the result, but it is not the same thing as the device that entered the command.

A [[hardware|hardware]] keyboard may be built into a laptop, placed on a desk, or connected wirelessly. Most use a familiar arrangement of letter and number keys, though key sizes, names, layouts, and languages can vary. Some computers instead show an on-screen keyboard. A mouse usually rests on a surface and moves a small pointer around the display. Laptop touchpads achieve similar results without requiring a separate mouse.

### The pointer is not the text cursor

The [[pointer|mouse pointer]] is a symbol that moves as you move the mouse or slide a finger on a touchpad. It may appear as an arrow, hand, or another shape depending on what it is pointing toward. The [[insertion-point|text insertion point]] is a blinking vertical line in editable text, showing where characters will appear when you type. People sometimes call either one a **cursor**, so the distinction matters.

For example, move the arrow over a blank part of a document. That does not automatically move the blinking insertion point. You normally click or otherwise focus an editable area to choose where to type. If letters do not appear, first check whether the intended text box is active.

### What is keyboard focus?

[[focus|Focus]] means that a particular field, button, link, or other interface control is currently ready to receive relevant keyboard actions. A text field with focus may accept letters, numbers, and Backspace. A focused button may respond when you press Enter or Space, depending on how the interface is designed. Merely moving the mouse pointer over a control does not always give it keyboard focus.

A computer may show focus with a border, highlight, or visible insertion point. On an accessible website, the Tab key can often move focus to the next interactive control. Understanding focus explains why the same key can produce different results in different locations.

### Think of an everyday task

Suppose you want to write a brief reminder in a notes application:

1. You use the mouse or touchpad to open the application and select an editable area.
2. You type `Remember the meeting.` with the keyboard.
3. The application displays the characters as they are entered.
4. You correct a mistake using Backspace or another editing command.
5. You save the note if needed, using the application's available Save or autosave process.

The keyboard and mouse are working together, but software determines what their signals mean. You do not need to understand the internal electronics to learn these everyday operations.

> **Remember:** A mouse pointer shows where you are pointing; a blinking insertion point shows where text will be entered. They may be in different locations.

## 2. What are the main parts of a keyboard?

Most desktop and laptop keyboards group keys by purpose. Keyboards may be full-size, compact, multilingual, or built into a portable device. Not every keyboard has a numeric keypad or the same labels. Practice recognizing a key by its function, not only by its position.

### Letter keys and number keys

The **letter keys** enter characters such as A, B, and C. On common English-language layouts, the top row of letters begins with Q, W, E, R, T, and Y; this is often called a **QWERTY** layout. Other languages and regions may use different arrangements. A keyboard can also have number keys above the letters and, on some larger models, a separate [[numeric-keypad|numeric keypad]] on the right.

When you type, use a light, comfortable touch. You do not need to strike keys forcefully. Typing accuracy matters more than speed at the beginning. If a symbol appears unexpectedly, the keyboard layout or a modifier key may be responsible.

### Space, Enter, Backspace, and Delete

The [[spacebar|Spacebar]] is the long key near the bottom; it inserts a blank space between words when typing in an editable field. The [[enter|Enter]] key often starts a new paragraph in a document, but in a form or search box it may submit a response or activate a selected command. Read the context before pressing it.

[[backspace|Backspace]] normally removes a character immediately before the insertion point in a text editor. [[delete-key|Delete]] on many full-size PC keyboards removes a character after the insertion point, but the exact effect depends on the keyboard, operating system, and application. On some Macs, a key labeled **Delete** behaves like Backspace on a Windows keyboard; forward deletion may use another key combination. The purpose is correcting text, not erasing a whole document automatically.

### Shift, Caps Lock, and punctuation

The [[shift|Shift]] key changes the effect of another key while held. Holding Shift with a letter commonly produces an uppercase letter, while holding Shift with a number or punctuation key often produces the symbol shown on the upper part of that key. For example, on a typical United States keyboard, Shift+1 types an exclamation mark (`!`); the location of symbols varies across layouts.

[[caps-lock|Caps Lock]] is a toggle that often makes alphabetic letters uppercase until switched off. It does not act as a substitute for every Shift function. If your words suddenly appear in capital letters, check whether Caps Lock is active. Some keyboards have a small indicator light; others show an on-screen notification.

Punctuation includes periods, commas, question marks, apostrophes, quotation marks, colons, and other symbols. Take time to locate them on the keyboard in front of you. A lesson example may not match a non-US keyboard exactly.

### Tab, Escape, and arrow keys

[[tab|Tab]] often moves keyboard focus from one form field or control to the next. In some text editors it instead inserts indentation. Shift+Tab commonly moves focus backward. Tab is particularly useful when completing a form or navigating without a mouse, but it does not guarantee that every program uses the same sequence.

[[escape|Escape]] or **Esc** often closes a menu, dismisses a temporary dialog, or cancels an action. It should not be assumed to undo every change. The [[arrow-keys|arrow keys]] (Up, Down, Left, and Right) may move the insertion point through text, move a selection, or navigate a menu. Their effect also depends on focus.

### Control keys and operating-system keys

Several keys are primarily used in combinations rather than to type letters:

- **Ctrl** (Control) appears on many Windows and Chromebook keyboards.
- **Alt** is often used with other keys for alternate commands on Windows and other systems.
- **Command (⌘)** is a common modifier for keyboard shortcuts on Macs.
- **Option (⌥)** is another modifier found on Macs; some keyboards also label it Alt.
- A **Windows key** is present on many Windows keyboards; Chromebooks have a **Search** or **Launcher** key on many models.
- **Fn**, found on some laptops and compact keyboards, changes access to certain hardware or function-key behaviors; its exact behavior varies.

A [[shortcut|keyboard shortcut]] means pressing a combination of keys to perform an action. You do not have to memorize many shortcuts to be successful in this course. The practical ones appear later.

### Function keys and numeric keypad

Some keyboards have a row labeled F1 through F12 or similar. These are **function keys** whose actions depend on the computer and application; on laptops, they may share keys with brightness, volume, or other controls. A separate numeric keypad may help with frequent number entry. On some keyboards its behavior depends on **Num Lock**. Neither feature is necessary for every beginner task.

*Common keyboard keys and their usual jobs*

| Key or key group | Typical use | Beginner example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Letters and numbers | Enter text and numeric information | Type a name or a date |
| Spacebar | Insert a space | Separate two words |
| Enter / Return | Start a new paragraph or activate a command | Begin a paragraph in a document |
| Backspace | Correct characters before the insertion point | Remove a mistyped letter |
| Shift | Temporarily modify another key | Type one capital letter |
| Caps Lock | Toggle a capital-letter mode | Type a short uppercase label |
| Tab | Move focus or indent, depending on context | Move to the next form field |
| Arrow keys | Navigate text, menus, or selections | Move within a sentence |
| Escape | Dismiss or cancel some temporary actions | Close an open menu |
| Ctrl / Command | Combine with other keys for shortcuts | Copy selected text |

> **Quick check:** Identify Spacebar, Enter, Shift, Backspace, Tab, and the arrow keys on your own keyboard. If you use a virtual keyboard or adaptive control, find how that input system performs the equivalent actions.

## 3. How do you type and edit text?

Typing becomes easier when you understand the order of operations: find a safe place to type, place the insertion point, enter text, and inspect the result. For practice, use an empty notes document or another ordinary text field in an application you already know. Do not use a password field, a public comment box, or a message addressed to another person.

### Step 1: Choose an editable area

Open a simple notes or text-editing application if one is available. You might use Notepad on Windows, TextEdit on Mac, a compatible notes application, or another trusted editor. Names and menus differ. Click an empty editable area once, or reach it using Tab. You should ordinarily see a blinking insertion point or another clear indication that text can be entered.

If you click a web page's blank background instead of a text field, typing may do something else or nothing at all. If you are unsure, stop and check that the correct window and field are active before continuing.

### Step 2: Enter a short sentence

Type the following practice sentence carefully:

`My computer helps me learn.`

Use the Spacebar once between words. To type the capital M, hold Shift while pressing M, then release Shift. Locate the period at the end. It is acceptable to type slowly. Stop briefly to check each word before moving to the next.

### Step 3: Correct a mistake

Suppose you accidentally type `learnn` with an extra n. Place the insertion point immediately after the extra n and press Backspace once. You should see `learn` again. If the incorrect character is in the middle of a sentence, click near the mistake and use an arrow key to move the insertion point precisely. Then delete only the character you intend to remove.

If you need to add a word between other words, click the location or use arrow keys. Add the missing word and any needed spaces. An editor usually inserts new characters at the insertion point; some applications may provide different editing modes.

### Step 4: Start a new line, when appropriate

In a document editor, pressing Enter normally creates a new paragraph or line. But in a search box, chat input, or online form, Enter may send or submit information. Read the label and confirm the context first. For this lesson, do any Enter practice inside a local note, not in a real message or submission field.

### Selecting text

A [[selection|selection]] identifies the text you intend to edit. Selected text is commonly highlighted. You might select one word, a sentence, or an entire paragraph. You can often click at the beginning, hold the mouse button, and drag to the end. Many applications also let you place the insertion point and hold Shift while using an arrow key to extend a selection.

Selection is not the same as deletion. Selecting text prepares it for a command; typing a new character over a selection may replace the highlighted text. Before you type, press Backspace, or use a shortcut, verify what is selected. Avoid using **Select All** in documents containing important information until you understand the effect.

### Copy, cut, paste, and undo

The [[clipboard|clipboard]] is a temporary holding area used by many operating systems for selected content. **Copy** places a copy of selected content on the clipboard while leaving the original visible. **Cut** generally removes selected editable content and places it on the clipboard. **Paste** inserts available clipboard content at the current insertion point or target. The exact behavior depends on the application and whether the content can be edited.

**Undo** is a command that reverses many recent editing changes, but the available undo history varies, and some actions cannot be undone. Undo is a safety net, not a substitute for saving or checking your work. Practice by correcting a harmless sample sentence before using these commands in important files.

### Typing safely and comfortably

Keep your hands relaxed. Take short breaks when repeating movements, position the keyboard comfortably, and avoid stretching to reach the pointer. If typing is uncomfortable, consider an adjusted setup or another supported input method. Do not enter real account credentials, payment card numbers, or other private data into practice worksheets or public websites.

> **Practice principle:** The goal is accurate, deliberate input, not typing speed. Re-read the result before deciding that the task is finished.

## 4. How do you use a mouse or touchpad?

A [[mouse|mouse]] is a pointing device that typically has a primary button, a secondary button, and often a scroll wheel. A [[touchpad|touchpad]] is a touch-sensitive surface on many laptops that moves the pointer as you slide a finger. Some touchpads have physical buttons; others recognize taps or presses on the pad. Their configuration is not identical across computers.

### Move the pointer

Place your hand on the mouse and move it gently across a usable surface, or slide a finger over the touchpad. Watch the pointer move on the screen. At first, practice going slowly toward a large, safe target such as an empty area or a non-destructive navigation control. Lifting and repositioning a mouse is normal when you reach the edge of your desk.

The pointer may change shape: an arrow often indicates ordinary pointing, a hand may indicate a link, and an I-shaped beam often appears over selectable text. The appearance is a clue, not a guarantee that clicking is safe. Read what a control says before activating it.

### Single click: Choose or activate

A [[click|single click]] means pressing and releasing the primary mouse button once, or using the equivalent configured touchpad action. Depending on the program, it may select an item, place the insertion point in a field, activate a button, or follow a link. On most standard setups, the primary button is the left button, but users can change that setting.

When practicing, distinguish between selecting an object and opening it. In a file manager, a single click may select a file without opening it. In a browser, a single click on a link usually follows the link. The action depends on the application and target.

### Double-click: Two quick primary clicks

A [[double-click|double-click]] means making two primary-button clicks in fairly quick succession without moving the pointer much. It commonly opens a file or folder in desktop file managers when the system uses double-click opening. Some environments use single-click opening instead. Double-clicking a web link is usually unnecessary and can cause confusing repeated actions.

If double-clicking is difficult, slow down and practice with a harmless file or folder. Operating systems may provide configurable double-click speed or alternative activation methods. **Do not double-click a dangerous or unfamiliar file just to practice.**

### Secondary click: Look for a contextual menu

A [[secondary-click|secondary click]] is commonly called a **right-click**, because the secondary mouse button is usually on the right. It often opens a [[context-menu|context menu]] offering actions related to the item beneath the pointer. On some Macs, a Control-click can open the same type of menu; touchpads may use a two-finger click or tap when configured to do so. Device settings can change the exact gesture.

A context menu can contain harmless options, but it may also contain **Delete**, **Rename**, or other commands that change files. Opening a menu is not the same as choosing a command. Read the options, and close the menu with Escape or by clicking elsewhere if appropriate.

### Scroll: See more content

[[scroll|Scrolling]] moves the view through content that does not all fit on the screen. A mouse wheel commonly scrolls vertically. A touchpad often supports scrolling with two fingers, although gestures vary. Scrolling is different from dragging an item; it generally changes which part of a document or page is visible, not the underlying text itself.

Try scrolling slowly through a long, ordinary page. If the page does not move, look for whether the pointer is above a separate scrolling panel or whether the window is active. Some applications have more than one area that can scroll.

### Drag and drop: Move with the primary button held

A [[drag-and-drop|drag-and-drop]] action commonly means moving the pointer over an item, pressing and holding the primary button, moving to a new location, and releasing. Some touchpads support a corresponding press-and-drag or configured tap-and-drag gesture. This can move windows, rearrange certain items, or select text, depending on the application.

Dragging files can sometimes **move or copy** them, so beginners should practice in a safe area rather than moving real files. Dragging the text cursor across a harmless sentence is a useful first exercise. Do not assume that every drag operation is reversible.

*Common pointer actions at a glance*

| Action | Typical result | Safe practice idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Move | Positions the pointer | Point to different corners of an empty window |
| Hover | May show a label or tooltip | Rest over a labeled control without clicking |
| Single click | Selects or activates, depending on context | Click inside a practice text field |
| Double-click | Often opens a file or folder | Open a known harmless folder if permitted |
| Secondary click | Often opens a context menu | Open a menu, read it, then dismiss it |
| Scroll | Moves through visible content | Read a longer page from top to bottom |
| Drag | Selects or moves an item | Select part of a practice sentence |

> **Remember:** A click can select, open, or submit depending on where it occurs. Read the target before you click, especially near Delete, Buy, Send, or Submit controls.

## 5. How do keyboard and mouse actions work together?

Most everyday tasks combine pointing with text entry. For example, you might click a search field, type a phrase, correct it, and then choose a result. Each input changes the application in a specific way. This section joins the individual skills into simple workflows.

### Example A: Write and revise a note

1. Open a trusted notes application or use a blank text area already provided for practice.
2. Click once inside the editable area to place the insertion point.
3. Type `Tomorrow I will practice.` and check the capitalization and period.
4. Move the insertion point before the word `practice` with a click or arrow keys.
5. Type `keyboard ` including the trailing space, producing `Tomorrow I will keyboard practice.`
6. Read the sentence and correct the awkward wording so it says `Tomorrow I will practice using a keyboard.`
7. Save the note only if you intend to retain it. If the app autosaves, verify where it is kept.

This task teaches that a screen can show text while the keyboard, pointer, and software determine where changes are made. It also demonstrates why reviewing the final words matters as much as entering them.

### Example B: Copy and paste within your practice note

1. Type `Computer skills improve with practice.` in a harmless note.
2. Select the sentence, or just the words `Computer skills`, using a mouse drag or another supported selection method.
3. Choose **Copy** from the application's Edit menu or context menu, if available.
4. Move the insertion point to a new empty line or safe location within the same note.
5. Choose **Paste**, then inspect the copied content.
6. Verify that the original text is still in place and the duplicate appears where intended.

If you choose **Cut** instead of **Copy**, the selected original may disappear from the editable area. Avoid cut-and-paste practice in any document where moving text could cause harm. Pasted formatting may vary among applications; this is normal.

### Example C: Open a menu and back out safely

Move the pointer to a menu or recognizable item in an app. Make a secondary click if appropriate, read the available commands, and press Escape or click outside to close it. Do not choose a destructive command. This exercise teaches that inspecting a menu is distinct from activating one of its entries.

### Example D: Move through a form without submitting it

In an ordinary form that is not connected to a real transaction or account, click the first field and enter fictional practice information. Press Tab once to move to the next field, if the form supports it. Watch which element gains focus. Use Shift+Tab to return if supported. Do not press a Submit, Send, Place Order, or other consequential button. If you have no safe form, simply describe the steps on paper.

### Useful editing shortcuts

On Windows and most Chromebooks, common editing shortcuts use **Ctrl**. On macOS, comparable shortcuts commonly use **Command (⌘)**. The table below gives typical behavior in ordinary text editors; each application can define exceptions. Shortcuts only apply to the appropriate selection or focused area.

*Common editing shortcuts by platform*

| Task | Windows / Chromebook | Mac | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Copy | Ctrl+C | Command+C | Copies selected content |
| Paste | Ctrl+V | Command+V | Inserts available clipboard content |
| Cut | Ctrl+X | Command+X | Removes and copies selected editable content |
| Undo | Ctrl+Z | Command+Z | Reverses a supported recent change |
| Select all | Ctrl+A | Command+A | Selects all content in the active context |
| Save | Ctrl+S | Command+S | Requests Save when the application supports it |

**A shortcut is not a universal promise.** For example, a web application might autosave, disable Save, or use its own commands. Select All can cover much more than the line you intended. Use copy, paste, and undo on a disposable practice note before working with important content.

### What if nothing happens?

Check three things before repeating an action: **Is the intended application active? Is the correct control or text area focused? Is the command supported there?** Pressing a key repeatedly while focus is in the wrong field can produce an unexpected result. If you accidentally open a menu, Escape often closes it without making a selection. If you accidentally edit text, review the result and use Undo only when the application supports it.

> **Practical habit:** Point or navigate to the correct target, confirm what has focus, perform one action, then inspect the result. This prevents many avoidable beginner errors.

## 6. What changes across devices and accessibility options?

The fundamental skills—entering characters, selecting controls, and navigating content—are broadly similar, but keyboards, gestures, and operating-system conventions vary. Learning the common pattern is more useful than assuming every machine behaves identically.

### Windows desktop and laptop computers

Windows computers often use a keyboard with Ctrl, Alt, and a Windows key. A mouse or touchpad normally offers a primary and secondary click. The primary button is often on the left, but it can be reassigned. Windows supports mouse pointer settings, on-screen keyboards, Sticky Keys, and other accessibility features. To explore available options without changing them, open Windows **Settings**, then look under **Accessibility** or **Bluetooth & devices**, depending on the setting.

For most simple text-editing tasks, Windows uses Ctrl+C for Copy and Ctrl+V for Paste. Windows key+U normally opens Accessibility settings. A shortcut can vary in a particular app, so the visible menus remain an important fallback.

### Mac computers

Mac keyboards commonly label their main shortcut modifier **Command (⌘)**. Copy and Paste are typically Command+C and Command+V, rather than Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V. Macs also have an **Option (⌥)** key. A secondary-click menu may be available by Control-clicking or by using a configured trackpad or mouse gesture. Settings for these behaviors appear in macOS system settings and vary with device and software version.

The key labeled **Delete** on many Mac laptop keyboards normally deletes backward, similar to Backspace on a Windows PC. This does not mean every keyboard or Mac application behaves identically.

### Chromebooks

Chromebook keyboards commonly use Ctrl for Copy and Paste. Many have a **Search** or **Launcher** key rather than a standard Windows key. Their top rows and touchpad gestures can differ from Windows or Mac keyboards. For example, supported Chromebook touchpads generally provide a two-finger secondary-click action. A built-in keyboard shortcuts reference and settings options can help users learn model-specific controls.

Some shortcuts and gesture arrangements are customizable. Check the actual Chromebook settings instead of forcing an unfamiliar key combination.

### Tablets and phones

Tablets and phones commonly display an [[on-screen-keyboard|on-screen keyboard]] when a text field gains focus. The user taps letters rather than pressing separate physical keys. Touch gestures replace many mouse actions: a tap often activates a control, a swipe often scrolls, and a long press may reveal a context menu. Not every app uses those gestures in the same way.

External keyboards and pointing devices may work with compatible tablets, but such accessories are optional and device-specific. Voice dictation is another possible input method. When a tablet is used with a physical keyboard, a familiar shortcut might be supported by some apps but not others.

### Accessibility: More than one way to enter information

[[accessibility|Accessibility]] means designing or adapting technology so people with varied needs can use it. A standard mouse and keyboard are not the only legitimate input arrangements. Depending on the operating system and equipment, options may include:

- An **on-screen keyboard** controlled with a pointer or another supported input device.
- **Sticky Keys**, which can help someone press multi-key shortcuts one key at a time on supported systems.
- **Mouse Keys**, which allows some Windows users to move the pointer with a numeric keypad.
- A trackball, alternative keyboard, switch control, or another adaptive device.
- Speech-to-text or voice input for entering certain kinds of information.
- Larger pointer sizes, modified pointer speed, reduced double-click demands, or keyboard-only navigation.

These features may require configuration and sometimes assistance. **No learner should be penalized for using an accessible input method instead of a conventional mouse.** The learning objective is to control the computer safely and effectively.

### Comfortable positioning and good habits

Sit or stand in a supported position with the screen visible and the input devices within easy reach. Keep wrists and shoulders reasonably relaxed, avoid sustained gripping or excessive pressure, and change position or rest when uncomfortable. If you experience pain, stop and adjust your setup or seek appropriate advice rather than forcing repeated motions. A clean, stable surface helps a mouse move predictably. Food or drink spills near a keyboard can cause damage.

When using a shared or public computer, ask before changing accessibility settings or peripheral configurations. Avoid installing unknown keyboard or mouse software and do not connect an unfamiliar device merely for practice.

*Practical differences across common systems*

| System or input method | Typical selection method | Common copy shortcut | Important note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Windows with mouse | Primary click | Ctrl+C | Settings may change button behavior |
| Mac with mouse or trackpad | Primary click or configured tap | Command+C | Command is the main common shortcut modifier |
| Chromebook with touchpad | Click or configured tap | Ctrl+C | Touchpad gestures and top-row keys vary |
| Phone or tablet touchscreen | Tap and contextual selection controls | Varies by app | A physical keyboard is not required |
| Adaptive input setup | Depends on configured device | Depends on software | Equivalent actions may use another method |

> **Accessibility note:** If a skill is difficult using one device, look for a supported alternative. A successful computer action is not defined by one particular hand motion.

## 7. Guided practice: Build keyboard and mouse skills

Practice in a harmless, private setting. If you have no keyboard or mouse available, read each step and explain what you would expect to happen. A trusted text editor is enough; no downloads, account creation, public posting, or software installation are required. When using a shared computer, follow its rules and do not change settings without permission.

### Activity 1: Keyboard identification (about 5–8 minutes)

1. Locate the letter keys and the number row.
2. Find Spacebar, Enter, Backspace, Shift, Caps Lock, Tab, Escape, and the arrow keys.
3. Identify which shortcut modifier your keyboard provides: Ctrl, Command, or another relevant key.
4. Locate at least one punctuation key.
5. Write down what four of these keys usually do.

*Keyboard identification worksheet*

| Key you found | Purpose | Your own example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spacebar | Inserts a space in editable text | Separate two words |
| Key 1 | ________ | ________ |
| Key 2 | ________ | ________ |
| Key 3 | ________ | ________ |
| Key 4 | ________ | ________ |

### Activity 2: Type and fix a short note (about 8–12 minutes)

1. Open a trusted empty text editor or imagine an editable text area.
2. Click or focus the text area.
3. Type `I can type with care.` exactly as shown.
4. Press Enter **only if you are in a document editor**, and type `Practice helps me improve.`
5. Intentionally create a small typo in the second sentence, then correct it using Backspace or an equivalent editing command.
6. Check the capital letters, spaces, periods, and word order. Save only if this is your own practice document.

:::answer Show Activity 2 sample result and checks
A completed practice note can read:

`I can type with care.`

`Practice helps me improve.`

There should be one space between ordinary words, a capital at the beginning of each sentence, and a period at the end. If you corrected the typo without damaging the rest of the sentence, you practiced both text entry and editing. On a device with a different keyboard layout, use the equivalent keys available to you.
:::

### Activity 3: Pointer-action observation (about 8–10 minutes)

1. Move the pointer slowly to different corners of an ordinary application window.
2. Hover over a harmless, labeled control and look for a tooltip or pointer change.
3. Single-click inside a blank practice field and observe whether an insertion point appears.
4. Scroll through a long page, then return toward the beginning.
5. If permitted, open a harmless secondary-click menu and dismiss it **without selecting a command**.
6. Explain when a double-click or drag would be appropriate; actual performance is optional.

:::answer Show Activity 3 expected observations
- **Move:** The pointer changes position but may not activate anything.
- **Hover:** An item may display a label or change appearance; not every item does.
- **Single click:** In an editable field, it generally positions the insertion point or gives the field focus.
- **Scroll:** The visible portion of a page or panel changes.
- **Secondary click:** A contextual menu may appear, which you can close without selecting an action.
- **Double-click or drag:** These are different actions and should be used only for a known, safe purpose.

## 8. Common misconceptions

### “The mouse pointer and the blinking text line are the same thing.”

Not quite. The pointer shows where the mouse or touchpad is aimed. The insertion point shows where typed characters appear in an editable area. You may need to click or navigate to a text field before typing.

### “A single click always opens a file.”

Not always. Single-clicking may select a file, activate a web link, position an insertion point, or press a button. The result depends on what you click and how the application is configured.

### “Double-clicking everything will make it work faster.”

No. Some items open with a single click; double-clicking unnecessarily may activate an action twice. Use double-click only when the software expects it.

### “Right-clicking deletes something.”

Usually a secondary click opens a context menu. **Choosing** a command from that menu may change or delete something. Read the menu carefully before selecting an option.

### “Pressing Enter always moves to the next line.”

In a document editor, Enter often creates a new paragraph. In a form, search box, or messaging tool, Enter may activate a command or submit information. Check the context first.

### “Caps Lock is the same as holding Shift.”

No. Caps Lock usually toggles uppercase letters. Shift temporarily modifies the key you press and is often needed for punctuation symbols. Their behaviors are related but not identical.

### “Copy and Cut do the same thing.”

Copy generally leaves the selected content in its original location. Cut generally removes selected editable content while placing it on the clipboard. This distinction matters when editing documents.

### “All computers use Ctrl for copying.”

No. Windows and Chromebooks commonly use Ctrl+C, while Mac computers commonly use Command+C. Some apps have different or additional controls.

### “Scrolling changes the content of a document.”

Scrolling normally changes which portion you are viewing. It does not, by itself, edit the written words or move the original file to another location.

### “The only proper way to use a computer is with a standard mouse.”

No. Touchpads, touchscreens, alternative keyboards, trackballs, assistive switches, voice input, and keyboard navigation can provide valid ways of interacting with computers. The right method depends on the task and the user's needs.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Terms with dotted underlines earlier in the lesson can be selected to open brief definitions. When an appropriate MGU Encyclopedia article has been verified, the tooltip also links to it for optional deeper reading. The same core concepts are summarized here.

- **Keyboard:** An input device with physical or on-screen keys used to enter characters and commands. [Input Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/input-devices).
- **Mouse:** A pointing device that controls a screen pointer and commonly provides primary and secondary buttons. [Input Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/input-devices).
- **Touchpad:** A touch-sensitive pointing surface, usually built into a laptop.
- **Input device:** Hardware that provides user actions or other information to a computer. [Input Devices](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/hardware-systems/input-devices).
- **Pointer:** A screen symbol, often an arrow, whose position is controlled by a pointing device.
- **Insertion point:** The position in editable text where typed characters will appear.
- **Focus:** The currently active interface control that receives relevant keyboard actions.
- **Spacebar:** A key that inserts a space in text.
- **Enter / Return:** A key that may start a paragraph or activate a command, depending on context.
- **Backspace:** A key that usually deletes the preceding character in an editable text area.
- **Delete key:** A key whose deletion direction and effect can differ by keyboard or platform.
- **Shift:** A key held while pressing another key to change the character or action.
- **Caps Lock:** A key that commonly toggles uppercase letters.
- **Tab:** A key often used to move keyboard focus to another control.
- **Arrow keys:** Keys used to move the insertion point or navigate items.
- **Shortcut:** A key combination that performs a supported action without selecting a menu item.
- **Selection:** Text or another item marked for a subsequent action.
- **Clipboard:** A temporary holding area for copied or cut content.
- **Single click:** One press and release of the primary pointing-device button.
- **Double-click:** Two quick primary clicks, used for certain activation tasks.
- **Secondary click:** An alternate click that often opens a context menu.
- **Scroll:** Move the visible view through content.
- **Drag and drop:** Hold and move a selected item, then release it at a destination.
- **Accessibility:** Design and accommodations that make computing usable through different needs and input methods. [Accessibility](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/human-interaction-systems/accessibility).
- **On-screen keyboard:** A software-displayed keyboard used with touch, a pointer, or an assistive method.
