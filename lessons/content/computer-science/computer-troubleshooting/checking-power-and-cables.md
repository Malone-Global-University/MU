Basic Computer Skills / Computer Troubleshooting Basics

## Lesson 2: Checking Power and Cables

You press a computer's power button, but nothing seems to happen. Perhaps the display is black, a laptop reports that its battery is low even though a charger is connected, or a printer suddenly stops communicating with the computer. A broken computer is one possibility, but it is not the only one. An unplugged power adapter, a switched-off outlet, a loose display connection, or a cable connected to the wrong port can create the impression of a major failure. Before replacing equipment or changing software settings, it is useful to check the simple, visible parts of the connection.

This lesson teaches a safe, systematic method: **identify what is not working, separate power from data or display signals, inspect without disassembling anything, check one connection at a time, and verify the result**. The emphasis is on routine checks that a beginner can perform without opening a computer or working with electrical wiring. Some problems require qualified assistance; recognizing when to stop is an essential part of troubleshooting.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 60–85 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lesson 1, Restart Before Panic, recommended · **Practice equipment:** A computer and its normal charger, power supply, or monitor cables; paper or a notes app for observations. All activities offer observation-only alternatives.

> **Essential idea:** A computer needs an appropriate source of [[electrical-power|electrical power]] to run, while many accessories need separate [[data-cable|data]] or [[video-cable|video]] connections. Check the power path first, distinguish it from the signal path, and do not handle damaged or unsafe equipment.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain the difference between an electrical power connection and a data, audio, or video connection.
1. Identify common power and peripheral cables and recognize that similar-looking connectors do not always perform the same function.
1. Perform a safe, external inspection of a computer, its power source, its charger, and selected accessories.
1. Follow a logical sequence of low-risk checks when a desktop, laptop, display, or peripheral seems unpowered or disconnected.
1. Recognize the limits of USB-C charging, charging indicators, power strips, surge protectors, and display cables.
1. Identify conditions that require stopping immediately and contacting a responsible person or qualified professional.
1. Record an observation, change one factor when safe, and verify whether the original symptom has changed.

## 1. Why check power and cables first?

### Small connection problems can look like large failures

A computer depends on several layers of equipment. A desktop computer needs electricity delivered through its power equipment before its operating system can start. A laptop may use its rechargeable [[battery|battery]], an external [[power-adapter|power adapter]], or a combination of the two. A separate monitor may require its own electrical connection even if the computer is running correctly. A keyboard, printer, external drive, webcam, and other [[peripheral|peripherals]] may also depend on their connections to communicate or receive power.

One loose connection can interrupt the chain. Suppose you press the computer's power button and the display stays dark. You might assume that the computer failed to start. But several different situations can look similar:

- The computer has no power because its external power cable is not connected.
- The laptop's battery is discharged and its charger is not providing sufficient power.
- The computer is running, but the monitor is switched off or has no electrical power.
- The monitor is on, but its input is set to a different connection.
- The video cable is disconnected or damaged.
- The computer is running and has entered a sleep or screen-off state.
- The computer has a hardware or software fault that cannot be resolved by checking cables.

The same visible symptom—**a black screen**—does not tell you which explanation is correct. The right first action is to gather simple observations, not to declare a diagnosis.

### Power and signal are different things

[[power-connection|Power connections]] supply energy. [[signal-connection|Signal connections]] carry information between devices. Some modern connections, especially [[usb-c|USB-C]], can carry both power and data; other connections serve a narrower purpose. Understanding the difference prevents unnecessary purchases and unsafe experimentation.

| Connection or component | Primary purpose | What a beginner should remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wall outlet and computer power cord | Supply electricity to appropriate equipment | An outlet may be off, unavailable, or unsafe; do not open it |
| Laptop power adapter and charging cable | Provide compatible power to a laptop | Physical fit alone does not prove electrical compatibility |
| Battery inside a laptop | Store energy for use away from external power | A fully depleted battery may need time on a correct charger |
| HDMI or DisplayPort cable | Carry audio/video or video signals to a display | Usually does not supply the monitor's wall power |
| USB-A cable | Carry USB data and often low-voltage power | Function depends on device, cable, and port |
| USB-C cable | May carry power, data, and sometimes video | Capabilities vary; not every USB-C port or cable is equivalent |
| Ethernet cable | Carry network communications | Usually not a computer's main power cable |
| 3.5 mm audio cable | Carry analog audio signals | Does not ordinarily power a speaker system |

An [[hdmi|HDMI]] cable connected to a monitor will not usually make the monitor turn on if the monitor's own power cable is missing. A USB-C plug may fit a laptop but still fail to charge it properly if the adapter, port, or cable cannot deliver the required power. The **function of the complete connection** matters more than the connector's appearance.

### Why this step comes after “Restart Before Panic”

Lesson 1 introduced a principle: observe first, then choose the least disruptive action likely to help. For a routine software problem, a normal restart can be appropriate. But if a laptop will not receive power, restarting software may be impossible or irrelevant. The problem may exist before any software starts. Similarly, restarting a computer is unlikely to fix a physically disconnected monitor power cable.

The two lessons work together rather than compete. **A routine app problem calls for software-oriented checks; an apparent power or connection problem calls for external hardware-oriented checks.** Neither approach justifies taking dangerous risks.

### A four-part observation

When something seems disconnected or unpowered, write down four facts:

1. **The symptom:** What exactly fails—starting the computer, charging, showing a picture, using a keyboard, printing, or reaching the internet?
1. **The device:** Is the problem happening to the computer itself, a monitor, a charger, or a separate accessory?
1. **The visible state:** Do you see a charging icon, a power light, a message such as “No signal,” or a normal desktop?
1. **The timing:** Did the problem start after a move, a cable change, a power interruption, or without an obvious event?

These facts let you choose the correct path. For example, “The computer is on and I can hear notification sounds, but the external monitor says ‘No signal’” points first toward the monitor's input and signal path—not necessarily the wall outlet or the computer's main power supply.

### Work from ordinary explanations toward specialized ones

A troubleshooting sequence should begin with checks that are easy to reverse: is the appropriate cable present, is it securely seated, is the correct charger in use, is an obvious switch off, and is the display's correct input selected? Do not jump to opening the computer case, changing firmware settings, rewiring an outlet, buying replacement hardware, or following random online reset instructions. Advanced work may be appropriate for a qualified technician, but it is outside this beginner lesson.

> **Key distinction:** A **connection check** examines how equipment is linked. It is not an electrical repair, an internal hardware repair, or permission to modify equipment you do not own or manage.

## 2. Safety first before you touch anything

### An inspection is not a repair

Electrical safety takes priority over restoring computer use. Ordinary computer cables are designed for routine external handling, but power equipment can still cause injury or fire when damaged, overheated, wet, or misused. Begin with a visual inspection. Do not touch exposed wiring, metal contacts inside an outlet, damaged insulation, or wet electrical equipment. Keep children and pets away from questionable connections. If you are unsure whether equipment is safe, leave it alone and ask for qualified assistance.

You do not have to prove the cause of a dangerous problem. Seeing one serious warning sign is enough to stop.

### Stop signs: do not continue an ordinary connection check

| What you notice | Why it matters | Safe next decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Smoke, sparks, arcing, or a burning smell | Possible fire or electrical failure | Stop using the equipment; follow emergency procedures and seek immediate help |
| A frayed cord, exposed conductor, melted plug, or scorched outlet | Possible shock or fire hazard | Do not touch or use the affected equipment; have it assessed or replaced appropriately |
| Liquid on equipment, a plug, or an outlet | Water and electricity can create a dangerous condition | Do not plug in, unplug, or handle wet electrical equipment; get qualified help |
| A swollen laptop battery or a bulging case | Possible battery failure and fire risk | Stop charging and using the device; seek manufacturer or professional guidance |
| Painful shock, tingling from exposed electrical equipment, or unexpected heat | Possible unsafe electrical condition | Stop and seek professional help; do not repeat the experiment |
| Repeatedly tripping breaker, power strip, or protection device | A fault or excessive load may be present | Do not repeatedly reset it; ask a qualified person to investigate |
| A plug that requires unusual force, bends, or will not align | Risk of damaging the connector or creating a poor connection | Stop rather than forcing it into place |

If smoke, active fire, or another immediate emergency is present, prioritize personal safety and follow local emergency procedures. Do not put yourself at risk to preserve a computer, collect evidence, or retrieve data.

### What a safe beginner may ordinarily do

For equipment that appears dry, intact, cool enough for normal handling, and under your control, the following checks are generally low risk:

- Observe power lights, charging icons, and on-screen messages without changing anything.
- Confirm that a clearly identified, undamaged power cord is seated in its intended external socket.
- Check whether an accessible power strip switch is in the expected position, without touching damaged or overloaded equipment.
- Check that the computer or display power button has been used normally.
- Identify an obvious cable mismatch, such as a display cable connected to a port that does not support the desired function.
- Select the correct input using a monitor's normal on-screen menu.
- With appropriate authorization, reconnect a safe, removable external cable after shutting down devices when the manufacturer's instructions call for it.
- Stop after a small number of low-risk checks and report what you observed.

Any check that requires opening an electrical panel, disassembling an adapter, removing an internal battery, opening a computer power supply, modifying a cord, or bypassing a safety device is **outside this lesson**.

### The correct way to handle ordinary external plugs

If you need to disconnect a visibly safe, dry, undamaged external plug, first follow the device's normal shutdown or disconnection instructions as applicable. Use the plug's insulated body, not the cord itself. Pull straight without twisting or excessive force. Keep hands dry. If a plug is stuck, hot, loose in the outlet, or physically damaged, do not try to free it through force.

Never remove the protective grounding prong from a power plug, use an inappropriate adapter to defeat grounding, or insert objects into an electrical outlet. Never use a cable simply because its connector can be made to fit.

### Who has authority to change a connection?

A work or school computer is often a [[managed-device|managed device]]. Even harmless-looking cables may connect a shared workstation, network device, storage unit, accessibility tool, medical system, or business-critical service. If the device is not yours to manage, follow the organization's procedures before disconnecting it. Do not unplug a server, shared network switch, backup device, or another person's computer as an experiment.

A support request can be simple: “The display is blank. I checked the front-panel power light and the visible cable ends. I did not unplug anything because the system is managed.” Such a description preserves both safety and useful evidence.

### Handling a suspected battery problem

A rechargeable [[lithium-ion-battery|lithium-ion battery]] can become unsafe if damaged. A battery that is swollen, leaking, smoking, abnormally hot, or distorting a device's enclosure must not be treated as an ordinary charging problem. Stop using and charging the device; follow the manufacturer’s safety and disposal guidance and seek qualified help. Do not puncture, bend, squeeze, open, or try to flatten a swollen battery. Battery replacement may require specialized procedures even when a product appears easy to open.

### Safety checklist before touching a cable

Ask these questions before moving anything:

1. Is the equipment dry and free of visible damage?
1. Are there no strange odors, smoke, sparks, or abnormal heat?
1. Do I know what the cable connects and whether I am authorized to handle it?
1. Is the action a normal external connection step supported for this device?
1. Can I stop and ask for help if the connection does not move or the result is unclear?

If any answer is **no** or **uncertain**, do not attempt the physical step. Complete the observation-only version of the exercise instead.

## 3. Follow the power from the source to the computer

### Think of the power path as a chain

For a desktop computer, an ordinary power path may run from a building's electrical supply through a [[wall-outlet|wall outlet]], possibly through an approved [[surge-protector|surge protector]] or [[ups|uninterruptible power supply]], through a power cord, and into the desktop's built-in power equipment. For a laptop, the chain may continue through an external adapter and a charging cable before reaching the computer. Each link must be suitable for the device.

This is a useful model, not permission to open the wall outlet, cord, power strip, charger, or computer case. You are checking whether visible, normal external connections are present and appropriate.

| Stage | Beginner observation | What the observation does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outlet | Is a known safe outlet available and apparently on? | It does not prove the outlet's internal wiring is sound |
| Switch or power strip | Is a normal switch visibly on? | A lit switch does not guarantee all connected equipment works |
| Power cord | Is the undamaged cord in its intended sockets? | A correctly seated cord can still be internally faulty |
| External laptop charger | Is the correct adapter connected without visible damage? | A light or label alone does not prove it is delivering proper power |
| Computer | Does it show expected lights, fan activity, or startup behavior? | An indicator does not prove the operating system has loaded |
| Monitor | Is its own power status visible? | A lit monitor does not prove a video signal is arriving |

### Step 1: Identify the actual equipment that is failing

Before checking an outlet, make sure you know which equipment needs power. A **desktop tower** and a **separate monitor** commonly have independent electrical connections. An [[all-in-one-computer|all-in-one computer]] combines display and computer electronics into one main enclosure, though it may still use an external power adapter. A laptop usually has an internal battery as well as a charger. A printer may have its own power cord while communicating with the computer over USB or a network.

Do not assume that one working component proves that all connected components have power. A lit keyboard does not necessarily mean a separate monitor is on. A monitor displaying “No signal” has enough power to display that message, but it may not be receiving usable video from the computer.

### Step 2: Check the source without experimenting with electricity

A wall outlet may be controlled by an ordinary wall switch in some spaces. A [[power-strip|power strip]] may also have an on/off switch. Begin by observing the expected position of accessible controls. If a dry, undamaged, correctly installed power strip has an ordinary switch, you may check that it is on if you are authorized to do so. A power strip is not a substitute for safe building wiring.

Sometimes people suggest plugging a lamp into an outlet to see whether it works. That is appropriate **only when the outlet, lamp, and surrounding area are clearly dry, undamaged, safe, accessible, and under your control**. This is an optional check, not a required student activity; never test an outlet that smells hot, sparks, is wet, looks damaged, or has repeatedly tripped a breaker. If the outlet seems questionable, stop and report it. A working lamp shows only that the outlet powered the lamp; it is not a certified electrical safety test.

### Step 3: Check the correct cord and visible connectors

Look at both ends of the **intended** cord, if both are safely accessible. A desktop power cord can loosen when a desk is moved. A detachable charger cable may be loose at the adapter as well as at the computer. A monitor can have a separate cord that is easy to confuse with the desktop's cord. An external device may need its own adapter even if its USB cable is connected to the computer.

If you observe a loose connection and it is safe and permitted to handle, use the appropriate plug body to seat it gently in the correct socket. Do not reach behind a heavy cabinet, pull a computer by its cables, or put your hand near an exposed electrical hazard just to reach the plug. If the connector resists normal insertion, stop and verify its type.

### Step 4: Look for ordinary indicators

A [[power-indicator|power indicator]] is a light or icon that may show whether equipment is on, sleeping, charging, or in an error state. Indicators vary widely; a white, green, or amber light on one device may mean something different on another. The manual or manufacturer guidance explains the meaning for that model. The absence of a light also does not always prove there is no power: some devices have no lights, and others hide them.

Look for the combination of signs rather than interpreting a single light in isolation:

- Does the computer produce a normal startup sound, fan movement, or other expected behavior?
- Is an external monitor's power indicator on?
- Does the monitor display an input menu or a “No signal” message?
- Does a laptop show a battery or charging status on screen?
- Did a previously visible warning change after a safe, documented connection check?

Record facts without converting them into assumptions. “A white light is on” is an observation. “The motherboard is fine” is a diagnosis that does not follow from that observation alone.

### Step 5: Keep power strips, surge protectors, and UPS devices in perspective

A surge protector may protect connected equipment against some electrical surges, subject to its design and condition. A basic power strip primarily provides additional receptacles; not every strip is a surge protector. An [[ups|UPS]] can provide temporary battery-backed power during some interruptions, subject to its rating and condition. These are distinct products with different functions.

Do not overload outlets or strips. Do not daisy-chain power strips or extension cords. Do not bypass a tripped protection device or repeatedly reset it. Consult workplace procedures or a qualified person regarding safe load limits, cable routing, and the suitability of equipment. When an interruption affects many devices, the likely scope may extend beyond the computer; focus on observation rather than improvising electrical repairs.

### Step 6: Observe and verify, instead of repeating the same attempt

If an undamaged, appropriate cable was visibly loose and you safely reseated it, test exactly the function that failed. Is the computer now able to start normally? Is the monitor displaying an image? Does the laptop's charging status change? A positive outcome suggests the connection mattered; it does not prove other issues are absent.

If the symptom remains, do not repeat the same action indefinitely. Record what you checked and move to the next relevant, low-risk observation. If safe external checks are exhausted, seek assistance rather than opening hardware.

## 4. How to check laptop power and charging

### A laptop has two possible energy sources

Many laptops can run from their internal rechargeable battery, an external power supply, or both. A laptop may start with battery energy even when its charger is disconnected. Conversely, a deeply depleted battery may need time connected to a compatible adapter before the computer responds normally. The system can also reduce charging speed or pause charging to manage heat or battery longevity.

A battery icon may show **plugged in**, **charging**, **not charging**, **charging paused**, **battery full**, or another state. These are not all equivalent. A full battery that temporarily stops charging may be behaving normally. A laptop that says it is plugged in while its battery level continues to fall under heavy use may be receiving insufficient power, may be experiencing a battery or power fault, or may be operating under device-specific conditions.

### Use a charger that is intended for the computer

A [[charger|charger]] is not just any plug that fits. The adapter, cable, and charging port must support the correct power characteristics. The device manufacturer may specify an adapter with a particular power rating and connector type. Follow those requirements and use a known compatible charger from a trustworthy source. Avoid improvised adapters, modified connectors, or suspicious aftermarket equipment.

With [[usb-power-delivery|USB Power Delivery]], supported devices can negotiate appropriate power over USB-C. However, **not every USB-C charger, cable, or port can support the requirements of a specific laptop**. A phone charger may fit the port but charge too slowly, not charge while the laptop is running, or not work at all. Similarly, a cable that carries USB data may not support the laptop's necessary power level or video function. Microsoft specifically notes that unsuitable chargers or cables can cause a PC to charge slowly or discharge while connected.

| Laptop situation | What to check first | What to avoid assuming |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No sign of charging | Correct adapter, safe power source, intact cable, supported charging port | The battery must be defective |
| Plugged in but slowly losing charge | Adapter and cable power capability, workload, manufacturer guidance | Any USB-C charger is sufficient |
| Charging status says full or paused | Current battery level and device charging-management settings | Charging must continue nonstop at 100 percent |
| Computer starts only while connected | Battery condition and device-specific guidance | The external adapter itself must be broken |
| Battery is fully depleted | Allow time on the correct charger as directed | It should always start instantly |
| Charging works only when cable is bent | Possible cable or port damage | Bending or taping the cable is a safe repair |
| Visible swelling or serious overheating | Immediate safety hazard | Continuing to charge is an acceptable test |

### An external checklist for Windows laptops

1. Read the computer's make and model, or identify it from a label or reliable documentation without disassembling the device.
1. Visually inspect the adapter and cable for tears, swelling, heat damage, loose connectors, or other warning signs. **Stop** if anything appears unsafe.
1. Check that the manufacturer's intended charger is connected to a known appropriate, safe outlet and correct charging port.
1. If Windows is already running, look at the battery icon in the [[taskbar|taskbar]] and note the actual status wording and battery percentage.
1. If the laptop has no visible response but is safely connected to its correct charger, allow a manufacturer-appropriate charging interval before judging the result.
1. Try an ordinary power-on action according to that computer's instructions. Do not perform forced shutdowns or hardware resets as part of an initial cable check.
1. Record whether the charge status, percentage, or startup behavior changes. If not, consult the manufacturer or authorized support.

Windows interface details can vary by device and software version. The battery icon is evidence about what Windows reports, not a guarantee that the charger, battery, or circuit is healthy.

### An external checklist for Mac laptops

The same general sequence applies to a MacBook: check the intended adapter, confirm the external cable connection, and observe the battery menu or charging indicator if the computer runs. Some models use [[magsafe|MagSafe]] and others can charge through supported USB-C connections. Light colors and patterns may have model-specific meanings; consult Apple's documentation rather than assigning them a universal interpretation.

If a Mac does not turn on or charge, Apple recommends appropriate external checks and trying a compatible power adapter or cable where available. Do not attempt battery disassembly, do not use improvised adapters, and do not assume that a brief pause in charging confirms a defect. Apple also describes conditions in which power equipment may stop charging because of heat or related protection behavior. If a charger repeatedly fails in an otherwise appropriate setup, have it professionally assessed rather than forcing it to operate.

### An external checklist for Chromebooks

Google's Chromebook guidance starts with checking that the adapter cables are fully connected at the Chromebook and wall outlet and that the outlet is working. If a safe, appropriate connection is present but the battery does not charge, Google's published procedure includes unplugging the charger from the Chromebook and wall, reconnecting it in the described order, and allowing **at least 30 minutes** of charging before evaluating the result. These steps are for equipment that is otherwise safe and intact. They are not instructions to handle damaged or wet plugs. School-managed Chromebooks may require an administrator's permission.

If the Chromebook still does not charge, Google describes additional device-specific troubleshooting and diagnostics. Those are **next-level procedures**, not prerequisites to this beginner course. Gather observations and consult the official instructions or support rather than experimenting with a hardware reset.

### Why a charging light may mislead you

A charging indicator shows what the device reports or detects. It is not a laboratory measurement of the battery's condition. Some indicators change color near a full charge; some flash for a fault; some turn off when charging is paused or completed. The correct interpretation depends on the model.

A more useful observation combines the charging status, battery level over time, whether the correct adapter is present, and whether normal operation is possible. Avoid repeated cable movements as a “test” of an intermittent port. Wiggling a loose or damaged connector can worsen a fault.

### Charging while traveling or using a docking station

A [[dock|dock]] or [[usb-c-hub|USB-C hub]] may deliver power through a supported connection, but some docks have their own power requirements and may not supply enough for every laptop. A monitor with USB-C may charge some laptops but not others. When charging is unreliable, compare the setup with the manufacturer's supported direct-charge arrangement, **only when you have permission and the equipment is safe**. If a shared workstation depends on the dock, report the issue before unplugging it.

Different regions use different plug forms and electrical systems. Travelers should use properly rated equipment and manufacturer-approved arrangements. A physical travel plug adapter does not necessarily convert voltage or provide the correct power output. Never improvise electrical conversions.

## 5. Check displays and other device connections

### A dark screen is not necessarily an unpowered computer

An external display usually needs two separate things: electrical power and a valid [[video-signal|video signal]] from the computer. The computer can be running with the monitor off, and the monitor can be running without receiving a picture. These conditions require different checks.

Begin by identifying **which screen** is affected. Does a laptop's built-in display work while a separate monitor remains dark? Is the external screen displaying “No signal” or an input name? Are multiple screens attached? Is the problem limited to a projector or a television used as a monitor? These differences narrow the next step.

| Visible symptom | Plausible explanation | Low-risk next observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monitor power light is off | Monitor may lack power or may be off | Check its own normal power control and visible power cable |
| Monitor says “No signal” | Display is on but lacks a usable signal | Check selected input and external video connections |
| Laptop screen works but external screen does not | The external display path may be involved | Check monitor input, cable type, and display controls |
| Computer and monitor both seem off | More than one part of the power path may be unavailable | Observe safe outlet and equipment controls; stop if unsafe |
| Monitor shows desktop but printer is missing | Display connection works; another peripheral path may be affected | Inspect the printer's independent power and connection status |
| Picture appears intermittently when cable moves | Cable, port, or connection may be damaged | Stop moving the cable and arrange a safe inspection |

### Check the monitor's own power first

A separate monitor has a power button or control that may be hidden on an edge or underside. It may also use an external power brick. If the monitor is off, inspect the normal power connection without changing the computer's operating system. Do not confuse a monitor's standby state with total loss of power.

Next check the [[input-source|input source]] selected on the monitor. Many monitors have HDMI 1, HDMI 2, DisplayPort, or USB-C inputs. The on-screen menu usually shows a corresponding name. If a computer is connected to HDMI 2 but the monitor is set to HDMI 1, the screen may display “No signal.” Selecting the actual connected input is a reversible check and generally safer than disconnecting multiple cables.

### Recognize common video connections

[[hdmi|HDMI]] and [[displayport|DisplayPort]] are widely used for external displays. Some computers and displays can carry video through a supported USB-C connection or an adapter. **Not all USB-C ports support video output**, and adapter compatibility varies. A working charging connection does not establish that the same port or cable supports a monitor.

A [[video-adapter|video adapter]] converts or adapts between supported connection types; its limitations matter. Do not assume that every cable can carry a signal in both directions or that an unpowered passive adapter will always work. Check device requirements before purchasing or replacing equipment.

### Check peripherals without unplugging everything

A printer, keyboard, mouse, webcam, external storage device, or speaker may have a separate power switch, removable battery, USB connection, network connection, or internal charging system. Ask whether the accessory itself appears on or responsive. If an accessory uses a cable, identify **which end connects to which device** and what the cable is intended to carry.

For wired USB devices, a loose plug can interrupt communication. For a wireless keyboard or mouse, the immediate issue may instead be a depleted battery, a missing receiver, or lost wireless pairing—not a cable. A wired printer may have its own power cord plus a USB or Ethernet connection. A printer that is physically powered on but not printing has different possible causes from one with no signs of power.

### Special caution: external drives and active data transfers

Do not disconnect a [[storage-device|storage device]] simply to test a cable while it may be writing files. Operating systems often provide a way to [[eject|eject]] or safely remove removable storage. Unplugging a drive during a copy, backup, synchronization, or software update risks data loss or corruption. On a shared or managed system, obtain authorization before changing a storage connection.

Similarly, do not disconnect a network cable from a server, shared device, or network switch without permission. An [[ethernet|Ethernet]] cable primarily connects network devices and can also support specialized Power over Ethernet equipment, but it is not a substitute for an ordinary computer's designated mains power connection. Specialized systems may carry power and data over the same cable. Beginners should **identify and report** these arrangements, not reconfigure them.

### Cable labels, ports, and matching

Connector shapes are clues, not complete specifications. USB-A typically has a broad rectangular shape, USB-C has a smaller reversible oval shape, HDMI is shaped differently from USB, and DisplayPort has its own design. Cable and port features may vary by model. Look for printed symbols, device labels, manufacturer instructions, or accessible documentation.

| Cable or connection | Common role | A useful check | Important caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Desktop AC cord | Computer or monitor wall power | Correct external socket and visibly safe seating | Never open a power supply or defeat grounding |
| USB-C | Charging, data, possibly video | Supported device, adapter, cable, and port capabilities | Similar plugs do not guarantee equal capabilities |
| USB-A | Accessory data and some power | Intended USB port and accessory state | Not a universal laptop power connector |
| HDMI | External video and often audio | Monitor input selected to the connected HDMI port | Usually does not supply monitor mains power |
| DisplayPort | External digital display signal | Intended ports and correctly selected input | Do not force incompatible connectors |
| Ethernet | Network connection | Is the device's network link intended and permitted? | Do not disturb shared network equipment |
| Audio cable | Sound input or output | Correct audio jack, mute and device selection | Some audio devices need independent power |

### When to test a different cable

A different cable can be a useful comparison **only when the replacement is known compatible, safe, undamaged, and approved for the equipment**. A manufacturer-specified charger is a better reference than an arbitrary cable. Do not borrow an unfamiliar adapter because the plug fits. Do not swap high-voltage cords or internal components. If a suspect cable is damaged, do not test it again; remove it from service through the appropriate process.

In a beginner classroom, an observation-only comparison is often enough: “This port is marked HDMI and that cable is USB-C, so I need the correct supported connection before proceeding.” No one needs to create a live equipment fault for practice.

## 6. Use a simple decision process

### The sequence: observe, classify, check, verify, escalate

A good troubleshooter does not perform every possible check in random order. First classify the symptom. Then choose only the checks relevant to that class. A practical five-stage method is:

1. **Observe:** Write down exactly what fails, what works, and any visible messages or indicators.
1. **Classify:** Is the issue power, charging, video, a connected accessory, or uncertain?
1. **Check:** Inspect one safe, external, relevant part of the connection path.
1. **Verify:** Repeat the original action and compare the result with your starting observation.
1. **Escalate:** Stop when a hazard appears, authorization is absent, or a small number of basic checks fail.

You can remember the pattern as **O-C-C-V-E**. These letters do not replace thinking; they prevent the common mistake of changing three connections and then not knowing which one mattered.

### Scenario A: A desktop does not seem to turn on

Assume the computer is yours to inspect, it is dry and undamaged, and no hazard is present. Record whether any indicator comes on when you use the normal power button. Identify the desktop power cord, its safe visible connection to the computer, and the visible status of any approved power strip. Check only what is safe and accessible. If the equipment is behind heavy furniture, do not move a powered workstation or pull cords to reach it.

If you discover an obviously disconnected, intact cord and can reconnect it in accordance with the equipment's instructions, test again. If there is still no response, do not open the case or power supply. A fault may be inside the computer, in the building's electrical system, or elsewhere. Ask for appropriate assistance.

### Scenario B: A laptop reports “plugged in, not charging”

Start with the battery percentage and the exact wording. If the battery is already nearly full, charging may be paused by normal battery management. If it is low, verify that the adapter and charging cable are appropriate for this computer and are connected to the correct port. Watch for damage, overheating, or a swollen case. Do not keep unplugging and replugging the cord during an active task, and do not bend it to force intermittent charging.

If the status persists and safe checks do not explain it, consult the device maker's instructions. Do not buy a replacement battery based only on one status message.

### Scenario C: The computer runs but the monitor reads “No signal”

The message itself is evidence that the monitor is operating enough to show text. Check the selected input and whether the video cable is connected to the intended ports. If a laptop's own display works, use that as an additional observation. Do not treat this as a guaranteed “computer has no power” condition.

If everything appears appropriate but no image appears, you can report the exact cable type, input selection, whether another display works, and whether the problem began after moving equipment. Additional display configuration or hardware work belongs to the appropriate next troubleshooting stage.

### Scenario D: A printer is on but disconnected

A powered-on printer still needs a usable communication route. Identify whether it is normally attached by USB, Ethernet, or a wireless network. Do not move or unplug cables randomly; check the device's visible connection status and note any message. The next course lessons examine printers and connectivity in greater detail.

### Scenario E: A charger or outlet seems physically damaged

Stop immediately. This is **not** the moment to test a second device, push a loose plug farther in, jiggle the cord, or reset a breaker repeatedly. Record the location and visible condition from a safe distance, keep others from using the equipment if you can do so without risk, and notify the appropriate support or emergency contact. Safety is the successful outcome of this scenario, not restoring power.

### Compare possible actions

| Situation | Best initial approach | Not recommended as a beginner's first move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Desktop appears unpowered | Inspect visible, safe, intended power path | Open computer case or power supply |
| Laptop charges slowly | Check manufacturer-approved adapter and cable capabilities | Assume any USB-C accessory is equivalent |
| Monitor says “No signal” | Check monitor input and video path | Replace the computer without testing the display path |
| External drive is busy copying | Preserve the transfer and request help if needed | Pull out USB cable to see what happens |
| Power strip repeatedly trips | Stop and report possible electrical problem | Repeatedly reset protection or overload another strip |
| School computer connection is uncertain | Observe and ask the administrator | Disconnect multiple managed devices |
| Equipment has a burning odor | Stop using it and follow safety procedures | Continue ordinary troubleshooting |

### Measure success by evidence

A useful conclusion states the original problem, the single action, and the observed result. For example: “The monitor displayed ‘No signal.’ Its input was set to HDMI 1, while the visible cable was connected to HDMI 2. I selected HDMI 2 and the picture appeared.” That is more informative than “I fixed the computer.”

If a problem remains, that is not a failed learning experience. “I observed that the charger is the correct model, no damage is visible, the laptop reports no charging, and I did not attempt repairs” is valuable information for technical support.

## 7. Guided practice: build a safe connection check

These exercises are designed to teach **decisions and observation**, not to manufacture a fault. **Do not unplug a working device, loosen a cable, drain a battery, trip a circuit, or handle damaged equipment for practice.** If you cannot safely access equipment, complete the written version. If you are in a classroom, the instructor can provide printed diagrams or photographs of normal setups.

### Activity 1: Map a power path without changing it

**Goal:** Distinguish the components that provide power to a computer and to a separate screen.

**Equipment:** Your own safe, working setup or a drawing of a desktop computer, an external monitor, two power cords, and a power strip.

**Steps:**

1. Observe where the computer receives power. Do not reach behind furniture or pull cables.
1. Observe whether the external monitor has its own power cord, an external adapter, or a clearly documented shared-power arrangement.
1. Write down the visible path using plain language, such as “wall outlet → approved surge protector → desktop power cord → desktop computer.”
1. Write a separate path for the monitor, if it has its own power connection.
1. Label the video cable separately, even when it follows approximately the same physical route.
1. Identify any cable you cannot see well enough to recognize; write “unknown” rather than guessing.

**Reflection:** Why would a working desktop power supply not necessarily make a separate monitor display a picture?

:::answer Show Activity 1 guidance
A typical desktop computer and external monitor require **separate electrical power paths**, while a video connection carries a display signal between them. The computer may be on when the monitor is off. The monitor may show a menu or “No signal” while the computer is off or not sending usable video. Different models can combine functions differently, so students should follow their real equipment's documentation. Unknown cables should remain unidentified until there is safe evidence to identify them.
:::

### Activity 2: Sort power cables from communication cables

**Goal:** Identify a cable's purpose without assuming that physical fit proves its function.

**Equipment:** A diagram, package labels, manual, or a set of pictures of HDMI, Ethernet, USB-A, USB-C, and a desktop AC power cord.

Read the following descriptions and write the most appropriate cable or connection type for each one. Do not connect any cables to complete this exercise.

| Description | Your classification | What to check in real life |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carries a display signal to a monitor | __________ | Monitor and computer video ports |
| Supplies a desktop from its intended wall power source | __________ | Correct cord and undamaged power sockets |
| Connects a computer to a wired network | __________ | Correct network port and authorization |
| Can carry data, charging power, and sometimes display output | __________ | Supported USB-C device, cable, and port |
| Connects many ordinary wired keyboards and accessories | __________ | Appropriate USB connection and accessory requirements |

**Reflection:** Why is “It fits” not a sufficient test for a charger?

:::answer Show Activity 2 classifications
- **Display signal:** HDMI is one common answer; DisplayPort may also be appropriate in a different setup.
- **Desktop wall power:** The correctly specified, undamaged AC power cord.
- **Wired network:** Ethernet.
- **Power, data, and possible video:** USB-C, *when all connected components support the intended function*.
- **Common wired keyboard and accessories:** USB-A or another supported USB connection.

A connector may physically fit while its adapter, port, or cable cannot provide the required voltage, power, charging negotiation, or data function. Proper equipment compatibility matters.
:::

### Activity 3: Identify the moment to stop

**Goal:** Recognize danger signs and choose not to handle unsafe equipment.

Consider five hypothetical observations:

1. A dry, undamaged power cord is visibly disconnected from an unused monitor you own.
1. A laptop case bulges near the battery compartment.
1. A wall socket produces a burning smell.
1. A cable looks normal, but it is connected to a shared office server.
1. A monitor's on-screen menu is accessible, and the selected input differs from the cable's documented input.

For each case, choose **observe only**, **safe normal external check if permitted**, or **stop and request qualified help**. Write one sentence explaining why. Do not create these conditions to test your decision.

:::answer Show Activity 3 decisions
1. A safe normal external check may be reasonable if the monitor is yours to manage, the cable is appropriate, the equipment is undamaged, and handling follows the device instructions. Observation-only practice is also valid.
1. Stop using and charging the laptop. A bulging enclosure may indicate a battery hazard; seek professional guidance.
1. Stop and report the outlet. Do not test, touch, or reuse it.
1. Observe only and contact the responsible administrator. Do not disconnect a shared system without authorization.
1. If authorized, select the matching input with the monitor's normal menu and verify the picture. No cable disconnection is necessary.
:::

### Activity 4: Evaluate a laptop charging setup on paper

**Goal:** Recognize why a suitable charger and cable matter.

Imagine a laptop that normally uses a manufacturer-approved 65-watt USB-C power adapter. The laptop is running several applications. Someone connects an unfamiliar phone charger that has a much lower maximum output, and Windows reports that charging is slow. The power adapter and cable are visually undamaged, but their exact capabilities are unknown.

**Questions:**

1. Does the fact that the charger plug fits establish compatibility?
1. What does the slow-charging message tell you, and what does it not tell you?
1. What would be the safest starting comparison?
1. Should you buy a replacement laptop battery immediately?
1. What would make this exercise a stop-and-report situation?

:::answer Show Activity 4 reasoning
1. **No.** USB-C shape alone does not prove that the charger and cable meet the laptop's power requirements.
1. The message reports that the system is not receiving or using power as expected for normal charging. It does not isolate the cause to one part.
1. Compare the setup with the manufacturer's specified, known-compatible charger, cable, and charging port. In a managed environment, ask support to do this.
1. **No.** The message is not evidence by itself that the battery is defective.
1. Visible damage, smoke, abnormal heat, swelling, sparks, wet equipment, or other electrical warning signs require stopping immediately.
:::

### Activity 5: Diagnose a “No signal” message without disconnecting cables

**Goal:** Separate a display power problem from a display input problem.

**Scenario:** A desktop computer seems to be running. A monitor displays the message “No signal.” The monitor's on-screen menu shows the selected input as **HDMI 1**, but the visible, safely identifiable cable from the computer enters the monitor's **HDMI 2** connector.

**Steps:**

1. Identify one fact that proves the monitor is showing at least some output.
1. Identify the likely mismatch.
1. Choose the first reversible change that does not require moving any cables.
1. Explain exactly what you would check after that change.
1. If the picture still does not appear, list two observations to report instead of repeatedly changing settings.

:::answer Show Activity 5 solution
The monitor is sufficiently powered to display “No signal” and its menu. The input selection points to HDMI 1 while the cable is in HDMI 2. The first low-risk change is to select **HDMI 2** through the monitor's normal on-screen menu. Then check whether the expected computer image appears. If it does not, record that the monitor showed “No signal,” which input was selected, the visible cable connection, and whether the computer shows other signs of operating. An additional fault may exist. Do not assume that the computer itself is broken.
:::

### Activity 6: Choose the correct next action in three situations

**Goal:** Practice classification and proportional response.

For each situation, choose a safe first approach and explain your reasoning:

- **Case A:** A Chromebook that was unused for a long period does not immediately power on. Its correct, intact charger is connected to a safe outlet; no warning signs are present.
- **Case B:** A portable external drive's indicator is active while a large backup is copying. The drive's cable looks slightly loose.
- **Case C:** A power strip turns off repeatedly when several devices are connected.

Complete the comparison table below in notes or on paper.

| Case | Priority | What to avoid | When to escalate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A: Chromebook | __________ | __________ | __________ |
| B: External drive | __________ | __________ | __________ |
| C: Power strip | __________ | __________ | __________ |

:::answer Show Activity 6 response plan
- **Case A:** Confirm the manufacturer-approved charging setup without unsafe handling. Google's official Chromebook guidance recommends allowing at least 30 minutes of charging in relevant situations. Avoid assuming immediate hardware failure or performing an unauthorized reset. Escalate if the computer still does not respond after the safe, documented basic checks.
- **Case B:** Protect the active backup and avoid disconnection while data may be writing. If there is no safety hazard, let the operation finish and follow the system's normal safe-ejection or support process before any cable changes. Escalate if the connection is physically damaged or data transfer reports an error.
- **Case C:** Stop using the potentially unsafe arrangement and notify the responsible person. Do not repeatedly reset the strip, redistribute an unknown load, or daisy-chain additional strips. Escalation is appropriate immediately because a protection device repeatedly tripping requires evaluation.

### Activity 7: Produce a useful support report

**Goal:** Turn observations into a concise and actionable request for help.

**Scenario:** A laptop is connected to its normal charger. The battery percentage declines from 42 percent to 36 percent during normal use. The user has verified that the charger model appears correct and its external cord shows no visible damage. The computer has not been opened or repaired. No smoke, smell, swelling, or abnormal heat is present. The user is unsure whether the adapter supplies enough power.

Write a support note using these fields:

| Field | Information to record |
| --- | --- |
| Device | Make and model if available; never guess |
| Problem | Exact symptom and when it happens |
| Power source | Charger model and connection, if known |
| Observations | Battery percentages, icons, lights, messages |
| Safe checks | What was observed or changed, and in what order |
| Result | Whether the symptom improved, remained, or returned |
| Request | What assistance is needed |

**Reflection:** How does the note help a technician avoid repeating checks and make a better decision?

:::answer Show Activity 7 example support note
**Device:** Laptop; manufacturer and model to be confirmed.

**Problem:** Battery declines while the device is connected to the normal charger during active use.

**Observations:** Battery percentage declined from 42 percent to 36 percent. No visible external cord damage or immediate physical warning signs were observed.

**Checks:** Visually compared the connected charger with the expected equipment. Did not disconnect or open the device. The adapter's actual delivered power was not measured.

**Result:** Charging problem remains unresolved.

**Request:** Please verify the manufacturer's charging requirements, the charger's actual compatibility, and whether further authorized diagnostics are necessary.

A useful support note separates what was **observed** from what is **suspected**. It avoids inventing a diagnosis, sharing unnecessary personal data, and encouraging unsafe repair attempts.
:::

### Practice review: choose the least disruptive check

| If you see this... | First question to ask | Why this helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blank external screen | Does the monitor have power, and is the correct input selected? | Separates monitor power from video connection |
| Laptop not charging | Is the correct compatible charger being used safely? | Avoids mistaking an inadequate adapter for a defective battery |
| Printer not responding | Is the printer powered and how is it normally connected? | Separates power from data and network issues |

## 8. Common mistakes and misconceptions

### “If the screen is black, the computer must be off.”

Not necessarily. The screen may be powered off or asleep, the input may be wrong, or the video signal may be absent. A computer can continue running while its display is dark. Identify which device is showing signs of activity before reaching a conclusion.

### “If the charger plug fits, it is compatible.”

No. Physical connector fit is only one requirement. The charger, cable, device, and port must support the correct electrical characteristics. USB-C is particularly easy to misunderstand because its connector is used for multiple capabilities.

### “Every USB-C cable is the same.”

No. Different USB-C cables may support different data speeds, charging current, maximum power, and video features. The label “USB-C” describes a connector family, not one universal set of capabilities. Consult trustworthy specifications when a device requires a particular cable.

### “An HDMI cable powers the monitor.”

Usually not. An HDMI cable transports signals. A typical separate desktop monitor needs its own power supply. Exceptions or specialized arrangements should follow the device documentation, not a generic assumption.

### “A lit monitor proves the computer is working.”

No. A monitor can display its own on-screen menu or “No signal” without receiving a picture from the computer. That display tells you something about the monitor, not the overall health of the computer.

### “Plugged in but not charging always means the battery is dead.”

No. Charging may be paused because the battery is full, because of charging management, because a charger supplies insufficient power, because the device is hot, or for another reason. Use the exact status, circumstances, and manufacturer instructions before drawing a conclusion.

### “I should keep unplugging and reconnecting until it works.”

No. Repeated manipulation does not establish a diagnosis and can wear connectors. If a cable appears damaged or only works when bent, stop using it and seek replacement or inspection through proper channels.

### “I can pull an external storage cable whenever I want.”

No. A storage device may be transferring data or maintaining files in use. Follow operating-system safe-removal procedures and respect active backups and organizational rules.

### “If a power strip shuts off, just reset it again.”

No. A protective device may be responding to an overload or fault. Repeated resetting can be dangerous. Stop using the unsafe arrangement and ask an appropriate qualified person to evaluate it.

### “A surge protector guarantees that equipment can never be damaged.”

No. Protection is limited by product design, installation, rating, condition, and the type of electrical event. Some power strips have no surge protection at all. Never rely on a surge protector as permission to overlook damaged wiring or overload warnings.

### “A power light tells me exactly what broke.”

No. A light is one observation. Its meaning depends on the model, and it may indicate charging, sleep, standby, startup, or a fault. Read the documentation for that particular device.

### “If I cannot fix it myself, I have failed at troubleshooting.”

No. Safe troubleshooting includes knowing your limits. A professional support report that isolates the symptom, documents basic checks, and flags any safety concern is a successful outcome. **The goal is informed action, not unauthorized or hazardous repair.**

### Rapid misconception correction

| Claim | More accurate statement |
| --- | --- |
| “No picture means no electricity.” | Check the computer, monitor power, input, and video signal separately. |
| “All chargers with the same plug are equal.” | Compatibility includes power requirements, cable capabilities, and device support. |
| “Every USB cable does the same thing.” | USB connection abilities vary by cable, device, and port. |
| “A working light proves the machine works.” | Lights provide limited model-specific information. |
| “Repeatedly reconnecting is an effective method.” | Observe, change one safe factor, verify, and stop if the issue persists. |
| “A beginner should open the computer to inspect power.” | External observation is appropriate; internal electrical work requires qualified assistance. |
| “The goal is to force equipment back into service.” | The first priority is user safety and preventing damage or data loss. |

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The important terms below also appear in selectable vocabulary popovers within the lesson. Focus on what each term means in everyday use. You do not need to memorize technical electrical specifications to perform these basic checks safely.

- **Electrical power:** Energy supplied to operate a device. A laptop may receive it from a battery or compatible adapter.
- **Power path:** The sequence of safe, appropriate connections carrying usable electricity to equipment.
- **Power cord:** An external cable intended to deliver electrical power to equipment.
- **Wall outlet:** The receptacle in a building where properly rated electrical devices connect.
- **Power adapter:** Equipment that provides the electrical output required by a device from an appropriate source.
- **Charger:** Common name for equipment used to supply energy to or charge a rechargeable device.
- **Battery:** A component that stores energy for use without continuous external power.
- **Charging:** Supplying energy to replenish the battery's stored charge.
- **Power rating:** A specification of how much power a product can provide or consume under stated conditions.
- **Watt:** A unit used to describe the rate at which electrical energy is transferred.
- **Volt:** A unit of electrical potential difference, relevant to equipment compatibility.
- **USB Power Delivery:** A supported system for negotiating power over compatible USB connections.
- **USB-A:** A familiar rectangular USB connector used for many peripherals.
- **USB-C:** A reversible USB connector used for varied power, data, and sometimes video functions.
- **Port:** A physical connection point on a device.
- **Connector:** The part of a cable designed to mate with an appropriate port.
- **Data cable:** A cable primarily used to transmit digital information between devices.
- **Signal connection:** A connection used to transmit information, such as audio, video, or network data.
- **Video signal:** Information that a computer sends to a compatible screen.
- **HDMI:** A common digital audio-and-video connection for displays.
- **DisplayPort:** A common digital display connection.
- **Input source:** The particular display connection selected through a monitor's controls.
- **Monitor:** A display device that presents visual output.
- **Peripheral:** Equipment connected to a computer, such as a keyboard, printer, or drive.
- **Power indicator:** A device-specific light or icon that communicates a power-related status.
- **Charging indicator:** A light or icon reporting some aspect of charging or battery status.
- **Power strip:** Equipment that provides multiple sockets from one appropriate supply, subject to its rating.
- **Surge protector:** Equipment designed to reduce damage from certain electrical surges within its limits.
- **UPS:** Uninterruptible power supply; a device that can provide temporary battery-backed power to supported equipment.
- **Overload:** Demand beyond the intended capacity of electrical equipment or a circuit.
- **Circuit breaker:** A protective device intended to interrupt unsafe electrical current under particular conditions.
- **Grounding:** An electrical safety arrangement that must not be deliberately defeated.
- **Frayed cable:** A cord whose covering or internal structure appears worn or damaged.
- **Exposed conductor:** A conductive part visible where insulating material should protect it.
- **Short circuit:** An unintended low-resistance electrical connection that may cause excessive current.
- **Overheating:** Equipment temperature exceeding safe or expected operating conditions.
- **Lithium-ion battery:** A rechargeable battery technology widely used in modern portable computers.
- **Swollen battery:** A battery that expands abnormally, creating an important safety warning.
- **Managed device:** Equipment administered according to an organization's policies.
- **Authorization:** Permission to take a particular action on equipment or systems.
- **Dock:** A device that connects a laptop to multiple accessories and may provide compatible charging power.
- **USB-C hub:** An accessory that expands the number or kinds of available connections.
- **Ethernet:** A common technology for wired computer networking.
- **Power over Ethernet (PoE):** A specialized networking method that supplies power and data over compatible Ethernet connections.
- **External drive:** Storage connected to the computer from outside the main enclosure.
- **Safe eject:** A software-supported procedure for preparing certain removable storage for disconnection.
- **Taskbar:** A Windows interface area that may display the battery icon and other status information.
- **No signal:** A monitor message indicating a usable picture signal is not currently being received.
- **Troubleshooting:** Choosing targeted checks, interpreting observations, and verifying a result.
- **Diagnostic observation:** A fact recorded to help distinguish possible causes of a problem.
- **Verification:** Testing the originally failing function after a change.
- **Escalation:** Passing an unresolved or unsafe problem to an appropriate support person.
- **Support report:** A concise record of the symptom, context, checks, and result.
- **Manufacturer documentation:** Device-specific instructions provided by a product's maker.

## 11. Further learning and next steps

### Summary: the safe connection-check workflow

A computer that appears broken may have an ordinary external connection problem. The right response is not to begin opening hardware. First describe the symptom and identify whether you are dealing with **power**, **charging**, **display signal**, or a **connected peripheral**. Inspect visible, intact connections and permitted normal controls. Understand that HDMI, Ethernet, USB, AC power cords, and laptop chargers serve different functions; in particular, USB-C products may have different capabilities even when their plugs look alike.

If it is safe to proceed, change one relevant factor at a time and test the original symptom. Preserve active files and transfers. A repeated electrical trip, damaged cord, liquid exposure, battery swelling, smoke, sparks, or a burning smell ends ordinary troubleshooting. In those cases, protecting people and reporting the hazard is more important than restoring device operation.

**Five questions to remember:**

1. What specifically is not working: power, charging, picture, or a peripheral?
1. Does this equipment have a separate power and signal path?
1. Are the cable, port, and adapter designed and approved to work together?
1. Is any warning sign present that requires stopping immediately?
1. What changed after my one safe check, and what do I need to report?
