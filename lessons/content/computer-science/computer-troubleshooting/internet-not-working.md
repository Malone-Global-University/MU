Basic Computer Skills / Computer Troubleshooting Basics

## Lesson 4: Internet Not Working

A computer shows several Wi-Fi bars, but a website will not load. A phone plays downloaded music but cannot retrieve a new message. A video meeting suddenly freezes. A laptop can connect at a library but not at home. These problems often sound alike when someone says, “The internet is broken,” yet they can have very different causes. The problem might be one website, one application, one device, the wireless connection, the home router, or the internet service itself.

This lesson teaches a calm, practical method for finding **where a connection stops working** without randomly changing network settings. You will learn the difference between Wi-Fi and internet access; recognize common connection messages; test one thing at a time; understand when to restart a device or network equipment; and decide when a provider, network administrator, or technical specialist should take over. The emphasis is on safe observations, reversible actions, and protecting accounts and work.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 65–90 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–3 recommended · **Practice equipment:** A Windows 11 computer, Mac, Chromebook, or phone if available; optionally a second device and a notebook. No activity requires disconnecting a working household network, changing router settings, installing software, or exposing private information.

> **Essential idea:** A Wi-Fi connection is not the same as a working internet connection. Identify whether the issue affects one site, one device, or multiple devices. Protect your work, begin with observations, and verify each change before doing more.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain the difference between Wi-Fi, a local network, an internet connection, and an online service.
1. Classify common internet symptoms by scope: website, app, device, local network, or service provider.
1. Use low-risk comparison tests without disclosing passwords or private information.
1. Recognize basic Windows, Mac, Chromebook, iPhone, and Android connection controls.
1. Describe when restarting network equipment is appropriate and why permission matters.
1. Identify misleading or unsafe troubleshooting advice, including scams and destructive reset procedures.
1. Prepare a clear support report that records evidence, tests, results, and the next authorized step.

## 1. What does “internet not working” mean?

### Start with the symptom, not an assumed cause

A [[symptom|symptom]] is something you observe: “My browser says it cannot reach this page.” A [[cause|cause]] is the reason behind it: perhaps a temporary website outage, a disconnected Wi-Fi link, an incorrectly entered password, or a failed internet service. The same symptom can have several causes. The fact that a page failed to load does not, by itself, prove that the computer, router, or internet provider is at fault.

Many people use the word “internet” to refer to everything online. During [[troubleshooting|troubleshooting]], it helps to be more precise. A [[browser|web browser]] opens websites; an [[application|application]] may connect to an online service; a [[local-network|local network]] links nearby devices; and an [[internet-service|internet service]] connects that local network to other networks. Problems at one layer can look like problems at another.

A good first question is: **What is the smallest set of things that are not working?** If only one online store fails while other sites load, concentrate on the store or browser before restarting household equipment. If every device in a home loses online access, investigate the shared network and service. If a phone works on cellular data but not the household Wi-Fi, the comparison narrows the investigation but does not alone prove which piece failed.

### Five common scopes of an internet problem

| What you observe | Possible scope | First low-risk comparison | What it does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One website will not load; others do | Website, browser, or site-specific route | Open a different familiar, trusted website | The entire internet is down |
| One app cannot sign in; browser works | App, account, or online service | Check another legitimate function in the app | The Wi-Fi router is defective |
| One laptop is offline; another device works on the same Wi-Fi | Device or its connection settings | View the laptop's Wi-Fi status | The laptop needs a factory reset |
| Several devices connect to Wi-Fi but have no internet | Shared router, modem, provider, or upstream service | Check a known service notice and equipment status | The provider definitely caused it |
| Device cannot see the expected Wi-Fi name | Wireless setting, coverage, router broadcast, or hardware | Confirm Wi-Fi is enabled and the device is in range | A missing network name always means an outage |
| Wi-Fi works in one room but drops in another | Wireless range, interference, or obstructions | Observe connection strength in both locations | Higher internet speed automatically fixes coverage |
| Online video buffers but a local document opens normally | Network quality, service load, or app workload | Compare another trusted online service | The computer's storage is necessarily full |
| A public network joins but opens a sign-in page | Captive portal or access requirement | Confirm the network name with the venue | Every portal asking for information is trustworthy |

The table is a set of **possibilities**, not automatic diagnoses. Use the observations to select the next safe test. A service can also have a partial outage: messages may work while photos fail, or a video platform may load its main page while calls do not connect.

### “Connected” does not always mean “online”

A device may show “Connected” because it has successfully joined a [[wifi|Wi-Fi]] network. It may still be unable to reach the broader [[internet|internet]]. Windows and other operating systems sometimes display **“No internet,” “Limited connectivity,”** or an exclamation symbol. Those messages are useful clues, but they depend on how the device checks connectivity and are not perfect proofs of the exact cause.

Another possibility is that the device is online but one page or app cannot reach the server it needs. A browser may show a message about a timeout, an unrecognized address, a blocked request, or a certificate problem. The wording matters. Rather than immediately clearing settings or bypassing an alert, copy the non-sensitive words into a note and determine whether the error is limited to one service.

### Recognize intermittent problems

A [[intermittent-connection|intermittent connection]] works sometimes and fails at other times. It may drop only during video calls, after the computer wakes, at a certain distance from the router, or during busy hours. These patterns are valuable evidence. Record when the failure happens, which devices are affected, whether the Wi-Fi symbol changes, and whether the problem resolves on its own.

For example, “Internet fails every evening” is not enough to identify the cause. “On three evenings, two devices kept their Wi-Fi connection but streaming repeatedly buffered between 7 and 9 p.m.” is a more useful observation. It still does not prove that congestion is the cause, but it gives a service provider or administrator something concrete to investigate.

### Worked example: one website versus the whole network

Maria opens a learning portal and sees a connection error. Her browser can still open a familiar public library site. Another person at home can use a different website normally. The evidence suggests that the problem may be limited to the portal, the browser session, or that service's route. It would be premature to unplug the household router. Maria notes the exact portal message, checks the service's official status information if available, and waits or contacts the portal's support channel. If other sites also stop loading, she reevaluates the scope.

> **Remember:** A working second website is evidence that *some* internet connectivity exists; it is not a guarantee that every site or application must work.

## 2. How a device reaches the internet

### Follow the path from the screen outward

A typical home connection can be described as **device → Wi-Fi or Ethernet → home router → modem or fiber terminal → internet service provider → destination service**. Some households use a single combination unit that performs several jobs. Some apartments, campuses, and workplaces have equipment owned or managed by someone else. Cellular phones may use a mobile carrier network rather than a home router.

Each part has a different role:

- A [[device|device]] is a computer, phone, tablet, or other connected product.
- A [[network-adapter|network adapter]] provides a wired or wireless interface for the device.
- A [[wireless-network|wireless network]] uses radio signals to connect devices within range.
- An [[ethernet|Ethernet]] connection uses a compatible physical cable and network ports.
- A [[router|router]] directs traffic between local devices and other networks; many home routers also provide Wi-Fi.
- A [[modem|modem]] or [[ont|optical network terminal]] connects certain home networks to a cable, DSL, or fiber service.
- An [[isp|internet service provider (ISP)]] supplies the connection beyond the home or local network.
- A [[website|website]] or online service depends on remote systems that may have their own technical problems.

This description is a mental model. Actual networks vary. A phone hotspot, satellite connection, managed mesh network, or building-wide network may not use every component shown here.

### Wi-Fi, internet, and cellular data are different

**Wi-Fi** is a way to connect a nearby device to a network using radio. **Internet access** is the ability to reach services beyond the local network. **Cellular data** is an alternative internet connection supplied by a mobile carrier, usually through a data plan. A phone can be connected to Wi-Fi without internet access. A phone can also reach the internet using cellular data while Wi-Fi is off. Some phones switch connections automatically, so be careful when interpreting a successful test.

Suppose a phone opens a webpage while the household laptop cannot. If the phone is using cellular data, it does not tell you whether the household Wi-Fi is healthy. Before comparing devices, confirm **which connection each is actually using**. Do not turn off an emergency or work-critical connection merely to run a practice exercise.

### Network name and password

A [[ssid|network name (SSID)]] is the name displayed when choosing a Wi-Fi network. It identifies the wireless network as advertised; it does **not** by itself prove that the network is owned by a trusted person. Networks can have similar or misleading names. A [[wifi-password|Wi-Fi password]] helps authorized users join a protected network. It is different from your computer login, email password, online account password, and router administrator password.

If a device sees several nearby networks, select only one you recognize and have permission to use. On a managed device, the correct network may be configured automatically by an organization. If a password is rejected, verify the network name, keyboard entry, and legitimate credential source. Do not try many guesses or ask other people to disclose their credentials.

### Signal strength is not internet speed

Wi-Fi bars indicate a rough measure of wireless signal quality or strength. More bars usually mean a stronger local radio connection, but they do not guarantee fast internet, a healthy ISP connection, or a responsive website. A device may have excellent wireless signal to a router that has lost its upstream service.

Conversely, weak signals, distance, thick walls, crowded frequencies, and competing devices can contribute to unstable connections even when the internet service is otherwise functioning. A useful low-risk experiment is to compare normal operation at two permitted locations without changing any settings or relocating electrical equipment.

### Four terms you may encounter

[[ip-address|IP address]] refers to a network address used to deliver data. [[dns|Domain Name System (DNS)]] helps translate readable website names into network addresses. [[latency|Latency]] refers to how long communication takes; [[bandwidth|bandwidth]] describes the amount of data a connection can carry over time. Beginners usually do not need to edit IP or DNS settings. These terms simply explain why an internet problem can involve more than a wireless signal.

| Term | What it helps describe | Beginner-friendly clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wi-Fi signal | Radio link between device and nearby wireless network | Signal bars and connection status |
| Internet access | Ability to reach external networks | More than one trusted site or service works |
| DNS | Finding the address for a site name | A site name may fail even when some connectivity exists |
| Latency | Delay between request and response | Calls may lag even if a page eventually loads |
| Bandwidth | Potential data transfer capacity | Large downloads may compete with video streams |
| Packet loss | Data failing to arrive successfully | Calls may break up or connections may stutter |
| Captive portal | A network sign-in or terms page | Public Wi-Fi may request venue-approved authorization |

Do not conclude that a DNS problem exists simply because a browser says “server not found.” The same kind of message can appear for misspellings, temporary service outages, blocked destinations, or other causes. Network technicians use more specialized tests when these possibilities need to be separated.

## 3. Start with safe observations

### The five-minute observation-first routine

When connectivity fails, begin with the least disruptive questions:

1. **Read the message.** Is the computer saying “No internet,” is the website timing out, or does one app say its own service is unavailable?
1. **Check the device's connection status.** Is Wi-Fi enabled? Is the correct network selected? Is airplane mode affecting the connection?
1. **Identify the scope.** Does another familiar site work? Does another authorized device on the *same network* work?
1. **Look for shared evidence.** Are other users reporting problems, or is there a verified provider notice? Are any network-equipment indicators visibly different from normal?
1. **Record the result.** State which tests were attempted and what changed. Do not repeat steps endlessly.

If a step requires changing settings, disconnecting devices, or restarting shared equipment, first consider the impact on other users, live calls, business operations, medical equipment, security systems, or your organization's network policy.

### Test one trusted website, then another

A reasonable test is to open one familiar, reputable site that you already use, then a second unrelated trusted site. Do not use a random pop-up, an unfamiliar “connection test” site, or a download offered by a search ad. If one site fails and the other works, note the difference. If neither works, you need more evidence before blaming the router.

A browser's cached copy of a page may look normal even if the network is unavailable. Some applications work [[offline|offline]] with previously downloaded content. If your browser reports a live connection error, capture the wording; if it shows an old page without new content, that is not conclusive proof of current access. An approved device status check can strengthen the evidence.

### Compare another device correctly

A second device can help identify whether the failure is shared. For an accurate comparison, confirm both devices are using the same local network, not one on cellular and another on Wi-Fi. If the second device can reach several independent online services, while the first cannot, focus further investigation on the first device's settings or applications. If both fail, the shared network or internet service becomes more plausible, but another common condition is still possible.

On a work or school network, do not connect unauthorized personal devices to run tests. Instead ask the network administrator whether an outage is known.

### Check Wi-Fi and airplane mode without guessing

[[airplane-mode|Airplane mode]] generally changes wireless connectivity, but the exact behavior depends on device and settings. Wi-Fi may be independently enabled in some airplane-mode configurations. Look at the actual Wi-Fi state instead of assuming that one icon explains everything.

If Wi-Fi appears off and you have permission to use the network, turn it on through your system's usual control and select the recognized network. If you do not know the right network or password, ask the owner. Do not alter advanced wireless drivers, firewall rules, network adapters, or device policies just to complete this introductory lesson.

### A simple status journal

When an issue occurs, make a short record. Example:

| Field | Example entry |
| --- | --- |
| Time | Thursday, 10:15 a.m. |
| Device | Home laptop; Windows 11 |
| Connection type | Wi-Fi; expected home network |
| Symptom | Browser says “No internet”; two sites fail |
| Comparison | Phone on same Wi-Fi also fails; cellular test not used |
| Observed equipment | Router appears powered; no equipment touched |
| Authorized action | Checked provider's official outage notice |
| Result | Provider reports service interruption in area |
| Next step | Wait for provider resolution; avoid unnecessary resets |

This record distinguishes **what is known** from **what you suspect**. It is especially useful when a problem recurs, when someone else must troubleshoot, or when multiple people are affected.

### Check for a reported outage using an alternate connection

If permitted, an unaffected cellular connection can be used to check the internet provider's official website, app, service-status page, or authenticated account notices. If that alternate route works, look for information relevant to your service area and current time. A broad social-media complaint is less authoritative than a provider's own outage notice, and neither proves that every local symptom has the same cause.

Avoid logging into account portals from unexpected links in text messages or emails claiming there is an outage. Instead open the provider's verified app or type its known address directly. If the provider reports a fault, restarting your equipment over and over may add confusion without solving it.

## 4. Check the connection on your device

### Windows 11: examine Wi-Fi and network status

On a Windows 11 PC, select the **network, sound, or battery** area near the right side of the taskbar, or open **Start → Settings → Network & internet**. Inspect whether Wi-Fi is on and whether the expected network is listed as connected. Check **Airplane mode** if it is available. Menu names and icon placement can vary with updates, equipment, and organizational configuration.

Microsoft provides a built-in network and internet troubleshooter through **Get Help**. If you have permission, you can open Get Help and use its network troubleshooting guidance. Read each proposed action before accepting changes. A diagnostic that checks status is not the same as a tool that resets network configuration, so do not approve steps you do not understand on a managed computer.

If Windows says **“Connected, no internet,”** treat it as evidence that joining the local network and reaching the wider internet are separate tasks. Compare a known site, the status of another authorized device, and any ISP notice before attempting more disruptive procedures. If Windows reports a wrong password or refuses to join a network, confirm that the selected name and credentials are correct rather than repeatedly guessing.

### Mac: inspect Wi-Fi status and use official guidance

On a Mac, use the Wi-Fi controls in the menu bar or **System Settings → Wi-Fi**, depending on the system version. Check whether Wi-Fi is enabled, whether the Mac joined the intended network, and whether the system reports connection recommendations. Apple's support materials also describe **Wireless Diagnostics**, a built-in method for analyzing problems when a Mac joins a router but cannot reach internet services.

The purpose of an introductory check is to **observe**. Some diagnostic procedures generate reports or propose configuration changes; read their prompts, protect private information, and follow the organization's support process if the Mac is managed. Do not treat a successful Wi-Fi association as proof that a particular website must load.

### Chromebook: verify the network and use Diagnostics

On a Chromebook, select the **time** at the bottom right to inspect the network controls and connection status. Confirm that Wi-Fi is enabled and the correct authorized network is selected. Google documents a **Diagnostics** app for testing aspects of device and network connectivity; its availability and controls vary by ChromeOS version.

Google's troubleshooting guidance emphasizes checking the connection, using network diagnostics, restarting the Chromebook when appropriate, and testing after each step. A Chromebook's inability to load one site does not always mean the entire network is offline. At a school or workplace, consult the administrator before changing settings or using another network.

### iPhone and iPad: distinguish Wi-Fi from cellular

On an iPhone or iPad, open **Settings → Wi-Fi** and inspect the selected network. A checkmark indicates a network selection; a message such as “No Internet Connection” suggests the network may not be providing usable access. If the device uses cellular data, check whether the successful app test is using cellular rather than the problematic Wi-Fi connection.

Apple recommends checking the router and the other devices on the network when troubleshooting, and contacting the ISP or network administrator when the broader connection is the problem. **Reset Network Settings** is not an initial observation: it can remove saved network configurations and cause additional setup work. Do not use it as the first remedy.

### Android phones and tablets: observe the actual network path

On many Android devices, open **Settings → Network & internet** or **Connections**, then inspect Wi-Fi, internet, and airplane-mode controls. The wording differs among manufacturers. Android guidance recommends checking whether the device has a working Wi-Fi or mobile-data connection and carefully comparing outcomes. A phone may reconnect automatically or change its preferred route, so note whether the phone is on Wi-Fi or cellular when testing.

A tablet without cellular capability cannot use mobile data without a compatible service and hardware. Do not buy a data plan, enable roaming, or pay for a hotspot simply to complete a class exercise. Any real-world fallback connection should be approved and its potential charges understood.

### Which controls are appropriate for beginners?

| Device | Safe first observation | Built-in help option | Save for advanced or authorized support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Windows 11 | Wi-Fi and Airplane mode in Network & internet | Get Help network troubleshooting | Network reset, command-line repairs, driver removal |
| macOS | Wi-Fi status in System Settings | Wireless Diagnostics and official support | Deleting network files or changing DNS without a plan |
| Chromebook | Network status from Quick Settings | ChromeOS Diagnostics | Powerwash, developer commands, managed policy changes |
| iPhone or iPad | Selected network in Settings → Wi-Fi | Official Apple Wi-Fi guidance | Reset Network Settings, erasing device |
| Android | Network or Connections settings | Device-maker or Android troubleshooting | Reset connectivity settings, installing unknown utilities |
| Shared home router | Check owner, physical status, and ISP notice | Manufacturer or ISP official support | Factory reset, changing credentials, firmware experiments |

The right-hand column lists actions that are not inherently forbidden. They are simply **higher-impact** actions and should not be a beginner's default response. A reset can erase saved networks, disconnect other people, disturb work, or require information that the learner does not possess.

### What about “forget network”?

Choosing **Forget** or **Remove network** deliberately removes a saved connection profile. It may be useful when an incorrect saved configuration prevents reconnecting, but it can also erase remembered network credentials or settings. Confirm the correct network name, the legitimate password, and your authority to rejoin *before* using this option. On a managed laptop, the organization may supply the profile automatically and may prohibit manual changes.

### What if Ethernet works but Wi-Fi does not?

If an authorized wired connection works while Wi-Fi fails, that comparison suggests the overall internet service may be available while the local wireless path has a problem. It does **not** automatically prove that the router is defective: the device's Wi-Fi adapter, password, wireless range, authentication, or interference could be involved. Do not swap cables or move network equipment without permission. The next observation is whether other authorized Wi-Fi devices are affected.

### Why restarting a device sometimes helps

A normal [[restart|restart]] can clear a temporary software state and reconnect network components. Before restarting, save work and confirm that no critical update or active task should be interrupted. Follow Lesson 1's normal restart procedure rather than forcibly powering off a responsive device. If a restart restores service, document that it helped; the result does not necessarily identify the underlying cause, especially if the failure returns.

## 5. Check the network equipment and service

### Identify what you control

A household may have a [[router|router]], a separate modem or [[ont|fiber terminal]], a [[mesh-wifi|mesh Wi-Fi]] system with several access points, and a power supply for each device. A cable provider may supply a combination modem/router, while a fiber provider may use a wall-mounted terminal connected to a router. Apartment buildings and workplaces can have centrally managed equipment you never see.

The first question is **who owns or manages the equipment?** If it belongs to a landlord, school, employer, public venue, or shared household, you may be permitted to observe lights but not to disconnect power or alter settings. The fact that you can physically reach a router does not mean you are authorized to reset it.

### Learn what visible status lights can and cannot tell you

Routers and modems often have lights labeled **Power**, **Online**, **Internet**, **WAN**, **Wi-Fi**, or **LAN**. Their meanings depend on the exact model. Some flash when traffic passes; others turn amber or red during a fault; some can be deliberately disabled through settings. Avoid generic rules such as “a blinking light always means damage.” Look up the model's official guide or ask the provider if light meanings are unclear.

A power light shows something about the device's electrical state. It cannot by itself prove that the internet service works. A Wi-Fi light may indicate that the router is transmitting a local wireless network while its upstream service is offline. Some devices automatically reboot or perform updates. Observe the sequence rather than touching unfamiliar controls.

### Cable observations without unsafe handling

If you can see a loose or visibly damaged cable, make a note. Do not touch exposed wiring, a damaged power adapter, a wet outlet, or a cable showing heat damage. Do not open power supplies, wall plates, fiber termination boxes, or sealed provider equipment. Fiber-optic connectors should not be looked into, and delicate fiber cables should not be bent sharply or manipulated casually.

An authorized adult responsible for the equipment may check accessible, undamaged plug connections using the manufacturer's instructions. A novice learner should not make a live-power exercise out of investigating a suspected electrical fault. The separate lesson **Checking Power and Cables** provides additional guidance on ordinary external connections and stop conditions.

### When a router restart might make sense

Restarting a router or modem can sometimes restore a stalled connection between equipment and the service provider. Microsoft and other device manufacturers include equipment restart as one possible troubleshooting action. But **a router restart disconnects multiple devices**, sometimes including phones using Wi-Fi calling, cameras, security devices, telework connections, and other networked equipment. It is not equivalent to restarting one personal laptop.

Before a restart, confirm all of the following:

1. You are the equipment owner or have explicit permission from the person responsible.
1. No one depends on the connection for a live call, monitored system, critical service, or ongoing task.
1. No warning indicates a damaged cable, overheating, water exposure, or another physical hazard.
1. There is no known provider outage or update that makes repeated restarts unnecessary.
1. You know which equipment is involved and can follow the correct manufacturer's or provider's restart procedure.
1. You can observe the outcome and stop rather than repeatedly cycling power.

A **normal restart** or supported power-cycle procedure is not the same as a [[factory-reset|factory reset]]. A factory reset can wipe network names, passwords, custom configurations, or provider settings. Never press a recessed **Reset** button on the assumption it means “restart.” If you are unsure, ask the device owner or provider to perform the operation.

### Why equipment restarts sometimes fail

A router cannot repair a service-provider outage, an account problem, a distant website's failure, or a laptop that has Wi-Fi disabled. An ISP can experience problems beyond the home, including network maintenance, regional faults, damaged lines, or access-system failures. The internet also consists of many independent organizations' networks, so an upstream route can be impaired while unrelated destinations still work.

If a service provider reports an outage, note its reference number and any restoration information actually provided. Avoid inventing an estimated recovery time. Once the provider reports restoration, verify the services that previously failed; if the connection remains down, contact the provider with the evidence you recorded.

### Public, school, and workplace connections

A public hotspot may require a [[captive-portal|captive portal]], which is a venue-controlled page for accepting terms or obtaining access. A device can join the wireless network yet not receive ordinary internet access until the portal process is complete. Verify the exact network name with a staff member. Do not enter an email password, banking password, or payment-card details into an unexpected page. Legitimate sign-in requirements vary; when in doubt, ask staff rather than following a suspicious redirect.

Some schools and workplaces intentionally restrict certain sites, require a managed account, use a [[vpn|VPN]], or route traffic through security systems. A block or authentication failure may be a policy decision rather than broken equipment. Do not disable security software, bypass filtering, change DNS to evade restrictions, or install unapproved VPNs. Report the error and ask the authorized IT team what access is permitted.

### Internet problems and account security

Unexpected browser warnings about certificates, an unfamiliar Wi-Fi login page, requests to install “Wi-Fi repair” tools, or unsolicited phone calls claiming to be technical support are reasons to stop. Do not enter credentials to “test” a suspicious page, bypass a certificate warning to reach a financial account, or grant remote control to a stranger. Confirm the service through independently known contact details.

An [[https|HTTPS]] connection helps protect communication with a legitimate site, but it does not guarantee that the site itself is honest. The padlock or browser security indication is not a substitute for recognizing the domain, understanding the service, and considering the request. Public Wi-Fi users should avoid transmitting sensitive information to unknown sites and follow organizational privacy policies.

## 6. Follow a step-by-step troubleshooting workflow

### The six-stage method: observe, scope, compare, act, verify, report

Good troubleshooting is a process of collecting evidence and making controlled decisions. The six stages below can be reused for other computer problems:

1. **Observe:** Write down the exact symptom, message, device, and time.
1. **Scope:** Decide whether one website, one app, one device, or several devices are affected.
1. **Compare:** Use a low-risk check, such as a second trusted website or an authorized device on the same network.
1. **Act:** If necessary, choose one reversible, permitted intervention appropriate to the evidence.
1. **Verify:** Repeat the original task and record whether it now works, partially works, or still fails.
1. **Report:** If unresolved, communicate the observations and tests to the responsible support provider.

The order matters. A careless “try everything” approach may erase the evidence that would have identified the problem. It may also make a brief outage worse by disrupting other people, deleting configuration, or causing avoidable account lockouts.

### Choose a next step from the symptom

| Observation after a first check | Appropriate next step | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One website fails; unrelated trusted sites work | Check the exact address, official service status, or site support | Resetting every network device |
| One app fails; normal browsing works | Review the app's own connection or account message | Assuming Wi-Fi is necessarily broken |
| Wi-Fi is switched off on your own device | Turn it on if allowed, then select the recognized network | Changing router administrator settings |
| Correct Wi-Fi network joined, but internet unavailable on one device | Compare other devices and approved built-in diagnostics | Immediately factory-resetting the device |
| All authorized devices on same network are offline | Check for provider notices and ask the network owner about shared equipment | Secretly rebooting a shared router |
| Device works near router but not far away | Record location, signal quality, and obstructions | Assuming an expensive speed plan is the solution |
| Public network opens an expected sign-in page | Confirm the venue's network and follow its authorized procedure | Entering unrelated account or banking credentials |
| Browser shows certificate or impersonation warning | Stop and verify the destination independently | Clicking through the warning casually |
| Connection works, but only video calls stutter | Record app, time, devices, and network type; compare another approved service | Deleting system files or disabling security controls |
| Issue persists after safe checks | Prepare a factual support report | Repeating restarts indefinitely |

### Worked example: connected to Wi-Fi, no internet

Jordan's Windows laptop shows that it has joined the apartment's Wi-Fi but has no internet access. Jordan verifies the network name, then checks two familiar sites; neither loads. A roommate's phone also cannot open the same sites **while on the apartment Wi-Fi**. A phone on cellular data can reach the ISP's official outage page, which reports a nearby service interruption. These observations suggest an upstream problem. Jordan records the outage notice, informs the roommate, and does not reset the router. When the provider reports restoration, Jordan tests the original sites again.

**What this example teaches:** Joining Wi-Fi and reaching the internet are separate. A second device helps locate the scope only when the connection types are comparable. An official outage notice supplies further evidence, and unnecessary equipment changes may distract from the real issue.

### Worked example: only one laptop cannot join

Sam's Chromebook cannot connect to the known home Wi-Fi network. Two other authorized devices on the same network work. Sam checks that Wi-Fi is enabled, reads the exact message, and verifies the selected network name. The Chromebook reports a password error. Rather than guessing or resetting the router, Sam asks the household network owner to confirm the legitimate credential. After entering the correct credential, the Chromebook joins the network and loads a familiar website.

**What this example teaches:** An error message and the comparison with other devices can help distinguish a local login problem from a provider outage. A network password is not interchangeable with an email or computer-login password.

### Worked example: internet works, online meeting does not

A remote worker can open websites and send email, but a video meeting shows intermittent audio. The worker notes whether the issue affects both audio and video, whether coworkers report a similar problem, whether it happens at particular times, and whether background downloads are occurring. These observations may point toward latency, congestion, the meeting service, or the local device. They do not prove a single cause. Because the computer belongs to the employer, the worker contacts IT rather than disabling the VPN or changing network security settings.

**What this example teaches:** The word “internet” can hide an application-specific or quality-of-service problem. An accurate support report is often more effective than changing settings without authorization.

### Worked example: a public Wi-Fi portal appears

At a community library, a learner selects the Wi-Fi network named on a printed library sign. The device connects to the network, but websites do not load. A portal appears asking the learner to accept the network's terms. The learner confirms with library staff that the network name and portal are legitimate, reviews the conditions, and follows the approved access procedure. If an unfamiliar portal asks for the learner's personal email password or payment information, the learner stops and requests assistance.

**What this example teaches:** A portal can be an expected part of public network access, but a similar-looking page can be deceptive. Verification of network ownership is essential.

### When to stop and escalate

Stop self-directed changes and contact an authorized professional or service provider when:

- The connection supports medical, safety, security, business-critical, or accessibility equipment that could be interrupted.
- The router belongs to an employer, school, landlord, venue, or someone else who has not granted permission.
- There are signs of physical or electrical hazards, damaged wiring, heat damage, swelling, smoke, or moisture.
- A website presents a suspicious certificate warning, impersonation request, credential prompt, or alleged support scam.
- You do not have the legitimate network credentials or authority to alter configurations.
- Multiple ordinary checks fail and the next proposed step is a reset, removal of software, DNS change, firewall modification, or other high-impact action.
- The problem involves repeated account lockouts, suspected compromise, sensitive records, or financial transactions.

Good judgment includes knowing when to preserve the current state. A **support handoff** is part of completing troubleshooting, not a failure.

### A reusable support report

A short, accurate report saves time for the next person:

| Report field | What to include |
| --- | --- |
| Device and system | Device type and relevant operating system |
| Network and owner | Home Wi-Fi, workplace network, public venue, or cellular; do not reveal secret credentials |
| Problem started | Approximate time and whether it is continuous or intermittent |
| Exact symptom | Error wording, affected sites or apps, observed status |
| Scope | One website, one app, one device, or several devices |
| Safe comparisons | Which trusted sites and authorized devices were tested |
| Actions taken | Only the steps you actually performed, in order |
| Results | What changed, if anything, after each step |
| Current impact | Whether important work or communications are blocked |
| Support request | What help you need and how to contact you through an approved channel |

Do not send full passwords, Wi-Fi keys, session tokens, one-time codes, or unnecessary personal details. For screenshots, crop or blur account names, network identifiers, IP addresses, and private content if they are not essential and the support channel is not appropriately protected.

## 7. Guided practice: seven low-risk activities

These activities are designed to build judgment **without deliberately breaking a working connection**. If you lack a device, complete the scenario analysis on paper. If you use a shared, managed, or accessibility-dependent device, do not modify settings without permission. Each activity includes a suggested response in an expandable answer section.

### Activity 1: classify the scope of six problems

**Goal:** Distinguish local failures from broader network problems.

Read each situation and identify the most likely *scope to investigate first*: one site or service, one device, shared local network, or uncertain until further checking.

1. A news website fails, but an online dictionary and email both work.
1. One tablet shows “Wrong password,” while two laptops use the same home Wi-Fi successfully.
1. Three devices join the household Wi-Fi but cannot load any trusted sites.
1. A mobile phone opens a site, but nobody knows whether it used cellular or Wi-Fi.
1. One streaming app cannot sign in, while browser-based services work normally.
1. A laptop has a weak Wi-Fi signal only in the detached garage.

Record one additional safe observation that would strengthen each classification.

:::answer Show Activity 1 guidance
1. **One site or service** is the first area to investigate. Check another trusted site and the service's official status.
1. **One device or its Wi-Fi credentials.** The password message and other devices' success narrow the focus.
1. **Shared network or upstream internet access** becomes plausible, but an official provider check can add evidence.
1. **Uncertain.** Confirm the phone's actual connection type before comparing it with the laptop.
1. **One app or its service.** Read the exact sign-in error and consult the app's official status.
1. **Wireless coverage in a particular location.** Compare normal operation closer to the access point without moving electrical equipment.

None of these classifications is a guaranteed root-cause diagnosis.
:::

### Activity 2: draw a simple network path

**Goal:** Explain how different parts of a connection relate to each other.

On paper, draw five boxes labeled **device**, **Wi-Fi or Ethernet**, **router**, **ISP**, and **website**. Connect the boxes in that order. If you know that your home uses a separate modem or fiber terminal, add it between router and ISP. Mark which boxes could still function if the ISP connection stopped working. Then explain why a Wi-Fi icon might continue to show a connection even if websites fail.

:::answer Show Activity 2 sample
A simple model is **device → Wi-Fi/Ethernet → router → modem or fiber terminal if present → ISP → website**. The local Wi-Fi link between the device and router may continue to operate even if the router cannot reach the ISP. That is why a device can report “Connected” while internet-dependent services remain unavailable. Actual networks can have different equipment and additional steps.
:::

### Activity 3: inspect the connection status without changing it

**Goal:** Locate useful status controls.

If you have a device you are authorized to use, open its normal network or Wi-Fi status view. Write down only non-sensitive observations: whether Wi-Fi is on, whether the device reports connected or disconnected, and whether the displayed network is one you recognize. Do not copy the network password or publish your address. Do not switch networks, forget connections, or disable any services for this exercise.

For Windows 11, view **Settings → Network & internet**. For Mac, view **System Settings → Wi-Fi**. For Chromebook, select the time and inspect network controls. For iPhone or iPad, view **Settings → Wi-Fi**. On Android, use **Network & internet** or **Connections**, depending on your device.

:::answer Show Activity 3 guidance
A successful response names the device and identifies where its connection status appears. For example: “On Windows 11 I opened Settings, selected Network & internet, saw that Wi-Fi was on, and observed that the device reported connected.” You do not need to disclose the actual network name or change a setting. If you do not have a device, describe the correct menu for one platform.
:::

### Activity 4: select a safe comparison test

**Goal:** Use comparisons that actually answer a question.

For each scenario, choose one low-risk comparison and explain what it would and would not establish.

1. Your browser fails to open a study site.
1. Your laptop is offline, but a phone appears online.
1. Wi-Fi works in the living room but not reliably in a distant room.
1. A video-call app stutters while local documents open normally.

Avoid using credentials, changing router settings, or downloading troubleshooting software.

:::answer Show Activity 4 guidance
1. Open a second familiar independent website. If it works, some connectivity exists, but the study site's failure remains unexplained.
1. Check whether the phone is on the same Wi-Fi or using cellular data. A cellular result cannot establish that the home Wi-Fi works.
1. Observe signal and connectivity in the two permitted locations. A difference suggests a coverage or interference question; it does not measure the whole ISP service.
1. Compare another approved online service and check whether the meeting platform reports an incident. A local document opening proves little about network quality.
:::

### Activity 5: distinguish a restart from a reset

**Goal:** Understand the risk of changing shared network equipment.

Read the following proposals and classify each as **safe observation**, **permission required**, or **do not proceed without qualified help**.

1. Look at a router's visible lights without touching anything.
1. Reboot the household router while another person is on a work call.
1. Press the recessed “Reset” button on a router because Wi-Fi seems slow.
1. Read an ISP outage notice in the provider's official app using an unaffected connection.
1. Open a damaged power supply to see if a cable is loose.
1. Ask the network owner whether a restart is appropriate after simpler checks.

:::answer Show Activity 5 guidance
1. **Safe observation** if you can access the area without risk or entering a restricted space.
1. **Permission required, and defer while it would disrupt the active call.** The restart affects others.
1. **Do not proceed without qualified/authorized help.** A factory reset can erase the router's configuration.
1. **Safe observation** when using an authorized and secure route to the official notice.
1. **Do not proceed.** Opening a power supply is an electrical hazard and not a beginner task.
1. **Safe request for permission.** The owner can decide whether and when to restart equipment.
:::

### Activity 6: decide how to handle three network messages

**Goal:** Avoid guessing from a message alone.

Write the safest first response to each case:

1. A hotel Wi-Fi portal asks you to enter your personal email password into a form with an unfamiliar address.
1. Your laptop reports “Connected, no internet,” and several devices on the same home Wi-Fi have the same symptom.
1. A browser warns that a financial website's certificate or identity cannot be verified.

For each case, identify the person or organization you would consult if the problem remained unresolved.

:::answer Show Activity 6 guidance
1. **Stop.** Confirm the hotel's correct network name and access method with hotel staff. Never enter unrelated account credentials into an unexpected portal.
1. **Compare and verify.** Check the ISP's official status or ask the network owner to inspect shared equipment; do not reset anything automatically.
1. **Do not bypass the warning.** Verify the destination independently and contact the financial institution through an official channel.

A network issue is not a reason to abandon ordinary account-security precautions.
:::

### Activity 7: write a support handoff

**Goal:** Produce an actionable report without exaggerating certainty.

Use this fictional case: At 2:30 p.m., a home Mac shows a Wi-Fi connection. Two familiar websites fail. A tablet using the same household Wi-Fi also fails. A phone using cellular data opens the ISP's official status page; no outage notice appears. No equipment has been touched, and there are no visible hazards. Write a four- to six-sentence note for the home network owner or provider. Include the symptom, comparisons, what has *not* been done, and the next help needed.

:::answer Show Activity 7 sample
“At approximately 2:30 p.m., the Mac still showed that it was connected to our home Wi-Fi, but two familiar websites did not load. A tablet on the same Wi-Fi experienced the same problem. A phone on cellular data could open our ISP's official status page, which did not show a reported outage at that time. We have not restarted or reset network equipment or changed settings. Could you help check the home network and internet service? We can provide the exact error messages if needed.”

This report preserves uncertainty: the absence of a visible outage notice does not prove the ISP is functioning correctly.
:::

## 8. Common mistakes and misconceptions

### “If the Wi-Fi bars are full, the internet must be working.”

Not necessarily. Bars largely represent the local wireless link; upstream service and a remote website can still be unavailable. Check actual access to trusted services rather than relying on signal strength alone.

### “If my phone works, the household Wi-Fi works.”

Not unless the phone was using the same Wi-Fi. A phone may have switched to cellular data. Verify the connection path and understand any data-plan consequences before running tests.

### “One website failing means the router needs to be reset.”

No. A single website or app may have its own problem. First test an independent trusted site and read the error message. Avoid disrupting everyone in the household without evidence.

### “A connected device can always browse the web.”

No. Joining a local network and reaching external services are distinct. A public network may require a legitimate captive portal; a home network may have no upstream internet access.

### “If I restart the router repeatedly, it will eventually fix the problem.”

Repeated restarts are not a diagnosis and can interrupt service for other users. If an authorized restart is appropriate, perform it according to the provider's instructions and test once; stop and escalate if it fails.

### “The reset button is another name for the restart button.”

No. A factory reset can erase network configuration, credentials, and custom settings. The device label and manufacturer instructions matter. Do not press an unfamiliar recessed control.

### “The Wi-Fi password is the same as the router's administrator password.”

Not necessarily. One controls joining a wireless network, while the other controls administrative access to the router. Both should be protected, and the administrator password should not be shared as a routine troubleshooting step.

### “An internet outage always affects everyone equally.”

No. Outages may be local, regional, provider-specific, service-specific, or even limited to certain routes. Some features may keep working while others fail. Avoid broad conclusions from one observation.

### “A speed test is the first thing I should run when disconnected.”

A speed test itself requires a working path to its server. If a device cannot connect at all, first establish whether network access exists. Even when it runs, a single test result does not explain every video call or website failure.

### “I must change DNS whenever a site won't open.”

No. A DNS issue is only one possible explanation. Changing network settings can cause new problems and violate managed-device policies. Use built-in guidance or qualified support after basic comparisons.

### “I can fix workplace Wi-Fi by turning off the VPN or firewall.”

Not without authorization. Those systems may protect business data or enforce organizational requirements. Contact IT and share the exact problem instead of bypassing security controls.

### “Public Wi-Fi is safe if it asks me to sign in.”

Not automatically. Network names and login pages can be imitated. Verify the venue's official network and never provide unrelated account passwords or payment information to a suspicious portal.

### “A browser padlock proves the website is legitimate.”

No. HTTPS protects a connection to the site named in the address bar, but a deceptive site can also use HTTPS. Check the actual address, context, and trusted access method.

### “A successful restart proves I found the cause.”

A restart may restore service temporarily by clearing a software state. If the issue comes back, additional observation is needed. Record the outcome without declaring an unconfirmed root cause.

### “A missing outage announcement means there is no outage.”

Not necessarily. Status pages may lag, serve limited areas, or omit a newly emerging incident. Share specific observations with the provider if the problem persists.

### “I should download a free network-repair program from the first search result.”

No. Unauthorized installers and fake support tools can introduce malware or steal credentials. Prefer device manufacturers' built-in diagnostics and independently verified official resources.

### “Every connection failure is worth a factory reset.”

Factory resets may erase saved settings and data, create reconfiguration work, and fail to solve a remote outage. They are not suitable first steps in beginner troubleshooting.

### “Asking an administrator for help means I didn't troubleshoot.”

No. Gathering reliable observations, avoiding disruption, and handing off a well-documented problem is a successful troubleshooting outcome when the next action exceeds your access or expertise.


## 9. Essential vocabulary

Some important terms appear earlier with selectable definitions. Practice explaining the words in ordinary language, because a person who can describe a connection accurately can usually obtain better assistance. You do not need to memorize engineering protocols, hardware specifications, or unfamiliar configuration menus.

- **Internet:** The global system of interconnected networks through which many websites and services communicate.
- **Wi-Fi:** Short-range wireless networking, often used to connect a device to a router or access point.
- **Local network:** Devices connected within a home, building, school, or other local setting.
- **ISP (internet service provider):** Company or organization supplying internet access.
- **Router:** Device that sends network traffic between networks; home routers often also supply wireless connectivity.
- **Modem or optical network terminal (ONT):** Equipment that provides the service-side link for some cable, DSL, or fiber internet connections.
- **Network adapter:** Hardware that connects a device to a network through Wi-Fi or a wired interface.
- **Ethernet:** Wired network technology used with compatible ports, adapters, and cables.
- **SSID:** A displayed wireless network name, not proof of who owns that network.
- **Wi-Fi password:** A credential for joining a protected wireless network, separate from an account password.
- **Airplane mode:** A device setting that affects wireless radios or connections, with behavior varying by device.
- **Cellular data:** Internet access supplied over a mobile carrier's network.
- **Captive portal:** Network access page that may require a sign-in or acceptance of conditions.
- **Signal strength:** A measure or indication of radio reception, not a guarantee of internet access.
- **DNS (Domain Name System):** A system that helps computers find numerical addresses associated with website names.
- **IP address:** A network address used to deliver information across networked devices and services.
- **Latency:** Delay associated with communication over a network.
- **Bandwidth:** Data-transfer capacity available over a connection.
- **Packet loss:** When transmitted network data fails to arrive successfully.
- **Outage:** An interruption to a service, connection, or network component.
- **Intermittent:** Working sometimes and failing at other times.
- **Troubleshooting:** Identifying the scope of a problem and testing possible explanations carefully.
- **Restart:** Turning a running device or system off and back on through a normal controlled procedure.
- **Factory reset:** Restoring equipment toward initial settings, potentially removing custom configurations.
- **VPN:** A virtual private network, sometimes required by an employer or school to access protected resources.
- **HTTPS:** A protocol that encrypts communication between a browser and a website, without guaranteeing a website's honesty.
- **Status page:** Official source that may show whether an online service or provider reports an incident.
- **Managed device:** Computer or phone whose settings and security are controlled partly by an organization.
- **Reversible action:** A change that can be undone without undue difficulty, disruption, or data loss.
- **Escalation:** Passing a problem to a person or team with the proper authority, tools, or expertise.

### Practice five plain-language distinctions

Try saying each pair aloud in one sentence: **Wi-Fi versus internet**, **router versus ISP**, **signal strength versus speed**, **restart versus factory reset**, and **symptom versus cause**. If your explanation uses another technical word, try replacing that word with a familiar example. Being able to explain the difference helps you make decisions without resorting to guesswork.

## 11. Further learning and next steps

### Summary: the five distinctions that matter

A reliable beginner does not need to know every networking term. A reliable beginner needs to distinguish five practical ideas:

1. **Wi-Fi connection versus internet access.** Joining a local network does not guarantee that external services work.
1. **One failing service versus a broad outage.** Multiple trusted comparisons help identify the scope.
1. **Same network versus different network.** A phone on cellular cannot directly prove that household Wi-Fi is functioning.
1. **Observation versus disruption.** Reading connection status is different from deleting profiles, changing DNS, or resetting shared equipment.
1. **Temporary recovery versus confirmed diagnosis.** If a restart works, verify and document the result without claiming more than the evidence shows.

### A reusable connection checklist

When internet access fails in everyday life, work through the following checklist without skipping the safety decision:

1. Read and record the exact non-sensitive message.
1. Decide whether one app, one site, one device, or several devices are affected.
1. Check Wi-Fi status, airplane-mode behavior, and the selected authorized network.
1. Compare two familiar, independent online services if safe and possible.
1. If authorized, compare another device on the **same** network and confirm it is not using cellular data.
1. Check any official provider or service-status notice through a legitimate, unaffected connection.
1. Make only one approved, reversible change at a time, and protect saved work first.
1. Verify whether the original task works and document the observed result.
1. Stop and request help before resets, security bypasses, electrical work, or changes to managed/shared equipment.

### Final decision table

| You can establish | You should say | You should not claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One trusted site loads; another fails | “Some internet services work; this site does not.” | “The entire internet is down.” |
| Two devices on same Wi-Fi fail | “The failure affects more than one device on this network.” | “The ISP has definitely failed.” |
| Phone succeeds over cellular | “Mobile internet access works on this phone.” | “Our home router is fine.” |
| Wi-Fi bars are strong | “The local wireless signal appears strong.” | “All external services should work.” |
| Normal restart restored access | “Service resumed after the restart.” | “We proved the precise cause.” |
| Provider's status page reports an incident | “The provider reports a service incident.” | “Every local symptom is conclusively explained.” |
| No safe steps remain | “We need an authorized support check.” | “A factory reset must be safe.” |

### Final reflection

Imagine calling a technical support representative and saying only, “My internet is down.” Now imagine saying, “Since 3 p.m., my laptop joins the home Wi-Fi but cannot open two familiar websites. The family tablet on the same Wi-Fi has the same problem. I checked the provider's official status and have not reset any equipment.” The second statement does not claim to know the cause, yet it gives the representative a much better starting point. **That is the professional habit this lesson is designed to teach.**
