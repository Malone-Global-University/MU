## Lesson 1: What Is the Internet?

You tap a weather app, send a message to a relative, join an online class, or open a school website. Although these activities look different, many depend on the same underlying system: the **internet**. A device connects to a network, information moves between devices and online services, and an answer, message, page, or video returns. Understanding that basic sequence makes everyday technology less mysterious and helps you respond calmly when a connection stops working.

This introductory lesson explains **what the internet is, what it is not, how devices connect, what happens when information travels, and how to recognize ordinary connectivity and safety conditions**. It distinguishes the internet from the World Wide Web, Wi-Fi, a browser, and a search engine. You do not need to study engineering or memorize technical codes. You do need a dependable mental model you can use repeatedly at home, at school, at work, and on a phone.

You will read illustrated-in-words examples, compare real-life situations, and complete observation-first activities. Windows 11 is the principal desktop example, with equivalent concepts for Mac, Chromebook, iPhone, iPad, and Android. Every required activity can also be completed from the provided scenarios without internet access. **No exercise requires you to create an account, enter a password, download a file, buy a service, modify network settings, or expose personal information.**

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 85–115 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Ability to identify a computer or mobile device; Computer Basics recommended · **Equipment:** Any device or printed lesson · **Practice rule:** Observe existing information; do not change network, privacy, or security settings without permission

> **Essential idea:** The **[[internet|internet]]** is a worldwide system connecting many separate [[network|networks]]. The **[[web|World Wide Web]]** is one service that uses the internet. **[[wi-fi|Wi-Fi]]** is one method of connecting a device to a nearby network. A **[[browser|web browser]]** is an application used to access web content. Those are related concepts—not different names for the same thing.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Describe the internet as an interconnected worldwide system rather than a single company, website, or device.
1. Explain the differences among the internet, the World Wide Web, a browser, Wi-Fi, and a search engine.
1. Recognize ordinary internet activities, including web pages, email, messaging, online meetings, and cloud-based services.
1. Identify the general roles of a device, a local network, an internet service provider, and an online server.
1. Distinguish common connection methods, including Ethernet, home Wi-Fi, mobile data, and public hotspots.
1. Follow a simplified sequence of how a browser request reaches a website and receives a response.
1. Interpret ordinary indicators such as connected, no internet, offline, Wi-Fi signal, and mobile data without overclaiming what they prove.
1. Distinguish a website address from a search phrase and describe the limited meaning of HTTPS.
1. Recognize how reliability, data plans, access permissions, security, and privacy affect everyday internet use.
1. Complete safe observation exercises and explain an initial, non-destructive response to a connection problem.

## 1. What is the internet?

The internet is a vast **network of networks**: many independently operated networks that connect with one another so participating devices can exchange information according to shared technical rules. A school may operate its own computer network, a company may operate another, and an internet service provider may connect thousands of homes. When these networks interconnect and use compatible communication standards, a person in one place can communicate with a service somewhere else in the world.

A [[network|computer network]] is simply a set of devices that can communicate through connected equipment and agreed rules. Some networks are small: a printer and two laptops on a home network. Others connect offices, campuses, cities, or regions. The global internet links many such networks. Not every network is publicly accessible, and being on a small local network does not automatically mean the internet is working.

### From individual devices to a worldwide system

Start with a [[device|device]] you recognize: a laptop, phone, tablet, desktop computer, or smart television. A device can store information and run programs by itself. When it connects to other devices through a [[local-network|local network]], it may share selected information or use a local printer. When that network has a working connection to the wider internet, the device may reach remote websites and internet-based services, subject to access rules.

**One simple model:**

`Your device → nearby network → internet provider → interconnected networks → online service`

The reply commonly travels back across connected networks to your device. This diagram is deliberately simplified. Actual connections may pass through several providers, routers, facilities, and networks; sometimes content is served from a nearby computer rather than a distant one. You do not need to identify every intermediate system to understand the basic process.

The internet includes physical [[infrastructure|infrastructure]]—equipment and communication links—as well as operating organizations and shared rules. It is not merely a cloud floating above the planet. Connections can use fiber-optic cables, copper wiring, cellular radio systems, microwave links, undersea cables, satellites, and data centers. The precise route varies, and much international internet communication travels through physical cables rather than satellites.

### Why do different networks work together?

Imagine two families using different telephone companies. They still expect to call one another. In a similar way, someone connected through one internet provider expects to reach services operated by other companies. That works because participating networks cooperate in moving information and use shared [[protocol|protocols]]—technical communication rules. The [[global-network|global network]] is therefore not one enormous home Wi-Fi system. It is a collection of networks with many operators and interconnections.

This design also explains why no single person owns the entire internet. Different companies and institutions own and manage portions of its infrastructure and services. Governments establish laws affecting internet activity in their jurisdictions; standards organizations and network operators help develop and implement common technical practices. A specific country, company, or service can restrict access, and a local failure can still occur, even though the internet as a whole continues operating elsewhere.

### What is actually being exchanged?

Devices exchange [[data|data]]: information represented in a form computers can process. A message may contain text, a photograph contains image data, and a video meeting exchanges sound and picture data. Data may be divided into smaller units called [[packet|packets]] that networks can carry toward a destination. The receiving system reassembles or otherwise processes what arrives. A useful comparison is a large set of labeled parcels moving through a transportation system, but unlike physical parcels, electronic packets may travel by different routes and systems can sometimes request missing information again.

A device typically asks for something, receives something, or both. Reading a web page involves sending a request and receiving content. Sending a photo involves transmitting information from your device. In a video call, information travels in both directions continuously. The internet is not only a place where you *look* at information; it also enables communication and transactions.

### Internet access is not a guarantee of unrestricted access

A working internet connection does not mean every site or service is reachable. Access can depend on subscription, location, account permissions, network policies, service availability, legal restrictions, and device compatibility. An online class might require a student account; a public website might be accessible without signing in. A website might be temporarily unavailable even while other sites continue to work.

Consider a home laptop that can print to a nearby wireless printer while no websites open. The laptop may still be communicating over the **local network**, even though its route to the global internet is interrupted. This example will help you interpret connection symbols later in the lesson.

| Term | Plain-language meaning | What it is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internet | Interconnected networks that exchange information worldwide | One company or one website |
| Local network | Nearby connected devices, such as a home or school network | A guarantee of access to all internet services |
| Data | Information represented for computers to use and exchange | Only numbers in a spreadsheet |
| Infrastructure | Cables, equipment, towers, facilities, and other underlying systems | An invisible wireless cloud without physical components |
| Protocol | An agreed rule for how systems communicate | A personal password or a brand of computer |

> **Remember:** A network is the general idea; the **internet** is the enormous interconnected global example. A home network may continue operating even when its internet service is interrupted.

### A worked example: an online weather forecast

Suppose a learner uses a weather application to look up tomorrow's forecast. The phone or laptop prepares a request. A working connection carries the request toward the weather service. One or more online systems process the request and send information back. The app presents temperatures, text, and pictures in a readable layout. The learner sees a simple screen, but behind it several systems cooperate.

Not all visible forecast data must be newly downloaded. Some apps can temporarily display an older forecast saved on the device. Therefore, a weather screen by itself does not prove that the device *currently* has a working internet connection. Later you will learn to look for refresh status, timestamps, and error messages instead of guessing.

## 2. How are the internet, web, browser, and search different?

The most important vocabulary distinction in this course is between the internet and the World Wide Web. Many people say they are “going on the internet” when they open a page in a browser. The expression is understandable, but it hides several different technologies. Separating the concepts makes future lessons easier: you will know whether a problem concerns a network connection, a browser, a website, a search service, or an account.

### The internet is the underlying connection system

The internet carries information for many kinds of services. Think of it as a set of connected transportation routes through which numerous forms of communication can pass. The internet itself is not a particular web page and does not require that every person use the same app.

### The web is one service built on the internet

The **[[web|World Wide Web]]**, often called the *web*, is a system of linked online documents, media, and applications. A [[webpage|web page]] is one page of web content. A [[website|website]] is a collection of related pages or resources under a web address or service. You may read an article, view a university catalog, or use a web-based form. A [[hyperlink|hyperlink]] lets you move from one resource to another.

The web became popular because information could be published as pages that link together. But the internet supports other activities as well. A dedicated video-call app, online game, or email application can use internet connections without being a conventional browser page. The exact technical boundaries can overlap: some apps display web content internally, and some services combine web technology with other internet communications.

### A browser opens web content

A **[[browser|web browser]]** is software for retrieving and displaying web pages and many web applications. Examples include Microsoft Edge, Google Chrome, Mozilla Firefox, and Apple Safari. A browser is **not** the internet. If you open Edge without internet access, the program may still start, display settings or saved content, and show a connection error when you try to visit a live site.

A browser usually provides an [[address-bar|address bar]] where you can enter a [[url|web address]], and may also allow a search phrase. The exact toolbar layout depends on the browser and device. The next lesson, **Using a Web Browser**, will provide a step-by-step walkthrough of browser controls; here we concentrate on understanding their purpose.

### A search engine helps locate information

A **[[search-engine|search engine]]** is a service that helps people find information, often by entering words that describe what they want. Google Search, Bing, and DuckDuckGo are examples. A search engine is not itself the internet, and the first result is not necessarily the best or most trustworthy. Results may include paid [[advertisement|advertisements]] as well as ordinary listings. Later lessons will cover searching and evaluating results in depth.

Compare these two actions:

- Typing the **known address** `https://maloneuniversity.org/` into a browser requests a particular website.
- Typing **beginner computer classes** into a browser's search field asks a search service to locate potentially relevant information.

Some modern browser address bars handle either kind of entry; that convenience does not erase the difference between an address and a search phrase. Never assume a search result for an organization automatically belongs to that organization. The URL and source need examination.

### A website address has meaning

A **[[url|URL]]** (Uniform Resource Locator) identifies a location or resource on the web. Consider the example `https://maloneuniversity.org/department/computer-science/`:

- `https://` indicates the secure web connection scheme used in the example.
- `maloneuniversity.org` is the [[domain-name|domain name]], the human-readable site name.
- `/department/computer-science/` is a path identifying a location within the site's published structure.

A web address is not always an email address. The name `student@example.com` is shaped like an email address, while `https://example.com/` is a web URL. Both include domain-related information, but they support different tasks. **The addresses `example.com` and `example.org` are reserved for examples**; do not treat sample domains or fictional messages in this lesson as instructions to contact a real person.

### Compare the concepts at a glance

| Concept | What it does | Everyday example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internet | Connects networks so information can travel | A home internet connection reaching a remote server |
| World Wide Web | Provides interlinked pages and web applications | Viewing an MGU course description |
| Browser | Displays and interacts with web content | Microsoft Edge, Chrome, Firefox, Safari |
| Search engine | Locates information in response to a query | Searching for a library opening schedule |
| Website | Publishes pages or services at an address | A public library's online catalog |
| Email | Exchanges electronic messages over internet services | Sending an assignment question to a teacher |
| Wi-Fi | Connects a nearby device wirelessly to a network | A laptop joining a home wireless network |

### A comparison that transfers to everyday life

Picture a city transit system. The **roads** are like the internet connection pathways. A **library building** is like a website that provides particular information and services. Your **vehicle** is like a browser—one way to reach and use certain destinations. A **directory** is like a search engine, helping you find a destination you did not already know. This comparison has limits: internet data does not travel exactly like cars, and applications do not always work like buildings. Its value is to remind you that these parts perform **different jobs**.

### Short check before continuing

If someone says, “My browser is the internet,” how would you respond? A precise beginner answer is: “The browser is a program used to reach the web; it needs a working connection for most live online content. The internet is the network system underneath.” If someone says, “I cannot load one site, so the whole internet is down,” you can respond: “That might be a problem with the site, the browser, or the connection; one failed page is not enough evidence.”

## 3. How do devices connect to the internet?

Every internet activity begins with some path from your device to a connected network. The first part of that path can be a cable, a radio connection, or another supported technology. The equipment at home is not necessarily the same as the equipment at school, a coffee shop, or on a mobile phone. **Recognize the connection method before deciding what a status icon means.**

### The role of an internet service provider

An **[[isp|internet service provider]]** (ISP) is a company or organization that supplies connectivity to the wider internet under a service arrangement. A home customer may pay a monthly subscription to an ISP. A school or employer may have a connection supplied by a provider and managed internally. A mobile phone carrier can also provide internet access through its cellular network.

An ISP connects its customer network to other networks. The provider does not need to create every website you visit, nor does it own all of the internet. If your provider experiences a local interruption, the global internet may still be functioning for other users. Some providers also supply equipment, account support, security features, and information about service limits.

### Modems, routers, and home network equipment

A **[[modem|modem]]** generally connects a customer's equipment to a particular access technology or provider service. A **[[router|router]]** directs traffic between networks; a home router commonly connects a local household network to the provider's connection. A **[[wireless-access-point|wireless access point]]** provides Wi-Fi connectivity for nearby devices. Some home gateways combine modem, router, wireless access point, and other functions in one physical box.

Do not memorize the number of boxes in your home. Instead, ask which *roles* are present. For example, a laptop may connect by Wi-Fi to a combined gateway, which reaches the ISP through a fiber terminal or cable connection. In another home, a separate router connects to a modem. In a business, managed access points and switches can connect many computers to a larger organization network.

**Illustrative wired-home path:**

`Laptop → Wi-Fi or Ethernet → home router/gateway → provider connection → internet → website's service`

The direction reverses for the reply. The real path could involve additional equipment and several independent networks. Many home users never need to adjust that equipment to complete normal web tasks.

### Wi-Fi: a wireless connection nearby

**[[wi-fi|Wi-Fi]]** allows devices to communicate over radio with a nearby wireless network. In a home, the Wi-Fi network is usually provided by the router or access point. In a classroom, campus, train station, or public library, the wireless network may be administered by an institution. A Wi-Fi symbol tells you something about the wireless connection; **it does not, by itself, certify that the internet is reachable or that a website is safe**.

Your device may show available Wi-Fi networks with names. A [[network-name|network name]] is often called an SSID. A protected network may require a [[network-password|network password]] or another sign-in procedure. Wireless network names can be imitated, and a public hotspot's name alone does not prove who operates it. Use the network details supplied by the owner or authorized staff rather than guessing.

**Important distinction:** A laptop can be successfully connected to its home Wi-Fi while the provider connection has failed. It might still communicate with other authorized devices inside the home but be unable to reach an online school website. That is why a signal indicator and an internet-access indicator are related but separate observations.

### Ethernet: a cable to a network

**[[ethernet|Ethernet]]** is a common way to connect a desktop computer or laptop directly to a router, wall network port, or other compatible device using a network cable. Ethernet often provides a stable local connection and avoids certain Wi-Fi signal problems, but it is not an automatic guarantee of internet access. If the ISP service is interrupted, a connected Ethernet cable cannot repair that external problem. Some laptops need an adapter because they do not include an Ethernet port.

A cable connection can be particularly helpful in environments with many wireless devices, but the best method depends on the equipment, permissions, and application. Beginners should not unplug organizational equipment, move network cables in a workplace, or purchase adapters as part of this lesson.

### Mobile data: internet access through a cellular network

A phone or cellular-enabled tablet can use **[[mobile-data|mobile data]]** through a [[cellular-network|cellular network]] operated by a carrier. This is different from connecting to a home Wi-Fi network. A phone may switch between Wi-Fi and cellular data based on its configuration, availability, and preferences. Names such as 4G and 5G describe families of cellular technologies; a displayed network generation does not promise a particular speed in all locations.

Mobile plans differ. Some charge for a fixed amount of data, some slow speeds after an allowance, some have restrictions on tethering, and some incur extra fees while roaming. A phone may have no mobile data plan, may lack coverage, or may have data temporarily disabled. **Do not use mobile data merely to complete a classroom exercise if doing so might incur a charge.**

### Hotspots and public Wi-Fi

A **[[hotspot|hotspot]]** is a place or device that provides network access to other devices, often over Wi-Fi. A library might operate a public Wi-Fi hotspot. A phone's personal hotspot feature may share the phone's cellular connection with a laptop. These cases can look similar to the laptop, even though the internet connection behind them is different.

When using **[[public-wifi|public Wi-Fi]]**, first confirm the correct network name and any instructions with a trusted employee or posted official source. Some networks use a **[[captive-portal|sign-in page]]** asking users to accept terms before gaining access. Others require approved institutional credentials. A network that is publicly listed is not necessarily safe, free, or authorized for every user. Do not enter sensitive information into a surprise sign-in page until you have verified the network and service.

### The technology delivering the connection to a building

A provider may reach a home or institution using several technologies. These are connection methods; they do not change the underlying meaning of the internet.

- **[[fiber|Fiber-optic internet]]** carries data as light signals over specialized cables.
- **[[cable-internet|Cable internet]]** commonly uses coaxial cable infrastructure associated with cable networks.
- **[[dsl|DSL]]** is a type of broadband delivery historically associated with telephone copper wiring.
- **[[fixed-wireless|Fixed wireless]]** connects a fixed site through radio links to a provider.
- **[[satellite-internet|Satellite internet]]** uses satellite communication for some portion of the connection; performance and equipment vary by service.
- **Cellular service** can provide a phone connection or, in some plans, home internet access through fixed or mobile equipment.

The word **[[broadband|broadband]]** generally describes an internet access service designed to provide substantial data capacity compared with older narrowband methods. Exact regulatory definitions, speeds, and plan descriptions vary by place and time. Do not interpret the word *broadband* alone as a promise that every online activity will work perfectly.

| Connection method | What connects the device or premises | Key consideration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home Wi-Fi | Device communicates by radio with nearby access point | Wireless connection can exist without working ISP access |
| Ethernet | Cable connects device to compatible network equipment | Wired link can work locally while internet service is down |
| Mobile data | Cellular-capable device uses carrier's radio network | Coverage, plan limits, and roaming costs can affect use |
| Phone hotspot | One device shares its available connection | May count against mobile data allowance or hotspot policy |
| Public Wi-Fi | Establishment's access point connects visitors | Confirm network identity, policies, and privacy expectations |
| Satellite or fixed wireless | Service uses radio links to reach the premises | Weather, terrain, equipment, congestion, and provider vary |

### Wi-Fi signal versus internet service: a practice scenario

A student sees four Wi-Fi bars but cannot open an online assignment. What does that prove? The symbol suggests a relatively strong local radio signal as interpreted by the device. It does **not** prove that the school service is available, that the provider is working, or that the student has the correct permissions. The correct beginner response is to note the message and context rather than deleting the assignment, resetting network equipment, or changing credentials.

> **Connection habit:** Identify **(1) the device**, **(2) its local connection method**, **(3) the provider or network owner**, and **(4) the service you are trying to reach**. Those four roles explain far more than the word “internet” by itself.

## 4. What happens when you visit a website?

A web page may appear a moment after you enter an address. That simple action involves several tasks happening in sequence or at nearly the same time. You do not have to memorize every protocol to be a capable internet user. A basic picture of how information travels helps you distinguish an address mistake from a connection problem, a website outage, or a misleading imitation site.

### Step 1: You select the destination

You open a browser and enter a known [[web-address|web address]] such as `https://maloneuniversity.org/`. Alternatively, you select a saved link or follow a hyperlink from a page you already trust. The browser interprets the destination. A URL may include a domain name, a path, and other details. The spelling matters because small differences can send you to another location or produce an error.

A **[[domain-name|domain name]]** is a human-readable naming system for internet resources. It is easier to remember a site name than a string of numerical network addresses. A website's brand, visual appearance, or logo alone does not establish that the domain is genuine. You should learn to identify the correct domain before signing in or transmitting personal information.

### Step 2: The name is associated with a network destination

Computers use **[[ip-address|IP addresses]]** for identifying network destinations and directing traffic. A naming service called the **[[dns|Domain Name System]]** (DNS) helps translate names into information needed to reach the intended service. It is reasonable to imagine DNS as a directory that helps locate a destination, but real DNS is a distributed system with multiple records, caches, and lookup stages. The browser or operating system may already have relevant information available, so a fresh lookup is not required on every single visit.

The beginner takeaway is simple: when a name cannot be resolved, a website might fail to load even though the local Wi-Fi link is present. Conversely, a successful name lookup does not guarantee that the website's server is responsive or that the website is trustworthy.

### Step 3: The request travels across networks

Your device, acting as a **[[client|client]]**, communicates with an online **[[server|server]]** or distributed service. A server is a computer system that provides content or a function to other devices. Traffic moves through intermediary equipment such as routers and across connected networks. A [[packet|packet]] is a manageable unit of data that can be routed or transmitted as part of the communication.

One web page may depend on information from several servers: the main page, photographs, fonts, videos, or other resources. This means a page can partly appear even if some supporting resources do not load. A video that fails on an otherwise functioning page is not proof that the entire internet has stopped.

### Step 4: The site responds and the browser displays content

A web service sends the relevant data back. Browsers interpret web documents and resources to display headings, paragraphs, links, images, forms, and interactive features. Some content is loaded or changed after the main page appears. The display you see is an interface assembled from received data and browser processing—not the server itself physically appearing on your screen.

If a request fails, the browser may report an address, connection, certificate, permission, or server problem. The exact error wording differs across browsers. Reading the wording matters because “site not found,” “you need to sign in,” and “no internet” describe different possible problems.

### HTTP, HTTPS, and what the padlock does not prove

Two important labels are **[[http|HTTP]]**, a protocol used in web communications, and **[[https|HTTPS]]**, which uses encryption to protect the connection between the browser and the web service at the relevant endpoints. **[[encryption|Encryption]]** helps prevent outsiders along the connection from reading or altering transmitted content in ordinary circumstances. It does not make a website's statements accurate, ensure the seller is legitimate, or guarantee that the service treats your personal data responsibly.

For example, an imitation shopping website can use HTTPS. The connection may be encrypted while the business itself is fraudulent. You still need to check the domain, who operates the website, what it asks for, and whether the request is expected. The Federal Trade Commission explicitly warns that HTTPS does not establish a site's legitimacy. This deeper verification will return in **Judging Search Results** and **Safe Web Browsing**.

### A simplified journey you can describe aloud

`Known web address → browser interprets it → name/address lookup if needed → network request → website service → response → displayed page`

This sequence is a teaching model, not a promise about one fixed technical route. Some steps are cached, some repeat, and modern websites may load multiple resources concurrently. The basic story remains useful: **you request; connected systems locate and deliver; your device displays**.

### What do upload and download mean here?

**[[download|Downloading]]** refers generally to receiving data from another service or system into your device or chosen location. When you load a webpage, the browser receives web resources, but that does not mean you have deliberately saved a normal document in your Downloads folder. A separate **Download** or **Save** action may be needed to keep a file where you can find it later.

**[[upload|Uploading]]** refers to sending data from your device toward another system, such as submitting a file to a school portal. It can disclose the file to the receiving service, so you should know the destination and permissions before doing it. These tasks receive their own later lesson, **Downloading and Uploading Files**. For now, remember the direction of travel: receiving from the service versus sending to it.

### Worked example: signing in to a school portal

Imagine a learner entering a known school portal address. The browser connects to the service; the learner sees a sign-in page. The network connection working does not prove that the learner is entitled to use the portal. A valid account and suitable [[authentication|authentication]] method may still be required. If the learner enters the wrong password, that is primarily an account-access problem, not evidence that Wi-Fi has failed. If the service reports a scheduled maintenance outage, changing a password or rebooting the computer will not repair the server.

| Observation | A reasonable initial interpretation | What it does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Other websites work, one page fails | The individual service or URL may be affected | That the global internet is down |
| Browser displays a sign-in form | The service is asking for account access | That the website is authentic or the login is appropriate |
| Wi-Fi icon is visible | The device has some wireless-network status | That every remote server is reachable |
| Page displays from browser cache | Some resources may be saved locally | That the latest version is online and reachable |
| Browser warns about a certificate | Connection identity or security validation has a problem | That bypassing the warning is safe |
| HTTPS appears on a site | A protected connection is being negotiated or used | That the site's claims, products, and operators are honest |

> **Security rule:** If the browser shows a certificate warning on a site requesting a password or payment, do not bypass it as classroom practice. Stop and use the organization's known contact channel or seek authorized support.

## 5. What do people use the internet for?

The internet is valuable because it supports many everyday services, not just web browsing. Knowing which service you are using helps you choose the appropriate application, understand where information may be kept, and recognize when an account or connection is involved.

### Finding information and learning

Websites publish encyclopedias, course materials, public notices, schedules, reference tables, and news. A library may offer a searchable catalog. Schools can provide lesson dashboards, assignments, and discussion tools. Finding a page is only the beginning: people must still consider the source, publication date, evidence, and whether a page is an advertisement or official resource. Online information can be useful, outdated, incomplete, or deliberately false.

A person can also learn with material saved locally. A PDF downloaded beforehand may remain available [[offline|offline]]. In contrast, an online classroom's newest assignment, grade, or announcement may require an active connection and appropriate account access. When your connection fails, first ask whether the task truly requires live information.

### Communicating with others

**[[email|Email]]** sends electronic messages between addresses using internet-related services. Messaging applications exchange short notes, pictures, and other information. **[[video-call|Video calls]]** connect people through audio and often video. These services may be used through browsers, desktop applications, or mobile apps. They may require different accounts even when used on the same device.

Sending a message is not the same as knowing it was delivered or read. Apps may show states such as *Sending*, *Sent*, *Delivered*, *Read*, or *Failed*, but their meanings vary. A disconnected phone may let you draft a message and queue it until a connection returns. Do not treat a message in an outbox as proof the recipient received it.

### Entertainment and streaming

**[[streaming|Streaming]]** delivers media as it is played instead of requiring you to save the entire file first. Videos, music, live broadcasts, and internet radio are examples. A stream may briefly buffer when the connection fluctuates. Some services allow downloads for offline access, but their rules may restrict devices, accounts, availability periods, or copying. Streaming a movie generally uses much more data than reading a text-only article; exact amounts depend on quality, duration, and platform.

Online games may also need a reliable connection, but their needs differ from video streaming. Some games are sensitive to delays even when the total quantity of data is modest. This is one reason that the word “fast” alone cannot describe every aspect of a connection.

### Work, government services, and daily administration

People use the internet to complete job applications, access bank accounts, review public service information, fill out online forms, book appointments, collaborate on documents, and participate in professional meetings. These activities can be convenient but also involve sensitive information and responsibility. A service should be approached through a known official address or another verified channel, especially when identity, money, or documents are involved.

An online form may contain both required and optional fields. Seeing a form is not a reason to submit real personal data for practice. This course teaches forms, accounts, and safe browsing in later lessons. The present lesson is limited to recognizing the kind of service and understanding what is exchanged.

### Cloud services and information stored elsewhere

A **[[cloud-service|cloud service]]** uses provider-managed computing resources reached through a network. A cloud storage account may hold documents on remote systems and make them available across several devices. A web email service may keep messages on remote systems and display them after sign-in. The word *cloud* does not mean data is outside physical reality; it is stored and processed in facilities operated by organizations.

An **[[account|account]]** associates access and information with an identity recognized by the service. A person may have a personal account, school account, and work account with different permissions. Being connected to the internet does not make all three accounts interchangeable. It also does not mean every service has permission to access every file or photo on the device.

### Devices and online services: what depends on the connection?

| Everyday task | Usually needs live internet? | Possible offline alternative or qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Read a current website or news update | Yes | Previously saved material may be outdated |
| Write a document in a local editor | No | Save locally; cloud sync may wait |
| Send a new email message | Usually yes | Draft may wait in an outbox, depending on app |
| Join a live video meeting | Yes | No equivalent live participation without a connection |
| Play downloaded music | Not necessarily | Availability depends on app license and saved content |
| Print a file to a local network printer | Not necessarily | A working local network may be sufficient |
| Access a cloud-only file | Usually yes | Some services support designated offline copies |
| Read a PDF saved on the device | No | Provided the device and reader can open it |
| Search a live online catalog | Yes | A static printed catalog is not an up-to-date substitute |

### A day-in-the-life example

Morning: a student opens an online weather forecast and receives live data. Later, the student drafts class notes in a document editor without needing internet access. At noon, the student submits an assignment to a school portal, which requires connectivity and approved credentials. In the evening, the student reads a previously saved textbook chapter without an internet connection. **Four computing activities do not all have the same connectivity requirements.** Recognizing that difference can help prioritize tasks when a connection is slow, unavailable, or costly.

### Digital access is not identical for everyone

Some communities have limited infrastructure, higher service costs, weak cellular coverage, equipment shortages, accessibility barriers, or restrictive data plans. Others have abundant fixed broadband, free institutional Wi-Fi, and several available providers. A learner using a public computer or shared phone may need special attention to privacy, time limits, and sign-out practices. Instruction should not assume every student has high-speed home internet or an unlimited mobile plan.

A [[digital-divide|digital divide]] describes disparities in access to, and effective use of, digital technologies. Learning to use the internet includes making practical choices under real limitations—not merely memorizing the names of devices. If access is limited, offline worksheets, authorized library services, and pre-downloaded material can provide meaningful alternatives.

## 6. How can you recognize connection quality, access, and basic safety?

A connection can be available but unreliable. A browser may be working while one website is unavailable. A device may show a strong wireless signal even when no internet traffic is getting through. Instead of calling all problems “the internet being broken,” gather a small set of observations and identify which layer is most likely involved.

### Connection status: what the icons can tell you

Windows 11, macOS, ChromeOS, iPhone, iPad, and Android present network status in different menus. You might see a Wi-Fi symbol, an Ethernet indicator, mobile signal bars, an airplane icon, or words such as *Connected*, *No internet*, *Limited*, or *Offline*. These indicators are clues, not a full technical diagnosis. A wireless signal reflects the local radio link; it does not confirm that all internet services can be reached.

An **[[offline|offline]]** condition means the device or activity is not presently communicating through the needed network connection. Some features can remain usable, such as reading a stored document. **[[airplane-mode|Airplane mode]]** is a device setting that changes wireless radio behavior; the exact controls and exceptions vary. Do not use it as a generic solution without understanding the device and the consequences, particularly on a shared or managed machine.

A device can also show an online status that has not updated yet. If something seems inconsistent, look at the actual task's error message and the network status together. Be cautious about webpages that claim “your connection is infected” and demand that you call a number or install a program. A website is not an authoritative device diagnostic simply because it displays a frightening message.

### Speed, bandwidth, delay, and reliability

**[[bandwidth|Bandwidth]]** is a measure of how much data a connection can carry in a given interval, often expressed as bits per second. An internet plan may advertise a maximum or typical **[[download-speed|download speed]]** and **[[upload-speed|upload speed]]**. Download speed influences receiving large files and streaming; upload speed influences sending files or video to others. Real-world results depend on the service plan, device, network conditions, remote server, and other activity.

**[[latency|Latency]]** is the delay involved in sending information and receiving a response. Video meetings and interactive games may feel sluggish when latency is high, even if a speed test reports respectable bandwidth. **[[reliability|Reliability]]** concerns how consistently the connection remains usable. A service that drops every few minutes may be unsuitable for a live class even if it sometimes downloads files quickly.

A **[[signal-strength|signal strength]]** indicator concerns the quality of the local wireless link. A strong Wi-Fi signal does not ensure excellent throughput, low latency, a working DNS service, or an accessible website. Congestion may occur when many devices or users compete for available capacity. Different kinds of problems need different explanations.

| Symptom | What you may reasonably note | What to avoid assuming |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong Wi-Fi bars but websites fail | Local wireless signal seems present | ISP and remote websites must be working |
| One online class page fails, other pages work | Could involve site, address, account, or permissions | The entire internet is unavailable |
| Video meeting repeatedly freezes | Connection quality or device performance may be affected | Buying a new computer is always necessary |
| Download finishes slowly | Size, plan, server, congestion, or device could contribute | The advertised plan speed applies to every transfer |
| Email says message is pending | Delivery may not be complete | The recipient already has the message |
| Mobile connection works but Wi-Fi does not | Different access paths have different conditions | Changing every router setting is appropriate |
| Device reports No internet | The system cannot confirm required connectivity | Saved offline files have been deleted |

### Data usage and cost

A **[[data-cap|data cap]]** or allowance is a service-plan limit on the quantity of data used in a period, or a threshold after which pricing or performance may change. On cellular connections, streaming high-resolution video, downloading large files, installing updates, and using a phone hotspot can consume significant data. Some public Wi-Fi services impose their own time or usage limits. **Do not turn on a paid service or test large downloads for this lesson.**

A student's best option may depend on whether the task requires live video, a small text submission, or reading a saved document. Choosing a lower-data method or postponing a large download until an authorized unmetered connection is available can reduce unnecessary costs. A plan's published terms, not the mere presence of a network symbol, determine charges.

### Privacy and security are separate considerations

**[[privacy|Privacy]]** concerns how information about you is collected, used, shared, and retained. **[[security|Security]]** concerns protecting information and systems from inappropriate access or manipulation. A service can encrypt a connection and still collect data about user activity. A connection can be technically available yet unsafe to use for a particular request.

**[[personal-data|Personal data]]** may include a name, contact information, location, school records, account credentials, or financial details. A service may request some information for a legitimate task, but that does not mean every request is justified. An app's **[[permissions|permissions]]**—such as access to the camera, microphone, location, or files—can affect what it can collect or use. These choices deserve attention, particularly on shared devices or while handling school and employer records.

**[[phishing|Phishing]]** is a deceptive attempt to obtain sensitive information or induce harmful actions by pretending to be a trusted source. Messages that urgently demand payment, password confirmation, or account recovery can be attempts to deceive. Do not use links or phone numbers supplied in a suspicious message to verify that same message; use an independently known official route. A realistic logo, an advertisement, or an HTTPS connection alone cannot establish legitimacy.

For online accounts, a long unique password and **[[mfa|multifactor authentication]]** can improve security when supported. MFA uses an additional form of verification beyond a password, though exact methods vary. **Never reveal passwords or one-time authentication codes in a classroom demonstration.** You will study these practices more fully in **Online Accounts and Logins** and **Safe Web Browsing**.

### Basic troubleshooting starts with observation

A **[[troubleshooting|troubleshooting]]** process means identifying a problem through reliable observations before choosing an action. At a beginner level, you can gather useful evidence without changing any settings:

1. Identify the device and the specific activity that failed. Is it one web page, all pages, an email message, or a video call?
2. Read the exact on-screen status or error message. Do not substitute your own guess for what the device reports.
3. Observe the available connection method—Wi-Fi, Ethernet, or mobile data—and note any reported *No internet* or *Offline* status.
4. Ask whether other **already trusted** services on that device are functioning, but do not visit unfamiliar websites just to test it.
5. Consider whether the activity requires a particular school or work account, permission, subscription, or current online service.
6. On a shared or managed device, report the observation to the responsible person. On a personal device, consult the provider or device's official support information before making changes.

This is an **observation sequence**, not an instruction to restart routers, reset settings, disconnect other users, disable security tools, or bypass a network restriction. A network administrator or household owner may be managing systems that other people depend on. Repeatedly entering passwords, installing “repair” tools from advertisements, or changing security settings can create new problems.

### Accessibility and inclusive use

The internet is often presented through small icons, complex menus, rapid videos, and time-limited forms. **[[accessibility|Accessibility]]** means enabling people with different access needs to understand and use technology. Captions, screen readers, larger text, keyboard navigation, transcripts, and accessible document formats can help. These supports are not dependent on a learner proving a disability, and students should not have to disclose private circumstances to practice.

A person who has difficulty reading network icons can use available text descriptions or an accessible system status view. A person who cannot join a live video due to bandwidth limitations may benefit from a transcript or a file provided in advance, when the course allows it. **The goal is successful access to information and participation, not conformity to one device or one input method.**

> **Five-point internet habit:** Identify the **device**, the **connection path**, the **service**, the **account or permission requirement**, and any **privacy or cost consequence** before you treat an online action as routine.

## 7. Guided practice: Build your internet mental model

These activities reinforce recognition before action. They are intentionally **[[observation-first|observation-first]]**: you identify roles, compare examples, and read available status information. If you use a real device, observe only your own device or one you are authorized to use. **Do not change Wi-Fi networks, turn cellular data on, enter login details, run speed tests, restart equipment, or visit unknown sites as part of the required lesson.** A paper-only route is provided for every activity.

Use a notebook or the space provided by your instructor. You can compare your responses with the sample explanations under each exercise. There is no need to share the identity of your home network, your exact location, your account details, or your internet provider in a public discussion.

### Activity 1: Name the five roles in a connection

**Goal:** Recognize the difference between a device, local network, provider, internet, and online service.

**Scenario:** Maya opens a university webpage on a laptop at home. Her laptop uses the home's Wi-Fi, the home network reaches an ISP, and the website is delivered from systems operated for the university.

**Procedure:**

1. Write the **device** that Maya directly controls.
2. Identify the **local connection** used by that device.
3. Name the kind of organization that provides access to the broader internet.
4. Identify the **online service** she is trying to use.
5. Draw five boxes connected by arrows, then label them: device, local connection, provider, internet, and website service.
6. Say aloud why a problem with the website's server is not necessarily a problem with the Wi-Fi signal.

**Model answer:** Device = laptop; local connection = home Wi-Fi and the home's network equipment; provider = ISP; internet = interconnected networks; destination service = university website. The wireless link can work normally even while the website's own server is unavailable. The five-box illustration is a logical teaching model, not a map of all physical routers.

**Repetition prompt:** Change the example to a phone using a cellular connection. Which boxes change? The device is now the phone, and the local connection and provider path involve the cellular carrier. The broad internet and the online service roles are still present.

### Activity 2: Sort internet, web, browser, search, and Wi-Fi

**Goal:** Distinguish different technologies rather than calling each of them “the internet.”

Copy the descriptions into your notes and match each one to **internet**, **World Wide Web**, **browser**, **search engine**, or **Wi-Fi**.

| Description | Your classification |
| --- | --- |
| A. A program that opens and displays web pages | __________________ |
| B. A wireless method for reaching a nearby network | __________________ |
| C. A worldwide system of interconnected networks | __________________ |
| D. A service that lists possible answers to a typed query | __________________ |
| E. The linked online pages and web applications you can visit | __________________ |

**Model answer:** A = browser; B = Wi-Fi; C = internet; D = search engine; E = World Wide Web. Each term has a different job, even when you encounter all five during one short activity.

**Extend the exercise:** Suppose someone sends an email through a dedicated phone app. It uses the internet, but you should not automatically describe the email as a web page merely because it travels across the same global network.

### Activity 3: Interpret connection symbols without guessing

**Goal:** Tell apart a local connection, confirmed service access, and an unresolved condition.

**Scenario A:** A laptop shows a strong Wi-Fi symbol. The browser says a school website cannot be reached.

**Scenario B:** A phone shows cellular signal and the weather app says *Last updated yesterday*.

**Scenario C:** A desktop uses Ethernet and can print to a nearby office printer, but an external website will not load.

**Scenario D:** A student reads a downloaded lesson PDF while the computer shows *Offline*.

For each scenario, write one thing you **know** and one thing you **do not yet know**. Do not change the settings to “fix” an example.

| Scenario | Supported observation | Unresolved question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Device reports a Wi-Fi connection | Is the ISP working? Is the site available? |
| B | App shows a saved forecast or old update | Can it fetch fresh data now? |
| C | The local printing path is available | Is the internet route working? |
| D | A local document is readable | Is the live online course reachable? |

**Model explanation:** A visible icon or a single working activity is evidence about that *particular* connection or service, not proof that the internet is fully working or fully broken. In Scenario D, offline reading can be productive because the relevant file is already stored locally.

### Activity 4: Follow a website request from beginning to end

**Goal:** Describe a complete page request without advanced network terminology.

Write these six items on separate lines, then put them in a logical order: **browser displays page**, **a person chooses a known address**, **online service sends a response**, **network request travels**, **browser interprets address**, **name or address information is obtained if needed**.

**Model order:**

1. A person chooses a known address.
2. The browser interprets the address.
3. Name or address information is obtained if needed.
4. The network request travels toward the online service.
5. The service sends a response.
6. The browser displays the page or an appropriate error.

**Think further:** Does the browser have to obtain a brand-new DNS answer on every visit? No. Relevant information might be cached. Could the page still fail at the final stage? Yes. The service might be unavailable, access might be denied, or a supporting resource might fail.

**Accessibility alternative:** Describe the sequence orally or with numbered cards. The learning goal is understanding the order, not having to draw a complicated diagram.

### Activity 5: Identify what needs live internet access

**Goal:** Choose realistic approaches when connectivity is limited or costly.

A student has access to a saved textbook PDF, a document editor, an online assignment portal, a live video class, and a local printer. The internet connection is unavailable for one hour. Decide what work can likely continue and what should wait.

| Proposed task | Continue, wait, or check? | Why? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Read the saved PDF | Continue | A locally saved readable copy does not normally need live internet |
| Draft an essay in a local word processor | Continue, then verify saving | Local editing can work; cloud sync may be deferred |
| Submit a new file to the class portal | Wait or use an authorized alternative | Submission ordinarily requires a functioning service and connection |
| Participate in a live video class | Wait or contact instructor through an approved method | Real-time participation depends on a live connection |
| Print to an available local printer | Check local connection | Some printers work over the local network or USB without internet |

**Model conclusion:** “Offline” does not mean “nothing can be done.” It means the learner should separate local tasks from services requiring live communication. Do not attempt to bypass school submission rules, use someone else's account, or incur mobile data charges without consent.

**Repeat with another scenario:** Suppose the internet works, but the school portal reports scheduled maintenance. The student may still research trusted sources and draft the assignment. The portal's unavailability does not imply the rest of the internet is down.

### Activity 6: Read a safe-use situation before acting

**Goal:** Distinguish connection availability from website trustworthiness and permissions.

**Scenario:** A public Wi-Fi network is available at a community center. A pop-up in a browser claims the device is infected and asks for a phone call and payment. Another tab contains a login page for an online service. A student needs to read a public class schedule.

**Decision sequence:**

1. Is the pop-up proof that the device is infected? **No.** A webpage can display an alarming message without performing a reliable diagnosis.
2. Should the student call the number or enter payment details? **No.** The student should close or leave the suspicious content using normal browser controls if safe, and seek trusted support if concerned.
3. Does the public Wi-Fi name prove the network is authorized? **No.** Verify the network with the community center's official instructions or staff.
4. Does the existence of a login page prove the service is legitimate? **No.** Check the correct organization and address independently before using credentials.
5. Does reading a public schedule normally require submitting private data? **Not necessarily.** If unexpected personal information is requested, stop and verify why it is necessary.

**Model conclusion:** An internet connection is a communication route, not a seal of approval for every message or website that appears through it. A cautious pause before entering information is a skill, not an inability to use technology.

### Optional: observe an existing network status on your own device

You may inspect the **status already visible** on a device you own or are permitted to use. Do not make any changes.

- **Windows 11:** Look at the network area on the taskbar or the available network status view. You may see Wi-Fi, Ethernet, or a system statement about connectivity. Interface details differ with device hardware and Windows updates.
- **Mac:** If available, inspect the Wi-Fi symbol in the menu bar or Control Center and read any displayed status. A connected Wi-Fi name is not proof the entire internet is functioning.
- **Chromebook:** Inspect the connection section in Quick Settings; distinguish Wi-Fi or cellular status from individual website access.
- **iPhone or iPad:** Observe the status indicators and, if already displayed, the selected Wi-Fi or cellular connection. Do not enable cellular data or hotspot functions to complete this exercise.
- **Android:** Observe the existing Wi-Fi or cellular indicators; names and menu layout vary by device manufacturer and Android version.

**Paper-only alternative:** Use Scenario A from Activity 3 and write: *“I observe a strong Wi-Fi symbol. I cannot yet conclude the ISP or the website is functioning.”* That single sentence demonstrates the same understanding without a device.

### Practice reflection and evidence of learning

Complete these five statements in your own words:

1. The internet is a worldwide system of **__________**.
2. Wi-Fi connects a device to a **__________**, not automatically to every website.
3. A browser is a **__________** that displays web content.
4. One failed website is not enough to conclude **__________**.
5. Before entering account details, I should verify **__________**.

**Suggested answers:** interconnected networks; nearby wireless network; program or application; that the entire internet has failed; the correct website and the legitimacy of the request. Other accurate wording is acceptable. Try answering again without looking at the lesson. Repetition is useful when it improves understanding, not when it merely copies words.

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

### “The internet is the same thing as Wi-Fi.”

Wi-Fi is a wireless local connection technology. The internet is the much larger global network system. A device can join Wi-Fi while the provider's internet connection is down. A phone can also use the internet through cellular service while Wi-Fi is off.

### “The internet is the same thing as the World Wide Web.”

The web consists of linked online content and applications reached through web technologies. Email, many online games, and other services also use the internet. The web is a major internet service, not the entirety of internet communication.

### “A search engine, a browser, and a website are interchangeable.”

A browser displays web content; a search engine helps locate resources; a website provides particular content or functions. Some browser address bars also submit search queries. That combined convenience does not turn the browser itself into the global internet.

### “All internet data travels through satellites.”

Global internet communication relies on a mixture of technologies, including terrestrial and undersea cables, wireless radio systems, and satellites. The proportion and route depend on the connection. A cable network and a satellite internet service both connect into the larger internet.

### “A strong Wi-Fi symbol means every website should work.”

It may indicate a strong local wireless signal, but a provider outage, DNS failure, account restriction, server problem, or other condition can still affect access. Ask which part of the activity is actually failing.

### “If one website fails, the whole internet has gone down.”

A site can be unavailable independently of other services. A browser may also be showing an incorrect address, sign-in restriction, or security warning. Record the specific error before drawing a conclusion.

### “A page appearing means the information is current.”

A browser or app may display cached information. Look for publication dates, refresh states, and appropriate source evidence when currency matters. A page that was saved yesterday might remain visible today while offline.

### “HTTPS means the business or information is trustworthy.”

HTTPS protects the communication channel under normal circumstances. Fraudulent websites can also use HTTPS. Confirm the domain, organization, and context, especially before signing in or submitting payment or identity information.

### “I must download a file every time I view a web page.”

A browser receives and processes page resources during viewing, but that does not necessarily create a deliberately saved document in Downloads. A separate Download, Export, or Save action may be required when you intend to keep a usable file.

### “Opening a local document always requires internet access.”

Many files and programs work offline. Remote files, cloud synchronization, app licensing rules, or collaboration features can add connection requirements, but local work and live online access are different concepts.

### “A public network is safe simply because it is free.”

A public network can be useful, but its name can be imitated and its privacy policies or security settings may differ from a home network. Verify the legitimate network, use trusted services, and avoid exposing sensitive information on unverified pages.

### “Using mobile data is always free once a phone has signal.”

A visible cellular signal does not establish plan coverage, authorization, pricing, roaming terms, or data allowances. Do not assume a phone hotspot or a video stream is free. Review the applicable plan through the carrier's legitimate channels.

### “Every problem can be solved by restarting the router.”

The problem might be an individual website, an account restriction, a network maintenance window, or a device-specific condition. Restarting shared network equipment can interrupt other users, and a beginner should not change managed equipment. Start with observation and authorized support.

### “A website that looks official must belong to the organization.”

Designs and logos can be copied. A similar-looking domain or a paid search advertisement may lead to an unrelated or fraudulent website. Use known official links, confirm the domain, and exercise care with unexpected requests.

### “The cloud has no physical location or owner.”

Cloud services depend on real servers, storage, network connections, facilities, and operators. The service provider's account, availability, permissions, privacy rules, and terms affect how information is stored and accessed.

### “Beginners must master every networking detail before using the internet.”

Practical competence begins with clear distinctions and reliable habits. It is enough at this level to know what the internet connects, the general role of devices and providers, how web access works, and why connectivity differs from security or trustworthy content. Later MGU materials provide the deeper engineering.

> **Troubleshooting principle:** Name the observed symptom, identify the relevant connection or service, and confirm who is authorized to change it. Do not solve uncertainty by installing unknown programs, bypassing warnings, or entering credentials into unfamiliar pages.


## 9. Essential vocabulary

The dotted-underlined terms in earlier sections open definitions in the MGU lesson dashboard. Where a verified MGU Encyclopedia or textbook reference is available, the tooltip may also lead to additional reading. For study without an interactive dashboard, this plain-text vocabulary list is complete. Practice describing the ideas in your own words rather than memorizing technical labels alone.

- **Internet:** Worldwide system of interconnected networks that exchange information using compatible communication protocols.
- **Network:** Two or more connected devices or systems able to exchange information.
- **World Wide Web:** System of interconnected online documents, resources, and web applications that operate using internet technologies.
- **Wi-Fi:** Wireless technology that connects nearby devices to a local network.
- **Web browser:** Application for viewing and interacting with web pages and many web applications.
- **Device:** Computer, phone, tablet, or other equipment able to process information and sometimes connect to networks.
- **Local network:** Network serving a limited area such as a home, classroom, or office.
- **Internet infrastructure:** Physical and logical equipment, links, facilities, and systems that support internet communication.
- **Protocol:** Agreed technical rules that enable computer systems to communicate.
- **Global network:** A network arrangement spanning many geographic areas and independently operated systems.
- **Data:** Information represented in a form computer systems can process or exchange.
- **Packet:** A portion of data organized for transmission across networks.
- **Web page:** One document or resource displayed through web technologies.
- **Website:** Collection of related online pages or services associated with a web address.
- **Hyperlink:** Selectable connection leading to another web resource or location.
- **Address bar:** Browser area where a user can enter a URL or sometimes a search request.
- **URL:** Web address that specifies a resource or location, often including a scheme, domain, and path.
- **Search engine:** Online service that helps users locate information in response to words or queries.
- **Advertisement:** Paid promotional content, including sponsored search listings.
- **Domain name:** Human-readable name used to locate internet services, such as example.org.
- **Internet service provider:** Organization providing internet connectivity to households, institutions, or other customers.
- **Modem:** Equipment that connects premises or customer systems to a provider access technology.
- **Router:** Equipment or software that directs data between networks.
- **Wireless access point:** Equipment that allows nearby devices to join a network by Wi-Fi.
- **Network name:** Name used to identify a Wi-Fi network, commonly called an SSID.
- **Network password:** Credential used to join some protected wireless networks.
- **Ethernet:** Common wired local-network technology using compatible network equipment and cables.
- **Mobile data:** Internet and data connectivity supplied through a cellular service plan.
- **Cellular network:** Radio communications system used by mobile carriers and compatible devices.
- **Hotspot:** Network-access service or device, often providing Wi-Fi to nearby devices.
- **Public Wi-Fi:** Wireless access offered in a public or shared environment.
- **Captive portal:** Web-based access or terms page some networks require before granting fuller connectivity.
- **Fiber-optic internet:** Internet service using fiber-optic communication cables for some or all of the provider access link.
- **Cable internet:** Internet access provided through cable-network infrastructure, typically including coaxial cable.
- **DSL:** Digital subscriber line; broadband method using suitable telephone copper wiring.
- **Fixed wireless:** Internet access to a fixed location through a wireless link.
- **Satellite internet:** Internet access using satellite links for part of the communication route.
- **Broadband:** Internet access generally offering substantial transmission capacity, with definitions varying by authority.
- **Web address:** Human-readable address entered into a browser to request an online resource.
- **IP address:** Network address used by internet protocols to identify interfaces or destinations and route data.
- **Domain Name System:** Distributed service that helps associate domain names with routing or other service information.
- **Client:** Device or program that requests resources or services from another system.
- **Server:** Computer system or program that provides resources or functions in response to requests.
- **HTTP:** Hypertext Transfer Protocol; a protocol for communication of web resources.
- **HTTPS:** Web communications protected using TLS encryption and appropriate authentication mechanisms.
- **Encryption:** Method of protecting information by encoding it so unauthorized observers cannot readily read it.
- **Download:** Transfer data from a remote source toward a receiving device or destination.
- **Upload:** Transfer data from a device toward a remote receiving system.
- **Authentication:** Process for verifying a claimed account or user identity.
- **Offline:** Not connected to, or not currently using, the network service needed for the activity.
- **Email:** System for exchanging electronic messages using electronic mail services.
- **Video call:** Live communication that can exchange sound and images between participants.
- **Streaming:** Delivery of digital media while it is being played, without requiring a complete saved file beforehand.
- **Cloud service:** Computing or storage service delivered through provider-managed systems over a network.
- **Account:** Service-managed identity and associated permissions or records.
- **Digital divide:** Inequalities in access to and effective use of digital technologies.
- **Airplane mode:** Device setting that controls wireless-radio behavior, with device-specific exceptions.
- **Bandwidth:** Capacity of a network connection to carry data during a period of time.
- **Download speed:** Rate at which data is received from a source over a connection.
- **Upload speed:** Rate at which data is sent from a device over a connection.
- **Latency:** Time delay involved in communication or receiving a response.
- **Reliability:** Consistency with which a network or service remains available and usable.
- **Signal strength:** Indication of the strength or quality of a local radio signal.
- **Data cap:** Usage allowance or threshold in a service plan that may affect charges or performance.
- **Privacy:** Control and practices related to collecting, using, retaining, and sharing information about people.
- **Security:** Protection of systems and information against unauthorized access, misuse, or alteration.
- **Personal data:** Information about an identified or identifiable individual.
- **Permissions:** Rules controlling what an account, device, app, or service may access or do.
- **Phishing:** Deception intended to obtain information or induce harmful acts by impersonating a trusted source.
- **Multifactor authentication:** Use of more than one category of evidence to verify account access.
- **Troubleshooting:** Systematic observation and testing to identify the cause of a problem.
- **Accessibility:** Making technology perceivable and usable by people with varied access needs.
- **Observation-first:** Learning method that starts by examining and identifying situations without changing settings or data.

**Study method:** First explain *internet, Wi-Fi, web, browser,* and *search engine* without looking at your notes. Next explain how a device, provider, and server cooperate. Finally, explain why *HTTPS* and a strong Wi-Fi signal do not prove that a website is trustworthy or reachable.
