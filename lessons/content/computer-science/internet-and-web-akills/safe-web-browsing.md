## Lesson 9: Safe Web Browsing

You open your web browser to check a library's opening hours. A search result appears above the library's normal listing and promises a special membership upgrade. A few minutes later, another page displays a frightening alert claiming that your computer is infected and giving a telephone number to call. Then an ordinary-looking website asks for permission to send notifications. What should you do? These situations involve different technologies, but they demand the same fundamental habit: **understand what a website is asking before you trust it, disclose information, or allow it to change your device**.

[[safe-browsing|Safe web browsing]] means using websites and online services with habits that reduce the chance of scams, harmful software, account compromise, privacy exposure, and avoidable mistakes. It is not a promise that nothing bad can ever happen online. A careful person can encounter a deceptive page, a genuine site can be compromised, and protective software can miss a new threat. Your practical advantage is to recognize warning signs, understand what you can verify, refuse unnecessary requests, and know where to find reliable help.

This is the final lesson in MGU's nine-part **Internet and Web Skills** sequence. Earlier lessons established how the internet works, how to navigate a browser, how to organize tabs and bookmarks, how to search and evaluate results, how to transfer files, how to complete forms, and how to manage accounts. Here you bring those skills together into a repeatable safety workflow. Microsoft Edge on Windows 11 is the principal desktop example; the principles also apply to Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Mac, Chromebook, iPhone, iPad, and Android. Screens and protective features can change, so learn the **purpose** of each control rather than a particular icon's exact location.

The required exercises are **observation-first** and can be done entirely from fictional records on paper. You will not be asked to visit a dangerous website, install software, disable protection, reveal personal information, open suspicious attachments, call an unfamiliar number, or experiment with a real account. A learner's ability to explain when to stop is an important outcome, not a failure to complete an activity.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 110–145 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Internet and Web Skills Lessons 1–8 recommended · **Practice equipment:** A computer, phone, tablet, or printed worksheet; live internet is optional · **Safety:** Do not intentionally visit suspicious addresses or interact with real scam messages for practice; use the fictional examples below

> **Essential idea:** Before you act online, use **STOP → INSPECT → VERIFY → DECIDE → CONFIRM**. Stop when a request is unexpected or consequential; inspect the actual address, message, and requested action; verify through an independently obtained trusted route; decide whether the action is necessary and authorized; and confirm what actually happened. If you cannot verify a request, doing nothing until you can obtain reliable help is a valid decision.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain safe browsing as a set of practical judgments and protective habits rather than an absolute guarantee.
1. Read a website's address closely enough to distinguish its actual domain from convincing-looking words elsewhere in a link.
1. Explain what HTTPS and a browser connection indicator can establish, and what they cannot establish about a site's honesty.
1. Recognize common warning signs of phishing, impersonation, fake technical support, malicious advertising, false urgency, and deceptive account messages.
1. Choose an independent verification method instead of following an unexpected link or trusting caller-supplied contact details.
1. Distinguish website content from browser and operating-system warnings, and respond carefully to suspicious pop-ups or unexpected prompts.
1. Explain risks associated with unfamiliar downloads, executable installers, extensions, notification permissions, and copied commands.
1. Protect account credentials, multifactor authentication requests, financial information, browsing privacy, and shared-device sessions.
1. Apply safer habits on home networks, public Wi-Fi, phones, tablets, and managed school or workplace devices.
1. Describe appropriate first steps when a suspicious page was merely seen, a secret was disclosed, a download occurred, or money may have been sent.
1. Complete six fictional scenarios and explain which evidence supports proceeding, pausing, verifying, reporting, or seeking assistance.

## 1. What does safe web browsing mean in everyday life?

To [[browse|browse]] the web is to use a browser to visit pages, follow links, read or watch content, interact with services, and sometimes submit information. Most people do this many times a day. They may look up a bus schedule, check a school calendar, compare prices, read a government notice, or sign in to a utility account. A [[browser|web browser]] such as Edge, Chrome, Firefox, or Safari serves as an interface between the person and websites, but it cannot decide every question of honesty, usefulness, or appropriateness on the person's behalf.

The goal of safe browsing is not to avoid the internet. It is to **make sensible decisions at the points where online activity creates risk**. Viewing an unfamiliar public page is not the same action as sending it your password. Reading a description of an app is not the same as installing the app. Seeing a shopping offer is not the same as paying. Those distinctions create opportunities to stop before a more consequential action.

### Five common kinds of online risk

Risks overlap, but sorting them by what can be harmed makes them easier to recognize.

| Risk | What could go wrong? | Ordinary example | Appropriate first habit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Phishing and impersonation** | A person or page tricks you into revealing secrets or authorizing an action | A fake library login page | Verify the service independently |
| **Malicious software** | Harmful code damages a device, steals information, or changes settings | An unexpected installer offered by a pop-up | Do not run unfamiliar downloads |
| **Privacy exposure** | Data about you is collected, shared, or displayed more broadly than intended | A form requests unnecessary personal details | Limit disclosure and review permissions |
| Financial deception | You lose money or payment details to a fraudulent request | A fake order-confirmation or support fee | Verify merchant and transaction through trusted channels |
| Misleading information | You make decisions using false, outdated, or misrepresented claims | A fabricated government announcement | Compare original and independent sources |

There is an important distinction between **risk** and **proof of danger**. A website that looks old-fashioned is not automatically malicious. A request for a mailing address may be appropriate in a genuine shipping workflow. Conversely, a modern design, professional logo, well-written message, or familiar brand name does not establish safety. The sensible question is: **Does this request make sense for the service I intended to use, and what independent evidence supports it?**

### A protective habit is more reliable than one memorized warning sign

Scams are not always full of spelling mistakes. Some suspicious pages use flawless language and copied design. Genuine services occasionally send urgent notices. A website may use a real encrypted connection and still misrepresent its purpose. Therefore, do not depend on any single visual detail, such as a padlock, a logo, a color, or a grammatical error. Use a sequence that considers **origin, destination, purpose, requested information, and consequences**.

Suppose a message says that a delivery cannot be completed until you pay a small fee. The message may mention a recognizable shipping company and your city. That does not establish that it came from the company or describes a real parcel. Instead of following its link, check an order or tracking number through the retailer's known account page or the shipping company's independently verified site. If you were not expecting a delivery, the request needs even stronger scrutiny.

### A five-stage decision process

**STOP:** Take a moment before entering credentials, downloading an attachment, approving a notification request, sending money, or installing an extension. This is especially important when the website pressures you to act immediately.

**INSPECT:** Identify where the request appeared and what it wants. Is it a website advertisement, the browser's built-in warning, a message from a stranger, or a service that you opened intentionally? Read the real destination address when practical; check the actual requested permission or payment.

**VERIFY:** Find the organization's known website through a trusted bookmark, previously saved official document, verified account application, or independently obtained contact number. Where appropriate, compare the claim with a second reliable source. Do not use a contact number supplied by the suspicious message itself as your only verification.

**DECIDE:** Ask whether you actually need the action, whether the destination is correct, and whether the request is proportionate. An unnecessary or unverified request can be declined. A clear warning is a reason to stop; bypassing a browser protection screen should not be an ordinary learning step.

**CONFIRM:** Observe the actual result of an intended action. A page may say *Request received*, *Download blocked*, *Access denied*, *Signed out*, or *Payment pending*. Do not substitute a guess for the reported status. When the outcome is unclear, verify through the recognized service before repeating a consequential action.

### The difference between visiting, interacting, and authorizing

Not every browser action has the same significance. Opening a public information page usually does not require credentials. A login asks you to prove account control. An upload can expose a private document to a remote service. Allowing a website to send notifications permits future messages to reach your device. Approving a multifactor prompt can give an account session access. Submitting a payment can move funds, and the consequences may not be reversible through the browser's Back button.

| Action | What the user is doing | Decision to make first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Read an ordinary article | Access public content | Is the information relevant and credible? |
| Follow a link | Navigate to another address | Where does the link actually lead? |
| Enter a password | Supply an authentication secret | Is this the correct service and sign-in flow? |
| Approve a security prompt | Authorize a sign-in or protected action | Did I initiate this exact request? |
| Download a program | Obtain software that may be runnable | Is it necessary, trusted, and approved for this device? |
| Install an extension | Grant a browser add-on potential access | Who made it, and what permissions does it request? |
| Allow notifications | Permit future browser messages | Does this website need to contact me this way? |
| Submit payment | Authorize a financial transaction | Are the seller, terms, amount, and recipient verified? |

### Your responsibility and the website's responsibility

Websites and software vendors have important obligations to protect systems and users. A beginner cannot inspect every server, audit every program, or prove every identity. You do not have to become a security engineer to use the web responsibly. Your practical role is to use supported tools, notice unusual requests, avoid circumventing warnings, and seek trusted assistance when a situation exceeds your knowledge or authority.

For a school or workplace account, local policies may govern which websites, software, extensions, or file transfers are permitted. A familiar website may still be inappropriate for confidential organizational records. **Technical ability to upload information is not permission to disclose it.** On a shared device, you also have a responsibility to respect other users' accounts and browsing data rather than exploring their history or saved passwords.

### A worked example: two websites with the same logo

Maya searches for the public library's website. Result A has the library's logo and says *Official Member Service — Limited Time*. Result B has a plainer description of library hours, a recognizable domain printed on the library's physical brochure, and a link to the library's ordinary services page. The copied logo in Result A is not evidence that it is genuine. Maya should use the known domain or another independently verified route to reach the service. She can still learn from public search results, but she should not enter login information or payment details until she knows she is on the intended site.

> **First operating rule:** Treat urgency, a familiar logo, and a polished layout as things you can observe—not as proof. Your confidence should come from a verified route, a legitimate purpose, and an appropriate request.

## 2. How do you inspect websites, links, and redirects?

A [[url|web address or URL]] identifies a resource on the web. Beginners often read addresses as if they were ordinary sentence fragments. That can be misleading because the part that identifies the website's registered domain may not be the first recognizable word that appears. Understanding a few structural elements makes it harder for a deceptive link to borrow another organization's name.

### Read the site address from the correct place

On a desktop browser, click or focus the [[address-bar|address bar]] to see the current address. On phones and tablets, the address bar may move between the top and bottom of the screen, or it may display a shortened address until activated. If the address is hidden, bring it into view before entering information. The browser's address bar belongs to the browser interface; a large text field drawn *inside a webpage* can be a website-created imitation and is not the same trustworthy reference for the current page's identity.

Consider the fictional URL `https://accounts.example.org/profile`.

- `https://` identifies the connection scheme, which requests an encrypted web connection.
- `accounts.` is a [[subdomain|subdomain]] within the example address.
- `example.org` is the main site domain in this illustration.
- `/profile` is a [[path|path]] that identifies a location or resource on that site.

The details differ among domains, including some multi-part country-code suffixes. A beginner need not memorize every suffix rule. The critical habit is to identify **which organization controls the actual destination**, not simply search the address for a familiar word.

### Familiar brand words can appear in misleading locations

Look at these **illustrative, non-operational examples**; they do not represent real login sites:

| Displayed address | What it actually suggests | Beginner's conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `https://accounts.example.org/profile` | A page under `example.org` | Possibly appropriate if `example.org` is independently known to be the intended service |
| `https://example.org.billing-check.test/login` | A page under `billing-check.test`; `example.org` appears earlier as subdomain text | Do not assume it belongs to `example.org` |
| `https://verify-example.test/secure` | A different domain with a familiar-looking word | Recognizable wording alone does not establish ownership |
| `http://example.org/help` | An unencrypted HTTP address | Avoid submitting secrets; look for the site's legitimate secure route |
| `https://example.org@check-session.test/login` | A URL whose actual host follows the `@` character | Do not trust the apparent brand before `@` |
| `https://example.org/reports` | A page beneath an example domain | The path alone does not establish the content is accurate or safe |

The `.test` suffix is reserved for test and illustration purposes. The rows are reading exercises, **not links to visit**. Real attackers may also use misspellings, additional words, hyphens, visually similar characters, or unexpectedly short links that hide the final destination. Do not experiment by opening suspect addresses to see what happens.

### HTTPS is important but not a certificate of honesty

[[https|HTTPS]] commonly encrypts communication between the browser and the addressed website, helping protect information in transit. The browser may show a connection or site-information indicator. However, HTTPS does not prove that the operator is legitimate, that a product is genuine, that a price is fair, that the site's statements are accurate, or that a requested download is safe. A fraudulent site can also obtain an HTTPS certificate.

An unencrypted login page is a strong reason not to enter credentials. An encrypted page is **not by itself** a sufficient reason to enter them. You still need to confirm the actual destination and purpose. If a browser reports a [[certificate-warning|certificate or connection warning]], do not routinely use an Advanced or Proceed Anyway option. For school or organizational resources, contact authorized IT staff through a known route if you believe a genuine site is misconfigured.

### Search results, sponsored results, and displayed addresses

A [[search-result|search result]] can contain a title, display text, website address, and description. Advertising entries may also appear. Search engines attempt to organize results, not issue a guarantee about every business or page. Being at the top of the results or appearing beside an official name does not establish that a listing is the official destination. **Sponsored** or **Ad** identifies promotion, not a security or authenticity review.

For public information, you can compare results and use source-evaluation methods from Lesson 5. For sensitive tasks, such as bank access, benefit applications, account recovery, or software installation, use a trustworthy saved bookmark, known official app, address from prior verified documentation, or other independent route when possible. A result that says *official* in its headline may simply be making a claim.

### Link text and actual destination are not always identical

An ordinary webpage link may display *Visit the library portal*, while its underlying [[link-destination|destination]] leads somewhere else. On many desktop browsers, hovering the pointer over a link can show a preview of the destination. On touch devices, interface behavior varies; long-pressing may offer a preview or context menu, but some gestures activate links or trigger site-specific actions. If you are unsure, avoid pressing the suspicious link and obtain the official destination another way.

A [[shortened-link|shortened link]] conceals some of the final destination, so it demands additional context. A [[qr-code|QR code]] can likewise encode a destination that is not visible in ordinary printed text. Before following a QR code, use the phone's preview if available, inspect the displayed address, and consider whether the code is in a trustworthy location and appears untampered with. A sticker placed over a legitimate parking-meter code is one example of why the physical setting alone is not enough.

### Redirects and unexpected sign-in pages

A [[redirect|redirect]] automatically moves a browser from one address to another. Redirects are common and can be legitimate: a website might send visitors from an older page to a replacement or from a public page to its proper sign-in provider. They can also be misused to conceal an unexpected destination. After a redirect, read the **current address**, especially before entering credentials or authorizing payment. Do not rely exclusively on the address you intended to open several steps earlier.

Some genuine services use a third-party [[identity-provider|identity provider]], such as an organization's recognized single-sign-on platform. The domain can legitimately change during that flow. Whether that change is expected depends on the service and its documented sign-in method. If an unfamiliar login appears, return to the known entry point and consult the service's official help rather than improvising.

### Worked example: the scholarship notice

A student receives an unsolicited message saying, *Your scholarship has been approved; use this portal now to confirm bank information*. The visible link text contains the school's name, but a preview shows a different domain with a hyphenated variation. The student should not supply banking details or attempt to test the link. Instead, they should open the school's known financial-aid website or contact its official office using contact information obtained independently. A legitimate financial-aid decision will have a verifiable institutional process; a message that demands secrets through an unexpected destination is not an appropriate shortcut.

> **Address habit:** Read the actual destination **before** entering a password, authorization code, payment card, or identity document. If a link and a trusted official route disagree, pause and verify; do not let a countdown timer decide for you.

## 3. How do you recognize scams and social engineering?

[[social-engineering|Social engineering]] is an attempt to influence people into performing actions that benefit an attacker. In web browsing, this may involve convincing you to share a password, approve a login, install software, pay a fabricated fee, provide remote access, or send information to a deceptive form. The attacker may imitate a company, government office, coworker, school, family member, or technical-support worker. A message can be delivered by email, text, advertisement, search result, phone call, social media, or a webpage.

### Phishing is not confined to email

[[phishing|Phishing]] broadly describes deception aimed at acquiring sensitive information or causing harmful actions under a false pretext. Variations may involve text messages, voice calls, QR codes, social-media messages, and cloned sites. A fake login page may look almost identical to a genuine one. The essential problem is not whether the message format is familiar; it is whether you can independently establish **who is requesting the action and why**.

A typical scam sequence might be: an unexpected message claims an account problem, provides a link to a lookalike sign-in page, asks for a password, and immediately demands a one-time code. The attacker may use those credentials to attempt entry into the real account. In some scams, an attacker already has part of the victim's information and uses it to sound convincing. Knowing your name or recent activity does not prove the sender's authority.

### Six common persuasion tactics

Watch for [[urgency|artificial urgency]], [[authority-spoofing|authority spoofing]], [[scarcity|manufactured scarcity]], [[fear-tactic|fear tactics]], [[reward-bait|reward bait]], and [[secrecy-demand|demands for secrecy]]. The table below shows how these patterns appear in ordinary messages and how to respond.

| Tactic | What it may sound like | Why it deserves caution | Better response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Artificial urgency** | “You have five minutes to prevent closure” | It discourages verification | Stop, use a known official channel |
| **Impersonated authority** | “I am from your bank's fraud department” | A title, number, or logo can be imitated | Contact the institution independently |
| **Manufactured scarcity** | “Only one guaranteed grant spot remains” | It pressures rushed decisions | Confirm offer, eligibility, and conditions |
| **Fear or alarming claims** | “Your device is infected; call immediately” | It may drive payment or remote-control access | Ignore page-provided support numbers; use trusted device tools |
| **Unexpected reward** | “Claim the prize after paying a processing fee” | The supposed reward motivates unsafe payment | Verify the program; refuse unexplained fees |
| **Secrecy or isolation** | “Do not tell anyone or the transaction will fail” | It prevents independent advice | Consult a trusted person or official organization |

One tactic is not always proof that every part of a message is false. Genuine deadlines exist. Schools, government agencies, and businesses have legitimate procedures. But an urgent demand to use an unverified link, provide a secret, move money, or install a tool is an excellent reason to stop until verification is complete.

### Technical-support impersonation and frightening pages

A common [[tech-support-scam|technical-support scam]] begins with a webpage saying that your device is locked, infected, or transmitting data. It might fill the screen, play sounds, display an imitation operating-system window, or provide a phone number allegedly belonging to Microsoft, Apple, or another vendor. A **webpage's own alert is not equivalent to a verified system diagnosis**. The FTC warns that authentic security alerts do not ask users to call a phone number supplied in a browser pop-up. A scammer may then request payment, remote-control software, or access to accounts.

If you encounter this type of page, do not call the displayed number, enter payment details, grant remote access, or download the suggested “repair” software. You can attempt to close the browser tab using normal browser controls. If the page prevents ordinary operation, use an appropriate system-supported method or seek trusted technical assistance; do not follow commands displayed by the suspect page. After leaving the page, consult your device's legitimate security software or official support channel if you have a reason to suspect an actual compromise.

### Fake verification steps and “prove you are human” traps

A [[captcha|CAPTCHA]] is a type of challenge used by some sites to distinguish automated traffic from people, though its design varies. A real-looking challenge can also be copied by scammers. A particularly dangerous pattern asks you to **open an operating-system command tool, paste text from the website, and run it** as a supposed verification step. This is not a normal beginner browsing requirement. The FTC issued a 2026 warning about deceptive CAPTCHA pages that use such tactics to induce malware execution.

For this lesson, the rule is simple: **Never paste and execute a command supplied by an untrusted page to prove you are human.** You do not need to learn how to run such commands to identify the problem. Leave the page, use a trusted destination, and report the suspicious prompt through an appropriate channel.

### Messages that exploit legitimate account security tools

Some sites send a [[verification-code|verification code]] or show a device [[mfa-prompt|multifactor authentication prompt]] during login. You learned in Lesson 8 that this is helpful only when linked to a sign-in you actually initiated. An attacker may falsely claim to be support staff and ask you to read out the code. Another may repeatedly trigger approval notifications and hope you approve one merely to stop the interruptions. Do not share codes or approve unexpected requests. Reach the actual account service independently, and use its official security-reporting workflow if unfamiliar prompts persist.

### Beware of “help” that moves you outside the normal process

A fraudulent seller might ask you to leave a platform's protected checkout system and pay through an unrelated account. A supposed employer might require buying equipment with a check that later proves invalid. A fake government service may demand unusual gift-card payment. A fake charity may use copied photographs and an emotional deadline. The details vary, but an **unexpected change of recipient, payment method, or verification process** deserves careful scrutiny.

Do not assume that a seller or service is safe simply because the page displays reviews, badges, stock photos, or testimonials. Reviews can be fabricated or copied. If buying something, examine the merchant's actual identity, return and refund terms, contact information, payment destination, and reputable independent information. If the transaction concerns a major amount or sensitive records, obtain appropriate help before authorizing it.

### Worked example: an unexpected account reset

A fictional user receives a text saying: *We detected suspicious activity in your school account. Tap here to reset your password immediately.* The user did not request a reset. The text contains a school-like logo and a short link. A second text says to reply with the security code sent to the phone. The correct first decision is to **avoid both links and code sharing**. The user can open the school's known portal through a bookmark or type the verified site address and inspect actual security notices. If a genuine account problem is found, the school's published recovery method or authorized IT office is the right next step.

> **Scam-recognition rule:** An unexpected request for your password, passkey approval, security code, remote-control access, software installation, or unusual payment requires independent verification—not faster compliance.

## 4. How do you handle downloads, pop-ups, and browser permissions?

Many online risks appear as requests to make something happen on your device. A website may offer a document, ask you to download an app, display an advertisement, request notifications, or ask to access your camera. Those are different actions and require different judgments. The safe question is not merely, *Can I click this?* It is, **What will be downloaded, installed, disclosed, changed, or authorized if I continue?**

### A web page is not the same as a program installed on your computer

Reading an article in the browser generally means viewing content delivered from a website. Selecting a [[download|download]] commonly transfers a file to your device or another selected destination. An [[executable-file|executable file]] or installer may be able to run software and make changes when executed and authorized. The fact that a file successfully downloads says nothing about whether running it would be appropriate or safe.

A document file can also contain risky content, such as malicious links or, in some formats, active features. A photograph or PDF is not automatically trustworthy simply because it is not an installer. Similarly, a compressed [[archive|archive]] can contain many items, including executable files. Beginner learners should inspect the expected filename, source, purpose, and file type before opening or running anything unfamiliar. **Renaming a file extension does not convert its actual content into a safe format.**

If you need software, start from the vendor's independently verified official website, your device's recognized app store, or an authorized school/work software catalog. Do not install it merely because a search advertisement or pop-up says it is required. If a trusted source issues a security warning about the download, stop rather than overriding the warning to complete the task. On a managed device, follow IT policy even when the program is legitimate.

### A step-by-step check before receiving a file

1. **Purpose:** What exact task requires this file? If you were only trying to read an article, an unexpected installer is probably unnecessary.
1. **Origin:** Did you reach the source through a known, verified route, or through an advertisement, forwarded message, or popup?
1. **Type:** Does the filename, extension, and browser description match what you expected? A worksheet intended as a PDF should not suddenly require an executable setup program.
1. **Warnings:** Does the browser or device block or flag it? Treat that warning seriously rather than looking for a bypass.
1. **Destination:** Where would the file go? Confirm the name and chosen folder rather than assuming it was saved in Documents.
1. **Next action:** Is downloading sufficient, or are you being asked to run, install, authorize, or upload something? Those additional steps deserve separate approval.

A browser may have a built-in download manager, accessible through a menu or familiar keyboard shortcut. The download list can help you see the reported status and, where supported, locate a file without opening it. Some files open in the browser instead of downloading, while others are saved automatically. The exact behavior depends on the browser, device, website, and settings; do not assume that every platform uses the same directory.

### Why fake download buttons are confusing

Some pages contain multiple large buttons labeled **Download**, sometimes as sponsored advertisements. An unfamiliar page may show a legitimate software link beside a deceptive promotion that tries to install an unrelated tool. The most visually prominent button is not necessarily the correct one. Read nearby context and the source website, inspect the destination before activating it, and avoid any unexpected additional installer or extension. A reputable download process should not need to disable your browser's security protections.

Suppose a student needs a PDF reading guide. The page presents **View PDF** in the article section and a bright **Install Fast PDF Opener** advertisement above it. The student can often read PDFs using the browser's built-in viewer or a school-approved application. The advertisement does not establish a need for the separate product. It may be deceptive even when it is not technically malicious. **A marketing message is not an instruction you must obey.**

### Distinguish advertisements, pop-ups, notifications, and system alerts

A [[pop-up|pop-up]] is a separate window, overlay, or page element that appears during browsing. Some legitimate sites use pop-ups for login help, accessibility notices, or confirmation messages. Others use them to pressure a user into calling an unfamiliar number, subscribing, downloading a tool, or paying. A [[browser-notification|browser notification]] is a different mechanism: after you grant a website permission, the browser or operating system may display future messages even when you are not looking at that site.

A website can draw a warning that *looks* like Windows or macOS. It cannot automatically make its own claims into a trusted diagnosis. By contrast, a browser's built-in protection feature may block navigation with a distinct warning interface. Because interfaces evolve and can be imitated, do not rely on color or icon alone. The safe default when uncertain is to avoid the proposed action and use the recognized browser or operating-system security center rather than the webpage's instructions.

| What appears | What it may be | What to check | Recommended beginner action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A webpage says “Virus detected — call now” | Fake security warning or scareware | Is it part of the page? Does it give a sales/support phone number? | Do not call or install; leave page and use trusted support |
| Browser says a site is deceptive or dangerous | Built-in reputation or phishing protection | Which browser feature issued it? | Do not bypass; use a trusted alternative route |
| Site asks “Allow notifications?” | Permission to send future messages | Do you really need messages from this site? | Deny or dismiss if unnecessary |
| Page asks to install a “required extension” | Request for browser add-on permissions | Who publishes it, what access is requested, and is it needed? | Decline unless verified and authorized |
| File appears in the downloads list | Transfer status, not safety verdict | Is it expected and from a trusted source? | Do not open unfamiliar or warned-about files |
| Device itself reports an operating-system update | Possible legitimate system update workflow | Is this in the device's recognized Settings or update service? | Follow official device-update instructions, not random links |
| Website asks to access your camera | Permission to use a hardware sensor | Is it needed for the service you intentionally started? | Grant only when legitimate and necessary |

### Website permissions are not minor decorations

A [[site-permission|site permission]] lets a browser-controlled website request access to a capability, such as notifications, approximate or precise location, camera, microphone, clipboard, or downloads, subject to device and browser rules. Permission screens can be legitimate. A video meeting may need access to a camera and microphone; a map may request location; a school notification service may offer optional reminders. However, permission is **not** proof the site is trustworthy, and the request itself does not establish that you must grant it.

Before granting a permission, ask four questions. **Which website is requesting it? What capability is involved? Is it necessary for the task? Can I continue by declining?** A news article usually does not need microphone access. A page offering a printed timetable usually does not need your precise location. A login page generally does not need permission to send notifications just to accept a password. If a site cannot explain a request, leave it unapproved and find another way to accomplish the task.

Permission choices can often be reviewed later in browser or device settings. The control name and location vary, and organizations may manage some settings. For this lesson, you do not need to grant permission or change settings. If you accidentally allowed a suspicious site's notifications, use the browser's **official site-permissions controls** or authorized support to revoke the permission. Avoid clicking notifications from the suspect site merely to investigate them.

### Browser extensions and add-ons

A [[browser-extension|browser extension]] is software that adds functionality to a browser. Some extensions provide useful accessibility features, password management, or productivity tools. Depending on their permissions, they may also be able to read or modify webpage content, view browsing-related information, or interact with sites. A popular-looking extension can still be compromised, misleading, or excessively intrusive; installation counts, ratings, and an attractive description are not complete security reviews.

Before installing an extension, identify the official publisher, the reason it is needed, its requested permissions, the organization's device policy if applicable, and whether the browser already provides the feature. Avoid installing an extension offered by an unexpected pop-up or a message claiming a virus must be fixed. If a browser or enterprise administrator has blocked an extension, don't attempt to bypass that decision. On a shared or managed computer, ask the person responsible for the device before changing extensions.

### Drive-by claims and the risk of overconfidence

Modern browsers include mechanisms intended to protect users from harmful websites and downloads. Nonetheless, protective systems cannot detect every new or disguised problem. You also should not assume that every visited page can infect a fully updated computer merely because it contains a suspicious graphic. Security risk varies with browser vulnerabilities, installed software, device condition, and user actions. The most useful beginner response is measured: **do not interact with the page's suspicious demands; keep supported software updated; and seek legitimate help if you observe an actual security problem**.

If you clicked an unusual link but did not enter data, install software, or approve permissions, first leave the site and inspect what actually occurred. Do not announce that your identity has been stolen merely because the page appeared. If something downloaded, that is a different question from whether it was executed. If a program ran or a secret was disclosed, the response becomes more urgent and specific; Section 6 explains why actions should match the incident.

### Worked example: the fake security update

A fictional page says *Your browser must be upgraded to view this image*. It offers a file labeled `image-update.exe` and tells the user to ignore any safety warning. The student did not visit the site to update software. The filename and requested action do not match the original image-viewing task. The site is making an unauthorized claim about the browser's maintenance. The safest response is to refuse the download and consult the browser's own recognized update mechanism or the device's approved software manager. Even if a genuine browser update is needed, the appropriate route is not an unexpected file offered by the image page.

> **File-and-permission habit:** Do not let an ordinary webpage promote itself into a software installer, support technician, or device administrator. Installation, execution, camera access, notifications, and remote-control permissions require a separate, informed decision.

## 5. How do you protect privacy, accounts, and payments online?

Safe browsing is not only about viruses. It also involves deciding what information you share, with whom, and under what conditions. A legitimate website may request information that is unnecessary for your purpose. A fraudulent site may collect data in the guise of an ordinary transaction. A shared computer may keep your session active after you close the browser. Privacy protection therefore combines careful disclosure with awareness of accounts, cookies, permission settings, and payment processes.

### Understand personal information by its consequences

[[personal-information|Personal information]] can include a name, address, email, phone number, photographs, dates, browsing history, location, account details, and information that becomes identifiable when combined. Some data may seem harmless by itself but reveal more when linked with other records. **Sensitive information** may include passwords, account recovery codes, financial credentials, identity numbers, private records, or documents that a service requires under a verified purpose and appropriate protections.

You do not need to refuse every request for information. A legitimate shipping service usually needs a delivery address; a class registration may need a contact method; a video call may require microphone access while you use it. Instead, use **data minimization**: provide only what is appropriate and necessary for the specific verified task. Avoid entering sensitive data merely to test a website, unlock a dubious “free” download, or participate in a survey that does not reasonably need it.

### Website forms can be sent to the wrong place

Lesson 7 introduced online forms and why clicking Submit differs from receiving a confirmation. Apply the same reasoning to safety. A page that asks you to upload a driver's license, bank statement, medical record, or school document should have a clear legitimate purpose and an independently verified service destination. A polished form or encrypted connection does not establish permission to request that information. If the requirement is unfamiliar, consult the organization's known help channel before proceeding.

Be careful with [[autofill|autofill]] and saved payment details. A browser may suggest older addresses, data from a different profile, or information inappropriate for the current website. Even when the suggestion is correct, filling a field can disclose information to a site before a final button is clicked, depending on its design. Review the destination before interacting with sensitive fields. On a shared device, do not save new private payment details or credentials unless the device owner and service policy expressly make that appropriate.

### Passwords, passkeys, and login verification

Lesson 8 covered [[password-manager|password managers]], unique passwords, [[passkey|passkeys]], and [[multi-factor-authentication|multifactor authentication]]. Those controls reduce specific risks, but no single sign-in method makes every website safe. A strong password cannot help if you type it into a phishing page controlled by an attacker. A login code does not help if you give that code to someone impersonating support. A passkey can provide stronger resistance to lookalike phishing sites when properly implemented, but account recovery, device security, and other risks remain.

A useful sequence for sensitive sign-ins is: use a known entry point, inspect the current address, identify the correct account, use the normal sign-in method, refuse unexpected verification requests, confirm whose account opened, and sign out appropriately when finished. If the service unexpectedly asks for an unusual form of identity proof or a payment to “unlock” the account, stop and use its published support process.

### A shared computer is different from your own device

At a library, school, workplace, or shared household computer, someone else may use the same physical machine after you. Closing a browser tab does not necessarily sign out of a website. A site may keep a [[session|session]] active through a browser cookie or other account mechanism. A browser may also offer to save passwords, payment information, or profile data. Do not assume that selecting private browsing will erase files you downloaded or end a website session that was left open.

Before leaving a shared computer, use the legitimate **Sign out** control for accounts you accessed and follow the site's completion prompts. Close your work when appropriate, avoid saving credentials, remove personal files only if authorized and safe to do so, and follow the organization's posted instructions. Do not clear other people's history, reset the shared browser, or attempt to inspect their accounts. If you cannot determine whether you signed out, ask the responsible staff for help using an authorized process.

### Private browsing and privacy controls

A browser's [[private-browsing|private or incognito mode]] usually changes what local browsing history and some site data the browser retains after that private session ends. It **does not provide anonymity on the internet**. Websites may still receive your requests and identify you when you log in. Network operators or organizations may be able to observe traffic metadata or apply policy controls. Downloaded files and some bookmarks may remain after a private session. The exact behavior depends on browser and device.

A [[cookie|cookie]] is a small piece of site-associated data stored by a browser. Cookies can maintain sessions, remember a preference, or support other website functions. Some cookies and related technologies are used for tracking and measurement. Cookies are not inherently malware, and clearing them does not automatically remove every trace of online activity or every kind of tracking. Blocking all cookies can also break legitimate sign-in or service features. Use trusted browser privacy guidance to understand available controls and their tradeoffs; you do not need to change settings to complete this lesson.

[[tracking|Online tracking]] may use cookies, identifiers, scripts, or other techniques to observe aspects of browsing behavior. Browsers offer varying anti-tracking tools, and websites may provide consent choices. When presented with optional advertising or tracking permissions, read the choices rather than selecting the most brightly colored button by habit. A privacy notice does not itself guarantee good practice, so be thoughtful about what data a site needs to perform your task.

### Location, photographs, and everyday oversharing

Websites may infer approximate location from network information even if you decline precise location access. When you grant a site location permission, the site may obtain more precise information depending on your device and settings. Before granting precise location to a local service, ask whether it truly needs your present position or whether entering a general city or ZIP code would be sufficient. A classroom exercise should not require students to disclose their actual home address or live device location.

Photographs and files sometimes contain identifying details or metadata. A photo of a form might reveal a full name, account number, QR code, or confirmation number. A screenshot may capture open tabs, messages, or account information in the background. If an organization asks for proof of a legitimate action, share only what is needed and use its approved channel. Do not upload private screenshots to a general public forum merely to receive troubleshooting help.

### Safe online shopping and payment decisions

[[online-payment|Online payments]] combine the risks of identity, authorization, recipient, price, and records. Before completing a purchase, check that you reached the actual merchant, that the item and total cost are clearly described, that return and refund terms are accessible, and that the final payment destination is consistent with the verified service. Compare independent information about unfamiliar merchants and recognize that an advertisement or star rating is not a guarantee.

A payment request made outside the recognized checkout workflow deserves extra scrutiny. For example, a purported support representative may ask for a gift card or a transfer to resolve a supposed account problem. Treat unusual payment methods, requests for secrecy, and pressure to act immediately as red flags. The appropriate course depends on the transaction, but a legitimate charge should have verifiable terms and a clear recipient. If you believe you have sent money to a scammer, contact the payment provider promptly using its official support route; different payment methods offer different possible recovery steps, and recovery is not guaranteed.

| Situation | Information or action at stake | Appropriate response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A public article asks for your full home address | Unnecessary location disclosure | Decline and read the public article elsewhere if available |
| A known merchant requests a delivery address during checkout | Information plausibly necessary to deliver an order | Verify merchant, order, destination, and privacy terms |
| A caller asks you to read a one-time login code | Account authorization secret | Refuse; check the account through its official route |
| An unfamiliar quiz asks for an identity document | Highly sensitive information | Do not submit; question the purpose and legitimacy |
| A website asks to save your card on a shared PC | Future exposure of payment details | Decline storage and use an authorized process |
| A website says payment failed after you pressed Submit | Uncertain transaction status | Check the account or payment provider before paying again |
| A browser offers to remember an account password | Persistent device or profile access | Consider device ownership and account policy before accepting |
| A service offers optional precise location | Extra data disclosure | Grant only if relevant, understood, and necessary |

### Public Wi-Fi: understand the actual risks

[[public-wifi|Public Wi-Fi]] is a network offered in a library, school, café, airport, or other shared place. It can be useful and is not automatically compromised. Risks may include an impostor network name, a malicious local participant, or confusion about which sign-in page belongs to the venue. HTTPS helps protect content in transit to the addressed website; it does not tell you whether a fake portal is asking for your data. A [[vpn|virtual private network]] can provide additional traffic protection in some circumstances, especially where organizational policy requires it, but a VPN does not make fraudulent websites honest or stop you from voluntarily disclosing secrets to them.

To use public Wi-Fi responsibly, confirm the intended network name through venue staff or signage, avoid installing unknown certificates or apps at a portal's request, keep the device and browser supported and updated, and carefully inspect payment and login destinations. Prefer a trusted network or mobile connection for tasks that require unusual permissions or sensitive access if you cannot verify the environment. On managed devices, follow the organization's required VPN and network procedures. If the network's login page looks suspicious, ask staff rather than improvising.

### Children, other household members, and accessibility

Safe browsing education should not require a person to disclose a diagnosis, age, financial status, or private experience. A learner may use an accessible browser interface, screen reader, speech recognition, larger text, captions, or assistive input without changing the safety principles. An advertisement can be deceptive in audio or spoken output just as it can be deceptive visually. On household or supervised devices, appropriate parental or organizational settings can support safety, but conversation, guidance, and independent verification remain important. Do not treat filtering software as a substitute for understanding online requests.

> **Privacy habit:** Know **which site** is asking, **which information** it wants, **why** it needs it, and **what happens after you agree**. The smallest appropriate disclosure is often the most responsible one.

## 6. How do you maintain browser protection and respond to trouble?

Good safety habits include maintaining the tools you already use and knowing how to respond when something unexpected happens. Modern browsers and operating systems provide multiple layers of protection against known harmful sites, suspicious downloads, outdated software, and other threats. These protections matter, but their names and settings differ. For a beginner, **keeping normal protection enabled and using official updates** is more valuable than repeatedly experimenting with advanced security options.

### What browsers can do to help

Microsoft Edge uses Microsoft Defender SmartScreen to help warn about malicious or phishing sites and suspicious downloads. Google Chrome offers Safe Browsing protections at varying levels, with different privacy and security tradeoffs. Firefox offers deceptive-content and dangerous-download protections. Safari and Apple platforms also provide website and security protections that depend on current device and browser configuration. These products change over time and do not use identical menus or definitions.

Recognize that a warning is useful information. A **false positive** is possible when a legitimate site is flagged, but that does not make bypassing the warning the standard response. When the destination is important, find its verified official route or consult authorized help. Some organizations configure security controls centrally. If your school or workplace manages the browser, do not disable those features without authorization. Site protection and software updates are layers of protection, not substitutes for evaluating individual requests.

### Keep supported browsers and devices updated

[[software-update|Software updates]] can fix security weaknesses, correct defects, and improve compatibility. A modern browser may update automatically, but installation or restart requirements can differ. A phone or laptop's operating system also needs appropriate security updates. If a device or browser is no longer supported, it may not receive important fixes; official vendor information can help determine a supported path forward.

An update should come from the browser's recognized update feature, an authorized app store, the operating system's official update controls, or the organization's managed software system. A random website claiming *Your browser is outdated — install this unknown fix* is not an acceptable substitute. Do not disable legitimate protection or run pasted commands as an alleged update procedure. For a public or managed computer, notify authorized staff rather than changing its security configuration.

### A quick maintenance checklist

| Habit | Why it helps | How a beginner can apply it safely |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use supported software | Security fixes reach current products | Consult vendor or IT guidance; use official updates |
| Keep device protection active | Built-in warnings can block known threats | Do not routinely override browser or OS warnings |
| Use strong, unique account credentials | Reduces damage from one password being stolen | Use approved password-manager or passkey methods |
| Use multifactor authentication | Adds a separate sign-in control | Approve only requests you initiated |
| Limit extensions and permissions | Reduces unnecessary access | Install only needed, trustworthy, authorized tools |
| Back up important work | Can help after device loss, error, or attack | Follow a verified backup method and test recovery appropriately |
| Verify suspicious messages independently | Prevents trust in impersonated contacts | Navigate to the known service, not the message's link |
| Learn how to report a problem | Helps contain mistakes and improve assistance | Use official support, organizational IT, or appropriate fraud reporting |

### Not all incidents require the same reaction

A beginner may encounter a misleading advertisement and leave it immediately; that is not equivalent to installing an unknown program. A user may type a password into the wrong page, requiring prompt account protection. Another user may send money to a fraudulent recipient, requiring immediate payment-provider contact. Good response depends on **the actual action and observed consequence**. Try to determine what happened without performing more risky experiments.

**Scenario A — You merely saw a suspicious page.** You recognized a fake technical-support number and did not call, install, approve, or enter information. Leave the page. You may record the URL or screenshot only if doing so is safe and does not disclose sensitive details. There is no reason to assume that merely seeing the message proves complete device compromise. If the browser itself reported a threat, follow its normal safety guidance.

**Scenario B — You downloaded a suspicious file but did not open it.** Do not execute the file, ignore instructions to disable protection, or send it to classmates. Seek the browser or device vendor's official guidance for safely removing or checking a suspicious download. On a managed device, report it to IT. Be precise about whether the file actually ran; merely downloading and executing are different events.

**Scenario C — You ran unfamiliar software or allowed remote access.** This merits stronger action. Stop the suspicious interaction, cease following the remote party's directions, and contact trusted device support or organizational IT promptly. If there are signs of active compromise, follow the authorized incident-response instructions for that device, which may include isolating it from the network. Avoid entering new passwords into a device you believe may be compromised until trusted help determines a safer recovery method.

**Scenario D — You entered a password or disclosed a security code on a fake page.** From a trusted device and the real site's verified address, change affected credentials if applicable, review active sessions and recovery information, and enable or review multifactor authentication. A code may already have been used; do not assume changing the password alone resolves every session or account issue. Contact the service's official security team or organizational administrator, especially when access to financial, school, work, or personal records is involved.

**Scenario E — You authorized a suspicious charge or sent money.** Contact the relevant bank, card issuer, payment provider, or platform using independently verified details as soon as possible. Review transaction status before repeating any payment. Keep truthful records, such as a date, transaction number, and official case reference, without publicly posting full account numbers or identification. Report suspected fraud through the appropriate official process. Outcomes and refund rights depend on payment method and circumstances; there is no universal guarantee.

**Scenario F — You granted a suspicious website notifications or another permission.** Stop interacting with its notices. Find the appropriate browser or operating-system site-permission controls through official documentation, revoke unnecessary permission if authorized to do so, and ask for help if the device is managed. A notification permission does not by itself mean the website has administrator access or that every account is compromised. Determine whether you also downloaded, executed, or disclosed anything.

### Response guide: what changed?

| What happened? | What you can reasonably conclude | Priority first action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unfamiliar webpage appeared | Page was displayed; further compromise is not established | Leave; inspect whether any interaction occurred |
| Suspicious file transferred to Downloads | A file may exist; it was not necessarily executed | Do not open; use trusted security guidance |
| Software was installed or run | The program may have affected the device | Stop interaction; contact trusted IT or support promptly |
| Password supplied to a fake sign-in | Credential exposure is possible | Use real site on trusted device to secure account and sessions |
| Unexpected MFA prompt approved | An account sign-in may have been authorized | Use official account security process immediately |
| Money paid to suspected scammer | A transaction may have been authorized | Contact the payment provider promptly via verified channel |
| Website notifications allowed | That website may send notices | Revoke unneeded permission through official controls |
| Browser warns of deceptive site | A built-in safety system flagged the destination | Do not bypass; find trusted route or request authorized assistance |
| Confirmation status is unclear | Outcome is not yet established | Check legitimate account or official support before repeating action |

### Document incidents without oversharing

When you ask for help, a clear factual description is useful. Record **what you were trying to do**, **what appeared**, **which action you took**, **what the device or site reported**, and **when it happened**. If instructed to preserve evidence, keep it through the approved channel. Do not forward malicious attachments casually or post secret codes, passwords, account identifiers, or private files in a public help forum. Screenshots should be reviewed for personal information before any authorized sharing.

A statement such as *“A pop-up offered a program, but I closed it and did not download anything”* tells support something different from *“I downloaded and ran a program, then gave a caller remote access.”* Accuracy helps the person assisting you choose an appropriate response. Don't exaggerate or minimize the action; if you're unsure, say so.

### The role of reputable reporting and recovery services

In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission offers consumer scam guidance and reporting at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/). People dealing with identity theft can consult [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) for situation-specific recovery guidance. The [Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world) provides public security-education resources. School or workplace incidents may also have required internal reporting procedures. These organizations can provide guidance, but a classroom exercise should not require a real report or disclosure of private records.

For a consumer who suspects fraud, reporting may help authorities identify patterns, but reporting alone does not guarantee money is recovered or accounts are restored. Do not pay a third-party “recovery expert” found through an unsolicited message or advertisement. Services claiming they can retrieve stolen funds in exchange for an advance fee may themselves be fraudulent. Use verified bank, platform, employer, and official-government channels rather than strangers promising instant results.

### A measured troubleshooting workflow

Start with the simplest supported explanation. If a page will not load, it may be a temporary network problem rather than an attack. If a sign-in fails, the account may be wrong, the password may have changed, or the service may be unavailable. If a file is blocked, the protection may have found a genuine concern or classified the file conservatively. Observe the actual message; consult the service's official help; avoid disabling security controls merely to make an error disappear. When a task is high-stakes, **verification and patience are safer than repeated blind attempts**.

### Worked example: a warning during an important application

A student tries to open a scholarship application's login page, but Edge displays a site-protection warning. The application deadline is approaching. The student might feel pressure to click **Continue anyway**. Instead, the student should pause, return to the scholarship organization's verified homepage using a known address, and check for official access instructions or a published contact channel. If the warning blocks the correct site, the student can ask the institution or authorized support for a safe way to proceed and document the access issue. The urgency of the deadline does not make ignoring a browser security warning a sound default.

> **Response habit:** Match your action to what truly occurred. A suspicious page viewed is different from software executed, credentials disclosed, payment sent, or access granted. Start with facts, use official recovery channels, and do not create a second problem while trying to solve the first.

## 7. Guided practice: make safe web decisions

These six activities assess what you would **notice, verify, refuse, and confirm**. They can be completed on paper or in the MGU dashboard without visiting any unfamiliar websites. All organizations, account details, and incidents are fictional unless explicitly identified as general public resources. Use the **STOP → INSPECT → VERIFY → DECIDE → CONFIRM** method, and compare your reasoning with the expandable worked solution after each activity. If you are teaching this lesson, invite learners to explain *why* a choice is sound rather than rewarding speed.

### Activity 1 — Read the real web address

**Skill:** Distinguish the site that actually owns a domain from familiar-looking brand words inside it.

A fictional institution named **Cedar Valley Learning Center** has confirmed through a printed brochure that its demonstration domain is `cedarvalley.example.org`. You receive a message offering a free certificate. The message presents three possible links and insists that the certificate will disappear soon. These sample domains are for analysis only, not browsing.

| Candidate address | What the text suggests | What you should check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `https://cedarvalley.example.org/certificates` | Under the stated demonstration domain | Whether the institution actually offers this service through its official navigation |
| `https://cedarvalley.example.org.verify-award.test/form` | Mentions the institution but actually belongs to `verify-award.test` | The real host rather than the familiar words |
| `https://award-cedarvalley.test/login` | A separate lookalike domain | Whether the institution officially documents the change of domain |

**Your assignment:** Identify the two addresses that cannot be presumed to belong to the institution. Explain why the first address is not, by itself, proof that a certificate award is real. Describe the independent navigation you would use before entering any details. Explain why a countdown timer must not affect the destination check. Finally, state what HTTPS can and cannot prove.

**Practice variation:** The first address suddenly redirects to an unexpected sign-in service. What information would you need before trusting the new domain? Should you enter your password because the original page appeared to be correct?

:::answer Show the worked solution to Activity 1
1. The second address is hosted under `verify-award.test`, and the third under `award-cedarvalley.test`. Both are separate from the brochure's `cedarvalley.example.org` address, even though they include familiar words.
1. The first address is structurally consistent with the stated site, but the content still must be checked against the institution's legitimate offerings and current navigation.
1. Use the independently known course website, its published certificate page, or verified staff contact details. Do not follow the urgent message's unfamiliar links to investigate them.
1. A countdown timer is a persuasion device and offers no evidence that the destination is legitimate.
1. HTTPS commonly protects the connection to the addressed host; it does not establish the operator's honesty, the truth of an award, or the appropriateness of the request.
1. A redirect may be legitimate, but the new sign-in host should match an officially documented account or identity-provider process. Until verified, do not supply credentials.
:::

### Activity 2 — Evaluate an urgent delivery message

**Skill:** Recognize impersonation and select independent verification.

**Scenario:** A student expects a textbook shipment. A text message says: *Delivery failed. Pay a $1.95 reprocessing fee within 15 minutes or the package will be destroyed.* It supplies a shortened link. After the student opens their regular email account, a second message arrives claiming to be the delivery company and requesting a phone verification code. The messages contain the student's first name, a shipping-company logo, and a plausible-looking tracking number. The student has not yet clicked either link or disclosed anything.

**Your assignment:** List at least four reasons to pause. What legitimate information could help verify the parcel independently? Explain why the student's name, a logo, and a tracking number do not conclusively authenticate the messages. Should the student give the verification code to the sender? Does the small fee make the request inherently safe? Describe the best next action.

**Practice variation:** The merchant's verified account shows *Delivered*, but the text says *Awaiting payment*. Which record deserves more weight, and what should the student do if the status remains disputed?

:::answer Show the worked solution to Activity 2
1. Red flags include the short deadline, threat of destruction, unexpected fee, shortened destination, code request, and pressure to use the supplied link.
1. Check the student's original order confirmation, the retailer's known account page, and the delivery company's verified tracking service, independently reached.
1. A name and logo can be copied; a tracking number may be obtained or guessed and may refer to a different parcel. These details provide context, not conclusive authentication.
1. Do not give an unrequested security code to the sender or approve a sign-in the student did not initiate.
1. A small payment can still expose payment details or enable further fraud. Verify the service and charge rather than judging safety by amount.
1. If legitimate records conflict, consult the retailer or carrier through its verified support route. Do not rely on the text's link or telephone number.
:::

### Activity 3 — Distinguish a real browser warning from scareware

**Skill:** Refuse unexpected executable downloads, fake support claims, and copied-command prompts.

**Scenario A:** While reading a public museum article, a large overlay says *SECURITY EMERGENCY — your files are infected!* It displays a telephone number and tells the learner to install `security-repair.exe` immediately. The webpage refuses to close when the learner clicks a fake X painted inside the overlay.

**Scenario B:** Another page shows *Verify you are human* but tells the learner to open a command-running utility, paste an instruction copied from the page, and execute it. The page claims this will unlock an ordinary article.

**Scenario C:** The browser itself displays a recognizable warning that the requested site has been reported as deceptive and blocks the navigation.

**Your assignment:** For each scenario, say what you should **not** do and describe the appropriate response. Explain why the fake X in Scenario A is not necessarily a genuine browser control. Explain how a suspicious “human verification” instruction differs from a simple on-page challenge. Explain why Scenario C should not be bypassed for routine practice. Identify one trustworthy source of security assistance.

**Practice variation:** The learner downloaded the supposed repair file but did not open it. What is known, what is not known, and what is the safest next step?

:::answer Show the worked solution to Activity 3
1. **Scenario A:** Do not call the displayed number, install the file, pay for a repair, or grant remote access. Use normal browser or operating-system controls to leave the page, and seek authorized help if it resists ordinary closure.
1. The fake X is drawn by the webpage and may not operate like the browser's genuine tab-close button. Treat the webpage's appearance as untrusted content.
1. **Scenario B:** Do not paste or execute a website-supplied command. A legitimate article should not require operating-system command execution as proof of humanity. Leave and use a trusted site.
1. **Scenario C:** Honor the browser's protection warning and find the verified official route, or consult authorized technical support if the site is genuinely required.
1. Trusted help includes the device's recognized security application, the browser vendor's official support pages, or school/work IT staff, not phone numbers from the alarming page.
1. A downloaded file may exist without having been executed. Do not open it. Use official browser or device guidance to handle the suspicious download and consult IT on a managed device.
:::

### Activity 4 — Decide which website permissions are appropriate

**Skill:** Evaluate notification, location, camera, microphone, and extension requests by purpose.

**Scenario:** A fictional community center offers four online features: a public event calendar, an optional monthly reminder service, a live video meeting, and a local bus-directions tool. While browsing, the following requests appear:

- The event calendar asks for **microphone** access immediately upon opening.
- The reminder page asks permission to send **browser notifications**.
- The video meeting asks for **camera and microphone** access when the student chooses to join.
- The bus-directions tool offers **precise location**, but also allows the user to enter a city manually.
- An unrelated advertisement offers a **browser extension** that promises to “speed up” the website and asks for access to read all visited pages.

**Your assignment:** Which requests might have legitimate purposes, and which are unexpected? Should the learner approve every request that appears within the same community-center page? Choose an appropriate response for each request and explain why. If the learner had already allowed notifications from a suspect site, how could they safely reconsider that decision? Identify one privacy-preserving alternative to precise location sharing.

**Practice variation:** A legitimate video meeting still shows a microphone-blocked icon after the user intentionally declines. Does the refusal prove the computer is broken? What are reasonable options?

:::answer Show the worked solution to Activity 4
1. A public calendar ordinarily does not need microphone access; decline or dismiss the unexpected request.
1. Notifications could be appropriate for an optional reminder service, but they are not mandatory simply because the site requests them. Read the purpose and decline if they are unnecessary.
1. A video meeting may reasonably need camera and microphone access when intentionally used. The learner can review the permission and use permitted alternatives, such as audio-only participation or a phone dial-in option where offered.
1. The directions tool might use precise location for convenience, but manual city entry is a less revealing alternative when sufficient.
1. An advertisement for a broad-permission extension is unrelated to the student's task. Do not install it merely to improve page speed; verify independently if an extension is ever genuinely needed.
1. Already granted permissions can often be reviewed or revoked through the browser's official site-permission controls; ask authorized IT for help on managed devices.
1. Declining a permission can prevent a feature from working without indicating any hardware failure. Review the site's accessible alternatives and grant permission only when intended and appropriate.
:::

### Activity 5 — Protect a session on a shared computer

**Skill:** Apply account, privacy, and payment habits in a public-device setting.

**Your assignment:** Explain what to do with the two save prompts. Does private browsing replace the need to sign out of accounts? What should happen to the learner's course session when finished? Why might a downloaded file remain after the window closes? Which actions on the shared computer should be left to library policy or staff rather than performed without authorization?

**Practice variation:** A screen remains signed into a previous patron's email account. Should the learner investigate the messages, clear the account history, or tell the responsible staff without accessing the contents?

:::answer Show the worked solution to Activity 5
1. Decline storing personal account passwords and payment details in the public browser unless a clearly authorized arrangement explicitly requires otherwise.
1. A private window changes some local history retention but does not guarantee anonymity or automatically end every account session.
1. Use the course portal's actual Sign out command, confirm the signed-out status if the service reports it, and finish through the library's posted procedures.
1. Files downloaded during private browsing may remain on storage after the window closes; the user should follow library policy for personal files and avoid changing other patrons' data.

### Activity 6 — Choose a proportionate incident response

**Skill:** Match actions to the severity and type of a reported event.

Four fictional learners describe incidents:

**Case A — No interaction:** Dana saw a suspicious pop-up while reading a public recipe and closed the tab. Dana did not click the offered link, download a file, disclose a secret, or approve permission.

**Case B — File downloaded:** Eli accidentally saved a file offered by a fake video player but did not open or run the file. The browser shows it in Downloads.

**Case C — Credentials disclosed:** Noor typed a password into an unfamiliar site and approved an unexpected verification prompt. Noor now suspects the page impersonated a genuine service.

**Practice variation:** In Case C, the device itself may have been compromised in addition to the password exposure. Explain why the learner should use a *trusted device and verified site* for recovery rather than immediately entering a new password into the suspected device.

:::answer Show the worked solution to Activity 6
1. **Case A:** Leave the suspicious page. Since no interaction beyond viewing is described, do not claim device compromise without evidence; consult legitimate protection tools if another warning or symptom appears.
1. **Case B:** Do not open the file. Use the browser's or operating system's recognized guidance to remove or check it safely, and notify authorized IT if the device is managed.
1. **Case C:** From a trusted device and verified service, protect the account promptly. Change affected credentials when applicable, review active sessions and recovery settings, and report the unexpected verification approval through the service's official channel.

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

### “A padlock or HTTPS means a website is trustworthy.”

HTTPS protects the connection to the addressed site but does not establish the site's honesty, its product quality, or its authority to collect particular information. Fraudulent services can use encrypted connections. Confirm the domain and purpose before entering sensitive information.

### “The first search result must be the official website.”

Results are ranked through search systems and may include advertisements. A result's position is not an identity certificate. For high-stakes access or downloads, independently confirm the official route. A top result can be relevant while still being a poor or unsafe choice.

### “If an email knows my name, it must really be from the organization.”

A person's name, email address, purchase history, or approximate location may be publicly available, previously disclosed, or obtained without authorization. Familiar details can make impersonation sound credible. Verify the sender's claim through the organization's known channel, not the message's own reply address or link.

### “Scams always contain spelling errors.”

Some do, but many use polished writing, convincing logos, professional graphics, or generated text. Evaluate the destination, claimed authority, requested action, and independent evidence. A grammatically correct request for a password code can still be fraudulent.

### “A website's virus alert proves my computer is infected.”

A webpage can draw a convincing-looking warning or play alarming sounds. That is not the same as a diagnosis from a recognized security application. Do not call support numbers or install tools offered by the suspicious page; use verified system tools or trusted support.

### “Every CAPTCHA is legitimate, so I should follow its instructions.”

A legitimate verification challenge is not permission to run an arbitrary command on your computer. If a page asks you to open a command utility and paste instructions from the website, stop. Such prompts are a documented scam pattern.

### “If an app is downloaded, it has already changed my system.”

Downloading a file and running or installing it are different actions. Certain exploits or vulnerabilities can create other risks, but a downloaded file alone does not establish that it executed. Determine what occurred and respond accordingly; do not open the file to investigate it.

### “Security software catches every dangerous site.”

Protective technologies reduce risk, but threats evolve and detections can be incomplete. Keep normal protections and updates active while still applying careful judgment. A warning is meaningful; the absence of a warning is not a blanket guarantee.

### “Private browsing means nobody can identify me.”

Private or incognito sessions chiefly change some local browser retention behavior. Signed-in services, network administrators, websites, and other systems may still have relevant records or information. Downloaded files may persist, and an account can remain exposed if not properly signed out.

### “Closing a tab signs me out of the account.”

Web sessions can persist beyond one tab or window. Use the actual Sign out command on shared devices and follow local policy. The browser's history and a site's active account session are different things.

### “Cookies are viruses and deleting them makes everything private.”

Cookies can support legitimate sessions and preferences, although some are used for tracking. They are not automatically malicious software. Deleting cookies may sign you out of sites but does not erase downloaded files, undo disclosures, or guarantee privacy from every observer.

### “A page that asks permission must need it.”

Websites can request notifications, location, microphone, camera, and other capabilities whether or not those requests are necessary. Declining an unrelated request is often appropriate. Review the site, task, and exact permission before approving it.

### “An extension with many users cannot be dangerous.”

Popularity, reviews, and professional presentation are not complete security guarantees. Permissions and publisher identity matter. Install only justified, trusted, authorized browser add-ons, and avoid any unexpected extension that claims to repair a virus or unlock ordinary reading content.

### “Public Wi-Fi is always unsafe, while cellular data makes all sites safe.”

Network types have different risks. Public Wi-Fi is not automatically compromised, and cellular access does not prevent phishing or fraud on the website you visit. Confirm network legitimacy, keep HTTPS and device protection in use, and verify high-stakes destinations whatever the connection.

### “If a transaction looks uncertain, submitting again is always safest.”

Repeated payment or form submissions can create duplicates. Check the legitimate account, receipt, order history, or payment provider before repeating a consequential action. A browser's loading indicator is not a substitute for the receiving service's actual transaction status.

### “If something looks suspicious, I must prove it is malicious by clicking it.”

You can refuse an unverified request without conducting a risky experiment. Do not open a suspicious installer, log in to an unexpected domain, or execute commands to gather proof for class. Use trusted sources and report through appropriate channels when warranted.

### “Any cybersecurity problem has the same fix.”

Seeing a suspicious page, downloading a file, running a program, disclosing a password, granting remote access, and authorizing a charge are distinct events. The response should fit the actual incident. Generic panic and indiscriminate deletion are not substitutes for careful triage.

### A compact decision guide

| When you notice... | Do not assume... | Safer first move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A familiar logo on an unfamiliar site | The organization controls that site | Reach the known official website independently |
| HTTPS on an unexpected payment form | The request is trustworthy | Verify merchant, recipient, and reason for payment |
| An urgent warning with a phone number | Your device was diagnosed by the vendor | Leave page; use trusted security or support tools |
| A surprising verification code request | You must read or approve it | Refuse and inspect the real account separately |
| A file download is blocked | The browser must be wrong | Respect warning and verify source |
| An article asks for microphone access | Microphone use is necessary | Decline until an appropriate purpose is established |
| A receipt is missing | The transaction definitely failed | Check official status before attempting another submission |
| Someone requests a secret screenshot | Sharing is required for support | Use official support and minimize disclosed information |
| A public device still shows another account | You may inspect or clear the data | Leave it untouched and notify authorized staff |

> **Final safety principle:** Online confidence does not mean clicking through every warning. It means being able to explain the intended task, the real destination, the information or permission requested, the evidence you used to verify it, and what would make you stop.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The dotted-underlined expressions in the reading use the established MGU dashboard linked-term convention. Selecting a supported term displays a short definition, and some entries can direct the learner toward related MGU reference material. The text below remains available even if interactive tooltips are not displayed. Vocabulary is grouped by practical function rather than alphabetical order; definitions in the final `:::terms` block provide the machine-readable equivalents.

### Browsers, links, and addresses

- **Safe browsing:** Using the web with deliberate steps that reduce avoidable security and privacy risks.
- **Browse:** Use a browser to visit and interact with web content.
- **Browser:** Software that opens and presents websites and web applications.
- **URL:** A web address identifying a resource or location.
- **Address bar:** The browser control that displays or accepts a web address.
- **Domain:** A name used to identify an internet destination; ownership must be verified for sensitive tasks.
- **Subdomain:** A named portion placed before a larger domain name.
- **Path:** The portion of a web address that identifies a page or resource under a host.
- **HTTPS:** A scheme that generally provides encrypted communication with the addressed web server.
- **Certificate warning:** A browser alert that it cannot establish expected connection-security assurances.
- **Search result:** A listing returned by a search service; not itself an authenticity guarantee.
- **Sponsored result:** Paid search or promotional placement, sometimes marked Ad or Sponsored.
- **Link destination:** The address a link points to, which may differ from the visible link wording.
- **Shortened link:** A compact address that may conceal the eventual destination.
- **QR code:** A visual code that a device may interpret as text, a web address, or other data.
- **Redirect:** A transition that moves a browser from one address to another.
- **Identity provider:** A service that manages sign-in for another application or organization.

### Scams, harmful content, and deceptive requests

- **Phishing:** Deception intended to obtain secrets or cause harmful actions by impersonation or other false claims.
- **Social engineering:** Manipulating people into granting access, disclosing data, transferring funds, or following harmful instructions.
- **Authority spoofing:** Impersonating an organization or person to create the appearance of legitimacy.
- **Urgency:** Pressure to act quickly rather than verify carefully.
- **Scarcity:** A claim that an opportunity or resource is limited, which may be genuine or manufactured.
- **Fear tactic:** A message designed to create alarm and weaken careful judgment.
- **Reward bait:** The promise of a prize, benefit, or gain to persuade someone to act.
- **Secrecy demand:** An instruction not to discuss or independently verify a request.
- **Tech-support scam:** Fraud that imitates technical assistance and may seek payment, device access, or account information.
- **CAPTCHA:** A website challenge intended to help distinguish humans from automated activity; fake versions can exist.
- **Malware:** Software designed or used to cause harm or unauthorized access.
- **Scareware:** Deceptive material that falsely reports a device problem and pressures the user toward a supposed repair.
- **Verification code:** A temporary value used in account verification; treat it as a secret unless the official process explicitly directs its use.
- **MFA prompt:** A request to approve or complete a second account-authentication step.
- **False positive:** A protective warning that incorrectly identifies a legitimate item as threatening.

### Downloads, applications, and permissions

- **Download:** Receive digital content from a remote source into a selected destination.
- **Executable file:** A file containing instructions or software that can run under suitable conditions.
- **Archive:** A packaged file containing one or more files, possibly compressed.
- **Installer:** A program or package intended to place software on a device.
- **Pop-up:** An additional webpage element, overlay, or window that appears during browsing.
- **Browser notification:** A message displayed through browser and operating-system mechanisms after a relevant website permission is granted.
- **Site permission:** Authorization for a website to use a browser or device capability, subject to its controls.
- **Browser extension:** Add-on software that changes or adds browser behavior, sometimes requiring broad access.
- **Software update:** A supported change to an application or system, often including security fixes.
- **Security warning:** A notice from a trusted security control about a potentially unsafe condition or action.
- **Remote access:** The capability for another person or program to operate or observe a device over a connection.

### Accounts, privacy, and financial choices

- **Personal information:** Information that identifies or relates to a person directly or when combined with other data.
- **Privacy:** Appropriate control and protection concerning the collection, use, and exposure of information.
- **Data minimization:** Limiting information collection or disclosure to what the legitimate task requires.
- **Autofill:** Browser or application assistance that suggests or inserts previously saved field values.
- **Password manager:** A tool that stores and helps use account credentials under protective controls.
- **Passkey:** A supported cryptographic sign-in credential associated with the service and device or account ecosystem.
- **Multifactor authentication:** Authentication using more than one distinct kind of factor or an additional supported verification step.
- **Session:** An ongoing relationship between a signed-in account and website or application.
- **Sign out:** Use the account's recognized control to end or reduce access to an authenticated session.
- **Private browsing:** A browser mode that limits some local retention but does not create complete anonymity.
- **Cookie:** A small piece of website-associated browser data often used for sessions and preferences.
- **Tracking:** Collection of information about browsing behavior through cookies, identifiers, scripts, or other methods.
- **Online payment:** A digital transaction that authorizes payment through a service or merchant.
- **Public Wi-Fi:** Wireless network access offered in a location that may be used by multiple people.
- **VPN:** A virtual private network that carries traffic through a protected connection under the VPN service's design and policy.
- **Incident response:** Actions taken to understand, contain, report, and recover from a security or privacy problem.
- **Independent verification:** Checking a claim or request through a trusted channel not supplied solely by the questionable message.

Most important, learners should be able to use the terms in a decision rather than merely repeat a definition. For example: *“The address bar shows a different domain; HTTPS does not prove this page is genuine; I will use the official website to verify the request.”* That short explanation joins three pieces of vocabulary into one safe action.

### Continue learning through responsible practice

When using a real website, pause before a consequential action. Identify its real domain, ask what information or capability it wants, obtain an independent verification route for unusual requests, and consider whether you need the action at all. Use a known browser or device security feature when help is needed, and keep official account and payment channels separate from unsolicited advertisements or messages. You do not need to seek out dangerous websites to practice these steps. Consistent attention during ordinary, harmless browsing is the better long-term method.
