## Lesson 5: Judging Search Results

You search for the hours of a public library. The first listing says the building closes at 6:00 p.m., a community calendar says 8:00 p.m., and a short answer at the top of the search page says it is closed today. Which answer should you use before making the trip? The practical question is not simply which result appears first or looks most polished. It is **which source is responsible for the information, whether that information applies to the correct location and date, and what evidence supports it**.

In Lesson 4, *Search Engine Basics*, you learned to submit useful searches, recognize result titles and snippets, and refine your keywords. This lesson begins where searching leaves off. You will learn to [[evaluate-source|evaluate a source]], recognize the distinction between [[relevance|relevance]] and [[reliability|reliability]], identify who stands behind a claim, compare independent information, check original records, and make a reasoned decision about whether evidence is sufficient for your purpose. The goal is not to become suspicious of every page. It is to build a repeatable method for using online information responsibly.

A learner who can judge search results can avoid wasting time on an outdated schedule, circulating a misleading quotation, trusting an impersonation website, or treating a generated summary as proof. The method matters even when the subject is ordinary: a school-calendar date, a repair instruction, a product specification, a photograph caption, or the meaning of a historical term. More consequential decisions involving health, law, finances, or personal safety require correspondingly stronger evidence and qualified professional guidance when appropriate.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 100–135 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Internet and Web Skills Lessons 1–4 recommended · **Practice equipment:** Windows 11 computer and web browser preferred; Mac, Chromebook, phone, tablet, or paper worksheet acceptable · **Safety:** Use public or invented examples; do not enter sensitive personal information, install tools, purchase products, contact strangers, or reveal private browsing records for an exercise

> **Essential idea:** A search result is a **lead**, not a verdict. Before relying on its claim, ask: **Who is behind this information? What evidence supports it? What do independent sources say? Does it apply to the right person, place, and date?** The strength of your verification should match the consequence of being wrong.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish a relevant result from a reliable source and explain why ranking is not a truth guarantee.
1. Identify a result title, displayed source, domain, snippet, sponsor label, date, and potential AI-generated answer.
1. Inspect a website's actual identity and distinguish an authoritative publisher from a merely official-looking design.
1. Distinguish a factual claim, opinion, prediction, advertisement, and unsupported assertion.
1. Identify original records, citations, data, qualified expertise, and meaningful limitations.
1. Use [[lateral-reading|lateral reading]] to investigate an unfamiliar source using other trustworthy information.
1. Trace a quotation, statistic, image, or summary toward its original context when feasible.
1. Check the difference between a publication date, event date, and later update, including location-specific information.
1. Recognize misleading headlines, altered media, recycled claims, coordinated repetition, and plausible AI errors.
1. Apply a practical verification routine to realistic searches without exposing personal data.
1. Decide when to use, qualify, verify further, or decline to share information based on available evidence.

## 1. Why must you judge a search result?

A [[search-engine|search engine]] helps locate potentially useful pages; it does not conduct a complete, personal fact-check of every statement it shows. A result can be relevant because its words match your [[query|query]] even if its assertions are unverified, its date is wrong for your purpose, or its publisher has a financial incentive to influence you. A result can be perfectly genuine yet still fail to answer what you actually asked. Learning to judge results means deciding **whether this particular information deserves to guide this particular action**.

### Finding and evaluating are separate steps

Imagine three search results for the fictional community center's swimming schedule. One is a dated news announcement from last year, another is a promotional directory that reproduces old hours, and a third is the center's current scheduling page. All three may mention swimming and opening hours. They have similar topic relevance, but they are not equally useful for deciding whether to travel there tomorrow. For a live schedule, the current, responsible operator's page will usually be the best starting point. If there is a closure notice, you may also need a current announcement.

That illustrates two independent questions. **[[relevance|Relevance]]** asks whether material addresses your need. **[[reliability|Reliability]]** asks whether you have good reason to trust its factual content for that need. A trustworthy library website could be irrelevant to the hours of a different library branch. A misleading page could be relevant to your search terms yet unreliable. Good judgment checks both.

A third question is **[[sufficiency|sufficiency]]**: even a credible item may not provide enough information to act. A government publication about one country's rules may be authentic but not establish the rules in another country. A scientific study may accurately describe one tested population but not justify a universal conclusion. An official page about a current service may omit holiday exceptions. Sometimes the correct response is not a yes or no—it is **"I need one more detail."**

### What are you trying to accomplish?

Before comparing sources, state your purpose plainly. *Find the date a public event happened* requires different evidence from *determine whether an event will happen tomorrow*. The first could be answered through an archive or reliable historical record; the second requires a current event schedule. *Understand what a computer virus means* can use a quality educational introduction, while *decide whether to run an unexpected downloaded installer* requires a security-specific process and may call for not running it at all.

Ask yourself four practical questions:

1. **Subject:** What exact person, place, product, event, or idea am I checking?
1. **Claim:** What exact statement am I trying to confirm?
1. **Time and place:** What date and jurisdiction or location does the question concern?
1. **Consequence:** What could happen if I rely on the wrong answer?

This four-question preparation can take less than a minute, yet it often prevents hours of wasted searching. Vague questions tend to produce answers about different situations. Clear questions make disagreement easier to diagnose: one page may be about a past policy, another about a proposed policy, and a third about the rule currently in force.

### A simple evaluation workflow

The core workflow for beginners is **Pause → Identify → Examine → Compare → Decide**. Pause before selecting or sharing. Identify the actual source and what it claims. Examine its evidence and context. Compare with suitable independent sources or original records. Decide whether to rely on it, qualify it, or seek further verification. This is a teaching sequence, not a universal formula that can automatically score every website. The sequence can be repeated when new information appears.

| Question | What to look for | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does this address my question? | Correct subject, place, date, and level of detail | A reputable result can answer a different question |
| Who published it? | Organization, author, account, actual domain, purpose | A title or logo alone may conceal the publisher |
| What supports the claim? | Original records, named sources, reasoning, documented methods | A confident statement may still be unsupported |
| Can I corroborate it? | Independent sources or the responsible primary record | Repetition does not equal independent confirmation |
| How current must it be? | Event date, publication date, revisions, applicable version | Old facts may be accurate historically but unusable now |
| What happens if it is wrong? | Minor inconvenience versus serious personal consequence | The higher the stakes, the stronger the needed check |

### Proportionate verification

You do not need an extensive investigation to find the spelling of a familiar word. Nor should you spend hours reviewing a claim whose consequences are trivial and whose source is plainly sufficient. However, if a page tells you to transmit a personal identification document, pay an unexpected fee, change medication, or bypass a security warning, ordinary search ranking is not an adequate basis for action. Stop, verify the responsible source through an independent channel, and use appropriate qualified guidance.

> **Working rule:** Decide what would count as a satisfactory answer **before** accepting the first convenient result. A source may be relevant, credible, and still incomplete for your particular task.

### Worked example: two school calendar dates

Suppose a fictional district posted a 2025–2026 academic calendar as a PDF and a 2026–2027 calendar on its main calendar page. A search for "first day of school" shows an older PDF first. The older PDF may be authentic, not fraudulent; it simply belongs to a different academic year. The sensible solution is to identify the district, confirm the school year, open the current calendar from the district's own website, and check for revisions or special school-level exceptions. You have improved judgment without accusing anyone of deliberately spreading falsehoods.

## 2. What can a search result tell you, and what can it not prove?

A [[search-results-page|search results page]] is a constructed display. It may contain conventional links, advertisements, image previews, maps, videos, short answers, question boxes, summaries, and [[ai-generated-summary|AI-generated summaries]]. Some elements originate from the search provider, some from a destination website, and some from advertisers. The display helps you choose where to investigate; it is not a substitute for viewing the underlying source.

### Read the complete listing

A conventional result often provides a [[result-title|title]], a displayed website name or address, and a [[snippet|snippet]]. A snippet may contain words selected to match the query rather than a balanced summary of the whole document. A bold phrase in a snippet can represent a small part of a longer argument. A page title may be written for attention or search ranking; it does not necessarily reflect the strength of evidence in the page itself.

Before following a result, read the destination shown, look for advertising labels, and consider whether the headline and date match your question. After opening it, check the **actual address in the browser**, because a displayed link and the final destination can differ after navigation or redirects. A browser's address bar shows the current address; it does not itself establish the publisher's truthfulness.

### Placement, sponsorship, and appearance

A top listing may reflect factors such as search relevance, the structure of the page, location, personalization, or a paid advertising placement when labelled. Its position is not a peer-review process. A [[sponsored-result|sponsored result]] is a promotional placement that should be evaluated as advertising, even when it resembles an ordinary search result. A commercial advertisement can be legitimate and useful, but **payment for placement is not independent evidence of accuracy, suitability, or safety**.

A result's design is likewise not a credibility test. A misleading site can imitate familiar colors, seals, badges, or page structure. A plain text webpage from a genuine public agency may provide stronger evidence than a glossy, beautifully designed commercial page. Look beneath the surface: who maintains the website, how can you verify that relationship, and what documentation supports the claim?

### The address and domain are identity clues, not proof

A [[domain-name|domain name]] is part of a website address, such as `example.org` in `https://www.example.org/program/`. Distinguish the important registered domain from words in a longer address. `example.org` and `example.org.fake.test` are not the same site: in the second example, the address is under `fake.test`, not `example.org`. Likewise, a page path such as `/official-notice` or a title reading "Official Service" does not by itself make a page official.

A top-level domain such as `.org`, `.com`, `.edu`, or `.gov` can provide a clue about naming or registration conditions, but it is not a complete trust certificate. Some domain categories have eligibility restrictions; others are widely available. Even a genuine institutional site can host outdated, inaccurate, compromised, or narrowly scoped content. Verify the specific publisher and page, not only the extension.

A connection beginning with `https://` generally uses [[https|HTTPS]] to encrypt traffic in transit between your browser and the communicating website or service. This helps protect connection confidentiality and integrity, but it does **not** certify that a seller is honest, a medical claim is correct, or an image is authentic. A fraudulent page can also use HTTPS.

| Result signal | Useful inference | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High position on search page | Search service has chosen to show it prominently | That the information is factually true |
| Sponsored or Ad label | Placement is promotional | That the offer has been independently endorsed |
| Familiar-looking logo | Page is presenting particular branding | That it belongs to the organization pictured |
| Recognizable domain | May help identify the hosting organization | That every statement on the site is accurate or current |
| HTTPS or padlock indicator | Connection uses a protected web transport | That the site's claims, products, or operator are trustworthy |
| Recent-looking date | Page or result displays an associated date | That the entire content was recently reviewed |
| Snippet or highlighted answer | Search display contains a short answer or extract | That omitted context agrees with the extract |
| Many similar results | Claim or wording has circulated widely | That sources independently verified it |

### Dates in search listings are not enough

A result may show a date beside its title. That date can be useful, but the provider's interpretation and source metadata may not match the underlying event date or the date the article's evidence was last checked. A page may have been modified for layout or minor edits without substantive re-verification. A headline may be reused. When the date matters, open the page and compare the **event date**, **publication date**, and **last substantive update**. If the source does not say, avoid guessing.

### AI answers and automated previews

Some search products present answers synthesized by AI. These may be useful overviews and may include links to resources; nevertheless, the generated text can omit qualifications, combine facts from different settings, invent details, or cite a source that does not actually support the particular statement. A citation-like link is a **path to check**, not a guarantee of support. Open and inspect the linked material, especially when a number, quotation, rule, or safety decision matters.

An AI-generated paragraph is also distinct from a [[featured-snippet|featured snippet]] that displays selected text from a web source. Both can lose context, but they are assembled differently. Use the actual original material to verify the wording and meaning. If the preview says "the museum opens at 10," confirm which museum branch, which day, and whether a closure notice applies. If the source conflicts with the preview, do not silently privilege the shorter version.

### An invented example of misleading presentation

A fictional search listing says: **"CITY TRANSIT — All buses now free"**, shows a professional-looking icon, and leads to `city-transit-updates.example`. The linked page has no named operator, no policy document, and a request for a card number to "verify eligibility." The title and appearance are not enough. Do not supply payment information. Independently locate the transit agency through a previously known route or a trusted public directory and look for an actual fare notice. The problem is not whether the result is visually plausible; it is whether its identity and claim can be verified.

> **Decision habit:** Read the result; then inspect the actual destination. If a listing contains a consequential claim, seek the supporting original record rather than treating a snippet as the record itself.

## 3. Who is behind the information?

One of the strongest beginning habits is to identify the [[publisher|publisher]]: the person, organization, agency, business, or group that is responsible for presenting the material. Source identity matters because publishers have different expertise, duties, methods, incentives, and degrees of direct access to information. A school district can normally provide its own calendar; a manufacturer's manual can explain the design of its product; a personal blog may offer useful firsthand experience, but should not be mistaken for an official product safety notice.

### Publisher, author, host, and quoted source

A page can involve several distinct parties. The **[[author|author]]** writes the words; the publisher distributes them; the website host provides a technical location; and a [[quoted-source|quoted source]] may be the person whose statement is repeated. These parties need not be the same. A news organization may publish an article by a named reporter quoting a public official. A community discussion forum may host a post by an unknown user. An institution's site may contain an opinion column that does not represent its official position.

Identify who makes the specific claim. If a headline states that a new rule exists, ask whether it quotes the agency that issued the rule or merely repeats someone's expectation that it will be adopted. An opinion attributed to an expert is still an opinion unless an underlying factual claim is supported. Names and job titles are helpful, but examine whether the person's qualifications match the topic.

### Confirm identity beyond the About page

An **About** or **Contact** page may tell you what the site says about itself. That is a starting point, not independent confirmation. Sites can describe themselves in flattering or misleading ways. Use [[lateral-reading|lateral reading]]: open a separate tab, search the organization's name, and find what suitably independent organizations, public registries, established institutions, or recognized professional bodies say about it. Look for a consistent history and an accountable operator. When checking a specific government or school service, navigate from its known parent institution to the relevant page rather than trusting a standalone promotional result.

Lateral reading is often faster than inspecting dozens of decorative trust markers on an unfamiliar site. Instead of asking only "Does this website look professional?" ask "What do other responsible sources say about this organization, and how do I know I am looking at its real site?" Scholarly work on civic online reasoning emphasizes these questions: who is behind information, what evidence supports it, and what other sources say.

### Expertise must fit the claim

[[expertise|Expertise]] is relevant knowledge or skill in a particular subject. An engineer may be qualified to discuss a technical design but not automatically an expert in legal interpretation. A person with hands-on experience may accurately report that experience without establishing what will happen to everyone. Degrees, uniforms, organizational titles, and follower counts are not substitutes for testing the **scope** of expertise and the support for the specific claim.

Consider an online statement about historical photographs. A reputable photo archivist may be well placed to describe collection provenance. A transportation planner may be better placed to explain a current bus route. Neither authority settles every question. Use the expertise that matches the issue, then evaluate the record itself.

### Consider incentives without making accusations

A [[conflict-of-interest|conflict of interest]] is a situation in which financial, professional, political, institutional, or other interests could influence a person's judgment or presentation. It does not automatically prove deception. A company can accurately describe its own product specifications while selectively emphasizing strengths. A public agency can accurately publish a regulation while having a particular institutional interpretation. A nonprofit may provide high-quality research while advocating for a policy position. Distinguish an incentive to persuade from evidence of actual inaccuracy, and compare material claims against independent documentation.

Ask whether the page primarily intends to inform, persuade, sell, entertain, recruit, fundraise, or collect data. Some pages do several things. A sales page can contain accurate specifications; a scholarly article can contain advocacy; a neutral-looking directory can carry paid placements. You are not required to dismiss every interested source. You are required to recognize what its role permits you to infer.

| Kind of question | Good starting source | Additional verification to consider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is a particular library branch open? | Library system's current branch page | Holiday notices and direct official contact where needed |
| What did a public agency publish? | Original agency notice or document | Effective date, jurisdiction, and later amendments |
| What are a product's technical specifications? | Manufacturer's documented specification | Product model/revision and independent tests for performance claims |
| What did a person actually say? | Original recording, transcript, or authenticated statement | Completeness, date, and credible reporting of context |
| How did an event occur historically? | Primary records plus professional historical scholarship | Multiple accounts, provenance, and interpretive disagreements |
| Does a health claim apply to me? | Authoritative clinical or public-health information | Qualified clinician, patient-specific context, and updated guidance |
| What is an unfamiliar organization? | Organization's disclosed identity plus independent documentation | Registries, reliable reporting, institutional affiliations |

### When an official source is and is not enough

An official source is usually especially well placed to establish **what that organization says, offers, issues, or has done**. It may not be an impartial judge of the quality or consequences of its own conduct. For example, an agency can establish the wording of a policy, while legal analysis may be necessary to understand its effect. A company's manual can specify supported charging equipment, while independent testing may be necessary for competing performance claims. The right source depends on the precise question.

### If you cannot establish the publisher

Sometimes the original author is unknown or a page lacks an identifiable operator. You may still learn that a claim circulated, but you should not present its assertions as verified. For serious claims, an unknown publisher is reason to seek stronger evidence, not reason to conclude the claim is automatically false. You can also leave the question unresolved. Saying "I could not confirm the source" is more responsible than inventing certainty.

> **Source identity checkpoint:** Ask who published the page, who authored the claim, whether the publisher really controls the displayed domain, what incentives or expertise matter, and what independent information confirms the identity.

## 4. What evidence supports an online claim?

A source's reputation is valuable, but it does not replace the evidence for the **specific statement you intend to use**. A strong online claim should be supported by information that another reader can reasonably examine: a referenced record, a dataset, a clear method, an authenticated interview, a documented observation, or an explanation that makes its reasoning testable. Some subjects permit direct confirmation; others involve interpretation or genuine uncertainty. Learning to recognize different kinds of support helps you avoid accepting or rejecting material by reputation alone.

### Identify exactly what is being claimed

A [[claim|claim]] is a statement presented as true or likely true. It may describe something that happened, a relationship between events, a measurement, a prediction, a recommendation, or a person's judgment. Before evaluating, rewrite the claim in one precise sentence. If it contains words like *always*, *every*, *proven*, or *guaranteed*, determine whether the evidence genuinely supports that breadth.

For example, "This method helped 20 volunteers complete a typing exercise" is narrower than "This method makes everyone a fast typist." The first describes a particular sample and observation; the second makes a universal performance claim. The first cannot, by itself, establish the second. Even if the experiment happened, the inference could overreach.

Distinguish a [[fact|factual assertion]], a [[value-judgment|value judgment]], an [[opinion|opinion]], and a [[prediction|prediction]]. "The park opened in 1974" is a statement about a historical event. "The park is beautiful" is a judgment. "Attendance will double next year" is a prediction that needs assumptions and uncertainty, not just past attendance. An opinion may be informed and worth reading; the problem arises when it is presented as established fact without supporting evidence.

### Original evidence and secondary explanations

A [[primary-source|primary source]] is an original record or firsthand source directly relevant to the question: a policy document for its text, an original research paper for what researchers reported, a recorded speech for a speaker's exact wording, or a historical letter for what its author wrote. A [[secondary-source|secondary source]] interprets, summarizes, contextualizes, or analyzes other evidence. Both can be valuable. A qualified analysis may reveal mistakes in a primary account; an original document may settle a disagreement about what was actually issued.

Do not treat "primary" as meaning "true in every detail." An eyewitness can misremember. A statistical dataset may contain measurement limits. An official announcement may be revised. A photograph can be authentic but framed narrowly. The purpose of tracing to an original is to know what it actually shows and **where its evidentiary limits begin**.

Imagine that a neighborhood newsletter states, "The city approved a new bicycle route." It links to a city meeting agenda. An agenda might establish that a proposal was discussed; it may not prove that the proposal passed. The stronger source would be the adopted ordinance, meeting minutes showing the vote, or current transportation map where relevant. Find the document that addresses the claim's **specific status**.

### A citation is not automatically support

A [[citation|citation]] points to another source. The presence of footnotes, links, or quotations can improve transparency because readers can inspect support, but citations can also be irrelevant, selective, broken, or misinterpreted. Check whether a cited item actually contains the claimed fact. Follow the cited passage, not just the homepage. Ask whether it refers to the same product version, event, place, or measured population.

For example, a promotional article may write "Researchers have proved that every person needs exactly eight hours of sleep" and cite a paper discussing average sleep patterns. Without reviewing the study's actual subjects, range, and findings, you cannot conclude that it proves a universal number. In consequential health matters, use reliable health information and qualified professional advice rather than converting a popular article's oversimplification into personal guidance.

### Data and numbers: three questions

When a page offers [[data|data]], examine the measurement, source, and comparison. A number with many decimal places looks precise but may rest on uncertain or incomplete input. A percentage increase cannot be interpreted without knowing **from what starting amount**. A chart's vertical scale, omitted categories, missing timeframe, and inconsistent units can change what readers think it shows.

Ask: *What is measured? Who collected it? How does the comparison work?* A city newsletter saying that library visits "increased by 50%" could describe a rise from 2 to 3 visitors at a small event or from 200,000 to 300,000 annual visits at a system. Those are very different stories even though the percentage calculation is the same. Also ask whether statistics refer to individual people, visits, transactions, dollars, or percentages. Mixing these units can create false comparisons.

A [[methodology|methodology]] is the procedure used to collect and analyze evidence. You do not need advanced mathematics to notice whether a survey says how many people participated, how they were selected, and what they were asked. An anonymous social-media poll of self-selected volunteers is not automatically representative of a city. A scientific paper's peer review can be a useful quality-control signal, but publication and review are not guarantees that every result is correct or will generalize.

| Evidence presented | Reasonable question to ask | Common limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Official calendar | Which branch, year, holiday, or revision applies? | A genuine archived calendar may be outdated |
| Quotation from a speaker | Is the wording complete and attributable? | Excerpts can omit qualifiers or surrounding questions |
| Photograph or video | Who recorded it, when, where, and of what? | Authentic footage may be paired with a false caption |
| Study or technical report | Who participated; what method and limitations? | A narrow result may be described as universal |
| Chart or percentage | What are the units, baseline, and full time range? | Selective scales or missing context can distort impression |
| Expert statement | Does the person's expertise cover the specific topic? | Credentials do not validate unrelated claims |
| Link to a document | Does the linked record support this exact conclusion? | The link may be to a proposal rather than an enacted rule |
| Multiple articles citing one another | What independent original record do they use? | Repetition may result from a single unverified source |

### Read beyond a headline

A [[headline|headline]] summarizes or attracts attention; it does not usually contain all qualifying details. The article may acknowledge uncertainty that the headline omits. An older article's headline may remain in search results after a correction. An image caption may contain distinctions that are lost in social-media reposting. Read the beginning, the source discussion, and the conclusion—and follow the relevant link to the primary material when the conclusion matters.

Some websites use [[clickbait|clickbait]]: emotionally charged or sensational framing designed to encourage clicks, sharing, or extended attention. The presence of a dramatic headline is a reason to inspect the evidence rather than an automatic proof of falsehood. An urgent official warning can be dramatic because the circumstances genuinely are urgent. Assess accuracy through sources and context, not tone alone.

### Recognize uncertainty and qualified language

Careful writing often distinguishes "suggests," "may," "associated with," and "causes." These words have different meanings. If two developments occur together, that alone does not establish that one caused the other. A source that gives uncertainty ranges, describes methods, explains limitations, and corrects mistakes may be more trustworthy than one that claims perfect certainty.

A correction note can show that a publisher acknowledges and addresses errors. It can also alert you that an earlier version was inaccurate. Inspect what changed and whether the corrected version now supports the claim. An absence of visible corrections does not prove the publication has never made mistakes. A retraction, where applicable, is a serious signal to re-evaluate the associated work, not a minor formatting change.

### Worked example: an out-of-context statistic

Suppose a search result reads, "Survey shows 80% of residents use the new transit route." The story itself says **80% of the 50 survey respondents had taken the route once**, and the questionnaire was distributed in a transit-app user group. The headline quietly changes the measured group from *respondents in a convenience sample* to *all residents*, and changes "used once" into a broader statement about use. A sound summary would preserve the sample and method rather than generalize. The issue is not arithmetic—the count of 40 out of 50 is indeed 80%—but unsupported inference.

### A practical evidence checklist

For a claim you are likely to repeat, try stating: "This source says [claim]. It bases that statement on [record or observation]. That evidence applies to [population/place/time]. It does not by itself establish [limitation]." If you cannot complete the sentence, the claim may need more verification before you pass it on. Your objective is not to eliminate all uncertainty; it is to avoid pretending uncertainty has disappeared.

> **Evidence checkpoint:** Strong evidence connects a particular claim to a relevant record or sound method. Names, citations, charts, and confident language are useful only insofar as they actually support the statement being made.

## 5. How do you verify claims across sources and time?

Verification is an active process. You do not merely compare how many pages repeat the same statement; you investigate whether different sources have **independent reasons** for believing it. A straightforward technique is [[lateral-reading|lateral reading]]—temporarily leaving the original page to check the publisher, claim, and original materials using other credible sources. This can be done in separate browser tabs, by writing down the claim and researching it independently, or on paper from supplied materials.

### First: search for the source itself

When an unfamiliar website claims to be the "National Technology Authority" or a "University Center for Digital Safety," do not rely solely on its own About page. Search the organization name and look for independent evidence of its existence and role. If it claims to be part of a university, try finding it through the university's own known site, institutional directory, or linked department listing. If it claims to publish a government rule, locate the official rule through the responsible government body. If it claims to be a manufacturer, compare it with the company's documented support and product pages.

Be careful about similarly named organizations. A well-known name can be copied or combined with plausible words. A registered business, a nonprofit, a professional association, and a government agency are not interchangeable simply because their titles sound official. The relevant question is whether the organization is **the particular authority responsible for your question**, not whether its name is impressive.

### Second: seek independent corroboration

[[corroboration|Corroboration]] means finding information from a separate source that materially supports a claim. Effective corroboration requires independence. If three websites reproduce the same press release, they may all originate from one source. If two news articles cite the same anonymous social post, their shared wording does not establish two eyewitnesses. Ask what each outlet actually observed, documented, or verified independently.

For a basic factual event, you might consult the official event organizer and a competent local news report. For a historic date, an archive and professional historical reference may be suitable. For a scientific claim, look for an original paper plus a careful review or professional guidance. The right combination depends on the question. Neither a fixed number of links nor a single universal "trusted websites list" can replace judgment.

### Third: trace the claim upstream

[[original-context|Original context]] includes the complete statement, surrounding material, date, and circumstances that give a quotation, image, or number its meaning. Tracing means following the claim toward its original documentation, rather than passing it from one summarizing page to another. Start with the links or citations, search an exact phrase, or find the responsible organization's record. If the original is unavailable, describe the gap rather than inventing what it must contain.

Suppose four posts say "The library banned laptops" and all cite a photo of a sign. An examination of the full sign may show "Laptops temporarily unavailable for loan due to repairs"—a policy about borrowing devices, not a rule against patrons bringing them inside. The rumor is not disproved by counting posts; it is resolved by restoring the missing context and checking the library's present policy.

### Fourth: check date, place, and scope

Separate four kinds of time references: **when the event happened**, **when an account was written**, **when the page was updated**, and **when a rule or schedule takes effect**. These may differ by months or years. Similarly, distinguish between nationwide rules, state rules, county policies, employer instructions, school procedures, and product-version requirements. An answer can be true for one setting and false in another without either source being fraudulent.

Location-specific search results can also reflect your device's estimated location or the search provider's assumptions. A "nearest branch" result may refer to a different city with the same name. Verify the full branch name, street or neighborhood, and published schedule. Do not share your exact home address just to complete a beginner verification exercise. Use a public landmark or fictional location if a location reference is useful for practice.

### A disciplined comparison table

| Question to compare | Source A | Source B | What you conclude |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who produced each statement? | Named operator | Unnamed repost | Investigate the operator and repost's origin |
| What is each claiming? | Event planned | Event already happened | These are different factual claims |
| What is the relevant time? | Prior-year announcement | Current-year schedule | Use the appropriate date and update |
| What original evidence is given? | Link to adopted policy | Link to a proposal | Only one may establish enactment |
| Are sources independent? | Direct record | Reprint of direct record | Reprint is not a second independent confirmation |
| What remains uncertain? | No holiday exception mentioned | Holiday closure alert | Resolve the exception before visiting |

### Lateral reading on a desktop

On Windows 11 with Microsoft Edge, you can read a webpage in one tab and open a second tab with **Ctrl + T**. Enter the unfamiliar publisher's name or the claim's key phrase. Review independent search results, examine suitable official or professional sources, and return to the first tab to compare. In Chrome and Firefox on Windows the general approach is similar; menus and shortcuts can vary. On a Mac, Command + T commonly opens a new tab in major browsers. On phones and tablets, use the browser's tab switcher. No specific browser brand is necessary to perform the reasoning process.

Do not turn the act of opening many tabs into proof of thoroughness. One original document and one genuinely independent, well-sourced account may answer a question better than 25 repetitive pages. Keep a short note: the claim, source, date, and unresolved question. If you choose to bookmark the resulting source, remember a bookmark stores a route back, not an independent record of the source's correctness or past content.

### When sources disagree

Disagreement can arise because sources discuss different definitions, time periods, populations, jurisdictions, versions, or stages of a process. Before deciding that one is deceptive, check whether the apparent conflict disappears when those details are aligned. For example, a news story might report a proposed policy while an official current document reports that the policy was never enacted. An older article may be accurate as a statement of what was proposed at the time; it cannot be used as evidence that the proposal took effect.

Sometimes genuine conflict remains. Then ask whose methods are transparent, who has direct access to the relevant evidence, whether a correction has been issued, and whether the claim is contested among qualified specialists. Avoid the false conclusion that two opposing statements must be equally supported merely because there are two "sides." Also avoid treating the majority of unexamined search results as a vote on truth. Evidence quality matters more than rhetorical symmetry or numerical repetition.

### Example: a miscaptioned historical photograph

A fictional image appears with the caption "City hall during the 1924 flood." A local archive describes the same photograph as "City hall during a 1932 parade." Both descriptions cannot apply literally to the depicted event without additional explanation. You might compare identifiable landmarks, archival catalog data, contemporary newspapers, photographer notes, and the image's collection provenance. The archival attribution is a useful lead, not an automatic end to historical inquiry. A responsible answer might be "The archive catalog identifies it as a 1932 parade; I have not confirmed the date independently," rather than confidently repeating the viral caption.

### A stopping rule for everyday verification

Verification should support decisions, not become an endless search. For a low-stakes question, stop when a suitable responsible source clearly answers the exact question and no meaningful contradiction remains. For a higher-stakes question, seek independent, qualified confirmation and use appropriate direct channels. When a claim is unresolved, you can state that uncertainty and refrain from sharing it as fact. A sensible stopping rule protects both accuracy and time.

> **Cross-check rule:** Compare sources that have distinct evidence, not just distinct logos. Restore dates, jurisdictions, definitions, and original context before treating a disagreement as a conflict of facts.

## 6. How do you handle misleading, uncertain, or high-stakes information?

The web contains mistakes, jokes, satire, old content, advertising, impersonation attempts, deliberate deceptions, and completely sincere misunderstandings. Not every inaccurate statement is intentionally malicious. You can respond to all of these with the same initial discipline: identify the claim, inspect the source, verify its evidence, and avoid amplifying material whose accuracy you do not know. Intent is often difficult to establish; the first practical task is assessing whether the claim is supported.

### Misinformation, disinformation, and missing context

[[misinformation|Misinformation]] is inaccurate or misleading information that may be shared without an intent to deceive. [[disinformation|Disinformation]] is misleading or false information deliberately created or distributed to deceive. A beginner usually cannot determine a stranger's intent from one post; do not assign motive without evidence. You can still identify a false statement, misleading attribution, or missing context. A genuine photo can be attached to a false event date; an authentic quote can be excerpted so its meaning changes; a correct statistic can be applied to the wrong population.

This is why verification should prioritize the **claim** over the apparent emotional sincerity of the person sharing it. A friend may forward something inaccurate in good faith. Respectfully asking "Where did this number originally come from?" is often more productive than insulting the person who shared it. Likewise, a site can publish a mistake and later correct it without having intended deception.

### Recognize common warning signs without overreading them

Potential warning signs include demands to act immediately, claims that all other sources are hiding "the truth," missing authorship, a suspiciously copied logo, an advertisement disguised as a neutral review, and a request for passwords or banking details unrelated to your information need. Other clues include impossible claims without evidence, reused photographs, sensational headlines that differ from article content, and pages that do not disclose a clear operator. These clues justify checking further; **none alone is a universal formula for truth or falsehood**.

A strong emotional reaction can also interfere with judgment. If a result makes you angry, frightened, excited, or unusually hopeful, that is a reason to pause before sharing. The reaction may be entirely reasonable; the pause creates time to assess evidence independently. Confidence, excitement, outrage, and the number of social-media shares do not establish whether a claim is true.

### Images, video, and manipulated media

An image can be edited, cropped, generated, mislabeled, or presented out of context. A video can show a real scene while a caption assigns the wrong place or date. [[synthetic-media|Synthetic media]] includes content produced or substantially altered by automated generation technologies. Some synthetic media are openly labeled art or simulation; other presentations can mislead. A plausible face, voice, weather scene, or official-looking seal is not by itself evidence that a depicted event occurred.

Look for the earliest credible record, the source's description, location clues, and independent reporting where available. Reverse image search or other investigation tools can help locate earlier uses, but results may be incomplete. A missing earlier match does not prove authenticity, and a visually strange detail does not by itself prove AI generation. In important cases, specialists may need original files, metadata, and provenance evidence. Do not attempt to identify private people from images as part of beginner coursework.

### Search summaries can sound more certain than their support

An [[ai-generated-summary|AI-generated summary]] may combine sources with different dates, paraphrase a sentence incorrectly, or present a plausible invented detail. It may also be largely accurate. Treat it as a provisional orientation, not as a certified reference. Click into the cited material and ensure the source actually makes the specific statement. If no appropriate source is provided, seek one independently. A generated bibliography, quotation, or legal citation can be wrong even when formatted convincingly.

For school or workplace writing, clearly distinguish your own summary from quoted material and identify the source of important facts. Do not present AI-generated wording as though it were a direct quotation from a named person unless you have checked the original. Follow your institution's rules about acceptable AI assistance, citation, and privacy.

### Risks involving personal information and money

A search result may lead to an unofficial payment portal, fake login page, or service that requests personal records. If a supposed utility, government agency, school, or bank asks you to "confirm" credentials through a link found in a surprising advertisement, do not type the information. Reach the organization through a previously known official app, bookmarked site verified earlier, official statement, or number from an account document—not from the suspicious page. Recognize that a top search placement, matching logo, and HTTPS do not establish legitimacy.

A beginner lesson should never require entering real passwords, account numbers, medical records, identity numbers, or home addresses. For an actual important transaction, pause when uncertain and verify through a trusted independent channel. Do not perform a test purchase or login merely to see whether a suspicious site works.

### High-stakes searches require more than ordinary source checking

Health guidance, legal requirements, investment decisions, benefits eligibility, tax deadlines, and emergency instructions can affect safety or major financial outcomes. An article may be informative but too general to guide a particular person's case. Check the responsible authority, jurisdiction, date, and underlying evidence; then consult an appropriately qualified professional when the decision requires individual interpretation. For urgent safety concerns, rely on established emergency services or responsible local authorities rather than an unverified snippet.

A purported treatment claim should be evaluated against reliable health sources and professional guidance. A claimed legal deadline may depend on the court, state, filing type, or date; verify the actual applicable authority. An investment promise that says "guaranteed returns" deserves special skepticism and independent regulatory verification. The goal is not to memorize every domain's rules in this class—it is to recognize when a web search is **not enough**.

| Scenario | Risk to notice | Responsible next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Search ad asks for a bank password to check an article | Credential collection unrelated to reading | Do not enter credentials; leave and use the bank's known official channel |
| Viral photo claims to show today's disaster | Date, origin, and location unverified | Check responsible authorities and credible independent coverage |
| AI answer gives a legal deadline | Jurisdiction and current procedure may differ | Verify the actual court/agency instructions; seek legal advice as needed |
| Product review compares two devices | Paid sponsorship or affiliate incentive | Check disclosures, original specifications, and independent tests |
| Public notice names a new school policy | Could be proposed, outdated, or from another district | Find the district's current adopted policy and effective date |
| Old article repeats as breaking news | Historical date detached from republication | Establish when the event occurred and what has changed |
| Search result offers a suspicious download | Unknown software and unverified operator | Do not download or run it; use the verified vendor source if needed |
| Research summary makes a sweeping numerical claim | Sample, units, method, and uncertainty omitted | Locate the research and read what was actually measured |

### Share responsibly

Before forwarding an eye-catching claim, ask whether you have identified the primary evidence and checked the relevant date. If you cannot, consider not sharing it. If you need to discuss it, phrase the uncertainty precisely: "I found this claim but have not verified it," or "This is an older proposal, not evidence of a current rule." Adding a caution is not a substitute for verification when the post could cause harm, but it is better than repeating an unsupported statement as settled fact.

### When to stop interacting with a page

If a page begins demanding payment, software installation, remote-control permission, authentication codes, or personal information in a context that does not make sense, **do not continue just to investigate**. You can leave the page, close the tab, and use a trusted route to the responsible organization. Do not download files from suspicious pages, bypass security warnings, or call numbers displayed by a possible impersonation site. Later lessons on online accounts and safe web browsing develop the protective workflow in greater detail.

> **Safety principle:** Verification is not an invitation to engage with suspicious material. When the claimed source or its demands cannot be authenticated, disengage and check through a separate trusted channel.

## 7. Guided practice: Evaluate before you rely

The exercises below are designed for [[observation-first|observation-first]] learning. You can answer entirely from the printed scenarios; opening websites is **optional**, not required for a passing demonstration. No learner should be asked to disclose real search history, personal accounts, health conditions, financial records, identity documents, or home address. The invented organizations and statements are teaching examples, not real notices or directions. Where a web address is needed to illustrate formatting, it is presented as an example rather than an endorsed destination.

For each activity, record four things: **the claim**, **the most suitable source**, **the missing information**, and **the responsible next step**. You may speak your response, type a short paragraph, or write it on paper. Compare your decision with the worked guidance. Different wording is acceptable when the reasoning is accurate.

### Activity 1: Choose the correct source for a real-world task

**Skill:** Distinguish relevance, authority, recency, and sufficient evidence.

**Scenario:** You need to know whether the fictional *Lakeside Public Library — North Branch* is open on the upcoming Monday, which is a scheduled public holiday. The search page presents:

- **Result A:** "North Branch now open until 8 p.m." — a 2023 community newspaper story about a normal weekday schedule.
- **Result B:** "North Branch — locations and hours" — a branch page on the library system's verified official website, with ordinary opening hours and a link titled "Holiday closures."
- **Result C:** "Popular places near Lakeside" — a directory listing that says open until 7 p.m., with no explanation of its update date.
- **Result D:** "Lakeside Library history" — a well-written encyclopedia account describing the branch's founding in 1992.

**Step 1 — Identify your task.** Write the branch and the particular date. Why would "What time does the library normally close?" be an incomplete form of the question?

**Step 2 — Rank the usefulness of the sources for this task.** Identify which result offers the most direct path to the current holiday schedule, which gives historical context, and which could be both genuine and outdated.

**Step 3 — Plan a safe verification.** State what you would inspect after opening the official page. If the holiday page is missing, what independent method could you use that does not involve trusting a search advertisement?

**Step 4 — Decide.** Can you presently state that the library will be open at 7 p.m. on the holiday? Explain why or why not.

**Worked guidance:** Result B is the best starting point because the relevant institution controls its own branch schedule and specifically links to holiday closures. Result A might be accurate about normal hours in 2023; it does not settle an upcoming holiday. Result C can help discover the branch but gives no firm update evidence. Result D is credible for historical background but largely irrelevant to today's schedule. A careful student checks the current holiday notice, location and date; if still unresolved, uses the library's verified main-site contact method. **No closing time can be concluded from these supplied facts alone.**

**Transfer question:** Would the same evidence be sufficient to decide when a different branch is open? No. The same library system may give branches different schedules, and a correct schedule for one branch is not a schedule for all branches.

### Activity 2: Inspect the actual destination, not the display name

**Skill:** Separate branding, result appearance, address identity, and secure transport.

**Scenario:** A search for a fictional training institution called *Harbor Learning Center* shows these illustrative addresses:

1. `https://www.harborlearning.example.org/classes`
1. `https://harborlearning.example.org.sign-in-check.invalid/verify`
1. `https://example.org/community/harbor-learning-review`
1. `https://example.org/harborlearning-advertisement`

These addresses are placeholders and are **not** meant to be opened. Assume a trusted public directory lists the institution's published domain as `harborlearning.example.org` for this invented example. One search listing uses the second address and a logo visually resembling the institution's own logo. Another is explicitly labeled "Sponsored." A student wonders whether every line with "harborlearning" can be trusted.

**Step 1 — Read the domains.** Identify which address is *under the listed institutional domain* and which contains that name merely as an earlier component of another host name. In the second address, what site name appears at the end of the hostname?

**Step 2 — Read the purposes.** Which example is a third-party review? Which appears to be a promotional page? Which path sounds like a credential check?

**Step 3 — Apply the security principle.** Does the presence of `https://` establish that the second site is controlled by the institution? Does a visually familiar logo establish it? Why not?

**Step 4 — Choose the next action.** If a webpage unexpectedly asks you to enter an institution password, what is safer than trying the form?

**Worked guidance:** Address 1 is under the stated example domain. Address 2 is under `sign-in-check.invalid`, not under `example.org`, and its `/verify` path cannot establish legitimacy. Addresses 3 and 4 are under `example.org`, with different apparent purposes; their content would need evaluation on its own. The string "harborlearning" can occur anywhere in an address without making the site official. The presence of HTTPS does not verify its operator or its honesty. Do not enter a password in the suspicious form. Use the institution's previously verified homepage or another established official channel.

**Accessibility alternative:** If reading a long URL is difficult, break it into chunks from the left, identify the hostname, and find the registered base domain toward the right. A screen reader may announce punctuation; a teacher can provide the names as spelled-out text without forcing a student to click a questionable link.

### Activity 3: Trace a statistic to its actual evidence

**Skill:** Identify a claim, measurement, sample, and exaggerated inference.

**Scenario:** A fictional article headlines: "Almost everyone favors the new community garden." Its body reads: "In a voluntary online poll sent to 100 members of the Garden Club, 40 people responded. Of those 40 respondents, 34 favored the proposed garden design." A separate social-media post repeats the headline as "85% of the town supports the proposal."

**Step 1 — Identify the actual measurement.** Who was invited to participate? How many answered? How many supported the design?

**Step 2 — Calculate the respondent percentage.** Divide 34 by 40 and convert to a percent. This basic arithmetic can be done on paper or a calculator. Does it tell you what every resident thinks?

**Step 3 — Identify the missing evidence.** How were participants selected? How many town residents were not included? Could voluntary responses differ from the whole population? What question was asked?

**Step 4 — Rewrite the result accurately.** Write a sentence that keeps the sample limitation and does not suggest a representative town-wide survey.

**Worked guidance:** Thirty-four of the 40 respondents favored the design, or **85% of respondents**. Only members of the Garden Club were invited, and 60 invited members did not respond. The poll does not establish the opinions of the entire town. An accurate statement would be: "In a voluntary poll of 40 responding Garden Club members, 34 supported the proposed design." It is possible to report an arithmetic fact accurately while declining the broader claim. The correct response is not "the percentage is fake"; it is "the percentage has been applied beyond the evidence."

**Extension:** Imagine the article supplies a colorful bar chart but omits that the sample consists only of club members. Would the chart change the evidentiary weakness? No. A clear graph can display limited data without making that data representative.

### Activity 4: Read laterally and identify copied information

**Skill:** Investigate a publisher and distinguish independent confirmation from repetition.

**Scenario:** A viral message says a fictional local museum will close permanently next week. Four websites repeat the statement. Your worksheet shows these source relationships:

- **Page A:** An anonymous forum message with no supporting evidence, published Monday.
- **Page B:** A community blog published Tuesday that links only to Page A.
- **Page C:** A local directory published Wednesday that copies Page B's wording without naming another source.
- **Page D:** The museum's own verified Wednesday statement: "The east gallery closes for repairs next week; the rest of the museum remains open."
- **Page E:** A local reporter's article quoting the museum director and linking to the repair announcement.

**Step 1 — Draw the information chain.** What are B and C actually relying on? Does having three pages (A, B, and C) establish three independent confirmations?

**Step 2 — Compare the actual claims.** What is different between "museum closes permanently" and "east gallery closes temporarily"? Identify the scope and duration.

**Step 3 — Check the evidence.** Which provided source directly describes the operator's current plan? What does the reporter add, and what would still need verification if there were a genuine contradiction?

**Step 4 — Draft a responsible response.** Suppose a friend sends the original viral message. What would you say without accusing your friend of dishonesty?

**Worked guidance:** A → B → C is one unverified claim repeated through multiple pages, not three separate investigations. The verified statement from the museum contradicts a permanent closure: it describes a temporary, limited gallery repair. The reporter provides an additional, attributable account, although a quotation by itself is not magically error-proof. A suitable reply is: "I found the museum's current notice. It says the east gallery will close temporarily for repairs, but the rest of the museum remains open. The original post appears to describe a much broader closure." This corrects the claim using documented scope and date.

**Reasoning check:** If the museum later announces a complete closure, should you continue repeating Wednesday's statement indefinitely? No. You would check the newer decision and explain the chronology. A source can be accurate on Wednesday and become outdated by Friday.

### Activity 5: Compare dates, stages, and summaries

**Skill:** Recognize changing plans, misleading snippets, and unsupported AI certainty.

**Scenario:** You are checking whether the fictional *Pinebrook School Board* has approved a later start time. Your worksheet shows:

- **January 10:** Staff presentation titled "Proposal: consider starting school at 9:00 a.m."
- **February 2:** News report titled "Board to vote on start time next month."
- **March 9:** Adopted minutes recording a vote to **postpone** the proposal pending a transportation study.
- **April 1:** Search AI summary: "Pinebrook schools now start at 9:00 a.m., according to district documents."
- **April 3:** District's current student calendar still lists the existing start time and says it has not changed.

**Step 1 — Make a timeline.** Identify which records describe a proposal, planned consideration, an actual decision, and currently published operating information.

**Step 2 — Test the AI summary.** What is the most important unsupported leap in the short generated answer? Why does including the phrase "according to district documents" not resolve that problem?

**Step 3 — Verify the current status.** Which specific original record would you inspect first? What additional documentation might change the answer in the future?

**Step 4 — Write a one-sentence conclusion.** Include the phrase "as of April 3" and avoid claiming that the proposal will never return.

**Worked guidance:** January presents an idea, February anticipates a vote, and March records postponement rather than approval. The April 1 generated summary turns a proposed schedule into an enacted one. The current district schedule is consistent with the postponement. A defensible conclusion is: "As of April 3, the district has not implemented the proposed 9:00 a.m. starting time; the board postponed the proposal and the current published schedule remains unchanged." A later vote or revised calendar could change that conclusion, so a present-tense claim needs a date.

**Follow-through:** A teacher may ask the class to underline words that signal legal or administrative status: *proposed*, *scheduled for a vote*, *adopted*, *postponed*, *effective*, and *withdrawn*. They are not synonyms. A headline that swaps one for another changes the factual meaning.

### Activity 6: Decide whether to use, qualify, check, or stop

**Skill:** Apply proportionate verification and privacy-conscious action to different stakes.

Read these five short cases. For each, choose a response among **Use for this narrow purpose**, **Use with qualification**, **Verify further**, or **Stop and use a trusted channel**. Some cases can require more than one action in sequence.

**Case A — Low stakes:** An established public dictionary defines an unfamiliar adjective, and the definition matches the sentence you are reading. No sensitive action is involved.

**Case B — Historical context:** A scholarly article discusses a nineteenth-century event but does not identify the collection holding a quotation. You plan to quote those exact words in an assignment.

**Case C — Shopping pressure:** A "special verification" webpage says that before you can read a consumer advice article, you must enter a card number and a password. Its result appeared at the top of a search page.

**Case D — Time-sensitive notice:** A public school's website displays an ordinary daily schedule, while its dated weather notice says buses are delayed today. You need to know today's arrival arrangement.

**Case E — Uncertain image:** A dramatic social-media image is captioned as a recent local event, but you cannot establish where it was taken and find only reposts of the same image.

**Worked guidance:**

- **A:** Use for the narrow definition, while still reading the example in context. Spending an hour locating five dictionaries would not necessarily improve this ordinary task.
- **B:** Verify further by locating the original document, archival citation, or trustworthy edition before using an exact quotation. An academic interpretation can be useful, but quotation accuracy calls for the actual words and context.
- **C:** Stop. Do not enter credentials or card information. Find the intended information through a verified source without engaging the apparent collection trap.
- **D:** Use the dated special notice alongside the normal schedule; confirm that it applies to the correct school and day. The daily schedule may be valid ordinarily but overridden today.
- **E:** Do not share as verified. Search for credible provenance or responsible reporting; if none is available, state that the event and location are unconfirmed or refrain from circulation.

### Practice record and scoring guidance

For a reproducible classroom check, students can use the following worksheet. One row may represent a whole activity or one claim being investigated. The point is to make the decision traceable, not to reward students for choosing the teacher's preferred website design.

| Worksheet field | What the learner records | Example of an adequate entry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question or claim | Exact statement to evaluate | "The North Branch is open on the holiday" |
| Context | Relevant time, place, category, or version | "North Branch, upcoming Monday holiday" |
| Source and origin | Publisher and what evidence it actually has | "Current official branch page links to holiday schedule" |
| Missing or conflicting information | Unresolved issue | "Normal hours do not establish holiday hours" |
| Decision and next step | Use, qualify, check, or stop | "Check official holiday notice before travel" |

An instructor can assess performance on five criteria, each scored 0–2: **accurate claim identification**, **appropriate source identification**, **evidence evaluation**, **attention to date/place/scope**, and **safe, justified decision**. A score of 2 means the student demonstrates the criterion clearly, 1 means the reasoning is partly present, and 0 means it is missing or contradicts the evidence. Ten points are possible per activity. This rubric is optional classroom guidance rather than a built-in dashboard gradebook feature.

**Practical success standard:** The learner can name a source and explain **why it is suited to the claim**, distinguish original evidence from repetition, recognize when the information is too old or too broad, and refuse an unsafe information request. A correct answer with no reasoning is less informative than a carefully justified answer that identifies what remains unknown.

### Optional live practice using a public, low-risk topic

A student who has permission to browse can choose a harmless, verifiable topic: the date a national park was established, an encyclopedia definition of a computing term, or the publication year of a public report. Search for the fact, identify the publisher, find the responsible original record if available, and compare an independent educational reference. Write down the question, source title, source URL, and the relevant date. Do not sign in or accept unexpected download prompts. If a public website is unavailable, use the prepared paper cases; a live connection is not necessary to master the reasoning.

For an MGU practice option, students may open the university's [Computer Science encyclopedia](/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/) as a starting point for discovering terminology. The fact that it is an institutional learning resource does not eliminate the need to distinguish introductory explanation from a technical primary standard or documented contemporary claim. Check the encyclopedia page's sources and pursue original documentation when precision matters.

> **Activity summary:** Your goal is not to find an answer quickly at any cost. It is to identify which claim is supported by which evidence, record important limitations, and take an action proportionate to the consequences of error.

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

### "The first search result must be the best factual answer."

A search service orders results using complex systems designed to satisfy user needs and business rules; paid results may also be displayed. These systems do not guarantee that the first result is most authoritative or correct. A lower result might be the actual responsible institution. **Corrective habit:** Identify the source and verify its relevant original material, especially when the issue is important.

### "If a page is sponsored, it is necessarily a scam."

An advertisement can direct people to a legitimate business or resource. Its label tells you about promotional placement, not a final verdict of fraud. The converse is more dangerous: a sponsored result is not automatically trustworthy because the search service displayed it. **Corrective habit:** Treat advertising as advertising, and verify the source and claims separately from the paid placement.

### "A `.gov`, `.edu`, or `.org` ending proves everything on the page is correct."

Domain categories have different rules, and some are restricted, but a domain suffix does not validate each page, author, revision, or claim. A real site may include archived information, commentary, notices with a narrow scope, or mistakes. A fraudulent page can imitate the name of an institution in its path or subdomain. **Corrective habit:** Confirm the responsible site and the exact content's date and purpose, not just the ending.

### "HTTPS means the business is honest."

HTTPS protects web communication in transit; it is not a consumer-protection certification. An impostor can operate an HTTPS website. A webpage's visible padlock or browser connection status cannot prove that an offer is legitimate. **Corrective habit:** Verify the operator independently before entering private information or making payments.

### "Every link in a cited article must support its main conclusion."

An article can link to evidence that is too old, measures a different outcome, discusses a proposal rather than enactment, or explicitly limits the conclusion. An AI answer may display a citation whose target does not support the generated wording. **Corrective habit:** Follow the particular link and locate the specific passage that relates to the claim.

### "An expert's title settles the question."

Expertise is subject-specific. Credentials can establish a basis for informed commentary but cannot substitute for relevant data, methods, and verification. Specialists can disagree because they use different definitions or evidence. **Corrective habit:** Match the person's relevant knowledge to the narrow question, examine the reasoning, and recognize uncertainty when appropriate.

### "Three websites saying the same thing equals three independent checks."

Three articles may rely on the same unsupported social-media post or press release. Copying multiplies visibility, not independent evidence. **Corrective habit:** Trace the claim upstream and identify what, if anything, each source independently confirmed.

### "A genuine photograph cannot be misleading."

An authentic image may be attached to the wrong date, place, person, or event; it may be cropped to omit surrounding circumstances. Its authenticity and its caption's accuracy are separate questions. **Corrective habit:** Trace provenance and original context rather than assuming a real image proves the story attached to it.

### "A recent update date means every statistic is current."

A page can be edited without revisiting old data; an automatically generated page date can change while its evidence stays the same. Conversely, an old article may accurately describe a historical event even though it is not a suitable source for today's policy. **Corrective habit:** Compare the underlying evidence date and the event or rule date with your actual task.

### "A professional design is a reliability rating."

Professional design can improve readability and accessibility. It does not establish the origin, competence, or truthfulness of the publisher. A simple archive page can contain a well-documented primary source, while a polished promotional site can contain unsupported assertions. **Corrective habit:** Prioritize accountable authorship and verifiable evidence over layout.

### "If a claim is repeated by people I trust, I do not need to check it."

Well-intentioned people pass along mistakes. Familiarity and personal relationships can guide where you begin, but do not eliminate the need for evidence when a statement may affect others. **Corrective habit:** Ask gently for the original source and investigate the claim without assuming anyone intended deception.

### "If I cannot verify it, it is definitely false."

Missing evidence does not prove the opposite claim. Sometimes records are inaccessible, the matter is developing, or relevant sources disagree. **Corrective habit:** Distinguish *unsupported*, *contradicted*, *not established*, and *confirmed false*. State what you actually know and what remains unresolved.

### "I can tell whether an image is AI-generated by spotting one weird detail."

Visual anomalies may appear for many reasons, including compression, perspective, edits, or imperfect generation. Sophisticated generated media may not contain obvious artifacts. **Corrective habit:** Investigate origin, context, documentation, and independent corroboration instead of treating a single visual clue as a definitive test.

### "The latest search summary must have resolved the dispute."

A summary may synthesize incompatible sources, confuse a proposal with a decision, or silently omit an important exception. A recent answer box can still rely on old information. **Corrective habit:** Inspect the primary records and the dates; identify the exact claim the summary is supposed to support.

### "If there are two opposing articles, the truth is automatically halfway between them."

Competing claims are not necessarily equally evidenced. One article may provide original records and transparent methods; another may repeat unsupported speculation. **Corrective habit:** Compare the quality and applicability of evidence rather than splitting the difference for the appearance of neutrality.

### "I should keep clicking until I know whether a suspicious page is real."

A doubtful website may use urgency, pop-ups, downloads, credential requests, or payment forms to push the user further. Continuing to interact can expose private data or devices. **Corrective habit:** Leave the page and verify the claimed organization through a separate known official route. Do not install unknown safety scanners or bypass browser protection to finish a class exercise.

### "Checking every claim must take hours."

Most everyday questions can be resolved with a small number of targeted checks. The time needed depends on how consequential the answer is and whether meaningful disagreement remains. **Corrective habit:** State the question clearly, find the appropriate original or accountable source, and stop when the evidence is sufficient for that task. Escalate only when the stakes or uncertainty justify it.

### Trouble signs and first responses

| What you notice | What may be happening | First responsible response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Result shows old hours | Archived schedule or stale directory | Check current official branch and holiday notice |
| A citation opens an unrelated page | Broken, irrelevant, or misleading reference | Locate the specific primary record independently |
| Two pages give different dates | Event date vs. publication date, revision, or different location | Align the timeline and jurisdiction |
| Several sites repeat identical wording | Reposts or syndication from one origin | Find their shared source and independent confirmation |
| An AI answer disagrees with a record | Synthesis or extraction error | Read the original record; do not rely on the summary |
| A page claims to be official but asks for credentials | Possible impersonation or unrelated solicitation | Stop; use a separately verified official channel |
| A photo caption seems inconsistent | Cropping, miscaptioning, or context loss | Investigate provenance and earlier credible records |
| You cannot find the original evidence | Claim may be inadequately documented | Qualify as unverified; do not circulate as established fact |

> **Troubleshooting rule:** When faced with conflicting online claims, first verify the precise question, responsible publisher, original evidence, date, and scope. Do not solve uncertainty by entering sensitive data, installing unknown software, or counting repetitions as proof.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Terms shown earlier with dotted-underlined help text use MGU's linked-term tooltip convention. The technical definitions appear in the `:::terms` block after the lesson. The plain-text glossary below provides equivalent access for learners whose browser or assistive technology does not use the interactive popovers.

- **Search result:** A listing or answer displayed after a search request; a candidate source, not a verification verdict.
- **Relevance:** The degree to which information addresses the actual question or task.
- **Reliability:** The justified dependability of a source or statement for a particular purpose.
- **Credibility:** The extent to which available evidence supports trusting a source or particular claim.
- **Source evaluation:** The process of judging the identity, evidence, currency, and applicability of information.
- **Sufficiency:** Having enough suitable evidence to support the decision or conclusion at issue.
- **Publisher:** Person or organization responsible for making content available.
- **Author:** Person or credited entity responsible for creating a particular work or statement.
- **Primary source:** Original record or firsthand evidence relevant to a specific question.
- **Secondary source:** Analysis or reporting that interprets or summarizes other sources.
- **Claim:** Specific assertion presented for acceptance, examination, or verification.
- **Fact:** Statement capable of assessment against evidence of the world, distinct from personal preference.
- **Opinion:** View or interpretation that may reflect judgment rather than a directly measurable assertion.
- **Prediction:** Statement about a future event or outcome, with uncertainty.
- **Citation:** Reference enabling a reader to identify or locate supporting material.
- **Evidence:** Observation, record, data, or reasoning used to support a factual claim.
- **Corroboration:** Independent information that materially supports a claim.
- **Lateral reading:** Checking an unfamiliar source by examining outside information about it.
- **Original context:** Surrounding words, date, circumstances, and setting necessary to understand a source accurately.
- **Domain name:** Human-readable website name used to help locate an internet service.
- **HTTPS:** Encrypted web transport; not a general guarantee of website trustworthiness.
- **Sponsored result:** Advertising placement identified by a search service.
- **Snippet:** Short search-result preview that can omit relevant context.
- **AI-generated summary:** Automated synthesized answer that may contain unsupported or inaccurate statements.
- **Source provenance:** Documented origin and history of a piece of information or media.
- **Publication date:** When a source was published, distinct from when the described event occurred.
- **Revision date:** When content was changed; not necessarily when all evidence was rechecked.
- **Effective date:** Date a rule or arrangement begins to apply under its governing terms.
- **Methodology:** Procedure used to gather, assess, or analyze evidence.
- **Sample:** Subset of people or things studied, which may or may not represent a broader population.
- **Selection bias:** Distortion arising from how observations or respondents are chosen.
- **Conflict of interest:** An interest that could affect impartial judgment or presentation.
- **Expertise:** Knowledge and skill relevant to a particular subject area.
- **Misinformation:** Inaccurate or misleading information, potentially spread without intent to deceive.
- **Disinformation:** False or misleading information deliberately intended to deceive.
- **Synthetic media:** Media created or substantially altered by generative or automated methods.
- **Clickbait:** Attention-seeking framing designed primarily to obtain clicks or engagement.
- **High-stakes decision:** A decision whose error could produce important consequences.
- **Independence:** An evidence relationship not merely copied from the same underlying assertion.
- **Qualification:** Limitation or context added to prevent a claim from appearing broader or more certain than it is.
- **Observation-first learning:** Inspect and reason about an example before changing anything.

**Vocabulary principle:** Knowing a technical label is useful only when you can apply it. A beginner should be able to explain, in ordinary words, why one result is sufficient for a narrow question and another requires independent checking. Memorization without careful application is not the goal.

### Exit task: Explain your evidence decision

Imagine someone says, "I found it online, and three websites agree, so it must be true." Explain in your own words: **(1)** What would you need to know about those websites' underlying evidence? **(2)** What is the difference between finding a claim and verifying it? **(3)** How could a real photograph still carry an inaccurate caption? **(4)** What would you check before treating an old online notice as a current rule? **(5)** What should you do if a promising result requests private account information unexpectedly?

A student who can answer these questions, justify one source choice, locate a relevant original record in an example, and recognize when to pause rather than acting has achieved the core objectives of the lesson.
