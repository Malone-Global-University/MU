## Lesson 4: Search Engine Basics

You want to know how to renew a library card, find a public map of national parks, and learn what a new computer word means. You could try guessing the address of each website, but a [[search-engine|search engine]] can help you locate relevant pages. You enter a few words, review the results, and choose where to go next. This familiar activity is not the same as asking the internet itself for one perfect answer. Search engines select and organize information from many sources, and **you remain responsible for deciding which result actually addresses your question**.

In Lesson 1 you distinguished the internet from the World Wide Web. In Lesson 2 you learned how to use a browser and its address bar. In Lesson 3 you learned tabs, bookmarks, and history. This fourth lesson brings those abilities together: you will learn what search engines do, write clear search queries, recognize the components of a [[search-results-page|results page]], distinguish sponsored material from ordinary links, try simple query refinements, and locate public information without disclosing personal data. The next lesson, *Judging Search Results*, develops evidence evaluation in greater depth; here the emphasis is **finding, navigating, and improving a search**.

Microsoft Edge on Windows 11 is the principal desktop example, but the skills also apply to Google Search, Microsoft Bing, DuckDuckGo, Firefox, Chrome, Safari, Chromebooks, Macs, phones, and tablets. Layouts, labels, advertisements, AI features, and search tools change over time and vary with location and account settings. The activities use public educational topics or fictional scenarios. No required exercise involves purchasing, logging in, sharing a real address, downloading a file, changing browser settings, or entering sensitive information.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 90–120 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Internet and Web Skills Lessons 1–3 recommended · **Practice equipment:** A web browser and optional internet connection; every required activity also has an offline worksheet version · **Safety:** Use public, non-sensitive examples; inspect result addresses and labels before choosing links

> **Essential idea:** A search engine helps you **find possible sources**; a browser helps you **open and use** them. Type a clear question or keywords, read the results as a collection of different options, check where a link would lead, and improve the query when necessary. A top-ranked link, attractive summary, or AI-produced answer is not proof that the information is correct.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Define a search engine and distinguish it from the internet, a browser, an address bar, and a particular website.
1. Explain in beginner terms how crawling, indexing, and ranking help a search service find material without containing every webpage.
1. Enter a simple search through a browser's address bar or a search service's search field.
1. Convert a broad need into clear keywords, a short question, or an effective phrase.
1. Identify an ordinary result, a sponsored result, a result summary, a domain, and common search page features.
1. Distinguish a search listing, an AI-generated summary, and the actual content of a source website.
1. Improve results by revising wording, adding context, checking spelling, or using available filters.
1. Use quotation marks and `site:` as optional refinements while recognizing that search operators and behavior vary by service.
1. Search on desktop and mobile devices without confusing search history with browsing history, bookmarks, or files saved offline.
1. Use privacy-conscious, accessible, and observation-first practices when locating information online.

## 1. What is a search engine and how does it work?

A [[search-engine|search engine]] is a service that helps people discover web pages and other information by entering a [[query|query]]: one or more words, a phrase, or a question. Search services include **Google Search**, **Microsoft Bing**, and **DuckDuckGo**. They present lists of results and may also display maps, images, video links, summaries, calculations, answers, or other specialized features. They do not all have the same database, interface, search rules, or privacy practices. Nor do they show exactly the same results to every person at every time.

It helps to picture the task as using a catalog. A library catalog helps you locate books; it is not the books themselves. Similarly, a search engine provides leads to resources. It may show an extract of a resource, but you often need to visit the original website to examine context, details, citations, dates, and conditions. Some information is unavailable to search engines at all: a private document behind a login, a newly posted webpage not yet discovered, a page excluded from indexing, or content that a search system cannot access. **Search results are a selection, not a complete inventory of the web.**

### Internet, web, browser, and search engine

An [[internet|internet]] connection allows computers and services to exchange data over interconnected networks. The [[world-wide-web|World Wide Web]] is one major service that uses those networks to offer linked resources. A [[browser|web browser]] is the application that retrieves and displays websites. A search engine is a particular online service that helps locate resources. The four terms are related but not interchangeable.

| Term | Plain-language job | Everyday example | What it is not |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Internet | Connect networks and deliver data | A home or mobile internet connection | Not a particular search website |
| World Wide Web | Provide linked web resources and applications | A public library information page | Not every internet service, such as ordinary email transport |
| Browser | Open and interact with websites | Microsoft Edge, Chrome, Firefox, or Safari | Not the search engine itself |
| Search engine | Find potential resources using a query | Google Search, Bing, or DuckDuckGo | Not an independent guarantee that a source is correct |
| Website | Host related pages or services | A government agency's public information pages | Not the entire internet |
| Address bar | Accept an address and often search text | The field at the top of Edge | Not always a separate search-engine page |

You can use **Microsoft Edge** to search with **Bing**. You can also use Edge to visit **Google Search** or DuckDuckGo. Google Chrome commonly includes a search feature using a configured default provider, but Chrome is the browser, not Google Search. Safari is likewise a browser, not the search engine you select within it. Some browsers allow you to change the default provider; that configuration is unnecessary for this lesson.

### Three simplified steps: discover, store references, and order

Many web search services use a combination of [[crawling|crawling]], [[indexing|indexing]], and [[ranking|ranking]]. These terms describe related functions, not a promise that every provider uses identical methods.

1. **Crawling or discovery:** Automated programs follow links or receive other signals that help locate web content. A crawler is sometimes called a *bot* or *spider*. A crawler cannot visit every part of the web at once, and some pages are inaccessible or restricted.
1. **Indexing:** The service processes discovered material and maintains an [[index|index]] that can help retrieve relevant resources. Search indexes are large, selective, changeable data structures—not exact copies of the whole internet.
1. **Ranking and presentation:** When you search, systems select and order results using many signals related to your words, the material available, context, freshness, usefulness, and other factors. They may also show advertisements or specialized answer features. There is no universal ranking formula shared by all providers.

Consider the fictional question `how do butterflies change during metamorphosis`. The engine tries to find pages connected to the terms *butterflies*, *change*, and *metamorphosis*. It may include pages about a butterfly's life cycle even if the page title does not match your entire sentence. If a site publishes a new butterfly guide tomorrow, it might take time before a given search engine finds or updates that listing. Another provider might discover it earlier or order it differently.

### Why a search engine does not know everything

A search engine can return the wrong page, an outdated page, duplicate pages, commercially promoted pages, or pages written for a different location. A high rank is a decision by a particular retrieval system for that query, not proof that the content is true. Search engines are also not responsible for every statement published by the websites they list. Results depend on a mixture of technical systems, accessible content, and the specific words you entered.

Some web pages are designed for searches: they feature keywords in their text, headings, or titles. The term [[search-engine-optimization|search engine optimization]] describes practices intended to help pages be discovered and presented by search engines. Some are useful for accessibility and organization; others can be manipulative. Beginners need not learn SEO techniques, but they should know that a page's visibility may reflect how it was published and promoted—not just its merit.

### Search is a process, not a one-click verdict

A practical search usually includes a short sequence: **define the need → write a query → scan the results → choose a promising source → read the actual page → refine or verify as needed**. Not every task requires many searches; sometimes a known official address is more direct. If the user already knows the correct website, typing its verified address may be faster and more reliable than searching for its name every time.

For instance, if you are already on an official library website and need its opening hours, use the site's navigation or internal search before a broad web search. A general search may show directory listings, old articles, sponsored services, or another library with a similar name. **Start with the narrowest trustworthy route that meets the task.**

### Worked example: finding a public explanation

Suppose a learner wants to know what causes rainbows. They type `rainbow colors explanation` into a search engine. They receive an educational article, an image gallery, a video, a shopping listing for rainbow decorations, and perhaps an AI summary. Those are different kinds of responses to related words. The learner wants an explanation of light and water droplets, so an educational science article is a better next destination than a product advertisement. The results page helps the learner choose where to read; it does not replace the need to understand the article.

## 2. How do you turn a question into a useful search?

A good query tells a search engine what you are trying to locate. It does not have to be a complete sentence or use special technical language. Search engines often accept natural-language questions such as `what do bees pollinate` and shorter keyword groups such as `bees pollination flowers`. Both may work. A beginner's primary skill is not guessing a magical phrase; it is learning to recognize **the important concepts in the question** and adjust them when needed.

### Begin with the information you actually need

Before typing, ask yourself: **Am I trying to learn a concept, locate a known website, find a local service, compare options, or perform a task?** The intended outcome changes what words are useful. `library` is vague. `how to get a public library card` specifies an action. `public library card requirements Spokane Washington` adds a location if the learner genuinely needs locality. A learner should not add their home address, phone number, or account number to a search simply to make it more specific.

| Information need | Very broad query | Better starting query | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Understand a topic | `cloud` | `what is cloud storage beginner explanation` | Identifies the subject and desired level |
| Learn a procedure | `print` | `how to print a document from Windows 11` | Names an action and platform |
| Find a public institution | `parks` | `National Park Service official website` | Provides the organization and purpose |
| Find a definition | `browser` | `web browser definition for beginners` | Reduces unrelated interpretations |
| Explore a science topic | `moon` | `why does the moon have phases NASA` | Adds the actual question and possible source |
| Check a local rule | `recycling` | `city recycling guidelines official [city name]` | Includes the local authority rather than assuming one universal rule |
| Find a document | `map` | `United States Geological Survey earthquake map` | Names a resource and responsible institution |
| Resolve a word with several meanings | `java` | `Java programming language introduction` | Separates software from coffee and geography |

Square brackets in the example `[city name]` mean **replace the placeholder with the appropriate city name**, not the person's street address. Search queries are sent to a search provider or otherwise processed by search systems. Do not include details that are unnecessary to your educational goal.

### Select two or three useful concepts first

A [[keyword|keyword]] is a significant word or phrase describing the subject you want. For an assignment about ocean tides, useful concepts might be *ocean*, *tides*, and *moon*. You might begin with `what causes ocean tides`. For a technical definition, useful terms might be *router*, *home network*, and *beginner*. The service may understand related forms of words or approximate phrasing, but you should still tell it what matters.

Avoid adding a long chain of unrelated terms: `ocean moon weather beach fish waves holidays how old facts`. Those words describe several possible tasks, not one clear request. Search works better when one query has one primary objective. If you have three separate questions, search them separately and compare what you find.

### Use context without giving away private details

Some searches need a region, language, time period, device type, school level, or other context. `how to turn on captions in Windows 11` is more precise than `captions`, while `weather forecast [city]` may be enough for a locality-based question. Avoid putting **your exact home address**, confidential workplace information, medical account numbers, passwords, payment information, or private student details into a public search field.

When an organization controls a rule—such as a school calendar or agency eligibility requirement—its actual website and the applicable location often matter more than clever keywords. A county and a city with similar names may operate different services. If you find a state or city page, check that it applies to your place and time before treating it as instructions.

### Natural-language questions and keyword phrases

Search engines generally accept both forms. A plain question can make your learning goal clear: `Why do some leaves change color in autumn?` A shorter version can be useful if the first search is too broad: `leaf color change chlorophyll autumn`. Neither format guarantees accuracy. You are practicing a method for producing a **useful set of results**, not trying to force one provider to display a particular answer box.

Writing complete questions can be helpful for accessibility, language learning, or topics you do not yet understand. Short keyword groups can help when you know key terms, need to reduce ambiguity, or use advanced search fields. For most beginners, begin with whichever form expresses the need most clearly.

### Use the address bar or the search website

On Windows 11, open an installed browser such as Microsoft Edge. Identify its [[address-bar|address bar]] and its currently displayed page. The address bar often accepts both a complete [[url|web address]] and a search request. If you type `how do volcanoes form` and press **Enter**, the browser typically sends that text to the configured search service, rather than treating it as the name of an existing website. You can also go to a known search-engine website, select its search field, and submit the query there.

If you type an address such as `https://www.nasa.gov/`, the browser navigates to the site named by that URL rather than doing an ordinary keyword search. **The same browser field can support two different actions**: visiting an address and requesting a search. Browser configurations differ, so inspect what the browser shows after you press Enter. A search suggestion below the address bar is not necessarily the website you intended to open.

### Entering, editing, and resubmitting

1. Choose the address bar or the search field on the selected search website.
1. Type a short, public question—for example, `what is an eclipse`.
1. Review the visible text and correct mistakes before pressing **Enter** or choosing **Search**.
1. Scan the results, including whether any listing is labeled sponsored or uses an AI summary.
1. If none are useful, change one or two words in the query and search again.

Use **Ctrl + L** on many Windows desktop browsers to focus the address bar. **Ctrl + A** may select all text *when the input field is focused*; pressing it elsewhere could select page contents instead. On Mac, **Command + L** commonly focuses the address bar. On mobile devices, tap the browser's address/search field and use the on-screen keyboard. Never let shortcut memorization replace recognizing the correct field.

### Spelling, synonyms, and other languages

Many search services suggest corrections for misspelled words. A proposed correction can be helpful, but read it before accepting: two similar terms may name different people, places, programs, or products. If `how volcano errupt` appears to be misspelled, a search engine may suggest *erupt*. If a proper name is unusual, an automatic correction may alter what you meant. Try the original spelling, quotation marks around a known phrase, or a more specific context when needed.

[[synonym|Synonyms]] are alternative words with similar meanings. Someone seeking *job training* might also try *workforce training* or *vocational courses*. A beginner learning about *flash drives* might find pages titled *USB drives*. Language filters and translated pages can make search accessible to a broader audience, but automatic translations may miss nuances. For legal, health, financial, and other consequential questions, use appropriate authoritative resources and do not rely on a translated snippet alone.

### Recognize when to stop rewriting

If a search already returns several promising official or educational resources, you may not need more advanced query syntax. Open a suitable result, read its actual content, and evaluate whether it answers the question. Endless query editing without reading sources does not improve understanding. Conversely, if results mostly concern shopping when you want an explanation, adding `definition`, `how it works`, or `beginner guide` may improve the type of material presented.

## 3. What does a search results page show?

A [[search-results-page|search results page]] is the collection of items displayed after you submit a query. It is often called a **SERP**, short for *search engine results page*. Its exact appearance depends on your search provider, device, query, settings, language, region, and current software version. Some pages emphasize conventional text links. Others contain advertisements, maps, video previews, images, lists of common questions, or AI-generated summaries. A beginner should learn to identify **what kind of item they are reading** before assuming that every large box is an ordinary webpage result.

### A typical ordinary result

A traditional text-based result often presents three visible elements: a [[result-title|title]], a destination address or recognizable [[domain|domain name]], and a short [[snippet|snippet]] or description. The title is often a clickable link. The displayed address gives a clue about the site, and the snippet provides a preview that might match your query. The snippet is not necessarily a complete quotation, may omit surrounding context, and can be generated or selected by the search service.

**Fictional practice illustration, not a live search result:**

> **Understanding Moon Phases — Example Science Resource**  
> `https://science.example.org/learn/moon-phases`  
> An introduction to how the Moon's appearance changes during its orbit ...

In this made-up example, the first line is the title, the second is the address, and the third is a short preview. The domain is `science.example.org`. Notice that `example.org` is reserved for illustrative use; this address is **not a recommended live site**. In real results, look at the actual destination, not just the words of the title.

### Search result versus the page itself

The result is **a description and route to a resource**. It is not necessarily the full resource. If a snippet says, “Learn about moon phases,” that tells you what the search provider thinks is relevant. You still need to open the source to see its explanation, supporting material, publication date when applicable, and context. A search result may continue to appear even after the destination has changed; the summary may lag behind a site's latest content.

It is also possible for a result title to contain a familiar organization's name while the destination belongs to a different organization. The [[domain|domain name]] and final address matter. In beginner exercises, practice reading the visible website name and address carefully before selecting a link; in the next lesson you will learn a more complete method for evaluating authorship, evidence, and reliability.

### Sponsored results and advertisements

Some search listings are advertisements paid for by advertisers. They may be labeled **Sponsored**, **Ad**, or an equivalent term, depending on the product and version. Their placement, design, and labels can change. Advertising is a way companies purchase visibility; it is **not an independent assessment of truth or safety**. A sponsored result can be useful, irrelevant, or deceptive, just as an ordinary result can be good or bad. Do not assume that the first prominent link is the official service merely because it sits at the top.

For example, a search for how to renew a passport may produce a sponsored intermediary offering paid assistance alongside official government information. The intermediary is not necessarily the government agency. A learner looking for an official form should verify the agency's authentic domain and whether fees or intermediaries are appropriate before proceeding. **Never enter payment or identifying information merely because a listing appears first.**

### Quick answers, featured snippets, and information panels

Some search engines show a highlighted answer, calculator output, conversion tool, weather summary, dictionary definition, [[featured-snippet|featured snippet]], or information panel. These features may be convenient for an everyday fact. They are still presentations created or selected by a search system, and their accuracy and currency can vary. A direct answer about a mathematical definition is different from a statement about a changing deadline, eligibility rule, or medical decision. For important questions, inspect the underlying source rather than relying entirely on the displayed box.

An information panel may combine facts drawn from multiple sources. A **People also ask** or related-questions area presents common follow-up questions, not a single verified answer to your original need. An image or video carousel can be useful when pictures or demonstrations are the goal, but a picture preview may not reveal the owner, license, context, or accuracy of the underlying material.

### AI summaries and generated answers

Some search products display a [[ai-summary|generative AI summary]] or answer synthesized from multiple webpages. It may give an immediate explanation, offer links, and suggest follow-up prompts. It can also combine sources incorrectly, omit qualifications, or produce false statements. A fluent answer is not necessarily a trustworthy one. **Treat a generated summary as a starting point to verify, not as a substitute for the original sources.** It also is not always obvious whether cited sources support every part of a synthesized claim; read the linked material when the details matter.

You do not need to use an AI feature to complete this lesson. Many tasks can be completed by reviewing ordinary links. If a search engine shows generated text above the link list, identify it as a different content type and decide whether you need direct sources. Students must never submit confidential school, employer, client, or personal information to an optional generated-answer box for a class exercise.

### Map, image, video, news, and shopping results

Result pages may show specialized groups. A map may display places, approximate locations, or business information. Images may show thumbnails linked to hosting pages. Videos may show titles and previews; news features may highlight timely reporting; shopping listings may contain prices and merchant information. These features serve different intentions, and **a result is not always a conventional explanatory webpage**.

| What you see | What it usually represents | What to check before relying on it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary link | A page that the search service considers relevant | Title, destination, publication context, and whether it actually answers the query |
| Sponsored or ad label | Paid search placement | Genuine destination and whether the advertiser is the entity you intended |
| Short description or snippet | Preview of information from or about a source | Open the full page for details and qualification |
| Featured answer box | Highlighted response or excerpt selected by the engine | Underlying source, limitations, and freshness |
| AI-generated overview | Synthesized answer created with AI | Support for each important claim in direct sources |
| Map or local listing | Place-related information | Correct place, address, official contact details, and current status |
| Image or video result | Visual media or a preview | Original publisher, context, accessibility, and applicable rights |
| News or recent-results area | Material associated with timely events | Publication and event dates; source and updates |
| Shopping result | Product, merchant, or promotional listing | Seller, conditions, total costs, and legitimacy |

### A result's position and its date

Search ranking can vary between users and over time. A result near the top may be relevant for common interpretations of your words but fail to meet your specific need. The date shown beside a result is also not always a simple publication date. It can reflect an update or information extracted by the search service, and some pages do not show a clear date at all. A source about current school closures, deadlines, or policies requires more attention to freshness than a basic explanation of what a keyboard is.

A useful first scan is to look at several links rather than clicking the first result reflexively. Read the title, check the visible destination, notice sponsored labels, and determine whether the result belongs to the kind of source you sought. Then open one promising page and decide if it actually contains what you need. This procedure is often faster than repeatedly opening irrelevant links.

### Worked example: searching for a historical photograph

A learner searches `public domain photographs Library of Congress`. Results could include the Library of Congress website, a blog discussing historical images, a paid printing service, and an image-search collection. The student wants a historical image with usable rights information, so the Library of Congress's own collection page is a promising destination. But **the search query alone does not establish that any particular image is public domain**. The learner must inspect the item's source record and rights statement. This illustrates the difference between *finding a possible resource* and *establishing that the resource meets a specific requirement*.

## 4. How do you improve or narrow search results?

A [[query-refinement|query refinement]] is an intentional change to search wording or available controls after you examine the first results. It is a normal part of searching, not a sign of failure. If `jaguar speed` returns mostly cars, and you wanted the animal, try `jaguar animal running speed`. If you wanted a car specification, try `Jaguar automobile model speed` and identify the precise model. A few well-chosen words can disambiguate a subject more effectively than a very long question.

A good improvement begins by asking **what went wrong with the first results**. Did they answer a different question? Are they aimed at an expert rather than a beginner? Are they about the wrong location or device? Are they dominated by stores when you wanted an explanation? Is the material outdated? Each issue suggests a different revision. Repeating a query exactly may produce a similar result set, so change the terms when you can identify the mismatch.

### Strategy 1: Add one missing detail

Suppose you type `screenshots` and see results about screenshots on many devices. If you are using Windows 11, change the query to `how to take a screenshot in Windows 11`. If you need a written explanation, add `step by step`. If the first pages discuss phone shortcuts, mention `desktop keyboard`. The goal is not to force the engine to understand your private circumstances, but to provide the non-sensitive, relevant context that changes the answer.

Keep details proportionate. `how to open a PDF on Windows 11` is precise enough for a beginner. Adding the device's serial number, household address, or a screenshot of private files is unnecessary. You can often learn the generic procedure first and then consult the correct software's help system if your situation differs.

### Strategy 2: Replace an unfamiliar word with a synonym

The information you need may use different terminology. A school may describe its offerings as *continuing education* rather than *adult classes*. A computer manual might say *wireless network* instead of *Wi-Fi*. A science article may say *precipitation* instead of *rain*. Searching `how does precipitation form` and `how does rain form` can reveal different levels of explanation even when the questions overlap. Try synonyms when a query is too narrow or produces unrelated results.

Sometimes a synonym is not an exact replacement: *computer memory* could refer to RAM, storage, or human memory aids, depending on context. Add qualifying terms if alternatives introduce ambiguity. Be especially careful with abbreviations; one abbreviation can refer to several organizations or technologies. Use the full organization name or write out the meaning on the first attempt.

### Strategy 3: Put a known phrase in quotation marks

Some search services support [[quotation-search|quotation marks]] to request a closer match to a particular phrase. An example is `"digital literacy" curriculum`, which asks for resources relating to the phrase *digital literacy* and curriculum. This can be helpful for an official program title, a short quoted line, or a name that the engine otherwise separates into different words. Google, Bing, and DuckDuckGo document forms of quotation-based searching, but implementations and result behavior may differ.

Do not put every query in quotation marks. A strict phrase can omit useful pages that use a synonym or a slightly different grammatical form. `how rainbows form` is often a better first query than `"how rainbows form"` if you care about the idea rather than an exact string. A successful search provides relevant material, not necessarily a page repeating your precise question.

### Strategy 4: Search within a particular website or domain

Where supported, the [[site-operator|`site:` operator]] narrows results to an indicated domain or site area. For example:

`moon phases site:nasa.gov`

This asks a compatible search engine to look for moon-phase information associated with NASA's `nasa.gov` domain. A different query such as `national parks site:nps.gov` can focus on National Park Service materials. Notice the absence of a space between `site:` and the domain: `site: nps.gov` is not the standard supported syntax. The `site:` feature is helpful when an organization's website is difficult to navigate, but it does not prove that the search provider indexed all pages from that site.

A domain restriction is **not a validation tool**. You must still check the actual result and recognize that pages might be old, incomplete, or unrelated to your question. The operator also does not make a fake-looking domain legitimate. Learn the real organization's domain independently instead of copying a suspicious address into the restriction.

### Strategy 5: Exclude an unwanted interpretation carefully

Some engines support a minus sign immediately before a word or site restriction to exclude certain results. For example, `jaguar animal -car` may help reduce results about automobiles. The exact matching behavior differs among providers, and removing one word may also remove useful pages that mention that word for comparison. Do not use exclusions by default. First try the positive description `jaguar wildlife facts`; then use a supported exclusion only if the unwanted topic still dominates.

Search symbols are **optional tools**, not a secret language all students must memorize. If a special operator appears to produce confusing results, return to a plain question or use the service's visible filtering controls. This is particularly appropriate for students who use speech input or assistive technology and may find punctuation-based commands inconvenient.

### Strategy 6: Use available filters rather than rewriting everything

Search services may provide buttons such as **All**, **Web**, **Images**, **Videos**, **News**, **Maps**, **Shopping**, or **Tools**, depending on the provider and device. A [[search-filter|filter]] limits or emphasizes certain types of results. If you need a photograph, an image filter may help; if you need a written explanation, a conventional web-results view is often more appropriate. If your question concerns a recent announcement, a date or news-related filter may reduce older material—but you must still check the actual source and date.

Google's documented filters can vary with the language, account state, and type of search, and the **Web** filter focuses on text-based links. Bing and other providers may use different labels or arrangements. A beginner should first learn to identify the *purpose* of each filter, not memorize a fixed position on the results page. Filters can also be broad: choosing **News** does not mean every displayed item is verified, and choosing **Images** does not confer permission to reuse an image.

| Search problem | First sensible adjustment | Example | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Too broad | Add the actual question or purpose | `planet` becomes `why does Saturn have rings` | Avoid a long chain of unrelated words |
| Wrong meaning | Add context or a clarifying term | `python` becomes `Python programming language` | One word may have several meanings |
| Too technical | Add audience or level | `DNS` becomes `DNS explained for beginners` | Simple explanations can omit advanced detail |
| Different platform | Add operating system or device | `change text size Windows 11` | Check the system version shown in source |
| Wrong organization | Specify a verified institution | `national parks site:nps.gov` | Confirm the correct domain |
| Exact title needed | Use a short quoted phrase | `"digital literacy" course` | Exact matching may omit useful variants |
| Unwanted topic | Add a positive qualifier; optionally exclude | `jaguar animal facts` | Minus operators are service-specific |
| Need images or video | Use available media filters | Search then select Images or Videos | Check rights and original context |
| Need newer information | Use date tools and inspect publication details | Search a current agency notice | A filter does not certify currency |
| Too few matches | Remove restrictive operators or use synonyms | Replace an exact quote with a plain question | Sparse results do not prove no resource exists |

### Strategy 7: Compare search services when needed

Different search engines may index, rank, and present different material. If a query is unproductive on one provider, another might show more useful public resources. However, switching providers is not always necessary and is not a substitute for checking the sources. It can also expose the same query to another company with its own privacy terms. For a beginner, a well-refined query on one familiar service is a good place to start.

Some providers emphasize privacy or use different result suppliers; others provide tightly integrated maps, local listings, or generated answers. The service choice can affect experience, but this class does not endorse one commercial provider. **The central skill transfers across products:** clarify the objective, review the type and destination of results, and check the source itself.

### Strategy 8: Know when to leave the search engine

When you identify a plausible official website, open it and use the site's own search, menu, or help pages. A search engine may find an announcement, but the organization's current information might now live on a different page. For example, once you reach an official university catalog, navigate to the specific course page rather than relying only on a broad result snippet that may have been written months earlier.

It is also reasonable to return to a known bookmark instead of searching for the same familiar URL every time. Lesson 3 covered this workflow. If you find a reliable public-reference page you expect to use again, save a bookmark after verifying its location. A bookmark records the address, not a guaranteed permanent copy of the page or its present text.

### Worked example: three deliberate changes

A learner wants a basic explanation of computer networks. **First search:** `networks` returns results about social relationships, television channels, and computer systems. **Second search:** `computer networks` reduces the ambiguity but includes advanced engineering pages. **Third search:** `computer networks explained for beginners` better states the desired difficulty level. If the learner already knows an approved educational website, they might search within that site or navigate directly. The improvement came from clarifying **subject** and **audience**, not using complicated symbols.

## 5. How do you search for different kinds of information?

Although search methods are similar across devices, the way the search field appears can differ. On a Windows laptop the address bar is usually near the top of the browser window. On a phone, the combined address/search field may be at the top or bottom of the screen depending on the browser and settings. A tablet may have a desktop-like browser layout or a mobile layout. Students should focus on recognizing the **field that accepts the query**, the method used to submit it, and the actual destination of any result.

### A simple Windows 11 walkthrough

1. Open Microsoft Edge or another approved browser, and identify the active tab.
1. Select the address/search bar using the pointer, touch, or **Ctrl + L** if supported.
1. Enter a public educational query, such as `National Park Service junior ranger program`.
1. Press **Enter**, then observe the provider's results page without clicking immediately.
1. Identify one normal result and any visible **Sponsored** labeling, if present.
1. Read the domain and result description, then choose an appropriate public source.
1. Use **Back** to return to results if you want to compare another page.

You can repeat the search with `site:nps.gov` to explore how narrowing to the National Park Service's domain may change the result set. No activity requires a personal account or a browser extension. If you are working on a managed school computer, follow its policies rather than installing an alternative browser or changing organization settings.

### Device comparison: the same objective, different controls

The same search task can be completed through a browser on different platforms. Exact button positions change with browser releases and user preferences, so treat the descriptions as patterns rather than fixed screenshots.

| Platform | Usual way to begin | How to submit a query | Practical point to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Windows 11 desktop or laptop | Select a browser address/search bar or search site's field | Enter or an on-screen Search control | Recognize the configured provider and actual result domain |
| Mac desktop or laptop | Select Safari or another browser's combined address/search field | Return or a visible Search control | Command + L often focuses the browser field |
| Chromebook | Select the Chrome address/search field | Enter or the available search button | Distinguish a browser search from Files or device settings search |
| iPhone or iPad | Tap the browser or search app's visible field | On-screen Search or Go | Address bar placement can differ by app or settings |
| Android phone or tablet | Tap the browser or search app's visible field | On-screen Search or Go | Some results open through another app; check the destination |

### Searching on a Mac or Chromebook

On a Mac, Safari, Chrome, Firefox, and Edge may use a combined address/search field; **Command + L** often selects it. On a Chromebook, the Chrome address bar generally allows normal web addresses and search words. The keyboard, touchpad, and menus may differ from Windows, but the basic actions—focus a search field, enter a query, submit, scan, open, return—remain recognizable.

Do not assume that every browser has the same default search provider. Someone using Safari may send queries to a configured provider; another using Edge may see Bing. A school or workplace may manage that setting. The lesson does not require changing it. When a search screen looks unfamiliar, locate the visible search field and look for the provider's name or address rather than guessing what service you are using.

### Searching on phones and tablets

On an iPhone, iPad, or Android device, you may use a browser or a dedicated search app. Tap the search field and type a short query with the on-screen keyboard. A keyboard's **Search** or **Go** key often submits the text. Some devices offer a microphone icon for [[voice-search|voice search]]. Voice features may need permission, process audio or a transcription, and interpret names incorrectly. They are optional. For private, sensitive, or shared-device tasks, typing a public non-sensitive query may be preferable.

Mobile results can show fewer ordinary links at once. A prominent map, answer box, image grid, shopping feature, or AI response may occupy most of the first screen. **Scroll thoughtfully** to find ordinary source links rather than assuming the first large card is the only answer. If a link opens in another app, inspect which application opened and whether it belongs to the intended organization.

Text enlargement, zoom, screen readers, and voice input may change how results are announced or displayed. If a result is hard to distinguish from an advertisement visually, look for its text label or use supported accessibility navigation. The instructor should not require a student to reveal a diagnosis or change personal accessibility settings as part of a search exercise.

### Known website versus unknown website

If you already know a verified address, entering the address can be more direct than searching for it. Suppose the user needs the official National Park Service homepage and already knows `nps.gov`. Going directly to that domain avoids a list that could include unrelated ads or third-party guides. But a familiar name does not mean you can safely guess its domain. For sensitive tasks, use a verified source, an existing trusted bookmark, a document from the relevant organization, or an authenticated application route rather than typing a plausible-looking name into a random site.

If you do not know the organization or the page address, a search engine can help discover candidates. Read the destination carefully, then verify it through independent or official context when the consequence of a mistake is serious. The next lesson develops that skill. For today's basic practice, choose low-risk topics such as science definitions, cultural heritage resources, and public educational materials.

### Searching for a definition

When you need a term explained, start with `what is` plus the term or `definition for beginners`. For example, `what is a web server for beginners` is more specific than `server`. Results may contain dictionaries, encyclopedias, technical documentation, videos, or generated summaries. A dictionary entry may provide a short meaning; a tutorial may explain how a term fits into a process. Choose the source type that matches the question rather than assuming the shortest result is sufficient.

A term can have different meanings by field. *Cache* is a computing term, but it may also appear in other contexts. *Java* can refer to software, an island, or coffee. Add context when a word has several meanings. If the definition itself uses unfamiliar words, search for one of those terms rather than abandoning the original topic.

### Searching for procedures

For “how do I” questions, include the task and applicable product or platform. `how to use Windows 11 Magnifier` identifies a feature and environment. A procedure may depend on the software release, permissions, and device type. Look for official documentation or a current, appropriately scoped tutorial. **Do not run commands, disable security controls, or install programs simply because a result says that is the solution.** Read and understand a recommended action before you attempt it.

A student trying to learn basic print settings can use a written example or inspect a print preview without actually printing. A learner studying download behavior can read instructions without downloading or opening a file. This keeps search practice focused on information retrieval rather than taking unrelated actions on the computer.

### Searching for places and local services

A search for a public library, bus service, office, museum, or clinic may show maps and directory listings. These can be starting points, but hours and addresses can change. Two organizations can have similar names in different cities. For a high-stakes visit, verify the official organization's identity and current information through its own channels. A local search may use general location context or request precise location access. Granting location permission is **not required** for this lesson; a city or regional name is enough for fictional practice.

The lesson's examples use placeholder cities to illustrate syntax, not actual real-time recommendations or verified opening hours. Do not assume that a search provider's map card establishes that a particular facility is open now. Real-world availability can differ from indexed listings or third-party directories.

### Searching for images, videos, and documents

Images and videos help illustrate concepts, but a thumbnail does not identify its complete source or license. Click through only when appropriate and inspect the original hosting page. Similarly, a search result pointing to a PDF or other file may be relevant to your topic, but file downloads can introduce additional risks. The later lesson **Downloading and Uploading Files** teaches those steps. For now, search students can practice recognizing a PDF-looking link without downloading it.

Some engines support document-specific operators such as `filetype:pdf`, and some offer filters for file types. The syntax and coverage vary. If you are not certain the source is trustworthy, do not download or execute the result. A PDF icon or `filetype` search says something about format, not accuracy or safety.

### A single search can involve several kinds of information

Suppose your project concerns the history of national parks. You might first search `history of US national parks` to understand the general topic. Next you might search `National Park Service park histories` to locate official collections. Then you might use a site search, a date filter, or the National Park Service's own navigation to find a specific park. Finally, you might bookmark the public resource for later study. Each step has a distinct purpose, so it is often better than trying to extract the entire project from a single query.

### A personal learning notebook without collecting private data

For a practice assignment, a learner can record four fields: **question**, **query used**, **type of promising result**, and **next query or action**. Recording a public page title is enough. No student should be required to copy real search history, account information, location data, private questions, or saved passwords into a class assignment. If the class needs evidence of comprehension, a fictional result page and a brief explanation are adequate.

## 6. How do you search safely and handle common problems?

Search is a basic information-literacy task, but it can also expose people to misleading links, requests for information, and unnecessary software downloads. A beginner-friendly habit is to begin with observation: **read labels, identify the target, and decide whether the action matches your goal before clicking**. This lesson does not require students to prove that they can identify every scam. It equips them to pause when a result's destination or purpose is unclear and to choose an approved source instead.

### Privacy starts with the query

Search providers may process the text you enter and other information according to their terms and settings. Your queries can be associated with a device, browser, account, network, or approximate location in different ways. A [[search-history|search history]] feature may store past queries, while a browser's [[browsing-history|browsing history]] records eligible visited pages. They overlap but are not the same thing. Search suggestions can also be influenced by popular or previously entered queries. **Do not treat a search field as a private journal.**

Use generic language for practice: `how does a router work` instead of entering a household's wireless password, `how do online accounts work` instead of including a real username and password, and `school enrollment documents general checklist` instead of submitting someone's identifying numbers. Keep real student and client records within the authorized institutional system, not in an open web-search query.

### Private browsing is not invisibility

A browser's [[private-browsing|private browsing]] mode generally limits certain browser records stored on the local device, but it does not make browsing or search activity universally anonymous. Search providers, websites, internet service operators, schools, employers, and network administrators may still receive relevant data in different circumstances. Downloaded files, saved bookmarks, website account actions, and other activity can persist depending on the service. **Private mode is not a promise of secrecy**, and you do not need to activate it to learn search basics.

Search engines also have their own settings for activity and personalization. Browser history controls are separate from some account-managed history controls. Do not delete real browsing history, change privacy settings, or disconnect managed accounts as part of the required class work. Use the supplied fictional scenarios instead.

### Verify the destination rather than trusting appearance

A search snippet, ad, and AI answer can all contain a familiar name while pointing to a different website. The [[url|URL]] includes a domain whose spelling and structure matter. A page may use the name or logo of a well-known organization in the title while being hosted on a different domain. If you seek an official service, open its recognized website through a verified route or check with the organization. Be cautious with look-alike spellings, unexpected extra words, and links in messages promising urgent access.

A [[https|HTTPS]] connection protects data in transit between the browser and the connected site against certain interception risks, but **HTTPS does not prove that the site's operator is honest**. Fraudulent websites can also use HTTPS. Do not share a password, one-time security code, bank information, or payment details because a link displays a lock icon or a polished design.

### Sponsored labels, pop-ups, and downloads

A result marked Sponsored is paid placement. It may lead to a legitimate business, but the label does not establish suitability for your task. If a website opens a pop-up claiming the computer has a virus or demands that you download a special browser tool, do not install the offered software merely to continue an ordinary search. Use familiar browser controls to leave the page and obtain assistance through an authorized channel if necessary. Similarly, be cautious of results whose purpose is to sell a paid document when the source organization provides it directly.

Browser prompts to allow notifications, share location, access the camera, or download files are separate from the basic act of searching. You can complete this lesson without granting any of those permissions. If a site demands permissions that do not fit your task, leave it or seek a more appropriate public source.

### Search results can be personalized or localized

Search engines may use language, region, device, prior activity, or configured preferences to shape the results. Some users see different filters, ads, or generated features for the same query. Two students can therefore report different first-page layouts without either student necessarily making a mistake. The learning target is **identifying components and selecting a suitable source**, not reproducing one particular screenshot pixel for pixel.

When teaching in a group, demonstrate with fictional example results rather than exposing a participant's real search history or asking classmates to log into one another's accounts. Compare how students describe result labels and choices, not private account information or personal interests.

### Accessibility and search navigation

A search page should be usable with more than one method. Keyboard users can focus the browser's address bar, enter a query, and move among links using supported navigation controls. Screen-reader users may navigate by headings and links and identify result titles from the page's accessibility information. People who benefit from enlarged text can use browser zoom, and users who prefer speech can optionally dictate a public query. Search providers and webpages vary in their accessibility implementation; do not assume a feature is present or named identically everywhere.

Instructors should give the learner an equivalent worksheet option if a live result page is too distracting, inaccessible, or requires an account. A text-only fictional results page can still test whether a student distinguishes a sponsored link, a title, a domain, and a snippet. The objective is making a reasoned choice, not demonstrating speed with a mouse.

### If you get no useful results

A search can return too few matches or completely unrelated material. First check spelling, names, and whether punctuation or quotation marks made the request overly restrictive. Next remove unnecessary words, try synonyms, or replace jargon with a simpler description. If you used `site:` or an exact quote, remove it temporarily and compare. If you're searching for a very new event, look for the organization's own update rather than assuming the search index is complete. An absence of results is **not proof that no such resource exists**.

A query with many repeated words, unusual abbreviations, or an accidental pasted paragraph may also confuse the objective. Edit the query rather than repeatedly hitting Enter. If a service reports an error, make sure your internet connection and browser are working for ordinary pages; do not respond by turning off security protection or changing advanced network settings.

### If a result is unrelated or unexpectedly commercial

Return to the results page using Back when appropriate, then revise the query with the missing educational context. For example, change `mouse` to `computer mouse types` if most results concern the animal. Add `overview`, `definition`, `history`, or `tutorial` to influence the type of result. Inspect the sponsored labels and scan several ordinary links. A product ad may be perfectly relevant to buying a device but irrelevant to learning what the device does.

### If a link opens a different page than expected

The website may have moved, redirected, become unavailable, required sign-in, or changed its content. The search listing may have been based on older information. Look at the new domain, do not assume the move is authorized, and use the organization's recognized homepage if appropriate. For an official form or policy, do not enter information on a redirect you cannot verify. An unrelated pop-up or unfamiliar app launch is not part of normal beginner search practice.

### If you see an AI summary without a clear source

Identify the generated text as a search-product feature, not a verified original document. If the question is simple and low-risk, it may help you learn the vocabulary needed for a better query. For an important or unusual assertion, search the claim directly or open cited primary sources and compare details. If the AI answer is vague, inconsistent, or does not provide an identifiable source, do not treat its confidence as evidence. Later lessons address source evaluation more systematically.

### A quick troubleshooting decision table

| What you notice | Possible explanation | First responsible response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Results are about another meaning of a word | The term is ambiguous | Add a clarifying subject, such as `animal` or `programming` |
| Search appears to ignore an exact phrase | Provider behavior or restrictive syntax | Try simpler words or the documented syntax for that engine |
| Very few results after `site:` | Domain restriction may be too narrow or pages not indexed | Check the domain and try searching the site directly |
| Mostly paid listings | Commercial intent or advertisement placement | Identify sponsored labels and refine toward informational terms |
| The first answer is generated by AI | The provider includes an AI feature | Seek and read direct sources for substantive claims |
| Old guidance dominates | Query lacks current context or indexed material is stale | Check official source dates and available time tools |
| Mobile page shows few ordinary links | Special cards consume visible screen space | Scroll and look for direct web-result links |
| An unexpected site requests a password | Wrong destination, misleading result, or login needed | Verify the official site rather than submitting credentials |
| A link asks for a download | The result targets a file or installer | Do not download for this lesson; use a suitable text source |
| The page cannot load | Network, website, or browser issue | Check other known pages; avoid changing protected settings |

### The four-question search habit

Before following an unfamiliar result, ask: **(1) What am I trying to find? (2) What type of result is this? (3) Which website or organization would I actually visit? (4) What do I still need to check on the source page?** If the response involves money, private data, legal rights, health, safety, or organizational policy, the stakes are higher and stronger verification is appropriate. This course's subsequent *Judging Search Results* and *Safe Web Browsing* lessons expand those responsibilities.

## 7. Guided practice: Build, read, and refine searches

These six activities are designed for independent learners, instructors, and small classes. The goal is **observable reasoning**, not the number of clicks made. Students can use a browser on a permitted device or complete each activity entirely from its written example. Do not use real names, addresses, account details, searches containing personal circumstances, or confidential institutional data as class evidence. Instructors can assess the written responses without requiring screenshots of browsing history.

### Activity 1: Separate a browser from a search service

**Goal:** Identify the tool you use to open webpages and the service you use to find them.

**Materials:** Any browser, or the following fictional description.

**Scenario:** A student opens Microsoft Edge, types `what is a galaxy` into the address bar, and presses Enter. A Bing results page appears. The student chooses a NASA article and reads it inside Edge.

**Steps:**

1. Identify the **browser** named in the scenario.
1. Identify the **search engine** used for the query.
1. Identify the **subject of the search** and the **website opened afterward**.
1. State which action is a search and which is navigation to a source.
1. Explain why it would still be possible to visit NASA's website even if the student did not use Bing for the initial search.

**Model interpretation:** Edge is the browser; Bing is the search engine; the query concerns galaxies; NASA is the source website in the example. The search service provided possible routes, and the browser displayed the chosen page. A browser can open a verified URL directly without requiring a search service to locate it first.

**Optional live version:** On your own approved device, look at your current browser and its displayed address field. Do not submit any query; just name the browser and where you could type an address or search words. If you choose to use a public educational query, record only the provider name and general result types—nothing about your personal browsing data.

**Success standard:** The learner distinguishes the browser, search engine, webpage, and address bar without treating them as one object.

### Activity 2: Convert a vague goal into a useful query

**Goal:** Write focused, non-sensitive queries for ordinary learning needs.

**Materials:** Paper, a document editor, or the worksheet below.

**Scenario A:** You are learning how photosynthesis works. Your first thought is `plants`.

**Scenario B:** You want a beginner explanation of computer keyboards. Your first thought is `keys`.

**Scenario C:** A fictional student wants the official public National Park Service website. Their first thought is `parks`.

**Steps:**

1. Write what the person actually wants to know for each scenario.
1. Replace the broad search with a query naming the subject and purpose.
1. Decide whether an organization name, audience level, or platform would improve each query.
1. Check that no revised query exposes private information.
1. Explain why the revised query is likely to help more than the original word.

| Scenario | Example focused query | Important improvement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: photosynthesis | `how photosynthesis works for beginners` | Adds the process and learner level |
| B: keyboards | `computer keyboard keys and their functions` | Separates computer hardware from other meanings of keys |
| C: public park agency | `National Park Service official website` | Identifies the intended institution |

**Optional live version:** Search one of the three focused queries using an approved browser. Read two ordinary result titles and their domains without signing in or downloading anything. You need not click a result; the practice is complete when you can explain which listing appears to match the learning goal.

**Success standard:** The learner turns a vague word into a short, purposeful query without adding unnecessary private details.

### Activity 3: Read a fictional search results page

**Goal:** Recognize sponsored material, source titles, snippets, domains, and AI-generated text.

**Materials:** This entirely fictional search page; no real searching is required.

**Question:** `how do telescopes work for beginners`

**Result A — Sponsored:** **Buy the Finest Telescope Today** · `https://shop.example.com/telescopes` · *View telescope products and accessories.*

**Result B — Ordinary link:** **How Telescopes Gather Light — Example Observatory** · `https://observatory.example.org/learn/telescopes` · *Beginner explanation of light, lenses, and mirrors.*

**Result C — Ordinary link:** **History of Telescope Technology — Example Archive** · `https://archive.example.org/history/telescopes` · *A historical survey of optical instruments.*

**Result D — AI-generated overview:** *Telescopes use optical or other systems to gather and study signals from distant objects ...* · *A generated explanation; check linked sources for important details.*

The `example.com` and `example.org` addresses above are illustrative domains only; they are not presented as live teaching resources or endorsements.

**Steps:**

1. Circle or identify the sponsored listing. Explain which word revealed its type.
1. Choose the result best suited to someone asking for a beginner explanation.
1. Find the domain in Result B and distinguish it from the result's title.
1. Explain why Result C may be valuable for a different question but is not the most direct first choice here.
1. Explain why Result D should not be treated as an original source on its own.
1. State what you would do after opening the best link to confirm that it actually teaches the topic.

**Model interpretation:** A is sponsored; B is the closest match to the beginner-learning goal; `observatory.example.org` is the displayed domain; C is a historical rather than a functional explanation; D is generated text, which needs appropriate direct-source verification. A practical learner would open B, confirm the page's actual topic and content, and continue searching if it did not meet the need.

**Success standard:** The learner recognizes result categories without believing that prominent placement guarantees suitability or correctness.

### Activity 4: Refine an ambiguous or unproductive search

**Goal:** Use information from the first result set to improve the next query.

**Scenario:** A student searches `mercury` for a science assignment about the planet. The results mix the planet, a chemical element, and other meanings. After changing the query to `Mercury planet`, the student still sees material far above their current reading level.

**Steps:**

1. Explain what made the original term ambiguous.
1. Propose a revised query that clearly identifies the planet.
1. Propose a second revised query suited to a beginner.
1. Name a plausible organization that could publish educational material about planets.
1. Decide whether an exact phrase, an exclusion, or an added context word would be your simplest first revision.
1. Explain what you would do if the second query returned no useful sources.

**One workable progression:** `mercury` → `Mercury planet` → `Mercury planet facts for beginners`. A further search might be `Mercury planet overview site:nasa.gov` if the learner wants to explore NASA material and has independently confirmed that `nasa.gov` belongs to NASA. The expected first improvement is **adding context**, not a complex operator. If results are poor, revise or simplify again.

**Optional live version:** Enter one public query about a planet, and then enter an improved version. You do not need to report the number or rank of actual search results: those can vary. Instead, record what *you changed* and what type of result you hoped to make easier to find.

**Success standard:** The learner gives a clear reason for each query revision and recognizes that a search result set may require more than one attempt.

### Activity 5: Match a search method to an information need

**Goal:** Choose an ordinary query, a known official website, a source-specific query, or a media filter for different objectives.

**Scenario 1:** A learner already has a verified bookmark for the MGU Internet and Web Skills course and needs the lesson on judging results.

**Scenario 2:** A learner wants an explanation of how a tornado develops and does not yet know a source website.

**Scenario 3:** A learner wants the official National Park Service homepage and knows the verified address `nps.gov`.

**Scenario 4:** A learner wants to see diagrams of the phases of the Moon and understand which organization provided each diagram.

**Scenario 5:** A learner wants an official NASA page about Saturn, and a general query keeps returning shopping items.

**Steps:**

1. Select a starting action for each case.
1. Name whether a bookmark, a direct address, a broad keyword query, a site restriction, or an image filter is appropriate.
1. Identify one limitation of the selected method.
1. State how you would move from a promising result to the original source.
1. Explain why image results, advertisements, and shortcuts do not automatically confirm rights, accuracy, or source identity.

**Model response:** For 1, use the known course bookmark and navigate within the site. For 2, search `how tornadoes form for beginners` and look for educational explanations. For 3, use the verified direct address. For 4, a public search and an Images filter can help discover candidate diagrams, but the original source and rights must still be inspected. For 5, `Saturn facts site:nasa.gov` is a possible source-specific search after confirming the domain. These are sensible options, not the only acceptable ones.

**Success standard:** The learner selects a method that fits the actual task instead of using a general search as the answer to every problem.

### Activity 6: Protect privacy and respond to a questionable result

**Goal:** Apply safe habits when a search result or page requests unnecessary information.

**Scenario A:** A school worksheet asks a student to search for `how passwords work`. A student suggests pasting their real password into the query so the engine can check it.

**Scenario B:** A result labeled **Sponsored** promises immediate access to a government form and asks for a credit card before showing the form.

**Scenario C:** A result that appears to discuss free science lessons opens a website asking permission to use the camera and exact location.

**Scenario D:** A search page presents an AI answer about a changing deadline without identifying when the relevant policy was updated.

**Steps:**

1. Identify the unnecessary or risky action in each scenario.
1. State a safer response that still allows learning to continue.
1. Explain why a sponsored placement, polished webpage, or generated summary is not proof of validity.
1. Choose what, if anything, should be written on the class practice record.
1. Describe when a learner should ask a trusted instructor, official organization, or authorized support person for assistance.

**Model response:** A student should never paste a real password into search; use a fictional example or general question. In B, verify the agency and form through the recognized official route before paying or sharing information. In C, deny unnecessary permissions and choose a different trusted resource; the class needs no camera or precise location. In D, consult the relevant official source and its current instructions rather than relying on a generated excerpt. The class record should contain **fictional scenarios and reasoning only**.

**Success standard:** The learner can decline unnecessary data collection, distinguish a result from an official source, and continue with a safer method.

### Optional live five-minute search routine

Students who have a suitable browser and permission to use it may practice this repeatable sequence with a **public educational topic**. Start with `why do seasons change`. Read the results list and label one normal link and any clearly marked sponsored or generated feature. Choose one educational source by checking its title and address. Use Back to return to results, then try `Earth seasons explanation for beginners`. Compare the *kinds of sources* presented. Finish by stating what the new query clarifies. **Do not submit real search history or screenshots of signed-in personal browser content to the instructor.**

### Practice record and scoring guidance

A learner can use a simple four-line note for each completed activity: **(a)** original goal, **(b)** query or action selected, **(c)** result type or limitation recognized, and **(d)** explanation of the next step. A satisfactory performance explains the choice, not just the wording of the query. The activity may be graded entirely from the written fictional examples. Repeating a search is encouraged; a wrong first query becomes useful evidence when the student can explain how to improve it.

### Exit practice: Explain your search process

Without looking at the lesson, answer the following: **(1)** What is the difference between a browser and a search engine? **(2)** How do you improve a broad query? **(3)** What distinguishes a sponsored listing from a normal result? **(4)** Why might an AI-generated summary or highlighted snippet need verification? **(5)** What should you avoid putting in a search query? **(6)** What would you do if a promising result led to a different or suspicious website?

If you can answer those questions and demonstrate one revised query, you have learned the fundamental process. The next lesson will teach you to judge the trustworthiness and suitability of the information you find.

## 8. Common misconceptions and avoidable problems

### “The search engine and browser are the same thing.”

The browser is the application used to view the web. A search engine is a service that helps locate resources. You can use different search providers within the same browser, and you can visit known website addresses without running a search. Recognizing this distinction helps you understand why a browser update and a search-results change are not necessarily the same event.

### “A search engine searches every page on the internet in real time.”

Search services depend on discovery systems, indexes, and other retrieval methods. Not every page is accessible or indexed, and newly changed pages may take time to appear accurately. A result is not a complete catalog or live verification of the source. If a current announcement is missing from results, go to the relevant organization's recognized website.

### “The top result must be the best or most truthful.”

Ranking reflects a provider's retrieval systems and the query's context, not an independent certificate of truth. The first result can be an advertisement, an irrelevant page, an older resource, or a page optimized for visibility. Scan several options and read the actual source before relying on substantive claims.

### “The first large answer box is always an ordinary website.”

A prominent section may be a sponsored listing, selected excerpt, calculation tool, map feature, or AI-generated synthesis. These are different result types with different limitations. Learn the labels and identify whether the content comes from an actual page, a provider-selected feature, or generated text.

### “Every snippet is the full answer.”

A snippet is a short preview and may omit context. A source might qualify a statement, describe exceptions, or have been updated after the preview was created. Open a suitable source when you need a complete explanation and verify important details.

### “If the result uses HTTPS, it is automatically legitimate.”

HTTPS protects a network connection in important ways but does not independently establish that the site is honest or authorized. A deceptive site may also have an encrypted connection. Check the organization's real domain and the task's context before entering private or financial information.

### “An ad at the top is the official government website.”

A paid placement is a commercial arrangement, not proof of official authority. A company can advertise alongside a government service. Identify the actual organization and destination, and use a verified official route for forms, benefits, requirements, and other consequential tasks.

### “Typing more words always improves a query.”

Useful context often helps; unrelated words can confuse the subject. `how does photosynthesis work for beginners` is clear, while a long string combining plants, holidays, weather, food, and unrelated school topics is not. One focused question at a time is a strong default.

### “Advanced operators guarantee an exact and complete answer.”

Quotation marks, `site:`, and exclusions can help narrow results, but their implementation varies by engine and they can hide relevant material. A source might not be indexed. An exact quote may be too restrictive. Use advanced syntax only when the problem calls for it, and return to plain keywords if it becomes unhelpful.

### “A search filter confirms the source is appropriate.”

Selecting News, Images, Videos, or a date range changes what is shown; it does not check every fact, rights statement, or publication date. The learner still must examine the relevant source. An image thumbnail is not permission to reuse the image; a news result is not a guarantee that a claim has been independently corroborated.

### “AI-generated search answers do not make mistakes.”

Generated material can sound authoritative while misstating a date, combining sources, or omitting important context. The presence of citations does not by itself establish that each claim follows from them. Open direct sources and assess the evidence, especially for high-stakes or rapidly changing matters.

### “If two students search the same words, the pages must match exactly.”

Results can differ by provider, region, language, time, device, account, and available features. Students should be assessed on their ability to identify result types and explain their choices, not their ability to duplicate a screenshot from another computer.

### “Search suggestions and spelling corrections always know what I mean.”

An automated suggestion may infer a popular interpretation that is not yours. Read the correction and retain important names or specialist terms when necessary. A suggestion is a tool, not an instruction that has to be accepted.

### “Searching for a document means I have downloaded it.”

A search result may point to a PDF, image, or downloadable file, but merely seeing the link does not guarantee that a copy exists in Downloads. Previewing or opening a page is also not equivalent to intentionally saving a file. The next units teach downloading and file management in more detail.

### “Search history is the same as browser history.”

A provider may keep search activity under an account or other settings; the browser may separately record page navigation. Some activity may appear in both systems, but they have different functions and retention rules. A private browser window does not guarantee that the search service or a connected network cannot observe activity.

### “Private browsing prevents anyone from seeing my searches.”

Private modes reduce certain local records, not all possible monitoring or account activity. On a shared or managed device, respect organizational policies and use an authorized account and procedure. Never test privacy claims by entering confidential information as a class exercise.

### “Every search problem should be solved by installing a plugin or extension.”

Basic searching requires no additional extension. If a result suggests installing a tool to access ordinary public information, stop and check the website and your institution's policies. Unnecessary extensions can request broad browser permissions or introduce compatibility and privacy issues.

### “If I cannot find it, it cannot exist.”

A resource may be unindexed, inaccessible, phrased differently, located behind a permitted login, or hosted by a smaller publisher. Try alternate terms, the official site's own navigation, a library catalog, or help from an appropriate person. An unsuccessful query proves only that this particular search did not find a useful match.

### The improvement is the decision, not the operator

| If the first attempt shows ... | Do this next | Avoid this reaction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The wrong subject | Add a clear meaning or category | Repeating the same ambiguous word indefinitely |
| Mostly advanced technical material | Add `for beginners` or an equivalent term | Opening random downloads to find an easier explanation |
| A convincing answer box with no context | Find the direct source and read its complete statement | Treating a confident summary as final proof |
| A suspicious or unrelated domain | Return to the results and locate a verified route | Entering passwords to see whether the site is legitimate |
| No result with restrictive operators | Remove a restriction, use synonyms, or visit the known site | Assuming the information must not exist |

### A dependable beginner checklist

Before ending a search, confirm that you can say what you wanted to find, what words you used, what result type you selected, which source you reached, and what you still need to verify. This habit makes searching more systematic and prepares students for the more demanding judgments in Lesson 5.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The dotted-underlined terms in the instructional sections can be selected in the MGU dashboard to display short definitions. Each used tooltip identifier is registered in the `:::terms` block at the end of this Markdown file. The definitions below are also readable without interactive controls; no learner is required to activate a tooltip to understand the lesson.

- **Search engine:** Service that locates potential online resources in response to a user's query.
- **Query:** Words or a question submitted to a search service.
- **Keyword:** Important word or phrase describing the subject of a search.
- **Search field:** Input area for entering a search query.
- **Search results page (SERP):** Screen showing listings and other features returned for a search.
- **Organic result:** Conventional unpaid search listing, distinct from an advertisement.
- **Sponsored result:** Paid search listing, identified by a label such as Sponsored or Ad.
- **Search advertisement:** Promotional content purchased for placement in search results.
- **Result title:** Text used to identify and often open a result.
- **Snippet:** Short result preview, which may omit important context.
- **Domain:** Main readable name associated with an internet service or website.
- **URL:** Web address used to identify a resource or location.
- **Address bar:** Browser field for entering a URL or, in many browsers, a search query.
- **Browser:** Application used to open and interact with web resources.
- **Internet:** Interconnected global networks that transport information between systems.
- **World Wide Web:** System of linked resources and applications using web technology.
- **Website:** Collection of related web pages or services.
- **Webpage:** One document or interface presented by a website.
- **Search provider:** Organization or service that processes search queries and provides results.
- **Default search engine:** Search service configured for queries entered into the browser.
- **Search suggestions:** Proposed queries displayed while or after text is entered.
- **Autocomplete:** Feature that proposes completions based on partial input.
- **Spelling correction:** Proposed revision of a word the system believes may be misspelled.
- **Synonym:** Alternative term with a similar meaning in a given context.
- **Ambiguous term:** Word or phrase with more than one plausible interpretation.
- **Natural-language search:** Query written as an ordinary question or sentence.
- **Query refinement:** Deliberate editing of search words or options to improve relevance.
- **Relevance:** How well a result fits the intended information need.
- **Search filter:** Control that narrows or emphasizes results by category or other condition.
- **Web filter:** Search option that emphasizes text-based web links where supported.
- **Images filter:** Search option focusing on images and their source links.
- **Video filter:** Search option emphasizing video resources.
- **News filter:** Search option emphasizing news-related results.
- **Date filter:** Search setting that selects results associated with a time range.
- **Quotation search:** Use of quotation marks to request closer matching of a phrase.
- **`site:` operator:** Search syntax intended to restrict results to a site or domain where supported.
- **Exclusion operator:** Syntax such as a minus sign used by some services to reduce unwanted matches.
- **Advanced search:** Optional search interface or syntax with additional constraints.
- **Crawling:** Automated discovery of accessible pages or resources.
- **Crawler:** Software that discovers content by visiting resources or links.
- **Indexing:** Processing and recording discovered material to support retrieval.
- **Search index:** Data structure or system supporting a search engine's ability to find resources.
- **Ranking:** Selecting and ordering result candidates for a query.
- **Search engine optimization (SEO):** Practices intended to improve a site's discoverability and search presentation.
- **Featured snippet:** Highlighted answer or extract chosen by a search provider.
- **Information panel:** Search page area presenting organized facts or related details.
- **AI-generated summary:** Synthesized text produced with generative AI; can be inaccurate.
- **Primary source:** Direct record, original evidence, or authoritative source relevant to a subject.
- **Official website:** Verified site controlled by the organization responsible for the information.
- **Source website:** Actual website hosting material found through a result.
- **Redirect:** Navigation that sends a browser to a different address.
- **Publication date:** Date a source was originally published, if reliably identified.
- **Update date:** Date content was changed or revised, if provided.
- **Local search:** Search for places or services connected to a region.
- **Location permission:** Device or browser authorization to share location data with a service.
- **Voice search:** Search performed by speaking a query to a supported application.
- **Accessibility:** Designing and configuring technology for people with varying access needs.
- **Screen reader:** Assistive technology that presents interface content through speech or other outputs.
- **Keyboard shortcut:** Key or key combination invoking an application command.
- **SafeSearch:** Provider-specific filtering feature intended to reduce certain explicit results.
- **HTTPS:** Encrypted web transport mechanism that does not establish a site's honesty.
- **Private browsing:** Browser mode that limits some local records but is not universal anonymity.
- **Search history:** Records of submitted queries, subject to provider and account settings.
- **Browsing history:** Browser-managed records of eligible visited pages.
- **Personalization:** Use of context or activity to adjust the experience or result presentation.
- **Bookmark:** Saved web address for later use.
- **Download:** Transfer of content into a receiving destination or device.
- **Public information:** Material intended for general public access.
- **Sensitive information:** Data such as passwords, financial account numbers, or private records that require careful protection.
- **Observation-first:** Learning approach focused on identifying and interpreting before making changes.
- **Result preview:** Text or media shown to help a searcher decide whether to open a result.
- **Answer box:** Search feature presenting a short response within the results page.
- **Result destination:** Actual page or resource reached by opening a search listing.
- **Site search:** Searching within a particular website, using the site's own search feature or supported engine syntax.
- **Search intent:** Practical reason or objective behind a query.

The core search process is straightforward: **state the need, enter clear terms, identify the kind of result displayed, choose a plausible source, and refine or verify when required**. Search engines can make large collections of information easier to navigate, but they do not replace thoughtful reading or the source's own authority.
