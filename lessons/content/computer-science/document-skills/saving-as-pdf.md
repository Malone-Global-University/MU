## Lesson 7: Saving as PDF

A document is useful only when its intended readers can open it and understand it. You may prepare a workshop announcement in a word processor, but the recipient might use a different application, operating system, or device. A [[pdf|PDF]]—short for *Portable Document Format*—is a common way to distribute a page-oriented copy while preserving much of its intended layout. This lesson teaches you how to create a PDF from an editable document, choose an appropriate filename and location, check the resulting file, and keep your original for later corrections. You will continue working with the fictional community-workshop notice developed in earlier Document Skills lessons. No printer, paid PDF editor, or sensitive personal information is required.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 85–115 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–6 of Document Skills recommended · **Practice equipment:** A computer with Microsoft Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice Writer, Apple Pages, or another word processor; a practice document; a browser or other PDF viewer; access to a local folder for saving files

> **Essential idea:** **Save the editable original, export a separate PDF, then open and inspect the PDF itself.** A successful export command does not guarantee that every page, link, table, picture, or accessibility feature is correct. Verify the version that people will actually receive.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain what a [[pdf|PDF]] is and identify appropriate uses for a PDF copy.
1. Distinguish an [[editable-source|editable source document]] from a [[fixed-layout|fixed-layout]] PDF.
1. Identify the difference between **Save**, **Save As**, **Export**, **Download**, and **Print to PDF**.
1. Locate a PDF-creation command in Microsoft Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice Writer, or Apple Pages.
1. Choose a meaningful [[filename|filename]], recognizable [[file-extension|file extension]], and suitable [[save-location|save location]].
1. Export an entire document rather than accidentally exporting a page selection or another unintended range.
1. Identify common choices involving [[page-range|page range]], [[quality-setting|quality]], and [[file-size|file size]].
1. Explain why [[accessibility|accessibility]], [[heading-style|heading styles]], [[alternative-text|alternative text]], and [[reading-order|reading order]] matter in an exported PDF.
1. Open a PDF using a suitable [[pdf-viewer|PDF viewer]] and inspect its contents, page count, layout, links, and readable text.
1. Correct an error in the editable source and export an updated PDF without confusing it with an older copy.
1. Complete a safe distribution check before attaching, uploading, or sharing a PDF.

## 1. What is a PDF, and when should you use one?

Imagine that you wrote a one-page announcement in a word processor. It contains a title, a short paragraph, a small schedule table, and an image with a caption. You want to send the finished announcement to someone who may not have the same software. A PDF offers a practical solution: it packages the page so that a compatible viewer can display the document with a reasonably consistent appearance across devices. People commonly use PDFs for notices, reports, forms, instructions, manuals, and documents prepared for printing.

A PDF is a [[file-format|file format]], not a brand of word processor. Different applications can create and open PDFs. A standard PDF may contain actual selectable text, images, hyperlinks, and document structure. Some PDFs contain mainly photographs or scans of pages instead. Files that look similar on screen may differ considerably in how easily their contents can be searched, copied, read aloud, or accessed with assistive technology.

### The source file and the PDF serve different jobs

You may work in Microsoft Word with a `.docx` file, in LibreOffice Writer with an `.odt` file, in Apple Pages with a `.pages` document, or in a cloud-native Google Docs document. These are common forms of an [[editable-source|editable source]]: they allow you to change the original text, resize pictures, adjust table cells, and revise headings. The corresponding PDF is normally a **distribution copy**. It is designed to be viewed or printed more consistently; it is not normally the preferred place for ordinary ongoing editing.

| Question | Editable source document | Exported PDF copy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary purpose | Create and revise content | Read, review, distribute, or print a finished version |
| Common examples | `.docx`, `.odt`, `.pages`, or a Google Docs document | `.pdf` |
| Changing a paragraph | Usually straightforward in a word processor | May require specialized editing or conversion |
| Layout across different programs | Can shift with fonts, versions, and settings | Usually more consistent, though not identical in every viewer |
| Best file to preserve for future revisions | Yes | No, not as the only retained copy |
| Accessibility | Depends on authoring and document structure | Depends on source quality, export settings, and PDF structure |

Do not delete the source document after exporting. If the workshop time changes next week, the efficient workflow is to update the original, save it, and export a new PDF. Converting the PDF back into an editable document can alter formatting and is not a dependable substitute for maintaining the source.

### A PDF is not a guarantee of accuracy or security

A PDF does not make incorrect words or dates correct. It does not establish that an organization approved the material. It is not automatically protected from copying, editing, or redistribution. Nor is it always accessible merely because the pages look correct. Some PDF viewers and editors permit comments, annotations, and modifications. Treat a PDF as a useful file format, not as proof of authenticity, confidentiality, or final approval.

### Common reasons to export a PDF

| Situation | Why a PDF may help | Important check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emailing an event notice | Recipients can often open it without the authoring program | Verify date, time, and attachment |
| Uploading a completed form | The recipient may request PDF specifically | Follow the organization's instructions |
| Sharing a short report | Page layout and tables can remain relatively consistent | Check fonts, tables, and page count |
| Sending printable instructions | Pages are arranged for convenient printing | Check margins and paper size |
| Publishing a public handout | One file can be distributed widely | Review accessibility and any private information |

> **Remember:** If the recipient specifically requires a Word document, accessible HTML, plain text, or another format, follow that requirement. PDF is common, but it is not always the best format for every audience or task.

### PDF, image, and scanned page are not the same

A screenshot of a document is usually an image rather than an editable document or a well-structured PDF. A scanned paper page may be stored in a PDF container but still consist largely of pixels. Without appropriate text recognition and structure, such a file may be difficult for a screen reader to navigate or for a reader to search. Exporting directly from a well-structured word processor generally provides a better starting point than taking screenshots of each page.

You may hear the term [[ocr|optical character recognition (OCR)]]. OCR attempts to identify letters in scanned pictures. It can be useful, but it can make mistakes, especially with low-quality scans or unusual layouts. It does not automatically repair headings, tables, reading order, or all other accessibility issues. This lesson uses directly exported PDFs, not scanned-page workflows.

## 2. How do you prepare the original document?

Before creating the PDF, make sure that the **source document is the version you actually intend to distribute**. If you export an outdated draft, the PDF will reproduce the outdated draft. If you leave an unfinished caption, placeholder, tracked change, or inappropriate comment in the source, you may inadvertently carry it into a shared copy or export.

### Step 1: Confirm the document's purpose and audience

Ask what the reader needs to know. For the fictional community-workshop notice, the reader needs a descriptive title, an unambiguous date and time, a location, registration instructions, and a schedule. The document should not contain invented contact information disguised as real information. For this practice, use fictional content; do not distribute the exercise as an actual event announcement.

Read the document from top to bottom. Check that the heading identifies the subject, instructions are in a logical order, and the language is clear. If the exercise includes an image, make sure that it contributes useful information rather than merely taking up space.

### Step 2: Save your latest edits in the original format

A word processor may show an icon, indicator, or status message when a file has been saved or synchronized. Use its ordinary save function as appropriate. In Word or LibreOffice, a common shortcut is **Ctrl+S** on Windows or **Command+S** on macOS. The exact behavior depends on whether the file already has a name and where it is stored. Google Docs normally saves changes automatically when connected and may display save or offline status. A cloud status indicator is worth checking; do not assume that every edit has synchronized if the connection has failed.

For example, save the editable copy as `community-workshop-notice.docx` or `community-workshop-notice.odt`. A Google Docs original can have the matching title without relying on a local filename extension. Your document's title and your exported PDF's filename can be similar so that the relationship is obvious.

### Step 3: Inspect the document in page view

A [[page-layout|page layout]] includes margins, page breaks, orientation, and how objects are arranged. Depending on your application, you may see a print layout view or a page preview. Inspect the actual page boundaries rather than assuming everything that fits on a scrolling editing screen will fit on a printed page.

| Inspect | A good result | A problem to correct before export |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title | The full title is visible | The end is clipped by a text box |
| Paragraphs | Sentences and line breaks are readable | Large accidental blank spaces appear |
| Headings | Clear order with appropriate heading styles | Text is bold but structurally indistinguishable |
| Schedule table | All rows and columns are visible | A column extends beyond the page |
| Image | It is proportional, relevant, and described | It is distorted or covers text |
| Footer or page number | Information is accurate where included | Old date or placeholder remains |
| Page count | The pages follow a sensible sequence | An unexplained blank final page appears |

### Step 4: Check accessibility in the source

A visually attractive PDF can still be difficult for someone using assistive technology. Much of PDF accessibility begins **before export**. The source document should use real [[heading-style|heading styles]] rather than only enlarged bold text, descriptive [[hyperlink|hyperlink]] labels, meaningful [[alternative-text|alternative text]] for informative images, and genuine table cells with understandable [[header-row|header rows]]. Maintain a logical [[reading-order|reading order]] and avoid using color alone to communicate essential instructions.

If your application offers an [[accessibility-checker|accessibility checker]], run it and review the findings. Automated checks do not catch every problem, so human inspection still matters. Some export workflows have a choice such as **Document structure tags for accessibility** or **Tagged PDF**. Choose a suitable accessible export option when available, and inspect the result. A successful accessible export option is helpful, not a full accessibility certification.

### Step 5: Handle comments, revisions, and hidden information deliberately

A document may include [[comment|comments]], suggestions, [[tracked-change|tracked changes]], hidden text, or document properties. Some PDF export methods can include markup, comments, or metadata depending on their options. Before publishing, confirm that the visible version does not contain internal notes, unapproved edits, private names, or unneeded identifiers. In a collaborative document, do not remove another person's changes without appropriate review or authority.

**Do not rely on covering text with a black shape, shrinking it, or hiding it behind an image to remove sensitive information.** Such methods may leave the underlying text recoverable. Use an approved redaction process if a real document requires redaction. For this beginner exercise, avoid sensitive information entirely.

> **Source-document checkpoint:** You should have a clean, correctly named, editable document with the intended content, checked page layout, and no unresolved information that would make the PDF misleading.

## 3. How do you create a PDF in common applications?

The exact menu wording can change by product version, device, or subscription. The goal is the same across the applications below: **keep the original editable file and create a separate PDF copy**. Start with a simple, non-sensitive practice document. Review each dialog box before confirming the export so you know which location and filename you selected.

### Microsoft Word on Windows

In many current desktop versions of Microsoft Word, you can use **File → Save As** or **File → Save a Copy**, choose a location, and select **PDF** in the file-type list. Depending on your version, **File → Export** may also offer **Create PDF/XPS**. Follow the controls shown in your installed version rather than assuming every ribbon looks identical.

1. Open the completed editable Word document and save its latest changes.
1. Select **File**, then **Save As**, **Save a Copy**, or a supported PDF export option.
1. Choose a folder you can find again, such as a private Document Skills practice folder.
1. Enter `community-workshop-notice.pdf` as the output filename, if needed.
1. Choose **PDF** as the output format. Confirm that you are not accidentally choosing XPS or another format.
1. If an **Options** control is available, check page range, whether revisions/markup are included, and whether accessibility structure tags are enabled when appropriate.
1. Complete **Save**, **Publish**, or the equivalent confirmation.
1. Open the resulting PDF and check it; do not stop merely because Word returned to the document.

Microsoft documentation describes PDF output options for page range, bookmarks from headings, and document structure tags. Availability and naming vary. A beginner creating an ordinary one-page notice generally needs the **entire document** and a readable, reasonably accessible PDF. Do not select a complex setting unless the task requires it.

### Microsoft Word on Mac or in a browser

Word for Mac commonly allows PDF output through **File → Save As** or **Save a Copy** with **PDF** selected as the file format. Some Microsoft 365 versions offer a choice optimized for accessibility and electronic distribution. Review the application's explanation of any option that uses an online conversion service before sending confidential material through it. A PDF can be made without assuming every conversion path has identical privacy or accessibility characteristics.

Word for the web may offer a PDF pathway through its File or Print options. The browser's save dialog can look different from desktop Word. Confirm that the resulting file is in fact `.pdf`, not an editable Word download, and that it opens correctly.

### Google Docs on a computer

Google Docs is usually edited in a browser and saves the working document in Google Drive. **Downloading** a PDF is separate from saving edits to the cloud document. To create a local PDF copy:

1. Open the correct Google Docs document and verify that its changes have saved.
1. Select **File → Download**.
1. Choose **PDF Document (.pdf)** from the available formats.
1. Locate the downloaded file. Depending on browser settings, it may appear in **Downloads** or another chosen folder.
1. Rename or move the file into your practice folder if necessary.
1. Open that downloaded PDF and inspect it.

For some large documents or particular browser situations, Google's help also describes a **File → Print → Save as PDF** route. That is an alternative, not a reason to ignore the downloaded file's quality or accessibility. Remember that downloading a PDF does **not** replace the original Google Docs document with a PDF in Drive. The cloud original remains a separate editable document.

### LibreOffice Writer

LibreOffice Writer supports PDF export through **File → Export As → Export as PDF**. Its export dialog can include page-range, compression, bookmark, and accessibility-related settings. For this lesson:

1. Open and save the `.odt` source document.
1. Choose **File → Export As → Export as PDF**.
1. On the export settings screen, use **All** or the equivalent option if you want the complete document.
1. Review any applicable **Tagged PDF** or document-structure option and make an appropriate selection.
1. Choose **Export**, select the destination folder, and enter the desired PDF filename.
1. Save the output and open it in a separate PDF viewer for inspection.

Some installations provide a toolbar icon for quick PDF export. The longer menu path is more useful for learning because it exposes settings that can matter for quality and accessibility. A toolbar icon may use stored defaults that you have not reviewed.

### Apple Pages on Mac

Apple Pages provides an export workflow for producing copies in other formats, including PDF. Current Apple documentation describes PDF conversion through its export controls; depending on the version, you may use **File → Export To → PDF** or an **Export and Send** action. To complete the task:

1. Open the correct Pages document and save the editable original.
1. Open the **PDF** export option from the File or sharing/export controls.
1. Check any quality or accessibility options shown by your version.
1. Choose a suitable name and location, then complete the export.
1. Find and open the PDF to check its actual pages.

Because Pages versions and interfaces differ, use the current **Pages User Guide** linked later in the lesson if the menu names on your Mac are different. Do not treat an exported PDF as replacing your `.pages` source.

### If your application is not listed

Look for **Export**, **Download**, **Save As**, **Share as PDF**, or a similarly named control. Many word processors provide PDF output, but not all support identical features. If the application offers only **Print → Save as PDF**, you may be able to produce a basic copy. You should still keep the original and examine the resulting PDF, especially if the document uses headings, links, tables, or images. Avoid uploading private documents to unknown conversion websites merely to obtain a PDF.

| Application | Typical desktop route | Where to look for result | Keep which editable source? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Word for Windows | File → Save As or Save a Copy → PDF | Chosen folder | `.docx` |
| Word for Mac | File → Save As or Save a Copy → PDF | Chosen folder | `.docx` |
| Google Docs | File → Download → PDF Document | Browser download folder or chosen location | Google Docs document in Drive |
| LibreOffice Writer | File → Export As → Export as PDF | Chosen folder | `.odt` |
| Apple Pages | Export To or Export and Send → PDF | Chosen folder | `.pages` |

> **Practice rule:** If your installed interface differs, locate the PDF-specific command through the application's menus or built-in help. Never guess a location and declare the export complete without finding the output file.

## 4. Which PDF options and file locations matter?

A PDF export dialog can display many settings. For ordinary beginner documents, you do not need to master every technical detail. You do need to understand which choices affect **what gets exported**, **how it looks**, **who can read it**, and **where it goes**.

### Export the correct pages

A [[page-range|page range]] determines which pages go into the PDF. The default may be the whole document, but there are exceptions. **All** usually means all pages; **Current page** means one page; **Selection** means only highlighted material if the application supports that operation. If you export page 1 from a two-page notice, the resulting PDF may look correct at first glance but still be missing half the information.

For your first exercise, choose all pages. Use a specific range only when the instructions clearly require part of the document. Always inspect the final page count afterward.

### Choose appropriate print or screen quality

Some applications offer a larger [[standard-quality|standard-quality]] PDF and a smaller [[minimum-size|minimum-size]] option. Larger image-heavy files may be clearer for print, but smaller files can be easier to send over a slow connection. Neither size nor the label alone proves accessibility or accuracy. For a simple workshop notice with text and one modest image, a default or standard setting is generally sufficient.

| Export choice | What it can affect | Beginner guidance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All pages or selected pages | Whether the entire document is included | Choose all unless you were told otherwise |
| Standard or print quality | Detail in graphics and images | Choose readable output suited to the recipient |
| Minimum file size | Compression and sometimes image quality | Recheck small text and pictures carefully |
| Tagged PDF or structure tags | Information used by accessibility tools | Preserve structure where supported and verify result |
| Bookmarks from headings | Navigation among sections | Useful for longer well-structured documents |
| Comments or markup | Whether review material is exposed | Do not include internal review information unintentionally |
| Password or security options | Opening or modification restrictions | Do not add unless a legitimate process requires them |

### Pick a folder you control

A [[save-location|save location]] is the folder, Drive area, or other destination where the new file is placed. A browser may download to **Downloads** without asking. A desktop editor may remember the last folder used. These behaviors are convenient but easy to overlook. After exporting, use File Explorer, Finder, or the browser's downloads list to find the actual file.

Consider a dedicated folder named `Document Skills Practice` containing these two files:

- `community-workshop-notice.docx` — editable source; or an `.odt` or `.pages` equivalent.
- `community-workshop-notice.pdf` — shareable PDF copy.

For Google Docs, the original may remain in Drive while the downloaded PDF is in a local folder. You can still organize them conceptually as matching versions. Do not assume they are in the same physical location.

### Use filenames that communicate the contents

A good [[filename|filename]] helps you and other people identify the file. `community-workshop-notice.pdf` communicates more than `Document (12).pdf` or `final2-new-REAL.pdf`. A meaningful revision name might be `community-workshop-notice-revised-2026-11-19.pdf` when the date is useful. Avoid using **final** as the only version control system; the word does not prevent another revision later.

**File extensions** identify the intended format. A PDF normally ends with `.pdf`. Do not attempt to create a legitimate PDF by merely changing `notice.docx` to `notice.pdf` in the filename. Renaming does not convert the file's contents. Use the application's PDF export command instead.

### Know the difference between export and print to PDF

A [[virtual-printer|virtual PDF printer]] appears as a printer destination, such as **Microsoft Print to PDF** or **Save as PDF**. Rather than sending paper to a physical printer, it creates a file. This can be helpful when an application has no direct export option. However, the print process may preserve visual appearance while discarding or weakening document structure, bookmarks, or other accessibility information. The precise result depends on the operating system and software.

For a document containing semantic headings, image descriptions, and navigational links, **prefer an application-supported accessible PDF export path** where available. Printing to PDF is a fallback; it is not inherently equivalent to structured export. If you must use it, test selection, reading order, navigation, and accessibility especially carefully.

### Avoid unnecessary security features in beginner practice

Some applications can create password-protected PDFs. Such settings may prevent a recipient from opening a file, increase support needs, or interfere with an organization's workflow. Do not set a password simply because the dialog offers one. For sensitive real-world documents, follow an approved security procedure and transmission policy; a password setting by itself is not a complete privacy strategy.

### Understand the PDF/A option without needing it today

You may see [[pdfa|PDF/A]], a PDF format family intended for long-term archiving. It imposes additional requirements on how a document is stored and represented. An organization may request a particular PDF/A version for records. The beginner task in this lesson does not require PDF/A, and you should not enable archival modes indiscriminately. Use them when a policy or explicit submission instruction calls for them, and verify that the created file meets that requirement.

> **Before you click Export:** Check **what** will be exported, **where** it will be saved, **what** the filename is, and whether the settings match the reader's needs.

## 5. How do you check the PDF for quality and accessibility?

Exporting is only the middle of the process. A file's existence does not guarantee that the file is correct. The strongest beginner habit is to close or move away from the editing view, open the actual exported PDF in a separate [[pdf-viewer|PDF viewer]], and compare it against the source and the document's requirements. Most modern browsers can display PDFs. Your device may also have a separate PDF-reading program. No paid PDF editor is required for the basic inspection in this lesson.

### A seven-part verification method

1. **Locate the correct file.** Confirm the filename and `.pdf` extension in its folder or download list.
1. **Open it successfully.** It should display in a PDF viewer without an unexpected error or password request.
1. **Count the pages.** Confirm that the document has the expected number of pages and no accidental blank pages.
1. **Read the beginning and ending.** Check the title, first paragraph, table, final line, and any footer or contact instruction.
1. **Inspect layout.** Look for cut-off text, odd breaks, distorted images, tiny fonts, and tables extending beyond margins.
1. **Test representative functions.** If your document has links, check that they lead where intended. Try selecting or searching for a known word if supported by the viewer.
1. **Review accessibility and privacy.** Check accessible structure where possible, verify meaningful alternative text and reading order with suitable tools, and ensure unapproved information has not been exposed.

| Item in the PDF | How to check | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filename and type | Inspect the file in its folder | Avoid sending the wrong format or version |
| Page count | View page thumbnails or page indicator | Detect missing or extra pages |
| Text | Read and search for a distinctive phrase | Detect omissions and image-only exports |
| Table | Follow each row and column | Detect broken alignment or missing values |
| Image | Inspect dimensions and position | Catch distortion and text overlap |
| Hyperlink | Open a safe, expected destination | Confirm navigation works |
| Accessibility | Use structural checks and assisted review as available | Detect obstacles not visible in ordinary page view |
| Privacy | Check visible content and permitted document properties | Reduce unintended disclosure |

### Text selection is a clue, not a full accessibility test

In many directly exported PDFs, you can select a sentence with a pointer and sometimes copy it. A viewer's Find function may locate a specific word. If the document is a page image, this may be impossible or depend on OCR. **Selectable text does not prove the PDF is accessible.** The reading order might still be wrong, images might lack appropriate descriptions, or a schedule table might be difficult to interpret with assistive technology.

For a more meaningful accessibility check, inspect whether your export preserved headings and table structure, and use an appropriate accessibility checker or screen reader when available. For public or formally regulated distribution, follow the applicable institutional accessibility process. A beginner's visual check is useful but cannot certify compliance.

### Consider the reading order

The [[reading-order|reading order]] is the sequence in which text and other document elements are presented to assistive software. A two-column page, floating text box, or picture placed between paragraphs may look obvious visually but be announced in a confusing order. The source document's logical structure and the export settings influence the result. When possible, choose straightforward layouts and test the PDF using available accessibility features.

### Check links and page navigation

A [[hyperlink|hyperlink]] that works in the source may become inactive or point to the wrong place after export. Test important links in the actual PDF. A long report may benefit from PDF [[bookmark|bookmarks]] generated from real heading styles. Bookmarks are a navigation aid, not a substitute for semantic structure in the underlying content. A one-page notice usually does not need them, but it is useful to recognize the term.

### If you find a mistake, repair the source

Suppose the PDF shows a workshop time of **1:00 p.m.**, but your approved fictional schedule says **10:00 a.m.** Do not try to hide the mistake with a shape over the PDF or assume you can distribute an explanation separately. Open the editable source, correct the time, save the source, and export a fresh PDF. Then inspect the corrected PDF before using it. This creates a clear and repeatable workflow.

> **Verification standard:** “I clicked Save” is not proof of completion. “I found the PDF, opened it, checked its contents, and confirmed the correct version” is a much stronger statement.

## 6. How do you manage and share the correct PDF?

Once your PDF passes the content, layout, and accessibility checks appropriate to the task, you should be able to distinguish it from the editable original and any older drafts. **File management is part of document accuracy**: sending an obsolete version can undo careful proofreading and export work.

### A simple two-file workflow

1. Keep a clearly named source such as `community-workshop-notice.odt` or the equivalent in your application.
1. Save all changes to that source before exporting.
1. Export `community-workshop-notice.pdf` to the intended folder.
1. Open and inspect the PDF; do not assume the preview is the same as the source.
1. If the source changes, export again and confirm which file is now current.
1. Retire or clearly distinguish older copies so you do not attach them accidentally.

A cloud-based workflow may require extra attention: the original can be in Google Drive, while a PDF downloaded last Tuesday remains in your device's Downloads folder. Opening the Drive document today does **not** automatically update the already-downloaded PDF. Re-download the PDF after making changes. Similarly, an email attachment made from an earlier file does not update itself when you edit the source later.

### Revision decisions

| Situation | Recommended action | Do not do this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You corrected a date in the source | Save and export a new PDF | Assume the old PDF changed automatically |
| You found multiple PDFs with similar names | Compare dates and contents; identify the intended version | Choose the first result without inspecting it |
| A recipient requests a corrected copy | Produce and clearly label the corrected PDF | Silently send the outdated file again |
| The source contains private notes | Review and follow an approved removal process before export | Assume PDF conversion will remove all private information |
| You need to continue editing next month | Preserve the editable source | Rely only on the PDF copy |
| A website requires an exact upload format | Follow its format, size, and accessibility requirements | Rename a different file extension to `.pdf` |

### Before attaching or uploading

A final [[distribution-check|distribution check]] asks four questions. **Is this the correct version? Is the content approved for the intended audience? Can the recipient open and use it? Does it meet the destination's stated requirements?** For an email, also confirm the recipient and attachment. For a portal, inspect any maximum file size or special naming rule. A file may be an excellent PDF but still be rejected because it exceeds the site's size limit or does not meet the requested format.

Be cautious with unknown conversion, compression, and PDF editing services. Uploading a file to a third-party site may disclose its contents or metadata to that service. For this exercise, use built-in export tools and a fictional notice. You can learn PDF skills without purchasing an application or uploading private records.

### Sharing permission is separate from file format

Turning a document into a PDF does not grant permission to use copyrighted images, reveal personal data, distribute someone else's drafts, or publish an unapproved report. Check the intended distribution scope and permissions. A PDF that you can technically create is not necessarily one you are authorized to share. This distinction is particularly important in offices, schools, and community organizations where many people can access drafts.

### Finish with a handoff record

For a simple task, make a small mental or written record: the source filename, the PDF filename, its location, the revision date if relevant, and what you checked. You do not need a complex records-management system. The goal is that a future reader—or your future self—can find the intended version and understand where to make corrections.

**Example handoff note:** “Editable document preserved. Exported a PDF copy to the Document Skills Practice folder. Verified one page, the workshop title, the schedule table, and readable text. Used fictional details only. No distribution outside practice.”

> **Key workflow:** **Edit source → save source → export PDF → find PDF → inspect PDF → share only after approval.**

## 7. Guided practice: Export and check a workshop notice

The exercises below build on the fictional community-workshop notice from Lessons 1–6. You can use the source file you previously created, or make a new practice document from the example in Activity 1. The information is invented for learning; **do not publish the notice or present the room and time as a real scheduled event**. ## 8. Common mistakes and troubleshooting

### “I clicked Export, but I cannot find the PDF.”

The application may have saved to its most recent folder or your browser's default Downloads directory. Open the program's recent files, a browser downloads list, File Explorer, or Finder as appropriate. Search for the distinctive filename rather than assuming the export failed. If several copies appear, compare their locations and actual contents. Do not create five additional copies before checking the first destination.

### “The file still ends with .docx even though I changed the name.”

You may have selected an editable format rather than PDF, or you may have typed `.pdf` into a filename field without changing the output format. Return to the source and explicitly choose **PDF** in the export or file-type controls. Inspect the saved file's type and reopen it. Renaming extensions does not convert file formats.

### “My PDF has only one page, but the document has three.”

Inspect the export or print page-range selection. You may have chosen **Current page**, **Selection**, or a limited range. Export **All pages** for a full copy, then inspect the PDF page count and ending. A file that opens successfully may still be incomplete.

### “The last row of my table is cut off.”

Check the source table's width, cell content, page margins, orientation, and page breaks. Avoid forcing a table onto the page by shrinking text until it is unreadable. Adjust the editable source using the formatting tools learned in Lessons 4 and 5, save it, and export again. Verify the repaired PDF, not only the editing view.

### “A picture looks blurry or stretched.”

The source picture may be low-resolution or may have been stretched disproportionately. An export option with aggressive compression may also reduce clarity. Return to the source: choose a suitable picture that you are authorized to use, maintain its proportions, and review export quality. Do not enlarge a tiny screenshot and expect professional print clarity.

### “The PDF opens, but I cannot select its text.”

You might have exported or received a flattened image-based PDF, or the viewer may restrict selection. Test another known text-based PDF in the same viewer if appropriate, and inspect how the file was produced. When you control the source, prefer direct export of actual text rather than converting page screenshots into a PDF. OCR may recover text from scans but requires verification and does not automatically create complete semantic structure.

### “The PDF looks correct, so accessibility must be finished.”

Not necessarily. A visually correct page may lack tagged headings, correct table associations, meaningful image descriptions, or logical reading order. Review the source structure and use accessible export options where supported. For formal publication, an appropriate accessibility review may be required beyond this beginner visual inspection.

### “My links worked in the source, but not in the PDF.”

Some export or print workflows may not preserve links as expected. Test a sample link in the generated PDF. If it fails, check the hyperlink in the editable source, try a dedicated export route with suitable accessibility and link preservation, and recheck the PDF. An underlined or blue-looking line of text is not proof of a working link.

### “I exported a PDF, but the document I emailed was an older version.”

The new file may have saved to another directory while an earlier attachment remained in Downloads. Match the attachment's actual filename and contents to your approved output. For a real corrected document, make it clear that a new version replaces the earlier copy. A modified source does not update files already attached to sent messages.

### “Google Docs says my edits are saved, but my downloaded PDF is still old.”

Google Docs saving and PDF downloading are different operations. The PDF is a separate snapshot taken when you downloaded it. After changing the cloud document, download a new PDF and inspect it. If your connection is offline or changes are not synchronized, verify save status before downloading or distributing a copy.

### “The file is too big for the upload form.”

Look for the destination's maximum size and any permissible image compression options. Reduce oversized images in the editable source or use a suitable supported PDF export quality option. Do not sacrifice readability, remove important pages, or upload the file to an unfamiliar online compressor without understanding privacy requirements. If a form enforces a size limit, follow that organization's specific instructions.

### “The PDF asks for a password and the recipient cannot open it.”

The output may have been protected during export. Do not use an unexpected password-setting option for ordinary practice. If a real process calls for encryption, follow its approved access instructions. Do not share confidential passwords in open messages or store them in the same place as protected files without authorization.

### “I see comments or tracked changes in the exported PDF.”

Your export settings or source may have included markup, notes, or review content. Inspect the source and the export choices, particularly options controlling document content versus markup. Do not delete or accept another person's changes without authorization. Produce and check an appropriate clean copy before sharing.

### “The PDF viewer displays a warning or cannot open the file.”

Confirm that the file is not an incomplete download or the wrong format with a misleading extension. Try opening a known safe PDF in the same trusted viewer to distinguish a viewer issue from a file issue. Re-export from the original if necessary. Do not install unknown software from a pop-up claiming it is required to view the file. Many current browsers and operating systems already support basic PDF viewing.

### “I accidentally overwrote the previous practice copy.”

If a source document remains intact, you can ordinarily export another PDF. For important real documents, follow applicable version-history and backup procedures rather than assuming a replace operation can always be reversed. The beginner habit is to identify the destination and filename **before** confirming an overwrite.

### A compact troubleshooting decision tree

| Symptom | Likely question | First safe action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Missing file | Where did the application save or download it? | Inspect Downloads and the last-used folder |
| Wrong extension | Was PDF selected as the format? | Export again with PDF chosen explicitly |
| Missing pages | Was a restricted page range selected? | Choose All pages and verify page count |
| Broken layout | Does the source have overflow or awkward breaks? | Fix the source and re-export |
| Poor accessibility | Were structure and descriptions preserved? | Review source semantics and export settings |
| Incorrect content | Was the approved source updated? | Correct, save, and re-export the source |
| Older file shared | Which copy was attached or uploaded? | Match the final attachment to verified PDF |

> **Troubleshooting principle:** Investigate one cause at a time. Preserve the editable source; correct the source or export settings; generate a new PDF; and inspect that result. Randomly changing several options at once makes the problem harder to understand.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Select the dotted-underlined terms earlier in the lesson, when your dashboard supports vocabulary tooltips, to read short definitions. Some words describe ordinary document work; others name options you might see during PDF export. The most important distinction is between an editable **source file**, an exported **PDF copy**, and the **verification** that establishes whether the output is suitable for its intended use.

- **PDF:** Portable Document Format, commonly used to exchange documents with consistent page-oriented presentation.
- **Editable source:** The working document used to make ongoing changes before a new export.
- **Export:** Creating a copy in another format while normally retaining the original document.
- **Download:** Transferring a file from an online application or service onto a device or selected storage location.
- **Save As:** Saving a file under a name, location, or format chosen in an application.
- **Print to PDF:** Using a virtual printer pathway to generate a PDF rather than producing paper.
- **Filename and extension:** The descriptive name of a file and its format suffix, such as `.pdf`.
- **Page range:** The selected pages included in an export or print operation.
- **PDF viewer:** Software used to open and read a PDF; a modern web browser can often do this.
- **Tagged PDF:** A PDF with structural information that can help assistive technology interpret the document.
- **Alternative text:** Text describing the information or purpose of an image where needed for nonvisual access.
- **Reading order:** The intended sequence of content as encountered by assistive technology.
- **Document structure:** Meaningful information identifying headings, paragraphs, lists, tables, and other elements.
- **Optical character recognition (OCR):** Automated recognition of printed or handwritten characters in an image or scan.
- **Quality setting:** An export choice influencing resolution, compression, and sometimes file size.
- **PDF/A:** A family of PDF standards for long-term preservation of documents.
- **Revision:** A subsequent changed version of a document.
- **Verification:** Checking the actual saved output against its requirements.
- **Distribution check:** Final review of accuracy, audience, permission, format, and attachment before sharing.

### Practical distinctions to remember

| Term A | Term B | Why they are different |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Save | Export | Saving retains the current working document; export creates another representation |
| Download | Cloud autosave | Downloading produces a separate file; autosave updates the cloud working document |
| PDF file | Word document | A PDF is primarily a distribution format; Word is usually the editable source |
| PDF export | Print to PDF | The export path may preserve more navigation and semantic structure |
| Visible heading | Structural heading | Enlarged text may look like a heading without being tagged as one |
| Searchable text | Accessible PDF | Searchability alone does not establish correct reading order, tables, or alternative text |
| Compression | Redaction | Smaller file size does not remove private information safely |
| Copy | Current version | A copy may be outdated; identify the approved, verified version before sharing |
