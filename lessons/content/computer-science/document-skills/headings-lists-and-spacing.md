## Lesson 4: Headings, Lists, and Spacing

A useful document is more than a collection of sentences. Readers need to recognize its subject, identify its sections, follow steps in the correct order, and move comfortably from one idea to the next. This lesson explains how to organize a beginner document using meaningful headings, genuine bulleted and numbered lists, and deliberate spacing. You will improve a fictional community-workshop notice while learning habits that transfer to letters, instructions, reports, assignments, and other everyday documents.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 80–105 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–3, *Creating a Document*, *Typing, Editing, and Saving*, and *Formatting Text*, recommended · **Practice equipment:** A computer with Microsoft Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice Writer, Apple Pages, or another word processor; a tablet or smartphone may be used with adapted controls

> **Essential idea:** Organize information by its **meaning**, not only by how it looks. Apply a real heading style to a heading, a real list command to a list, and paragraph-spacing controls to separate ideas. Then inspect the result, save a distinct copy, and reopen it to verify the structure.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish a document [[title|title]] from its [[heading|headings]] and [[body-text|body text]].
1. Explain why a [[heading-style|heading style]] communicates structure more reliably than bold text or a larger font alone.
1. Use headings in a logical [[hierarchy|hierarchy]], without skipping levels only to obtain a preferred appearance.
1. Create, continue, and end a real [[bulleted-list|bulleted list]] for items that do not require a fixed sequence.
1. Create a real [[numbered-list|numbered list]] for procedures in which order matters.
1. Recognize a [[nested-list|nested list]] and manage list levels without inserting repeated spaces.
1. Distinguish [[line-spacing|line spacing]], [[paragraph-spacing|paragraph spacing]], and the appearance of a [[blank-paragraph|blank paragraph]].
1. Apply basic [[alignment|alignment]] and [[indentation|indentation]] without using spacebar alignment or unstable tab sequences.
1. Use document navigation or an outline, when available, to check whether headings are recognized.
1. Save and reopen a well-organized fictional notice and explain the formatting decisions used.

## 1. Why do headings, lists, and spacing matter?

Imagine a notice describing a community workshop. It contains the event's date and location, what participants should bring, and how to prepare. If every sentence looks identical, the reader must examine the entire notice to find each detail. The same information becomes easier to use when the document has a clear title, short labeled sections, a list of supplies, and an ordered set of preparation steps.

[[document-structure|Document structure]] is the meaningful arrangement of information. A [[heading|heading]] announces a section's subject. A list groups related entries into items. [[spacing|Spacing]] makes relationships easier to perceive without changing the wording. Together, these features create a path through the document.

### Appearance and meaning are not the same thing

A heading is not simply a line that looks large. Many word processors have named [[paragraph-style|paragraph styles]] such as *Title*, *Heading 1*, *Heading 2*, and *Normal* or *Body*. Applying an appropriate style can identify the line's role, support an outline or navigation pane, and make formatting more consistent across the document. A person using assistive technology may also benefit from correctly marked headings, depending on the file format, program, and reading tool.

A bulleted list is not merely a row of typed symbols. The word processor can store a list as a structured set of items, control how the markers align, and keep indentation consistent when text wraps onto another line. Likewise, a numbered list can manage numbers when an item is inserted or removed.

| Document element | What it communicates | Appropriate use | Unreliable substitute |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Title | The subject of the whole document | “Community Workshop Notice” | An unexplained large-font line |
| Heading | The subject of one section | “What to Bring” | Bold paragraph text without a heading role |
| Bulleted list | Related items with no required sequence | Notebook, pencil, charging cable | Manually typed symbol and spaces on every line |
| Numbered list | Steps with a meaningful sequence | Open the file, edit it, save it | Manually typed numbers that must be repaired |
| Paragraph spacing | Visual separation between complete ideas | A small gap after a heading | Repeated Enter presses |
| Indentation | Relationship or position of a paragraph/list level | A subitem inside a list | Repeated spaces at the start of a line |

### The reader's questions

Before editing a document, ask: **What is this? Where is the information I need? Which items belong together? What should I do first?** If the layout answers those questions quickly, it is performing its job. A good structure need not be elaborate: a short notice might require one title, two or three sections, one bulleted list, and one numbered list.

A document that is easy to scan is not necessarily accurate. Verify dates, times, locations, and instructions before emphasizing them. Formatting improves access to information; it does not replace checking the information itself.

### Continue the same practice project

In the previous lessons, you created, edited, saved, and formatted a fictional community-workshop notice. Continue from a **separate practice copy** rather than changing the original. If you did not complete those lessons or cannot find the file, the practice text in Section 7 provides a fresh starting point. The activities use imaginary details so you do not need to disclose personal information or work with a real business or school document.

> **Remember:** Select structure according to the purpose of the content. The same words can look attractive without being organized meaningfully; choose the command that gives each part the correct role.

## 2. How do you create meaningful headings?

The [[title|title]] states what the entire document is about. A [[heading|heading]] introduces a major section. A [[subheading|subheading]] divides a major section into a smaller, related topic. The difference is partly logical: a subheading should belong to the heading above it. A paragraph is not a heading merely because it is short, bold, or displayed by itself.

### A simple heading hierarchy

Think of a document as a set of labeled folders. A major section has its own subject; a subsection belongs inside that subject. In many word processors, built-in heading levels express this relationship:

| Role | Common style name | Example in the practice notice | What follows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Whole document title | Title or equivalent | Community Workshop Notice | An overview or first section |
| Major section | Heading 1 | Workshop Details | Date, time, and location |
| Another major section | Heading 1 | What to Bring | A short supplies list |
| Subsection within a major section | Heading 2 | Optional Supplies | Items that belong under What to Bring |
| Ordinary description | Normal or Body | The session introduces basic document skills. | Other sentences in the paragraph |

The names may vary: some apps offer *Title*, *Heading*, *Heading 1*, or custom paragraph styles. The crucial feature is that the paragraph has a meaningful heading role. Using **Heading 2** just because its font size looks attractive is not a good reason to skip **Heading 1**. If the text is a major section, choose the level that represents a major section and then adjust the style appearance if needed.

### Heading 1 does not mean the first line

The number in **Heading 1** indicates the first level in the heading hierarchy, not necessarily the first line of the document. The title may use a dedicated *Title* style while the first major section below it uses *Heading 1*. An appropriate document might be organized this way:

**Community Workshop Notice** — document title

**Workshop Details** — first major heading

**What to Bring** — second major heading

**Optional Supplies** — subsection of What to Bring

**Preparation Steps** — third major heading

Avoid jumping from Heading 1 directly to Heading 3 simply because Heading 3 has the desired size or color. A visual preference should not dictate an illogical outline. The same principle applies to nested lists: a second-level item should depend on a real first-level item.

### Apply a heading style to an existing paragraph

1. Open your **practice copy** in a word processor.
1. Click anywhere inside the line that says **Workshop Details**; some apps allow the insertion point alone to identify the paragraph.
1. Locate the paragraph-style or styles control, often labeled **Normal**, **Body**, or with the name of the current style.
1. Choose **Heading 1** or the equivalent first-level heading style.
1. Repeat for **What to Bring** and **Preparation Steps**, making them the same structural level.
1. Select **Optional Supplies**, if present, and apply **Heading 2** because it is a subsection of What to Bring.
1. Inspect the whole notice and make sure ordinary sentences remain in the body-text style.

Changing a heading style may also change the font, size, weight, color, or spacing. That is expected: paragraph styles often contain several formatting properties. A heading does not need to resemble the example's exact font or color. The meaningful style and consistent hierarchy are more important than a particular visual design.

### What happens when you press Enter after a heading?

When you press **Enter** (or **Return**) at the end of a styled heading, many editors switch the new paragraph to an ordinary body-text style. Other applications or custom templates may preserve the heading style. Before typing the next paragraph, look at the active style. If it still says **Heading 1**, apply the body style rather than making an entire explanatory paragraph into a heading.

The **Enter** key generally creates a new paragraph. **Shift+Enter** often inserts a line break within the same paragraph, but behavior varies by editor and platform. Use a new paragraph for separate ideas and a normal continuation for a paragraph's remaining sentences; do not rely on manual line breaks to manufacture heading structure.

### Use an outline or navigation pane as a check

Many word processors provide a [[document-outline|document outline]], [[navigation-pane|navigation pane]], or a comparable heading view. When available, turn it on and look for **Workshop Details**, **What to Bring**, and **Preparation Steps**. If one heading does not appear, check whether it was only enlarged and bolded rather than assigned a heading style.

- **Microsoft Word:** Look in **Home → Styles** to apply heading styles; Word also provides a navigation view for headings.
- **Google Docs:** Use the toolbar's paragraph-style menu to apply headings; open the document outline to inspect them.
- **LibreOffice Writer:** Use the paragraph styles controls to apply heading styles; the Navigator can display recognized headings.
- **Apple Pages:** Apply paragraph styles through the Format sidebar; navigation and outline features vary by document mode and platform.

Do not use an application's outline as the sole proof of accessibility. Different export formats and reading tools handle structure differently. For this beginner exercise, the outline is a useful check that styles were applied as intended.

### Why styles make revision easier

Suppose your notice grows to ten sections. If you manually enlarge every section heading, changing their appearance later could require ten separate edits. With a named style, an application that supports style customization can let you revise that style once and update all matching headings. You are not required to customize a style in this lesson; it is enough to understand why consistent style use simplifies future work.

> **Decision rule:** A title names the whole document; Heading 1 names a major section; Heading 2 names a subsection of that major section; ordinary explanation remains body text.

## 3. When and how do you use bulleted lists?

A [[bulleted-list|bulleted list]] presents related items without claiming that one must happen before another. A supply list, a set of topics, or several meeting reminders often belongs in bullets. The visible marker may be a dot, dash, or another symbol, but the underlying structure should be a real list created by the word processor.

### Choose bullets when order is not the message

Consider the notice's supply information:

**What to Bring**

- A notebook
- A pen or pencil
- A charged laptop, if available

You can bring these items in any order. The document is asking **which items** to bring, not **what action to perform first**. This makes bullets the natural choice. By contrast, opening a file before editing and saving it is a sequence; that belongs in numbers.

| Situation | Better initial choice | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Materials to bring | Bulleted list | All materials are needed; no sequence is implied |
| Topics covered in a workshop | Bulleted list | Topics are grouped for scanning |
| Three steps for submitting a form | Numbered list | The actions may have an order |
| One sentence explaining the date | Ordinary paragraph | There is no collection of separate items |
| A definition followed by explanation | Paragraph with a label, or heading and paragraph | The information is not a list of peer items |

### Make a real list instead of typing symbols

In a word processor, a list command typically adds markers, controls indentation, and recognizes individual paragraphs as items. The recommended beginner workflow is:

1. Type each item on its own paragraph, without typing bullet symbols manually.
1. Select the paragraphs that belong together. Alternatively, place the insertion point on the first empty item line.
1. Choose the **Bullets** command, often shown as dots beside short lines.
1. Check that every intended item has exactly one list marker and that text is aligned consistently.
1. Insert a new item by pressing Enter at the end of an existing item, if the app supports that behavior.
1. End the list with the editor's stop-list command or, in many apps, press Enter again on an empty list item.

The final step is worth practicing. Beginners sometimes unintentionally continue a list into the next ordinary paragraph because the editor expects another item. If a new paragraph retains a bullet, choose the bullet command to turn it off or use the application's list-exit behavior. Never press many spaces to push the bullet out of the way.

### What happens to long list items?

A list item may wrap onto a second display line when its wording is long. A proper list generally aligns that continuation with the **item text**, not under the bullet itself. This is often handled with a [[hanging-indent|hanging indent]] or the list's built-in indentation settings.

Try typing a longer item in your practice document: “A charged laptop or tablet, if available, with any required accessibility accessory.” Narrow the window or increase zoom so the line wraps, then inspect whether the second line stays aligned with the first line's words. You do not need to adjust the exact indent measurement; simply recognize what consistent alignment looks like.

### Do not make every paragraph a bullet

Bullets work best for genuinely separate, parallel items. If an instruction requires three sentences of context, you may need a short introductory paragraph followed by a few concise bullets. Avoid turning an entire essay or letter into disconnected fragments because list markers look orderly. The structure should help the reader understand relationships, not conceal them.

### Accessibility and clarity in lists

Use the application's genuine list formatting rather than manually typing decorative icons or unusual symbols that may be read inconsistently by assistive technology. Keep the items grammatically parallel when practical: for example, begin all supplies with nouns rather than mixing “Notebook,” “You should bring a pen,” and “Remember to charge your laptop.” A short list is easier to scan when the structure and wording follow a consistent pattern.

> **Remember:** Bullets answer “Which items?” or “What are the options?” They generally do not mean “First, second, and third.”

## 4. How do you create numbered and nested lists?

A [[numbered-list|numbered list]] identifies items in a sequence or in a clearly ordered reference series. Numbering is especially helpful when a reader must complete one action before the next. A genuine numbered list can update its item numbers when you insert, remove, or reorder entries.

### Numbers communicate sequence

For a beginner preparing a document, the procedure might be:

1. Open the practice document.
1. Check the event details.
1. Add the required supplies list.
1. Save the changes.
1. Reopen the saved file and verify the result.

If you remove step three, an application-managed numbered list should normally renumber the remaining steps. Manually typing `1.`, `2.`, and `3.` as ordinary characters can create unnecessary correction work and may not form a list the program recognizes as such.

### Create a numbered list

1. Start with a short sentence introducing the procedure, such as **Preparation Steps**.
1. Put each action on its own paragraph without manually adding numbers.
1. Select the paragraphs that contain the actions, or place the insertion point at the intended start.
1. Choose **Numbering** or **Numbered list**, often shown as `1 2 3` next to lines.
1. Inspect the numbering and order. Correct wording before adding more items.
1. Add one extra action between two existing items and confirm that later steps renumber automatically.
1. Use the application's list-ending behavior to return to ordinary body text.

Do not confuse numbered list items with numbered **headings**. “1. Workshop Details” might be a heading in a long report, while “1. Open the document” is a procedural list item. A document may use both, but they serve different purposes. Advanced heading-numbering schemes are beyond this beginner lesson.

### Nested items express a relationship

A [[nested-list|nested list]] has an item inside another item. This is useful when a main step has a smaller subordinate action or when a main supply category contains examples. For example:

1. Gather your materials.
   1. Find a notebook.
   1. Prepare a pen or pencil.
1. Open the practice document.
1. Review and save the updated notice.

The two smaller actions belong under **Gather your materials**. Their indentation communicates that relationship; it should not be added purely to make the page look decorative. Some word processors may show letters or different number patterns for the subordinate level. Those visible markers are application-dependent.

### Change list levels deliberately

Many desktop editors let you use **Increase Indent**, **Decrease Indent**, **Increase List Level**, or **Decrease List Level** to move an item into or out of a nested level. In some apps, **Tab** at the beginning of a list item creates a deeper level and **Shift+Tab** returns it. This is **not universal**: Tab can insert a tab character or move focus between controls, particularly on the web or with accessibility settings. When uncertain, use the labeled list-level command instead.

| Action you want | Prefer | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Make an item a subitem | Increase List Level or equivalent | Typing repeated spaces before the item |
| Return a subitem to the main list | Decrease List Level or equivalent | Deleting random indentation characters |
| Insert a new item between steps | Press Enter at the correct position and type | Renumbering all items manually |
| End numbering and begin a paragraph | End-list command or empty-item exit | Leaving a number in front of ordinary text |
| Start a separate list at 1 | Use restart-numbering control when available | Hiding an unwanted continuing number with spaces |

### Continue numbering or restart numbering?

Editors may automatically continue a previous numbered list. This is useful when a procedure is separated by explanatory text but remains one sequence. It is not useful when you begin a new, unrelated procedure. Check whether the new list should begin at **1** or continue with the prior number. Word processors often offer **Restart at 1**, **Continue Numbering**, or **Set Numbering Value**. The exact labels vary. Pick the option that matches the meaning; do not merely change the visible numeral by typing over it.

### Numbering is not a substitute for headings

If your notice has three major sections, give them heading styles. Do not number everything just to create a sense of order. Use a numbered list for actions or indexed items, bullets for unordered collections, and styles for section names. This keeps each tool responsible for a clear communication task.

> **Decision rule:** Use numbers when order or reference positions matter. Use a nested level only when an item genuinely belongs within a parent item.

## 5. How do line and paragraph spacing work?

Text needs room to be read, but the room should be controlled by document settings rather than accidental empty lines. The phrase **spacing** can describe several different things. In this lesson, focus on spacing **within** paragraphs and **between** paragraphs. A page margin is different again: it separates page content from the edge of the page.

### Line spacing is the distance between lines in a paragraph

[[line-spacing|Line spacing]] controls the vertical distance between the lines of a single paragraph. Common choices in word processors include single, 1.15, 1.5, and double spacing; some applications also support fixed points or proportional measurements. The label “1.5” generally means more space than a single-spaced setting, but exact results depend on the program, font, and paragraph style.

A paragraph of five lines remains **one paragraph** when you change its line spacing. You are adjusting the way those lines sit on the page, not creating additional paragraphs. A short notice often looks orderly with moderate line spacing, but a class assignment or professional template may specify something different. Follow any applicable requirement rather than treating one setting as universally correct.

### Paragraph spacing separates ideas

[[paragraph-spacing|Paragraph spacing]] controls extra space **before** or **after** a paragraph. Suppose your notice has a paragraph introducing the workshop and another giving arrival instructions. A small space after the first paragraph can show that the second is a new idea. With paragraph-spacing controls, the app maintains that gap as content moves and reflows.

The difference matters because **two blank lines typed by pressing Enter** create additional empty paragraphs. Those empty paragraphs may be inconvenient to edit, may move unpredictably when formatting changes, and may create unwanted pauses in some accessibility workflows. For ordinary separation between blocks of text, paragraph spacing is usually more reliable.

| Setting or action | What it affects | Example | Common problem if misused |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Line spacing | Distances among lines of one paragraph | 1.5-spaced body paragraph | Every long paragraph becomes unnecessarily tall |
| Space before | Gap above a paragraph | Extra room before a heading | Duplicate gaps if also added manually |
| Space after | Gap below a paragraph | Modest separation after a heading | Inconsistent distances when settings vary |
| Blank paragraph | An extra empty paragraph object | Press Enter twice between sentences | Large or irregular gaps; extra editing work |
| Page margin | Boundary between content and the page edge | One-inch margin in a template | Mistaken for paragraph indentation |
| Document zoom | How large the page looks on screen | Zoom to 125% to read comfortably | Confused with saved spacing or font size |

### Practice measuring spacing by observation

You do not need a ruler or knowledge of points to begin. Make a practice paragraph with four lines of text, then try the application's line-spacing control. Compare **single** and **1.5** spacing and decide which is easier to read. Reset to an appropriate choice before saving. Next, place the insertion point in a separate paragraph and try a little **space after** it. Observe how the gap is added without making a new empty paragraph.

When a style already defines spacing, changing it directly may create a formatting override. This is not necessarily an error, but in a long document, consistent styles usually provide a cleaner way to manage repeated heading gaps. For a short notice, it is enough to understand the distinction and avoid conflicting changes.

### Headings need space, but not isolation

A heading should be visibly connected to the paragraph or list it introduces. If the gap **below** a heading is larger than the gap **above** it, the heading can appear detached from its own section. Many styles are designed with appropriate spacing already; inspect before changing anything. Some applications also offer **Keep with next**, which helps a heading remain on the same page as the following paragraph. That option is valuable in longer documents, but you do not need to master page-break controls in this lesson.

Consider these common situations:

- **Crowded notice:** Improve line spacing or add modest paragraph spacing, rather than inserting a blank paragraph after every sentence.
- **Heading separated from its section:** Reduce the gap below the heading or check the heading's style and “Keep with next” behavior.
- **Extra empty page:** Look for repeated empty paragraphs or manual page breaks before changing margins or deleting content indiscriminately.
- **Assignment with required double spacing:** Follow the assignment's specified settings and verify the finished page layout.

### Show nonprinting characters when available

Many editors offer **Show/Hide formatting marks**, **Nonprinting Characters**, or an equivalent view. This can reveal paragraph boundaries, space characters, and tabs. The characters you see in this mode generally do not print as ordinary document content. Looking at formatting marks is a useful way to distinguish a real paragraph break from a manual line break or a series of spaces.

The commands vary; Word commonly has a paragraph-mark button (¶) in the Home ribbon, while Writer has a nonprinting-characters view. Google Docs also offers a non-printing-characters option in supported interfaces. Do not assume the exact same menu path on every platform. You can complete the lesson without this feature by carefully observing the insertion point and using Undo after each experiment.

### Consistency is more important than arbitrary measurements

A polished one-page notice usually needs predictable distances: the same gap between comparable sections, the same line spacing in equivalent body paragraphs, and enough separation to recognize list boundaries. Do not use one paragraph at double spacing, the next at single, and the last at 1.15 merely to fit or fill a page. If the document seems too long, first consider whether the wording is needlessly repetitive or whether you added unnecessary blank paragraphs.

> **Remember:** **Line spacing** separates lines inside a paragraph. **Paragraph spacing** separates paragraph blocks. Pressing Enter repeatedly is not a substitute for either setting.

## 6. How do you control alignment, indentation, and layout?

[[alignment|Alignment]] describes where a paragraph's text sits horizontally within its available area. [[indentation|Indentation]] moves the start or boundary of a paragraph inward relative to its usual position. A [[page-margin|page margin]] is the reserved area around the page; changing a margin affects the space available to many paragraphs, not just a selected one. These concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable.

### Start with readable alignment

In left-to-right writing, most ordinary instructional paragraphs are comfortably read with [[left-alignment|left alignment]], meaning the text begins at a consistent left edge. [[center-alignment|Centered alignment]] can work for a short title, but large centered paragraphs are harder to scan. [[right-alignment|Right alignment]] may serve a specific form field or label, while [[justified-alignment|justified alignment]] adjusts spacing so both sides of the paragraph appear aligned. Justification may introduce uneven gaps between words, especially on narrow lines.

The appropriate alignment depends on script, language, document purpose, and reader preferences. For right-to-left languages, the expected text direction and alignment may differ. Do not force left alignment onto text that requires another writing direction. For the English-language practice notice, use left-aligned body paragraphs unless the template or purpose calls for a different arrangement.

| Alignment | What it does | Reasonable beginner use | Potential drawback |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Left | Begins lines at a common left edge | Body text in the English-language notice | Not appropriate for every writing direction |
| Center | Places each line near the horizontal center | Short title, if appropriate | Hard to track in long paragraphs |
| Right | Ends lines at a common right edge | Special dates or labels in a template | Makes ordinary left-to-right paragraphs harder to scan |
| Justify | Adjusts line spacing to align both edges | Some formal layouts | May produce wide spaces or inconsistent word gaps |

### Paragraph indentation is not a page margin

An [[indentation|indent]] moves a paragraph or a list item inward. A [[first-line-indent|first-line indent]] changes the starting position of only the paragraph's first line. A [[hanging-indent|hanging indent]] keeps the first line farther to the edge while following lines start farther inward; hanging indents are often useful in lists and references. A **left indent** moves the entire paragraph inward. A page margin defines the outer content boundary for the page or section instead.

If you need to move one paragraph slightly inward, do not change the page's margins. If your list markers and text are misaligned, use the list's indentation controls rather than typing repeated space characters. If an assigned document requires an exact first-line indent, use the application's paragraph or ruler controls so the text retains its relationship when it wraps.

### The spacebar does not create a reliable layout grid

Using ten spaces to center a title is fragile: the result changes with font, size, window width, or export. Use the **Center** alignment command instead. Similarly, using repeated spaces to move a list item inward may look correct briefly but fail when an item wraps or another person opens the file. Real paragraph indentation and list-level controls store the intended arrangement.

A [[tab-character|tab]] can serve useful purposes in certain layouts, and programs may have [[tab-stop|tab stops]] for aligning content. But pressing Tab repeatedly to simulate a table is not a sound beginner approach. In the next lesson, *Inserting Images and Tables*, you will learn to use a real table when information needs rows and columns.

### Line break, paragraph break, and page break

These editing actions have different purposes:

- A **paragraph break** ends the current paragraph and begins another, often created with Enter.
- A **manual line break** moves to a new line within the same paragraph, often created with Shift+Enter where supported.
- A **page break** moves subsequent content to a new page in page-based documents; it is not a substitute for paragraph spacing.

For the workshop notice, use paragraph breaks to separate its heading, introductory paragraph, list items, and closing note. Do not insert manual page breaks to create gaps between sections. If your app offers a pageless view, page-break behavior may be unavailable or different; focus first on meaningful text structure.

### Locate controls in your word processor

The commands in this table describe common desktop interfaces, not fixed locations across all releases. Word for the web, Google Docs mobile, Pages on iPad, and other variants may have fewer or differently placed options.

| Word processor | Headings | Lists | Spacing and alignment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Microsoft Word | **Home → Styles** | **Home → Paragraph**, Bullets and Numbering | **Home → Paragraph**; more options in paragraph settings or Layout |
| Google Docs | Paragraph-style menu, often showing **Normal text** | Toolbar or **Format → Bullets & numbering** | **Format → Line & paragraph spacing**; toolbar alignment controls |
| LibreOffice Writer | Paragraph-style selector or Styles sidebar | Formatting toolbar or **Format → Bullets and Numbering** | **Format → Paragraph**, toolbar alignment, and paragraph-style settings |
| Apple Pages | **Format** sidebar, paragraph-style menu | **Bullets & Lists** settings | **Format** sidebar, text alignment, and **Spacing** controls |

If your toolbar differs, search the application's Help menu for **heading**, **bullets**, **numbering**, or **paragraph spacing**. Look for the command's descriptive label rather than choosing an unfamiliar icon at random. If working on a shared or institutional device, do not modify default templates or global application preferences just to complete this lesson.

### A limited shortcut reference

| Task | Often on Windows | Often on Mac | Important note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Undo a recent action | Ctrl+Z | Command+Z | Use promptly if an experiment affects the wrong text |
| Save the active document, when applicable | Ctrl+S | Command+S | Cloud documents may save automatically |
| Select all document text | Ctrl+A | Command+A | Confirm the document has keyboard focus |
| Add a paragraph break | Enter | Return | Usually creates a new paragraph |
| Add a manual line break | Shift+Enter | Shift+Return | Behavior varies by app and context |
| Indent or outdent list item | App-specific commands | App-specific commands | Tab behavior is not universal; use menu buttons when uncertain |

### Accessibility and preview checklist

A well-organized document should have a meaningful order, readable headings, consistent lists, and clear spacing. Do not communicate critical instructions by alignment or indentation alone. In a list, explicitly write the action: “Save the document,” not merely “Save” if a beginner could mistake what must be saved. Check how content looks at normal zoom and at a larger zoom if possible; long lines should reflow without creating a chaotic list structure.

When the word processor offers a heading outline, check that it reflects your section structure. Where practical, use the app's accessibility checker before distributing a final document. These checks are aids, not guarantees that every person or reading tool will interpret the document identically.

> **Practical workflow:** First establish headings and lists, then adjust spacing and alignment. Avoid using appearance fixes to conceal a structural mistake.

## 7. Guided practice: Organize a workshop notice

## 8. Common mistakes and troubleshooting

### “My heading is bold, but it does not appear in the outline.”

You probably applied direct text formatting rather than a named heading style. Place the insertion point in the paragraph and choose an appropriate heading style. Check the outline again. Some applications offer additional manual outline controls, but do not mistake bold appearance for confirmed structure.

### “Everything I type after a heading becomes a heading.”

The new paragraph may have inherited the heading style. Choose the ordinary body style before continuing. If an existing explanatory paragraph has the wrong style, click inside that paragraph and correct it. Avoid deleting all formatting from the entire document.

### “I see a Heading 3 because I liked its size.”

A heading level expresses a relationship, not a font-size preference. Use Heading 1 for a major section, Heading 2 for a subsection, and adjust appearance through styles or formatting where appropriate. Do not skip levels to produce a visual effect.

### “I typed dots, but my list is uneven.”

Delete manually typed bullet characters if appropriate, select the actual item paragraphs, and apply the word processor's Bullets command. Let the app handle marker positioning and wrapped lines. Do not add repeated spaces in front of items.

### “The second line of an item starts under the bullet.”

Inspect the list's indentation or hanging-indent settings. A true list usually aligns wrapped text with the first line's words. If the item uses a typed bullet and spaces, convert it to a real list first.

### “My numbered list started at 5.”

The editor may be continuing an earlier sequence. Use its **Restart at 1** or equivalent command when the new procedure is independent. If it should continue a prior sequence, preserve continuation. Do not manually replace the number's display characters.

### “Pressing Tab made the wrong thing happen.”

Tab can change a list level in some contexts, insert a tab character in others, or move focus to another control. Use **Increase List Level**, **Decrease List Level**, or the application's list controls when unsure. Undo accidental changes promptly.

### “The whole paragraph moved when I tried to indent one line.”

You may have applied a paragraph indent rather than a first-line indent. Decide whether you intend to move the whole paragraph, only its first line, or the continuation lines of a list. Then select the matching paragraph control.

### “There are huge gaps between paragraphs.”

Check for repeated empty paragraphs, unusually large Space Before or Space After values, and inherited paragraph styles. Remove unwanted empty paragraphs carefully and adjust one consistent spacing rule. Do not immediately shrink the font to hide the spacing problem.

### “The document is too spread out after choosing double spacing.”

Double spacing changes distances between lines. If the requirement does not call for it, return to a suitable line-spacing setting. Also inspect paragraph spacing; the two settings can combine to make an unnecessarily tall document.

### “My heading sits alone at the bottom of a page.”

Some word processors offer **Keep with next** or related paragraph-pagination controls. Use an appropriate heading style first; then check whether the app supports keeping the heading with its following paragraph. Avoid manually inserting many blank lines to force a different page position.

### “The title shifted when I opened the document elsewhere.”

Check whether you centered it using real paragraph alignment or manually inserted spaces. Fonts and page widths also vary between environments. Use the alignment command and common supported formatting rather than spacebar positioning.

### “I cannot find the exact menu shown in the lesson.”

Application editions, device sizes, and mobile interfaces change available controls. Use the application's Help or command search, look for labels such as **Heading 1**, **Bullets**, **Line spacing**, and **Paragraph**, or complete the concept exercise on paper. Do not install an unknown extension just to reproduce the screenshots or menu names from another app.

### Quick troubleshooting guide

| Symptom | First question to ask | Safe next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heading missing from outline | Is it a real heading style? | Apply the appropriate paragraph heading style |
| List markers uneven | Are they typed manually? | Replace with the app's list command |
| Numbered list continues unexpectedly | Is this a new procedure? | Restart numbering if it is independent |
| Text too close together | Is it line or paragraph spacing? | Adjust the relevant setting, one at a time |
| Gap too large | Are there empty paragraphs or extra spacing values? | Inspect formatting marks and spacing controls |
| Paragraph sits too far inward | Was indentation applied accidentally? | Use Undo or adjust that paragraph's indent |
| Save appears to lose formatting | Did you reopen the correct file and compatible format? | Check filename, location, app, and actual saved copy |

### A repeatable repair sequence

When the layout seems wrong, **stop and identify the affected paragraph**. Use Undo if the problem was caused by a recent command. Otherwise inspect whether the issue concerns the heading style, list type, list level, line spacing, paragraph spacing, or alignment. Change one setting, look again, and save only when the intended result is visible. Avoid selecting the entire document unless you genuinely intend to change every paragraph.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Words underlined with a dotted line earlier in the lesson can be selected for brief explanations. When an authoritative reference is appropriate, an additional link may be included. The following terms cover the principal concepts needed for the activities and knowledge check.

- **Document structure:** Meaningful organization of titles, headings, sections, lists, and paragraphs.
- **Title:** Wording that names the entire document rather than one section.
- **Heading:** A label introducing a section of content.
- **Subheading:** A heading for a section contained within a larger section.
- **Heading hierarchy:** The order and relationship among major headings and subordinate headings.
- **Heading style:** A named paragraph style that indicates a heading's structural role.
- **Body text:** Ordinary explanatory text that appears beneath a heading or inside a section.
- **Paragraph style:** A named collection of settings and, sometimes, structural meaning for a paragraph.
- **Document outline:** A structural view of recognized headings and their relationships.
- **Navigation pane:** A program area that may let users move among document sections or search results.
- **Bulleted list:** A list for items where order is generally unimportant.
- **Numbered list:** A list using ordered markers for steps or referenced positions.
- **List item:** One entry within a bulleted or numbered list.
- **Nested list:** A list with subordinate items grouped under another item.
- **List level:** The position of an item within a multi-level list structure.
- **Hanging indent:** Indentation in which following lines start farther inward than the first line.
- **Line spacing:** Distance between lines within a paragraph.
- **Paragraph spacing:** Additional distance before or after a paragraph.
- **Blank paragraph:** An empty paragraph produced by a paragraph break without substantive text.
- **Alignment:** Horizontal placement of text within its available space.
- **Indentation:** The inward offset of paragraph or list content relative to its normal edge.
- **Page margin:** Reserved space between page content and the page's outer boundary.
- **Paragraph break:** A boundary separating one paragraph from the next.
- **Manual line break:** A break to a new line without necessarily creating a new paragraph.
- **Page break:** A boundary that advances content to another page in a page-based document.
- **Keep with next:** A pagination setting intended to keep a paragraph with the following paragraph.
- **Formatting marks:** Visible aids showing characters such as spaces, tabs, or paragraph breaks.
- **Save:** Store changes to a document for later retrieval.
- **Verify:** Check that the saved file matches the intended structure and appearance.
