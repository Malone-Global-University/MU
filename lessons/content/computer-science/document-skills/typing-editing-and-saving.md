Basic Computer Skills / Document Skills

## Lesson 2: Typing, Editing, and Saving

Creating a document is the beginning of useful computer writing. The next skill is keeping control of the words after they appear on the screen. You need to enter text, move to the right place, correct errors without losing good work, and save changes so the document is available when you return. These habits matter when writing a school assignment, preparing a work notice, keeping meeting notes, revising a letter, or updating a simple set of instructions.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 75–95 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lesson 1, *Creating a Document*, recommended · **Practice equipment:** A keyboard and an available word processor are recommended; a tablet, smartphone, or paper practice sheet can be used for adapted exercises

> **Essential idea:** A dependable writing cycle is **type a little → read what appeared → make small corrections → save or confirm automatic saving → reopen and verify**. The text visible on your screen is not, by itself, proof that the latest changes have been stored.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Place the [[insertion-point|insertion-point]] in a document and enter text accurately at a comfortable pace.
1. Use Space, Enter, Backspace, Delete, Shift, and common navigation keys for ordinary writing.
1. Distinguish inserting text from replacing a selected passage.
1. Select a character, word, sentence, or paragraph and make targeted edits.
1. Explain and safely use [[undo|undo]], [[redo|redo]], [[copy|copy]], [[cut|cut]], and [[paste|paste]].
1. Recognize the difference between an automatic line wrap and a deliberate paragraph break.
1. Save a first document, save later revisions, and make a separate copy when appropriate.
1. Understand the limits of [[autosave|autosave]], [[synchronization|synchronization]], and recovery tools.
1. Reopen a practice file and check that a specific revision was preserved.
1. Use safe, accessible, and repeatable habits when editing on shared or personal devices.

## 1. What happens when you type in a document?

A [[document|document]] is a useful record of written information. In a [[word-processor|word-processor]], you can enter text, rearrange it, and preserve the result. The screen shows the document's current appearance, but editing and storing the document are separate activities. You may have made a change that has not yet been successfully saved, synchronized, or made available on another device.

Most document tasks can be divided into four actions:

| Action | What you do | Example | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Type** | Enter new characters | Write a sentence announcing a meeting | The words appear where intended |
| **Edit** | Correct or rearrange existing words | Change the meeting time | Only the intended words changed |
| **Save** | Store the current work | Save the revised notice | A name, location, or successful save status is known |
| **Verify** | Retrieve and inspect the stored work | Close and reopen the file | The corrected meeting time remains |

**Typing** is entering letters, numbers, symbols, and punctuation. **Editing** is improving or correcting content that already exists. **Saving** is preserving the document or its changes for later retrieval. **Verifying** is checking that your expectation matches the file or cloud document actually available to you.

### Why editing is not the same as typing

Imagine a notice that says, “The workshop begins at 2:00 p.m.” You discover that it should say “3:00 p.m.” You could delete and retype the entire document, but that creates unnecessary chances for mistakes. A better approach is to locate the time, change only the incorrect part, read the surrounding sentence, and save the revision.

Effective editing uses the smallest reasonable change. You do not need to be a fast typist to be an effective writer. Accuracy, recovery, and consistency matter more than speed when you are learning.

### Your practice document

You can reuse the fictional meeting notice from Lesson 1, provided that it is your own practice file. If it is not available, create a **new** document called **Community Workshop Notice — Practice**. Do not type into an unfamiliar document or overwrite a school, work, or personal file. Every exercise in this lesson can be completed with imaginary details; no private names, addresses, account numbers, or passwords are required.

> **Core rule:** When in doubt, work on a separate practice copy. Changing the original by mistake is easier than recovering information that was overwritten.

### The three different things to recognize

A document editor often has three independent signals: the text on the page, the name of the document, and the save or synchronization status. A visible sentence is **content**, not a guarantee of storage. A filename identifies the item, but does not prove every recent edit was stored. A status indicator is helpful, but you must understand what it means in the application you are using.

## 2. How do you type accurately?

Typing begins with the [[keyboard|keyboard]] or an alternative text-entry method, such as an on-screen keyboard or [[dictation|dictation]]. On most physical keyboards, individual keys enter characters. Some keys change how characters appear, move the [[insertion-point|insertion-point]], or issue commands. On mobile devices, the same ideas apply even though the controls may look different.

### Find the insertion point first

The insertion point is a thin, often blinking line that marks where text will be entered. It is not the mouse pointer. Before writing, click or tap inside the document and confirm that the insertion point is where you want the next letter to appear. In some applications, typing while another control has [[focus|focus]] can place words into a search box, filename field, or browser address bar instead of the document.

Try typing: **The workshop starts at noon.** Observe exactly where the sentence appears. Then stop and read the whole sentence. That small pause is part of the habit this lesson teaches.

### Important keys for beginner writing

| Key | Typical purpose | Useful example | Common caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Letter and number keys | Type ordinary characters | `meeting 12` | Keyboard language and layout affect symbols |
| **Space** | Put a space between words | `Community workshop` | Avoid repeated spaces for alignment |
| **Enter / Return** | End a paragraph or confirm a command, depending on context | Begin a new paragraph | In some dialogs Enter activates a button |
| **Backspace** | Remove text to the left of the insertion point | Correct `notcie` | May delete a whole selection instead |
| **Delete / Forward Delete** | Remove text to the right of the insertion point | Remove a stray character | Some compact keyboards lack a separate Delete key |
| **Shift** | Type uppercase letters or upper symbols while held | `W` in `Workshop` | Do not hold it for ordinary lowercase typing |
| **Caps Lock** | Toggle letter capitalization on many keyboards | An acronym, if intended | May cause an entire sentence to appear in capitals |
| **Arrow keys** | Move the insertion point | Move left one character | They do not change text by themselves |
| **Home / End** | Often go to start/end of a line | Return to the start of a line | Behavior varies by app and platform |
| **Tab** | Move focus or add a tab stop, depending on context | Move through dialog fields | Do not use repeated tabs to lay out a document |

Keyboard layouts vary. Some laptop keyboards require an **Fn** modifier for certain keys. Touchscreens may display a context-dependent keyboard. Screen readers and switch-access systems may offer equivalent operations through different controls. The skill to learn is the **effect**, not an identical key arrangement on every device.

### Capital letters and punctuation

Use uppercase letters intentionally: usually at the beginning of a sentence and for proper names. A period ends an ordinary statement; a question mark ends a question; a comma helps organize parts of a sentence. Do not add extra spaces before ordinary punctuation. In English-language writing, place one normal space after a period before the next sentence, unless your application or style guide specifies otherwise.

Compare these examples:

| Needs correction | Improved text | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `we will meet friday` | `We will meet Friday.` | Initial capital, proper capitalization, and period |
| `Pleasebringnotebooks.` | `Please bring notebooks.` | Added word spaces |
| `The class , starts soon .` | `The class starts soon.` | Removed unnecessary spaces and comma |
| `THERE WILL BE A WORKSHOP` | `There will be a workshop.` | Changed unintended capitals and added a period |

These are ordinary writing examples, not a substitute for the fuller proofreading work in Lesson 6. For now, concentrate on seeing each character accurately and correcting simple errors immediately after a short passage.

### Word wrap is not Enter

A word processor normally uses [[word-wrap|word-wrap]]: when a sentence reaches the right edge of the text area, the next word continues on the following visible line automatically. You do not need to press Enter at the end of every screen line. Press Enter when you intend to start a **new paragraph**, not whenever the text reaches the margin.

If you repeatedly press Enter to make a large blank area or press Space many times to center a title, the layout may shift when fonts, screen widths, or printers change. The later *Headings, Lists, and Spacing* lesson teaches appropriate paragraph and layout controls.

### Comfortable typing habits

Read the text in short groups, type at a pace you can control, and check each sentence before continuing. Use the application's zoom or device accessibility features if text is difficult to see. Take brief breaks as needed and use a comfortable posture. Typing speed is not graded in this lesson; successful written communication is the target.

## 3. How do you move around and select text?

Editing often begins with moving the insertion point to the correct position. On a computer, you can click with a mouse, tap on a touchpad, or move with keys. On a touchscreen, tap in the text and, when available, drag the insertion-point handle. The precise behavior varies by application, device, and accessibility settings.

### Place the insertion point carefully

Suppose your document says: **The train leaves at six.** You want to add the word “early” before “train.” Click between “The ” and “train,” or navigate there with the arrow keys. Type **early **, including a space after it. The result should read: **The early train leaves at six.**

If your words appear at the end of the document, you have probably placed the insertion point there rather than where you intended. Stop and reposition it. If the text you type replaces something unexpectedly, check whether a passage was selected.

### What does selected text mean?

A [[selection|selection]] is a range of content marked for an action. In many editors it appears highlighted. You might select a single word to replace it, a complete sentence to move it, or several paragraphs to copy them. When text is selected, ordinary typing usually **replaces the selection**. Pressing Backspace or Delete may remove the selected content. This is powerful but easy to misuse.

| Goal | Common mouse or keyboard method | Before changing anything |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Move to a particular place | Click within the text or use arrows | Look for the blinking insertion point |
| Select a short passage | Click and drag across the intended characters | Check the start and end of the highlight |
| Select one word | Double-click the word in many desktop editors | Confirm only the intended word is highlighted |
| Expand a selection with a keyboard | Hold Shift while using arrows | Avoid selecting adjacent punctuation accidentally |
| Select all document text | Ctrl+A on many Windows/Linux editors; Command+A on many Mac editors | Make sure the document body has focus |
| Remove a selection without editing | Click elsewhere or press an arrow, depending on app | Confirm the selection disappeared |

Touch devices often use a long press and selection handles; screen-reader users may use dedicated selection commands. Some editors automatically select an entire word or add punctuation to a double-click selection. Always inspect the highlighted text before deleting or replacing it.

### Inserting, replacing, and deleting are different

- **Insert:** Put new text at the insertion point without removing existing content.
- **Replace:** Select existing text, then type the new text over it.
- **Delete:** Remove an unwanted character or a selected passage.

With the sentence **The lesson begins at 9.**, try three separate changes: insert **morning** before **lesson**, replace **9** with **10**, and delete **morning** again. After each action, reread the sentence. These techniques are more reliable than repeatedly erasing the entire line.

### Keyboard shortcuts: helpful, not mandatory

[[keyboard-shortcut|Keyboard shortcuts]] combine keys to perform an action. The most common desktop shortcuts use **Ctrl** on Windows and many Linux systems, and **Command (⌘)** on Mac. Some browser apps reserve or reinterpret particular combinations, so menu commands remain a useful fallback.

| Action | Often on Windows desktop | Often on Mac desktop | Safest fallback |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Select all text in active editing area | Ctrl+A | Command+A | Edit menu → Select All |
| Copy selection | Ctrl+C | Command+C | Copy command |
| Cut selection | Ctrl+X | Command+X | Cut command |
| Paste clipboard contents | Ctrl+V | Command+V | Paste command |
| Undo recent change | Ctrl+Z | Command+Z | Undo command |
| Redo an undone change | Ctrl+Y or Ctrl+Shift+Z, app-dependent | Command+Shift+Z, commonly | Redo command |
| Save in many desktop editors | Ctrl+S | Command+S | File menu → Save |
| Find text | Ctrl+F | Command+F | Edit → Find or Search |

Do not treat a shortcut as a guarantee of the same behavior everywhere. For example, in a browser application, a save shortcut may be intercepted by the browser or may not be needed because the document is saved automatically. A shortcut can also act on a filename box or browser page rather than the document if that is where focus currently is.

## 4. How do you edit text safely?

Editing means changing an existing [[draft|draft]] with control. The basic sequence is **locate → select or place the insertion point → change → reread → save**. Good editors make small, reversible changes and confirm the final sentence still means what it should.

### Replace a mistake without retyping everything

Imagine this fictional sentence:

> The public workshop will start at 2:00 p.m. on Thursday.

The time changes to 3:00 p.m. Select only **2:00** and type **3:00**. Do not select the whole sentence unless you actually intend to replace it. The revised sentence should read:

> The public workshop will start at 3:00 p.m. on Thursday.

This method also works for a misspelled word. If you wrote **communtiy**, select only that word and type **community**. If you are unsure what is highlighted, click away and start the selection again.

### Undo and redo: recover from a small mistake

[[undo|Undo]] usually reverses the most recent editing action. If you accidentally delete a sentence, immediately look for **Undo** in the Edit menu or toolbar. [[redo|Redo]] usually reapplies an action that was just undone. An application may allow several consecutive undo steps, but the length and persistence of its undo history vary. Closing and reopening a document may clear the available undo history.

A safe practice is to write a disposable sentence, delete one word, and undo the deletion. Then try redo. Notice the difference between **undo** (reverse an edit) and **reopen** (load stored content). They are not interchangeable recovery methods.

> **Important:** If you discover an accidental deletion or replacement, stop making unrelated changes. Each additional edit can complicate recovery. Check Undo first; then inspect available saved copies or version history if needed.

### Cut, copy, and paste

[[copy|Copy]] places a duplicate of selected information onto the [[clipboard|clipboard]] while leaving the original in place. [[cut|Cut]] places selected information on the clipboard and removes it from the current location. [[paste|Paste]] inserts the clipboard content at the insertion point or replaces a selected passage.

Use **Copy** when you want the same text in two places. Use **Cut** when you want to move text from one place to another. Before cutting, confirm that the source is your practice text, that the complete passage is selected, and that you know where it should go. If you cut and paste in the wrong location, Undo may help.

### Why pasted text sometimes looks different

Copying text from a website or another document may carry [[formatting|formatting]] such as font choices, links, or spacing. This is not necessarily an error. For this lesson, prefer copying plain practice words from your own document. If pasted text carries unwanted appearance, look for a **Paste as plain text**, **Keep text only**, or similar option where available. The exact wording and shortcut differ by app; the formatting lesson covers this more fully.

Never paste passwords, private customer records, payment information, or confidential workplace content into a practice file. Some clipboard history tools retain copied items. When using a shared device, keep all exercises fictional and non-sensitive.

### Find and replace: helpful for repeated words

A [[find|Find]] command searches for a word or phrase. A [[find-and-replace|Find and Replace]] command can locate text and substitute a different expression. For example, an imaginary training document might mention **Thursday** three times; if the event moves to Friday, you can find each mention and update it. **Review each occurrence** rather than using Replace All without checking. Replacing every instance of a short word can unintentionally change unrelated sentences or parts of longer words.

Find and Replace may appear under **Edit**, **Home**, a search icon, or another application menu. It is an optional introductory tool here. For a short document, carefully selecting and replacing one word at a time is often easier.

### Edit for meaning, not just spelling

Compare these two sentences:

- *Original:* “Bring something for the workshop.”
- *Improved:* “Bring a notebook and a pen to the workshop.”

The second sentence is more useful because it tells the reader exactly what to bring. Editing may involve adding needed information, removing repetition, improving word order, or correcting a factual detail. Later lessons address spellcheck and systematic proofreading, but you can begin by asking: **Does my revised sentence say what I intend?**

## 5. How do you build and revise clear paragraphs?

A [[paragraph|paragraph]] is a related group of sentences that develops one idea. When you press Enter in an ordinary word-processing document, you generally begin a new paragraph. A [[line-break|line break]] within the same paragraph is sometimes possible, but the exact method depends on the editor. For beginning document work, separate distinct ideas into paragraphs rather than trying to make every line break manually.

### Build a practical three-paragraph document

Use the following fictional draft as a model. Type it into your own practice document; do not use real participant data.

> **Community Workshop Notice**
>
> The community center will host a free workshop on Thursday at 2:00 p.m. The workshop will introduce basic computer skills.
>
> Participants should bring a notebook and pen. Computers will be available for guided practice.
>
> Please contact the organizer before the event if you need an accessibility accommodation.

This model has one short title and three body paragraphs. The title does not need a special font yet. Use a normal line between each paragraph if the app's standard paragraph spacing is not obvious. Avoid inserting many empty paragraphs for decoration.

### Make meaningful revisions

Now make the following changes in the practice notice: change **Thursday** to **Friday**, change **2:00 p.m.** to **3:00 p.m.**, change **a notebook and pen** to **a notebook and a pen**, and add the sentence **The session will last one hour.** to the first body paragraph. Do not rewrite the entire notice; edit the existing words.

After the changes, compare your work with this target:

> **Community Workshop Notice**
>
> The community center will host a free workshop on Friday at 3:00 p.m. The workshop will introduce basic computer skills. The session will last one hour.
>
> Participants should bring a notebook and a pen. Computers will be available for guided practice.
>
> Please contact the organizer before the event if you need an accessibility accommodation.

The difference between the original and the target demonstrates four normal editing actions: replacing a day, replacing a time, inserting a missing article, and adding an entire sentence. All details are fictional and for practice only.

### A review routine for every paragraph

Read the paragraph once for **meaning**: does the message make sense? Read a second time for **accuracy**: are numbers, dates, and words correct? Finally, check **placement**: is each sentence in the intended paragraph, and were any words accidentally duplicated or removed? You can make this check visually, using a screen reader, or by reading the text aloud if that is useful and appropriate for your setting.

### Avoid over-formatting during a draft

The purpose of Lesson 2 is to control text and save revisions. You do not need to change colors, choose decorative fonts, insert images, or create tables inside the practice document. Those skills are introduced later in the Document Skills sequence. A clear, accurate paragraph with a reliable saved copy is a successful outcome.

## 6. How do you save changes reliably?

[[save|Save]] means storing the current document or its latest changes. Some editors ask you to choose a [[filename|file name]] and [[folder|folder]]. Others save a document to an online account as you work. These methods are useful, but they work differently. Learning to **verify** a stored change is more important than memorizing one button.

### First save, later saves, and a separate copy

| Situation | Typical command or behavior | Main purpose | Important check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brand-new desktop document | Save or Save As, depending on app | Create the first stored file | Choose a name, format, and location |
| Existing editable document | Save | Preserve changes in the same item | Confirm the intended file was modified |
| Need a second independent file | Save As, Save a Copy, or Duplicate | Keep an original and a separate version | Make sure the second item has its own identity |
| Cloud document that saves automatically | Automatic saving or syncing | Preserve changes through the service | Look for successful save/sync status and correct account |
| Need a more fixed sharing format | Export or Download as PDF | Produce a distribution copy | Usually handled in the later Saving as PDF lesson |

**Save** usually updates an existing document. **Save As**, **Save a Copy**, or **Duplicate** may create a separately named item, but the exact options depend on the application and whether the document is local or cloud-based. Do not assume choosing a different name always leaves the original untouched; confirm the title and location of each copy.

### Choose a descriptive name

For this lesson, use **community-workshop-notice-practice** as your base name. Your application may add or display a [[file-extension|file extension]] such as `.docx`, `.odt`, or `.pages`, or it may use an online document identity without showing an extension. You do not need to force a particular format. Avoid names like `Document1`, `finalfinal`, or `new thing` because they are difficult to recognize later.

The file name and the text heading inside the document are separate. Renaming the file does not necessarily change the on-page heading. Similarly, editing the on-page heading does not necessarily rename the stored item.

### Know the storage location

[[local-storage|Local storage]] refers to files held on a device or directly accessible storage. [[cloud-storage|Cloud storage]] refers to documents stored through an online service or account. A file in a synchronized folder may have both local and online copies, but the availability and currentness of those copies can depend on settings and connection status. A cloud document that is visible in a browser is **not** necessarily a separately downloaded file on the computer.

Before closing, answer these questions: **What is the document called? Which application or account holds it? In which folder or online location should I find it? Have the latest edits been stored successfully?**

### Microsoft Word: save the practice revision

On Word desktop, use **File → Save** or the application's Save control. On the first save, Word may ask for a name and location; follow those prompts and choose an appropriate folder or configured cloud destination. For later changes to the same document, save again if automatic saving is not active. If you need a separate copy, look for **Save As** or **Save a Copy**. Menu details differ by Word version and storage location.

**AutoSave is not universal.** In supported Microsoft 365 configurations, Word can automatically save supported files stored on OneDrive or SharePoint when AutoSave is enabled. A local file, unsupported format, or configuration without that feature may require an ordinary save. Check the application's actual status instead of assuming every Word document is saved continually.

### Google Docs: confirm your account and save status

Google Docs normally stores edits automatically in the associated Google Drive account when its online synchronization is functioning. Give the document a descriptive name, confirm you are using the intended account, and look for signs that changes have been saved or are still being processed. If connection problems occur, a document may not yet be available in the account's current online version. Offline editing requires appropriate setup and may sync later.

You do not normally need to use a traditional desktop **Save As** step for a native Google document. To keep a separate editable copy, look for **File → Make a copy** or the corresponding app option. To create a downloaded file, use a suitable download option; a download is not automatically synchronized with the original Google Doc.

### LibreOffice Writer: save the edited file

In LibreOffice Writer, use **File → Save** for the current document. For a new item, the Save dialog ordinarily lets you choose its name, folder, and format. Writer commonly uses `.odt` as its default word-processing file format. Use **File → Save As** when you deliberately need a new name, location, or supported format. If the program warns you about possible formatting differences in another format, read the warning before confirming.

Writer also has recovery features in some circumstances, but their presence does not guarantee that every unsaved change can be restored. Continue to make intentional saves and verify that the saved file opens correctly.

### Apple Pages: name and preserve the document

In Pages on Mac, choose **File → Save** to name and place a new document when prompted. Later work may be saved automatically through the application's document-management system. You can rename a document or create a separate duplicate using supported commands. When working with iCloud Drive, confirm you are viewing the intended account and that syncing has completed before expecting the latest version on another device. On iPhone or iPad, controls and storage options differ from the Mac interface.

### Why the save indicator matters

A [[save-status|save status]] may say **Saved**, **All changes saved**, **Saving**, **Offline**, or present an icon. The wording varies across apps and versions. **Saving** can indicate an operation is still in progress. **Offline** may mean the app is not currently communicating with online storage. A warning can indicate that action is required. Do not ignore errors, close the app immediately after a questionable status, or assume a different device already has the latest version.

### A two-minute verification test

After editing your harmless practice notice:

1. Confirm the name and location of your practice file or cloud document.
1. Use Save if required and wait for any visible processing to finish.
1. Check that your revised text says **Friday at 3:00 p.m.**
1. Close only the practice document, after confirming there is no warning about unsaved changes.
1. Reopen that same document through its saved location or document list.
1. Find **Friday at 3:00 p.m.** again. If it remains, you have evidence that the revision was preserved in the copy you reopened.

Reopening the correct document is the most concrete beginner-level check. A recent-files shortcut can be convenient, but if it opens an older similarly named file, you have not verified the right copy. Match the document name and, where appropriate, its location.

### Save, sync, version history, and backup are not synonyms

[[synchronization|Synchronization]] moves changes between a device and a service or between connected devices. [[version-history|Version history]] may let you examine or restore previously saved states of a document. A [[backup|backup]] is a separate copy made to protect against loss. [[recovery|Recovery]] features may help after an application or device problem. These capabilities differ by app, account, and configuration, and none should be assumed to exist without checking.

A cloud status that says **saved** does not necessarily mean you have an independent backup. A version-history entry is not necessarily a local file. A backup made yesterday may not contain today's changes. In this lesson, focus on reliable saving and verification; later lessons cover organization, exporting, and more deliberate file management.

### Shared and public computers

If you are on a shared machine, do not use personal or confidential material for practice. Use an authorized account, avoid uploading documents to the wrong service, and make sure the session is ended according to the location's policy. Do not remove or alter another person's documents. An incognito or private browser window does not by itself guarantee that files, downloads, clipboard entries, or account data have been removed from a shared computer.

## 7. Guided practice: Type, edit, and save

These exercises build on one another. Use a new practice document or a copy of your own Lesson 1 file. If you have no suitable editor, you can write answers on paper and describe the expected key or command; however, the final save-and-reopen exercise requires an application with storage. **Never use a real password, identifying record, private letter, or another person's document for these activities.**

### Activity 1: Enter text and identify the insertion point (about 8–10 minutes)

**Goal:** Type two short sentences without adding unnecessary line breaks.

1. Open your practice document and click or tap in the editing area.
1. Look for the insertion point before typing.
1. Type the sentence: **We are learning to write useful documents.**
1. Press Space once after the period and type: **Accuracy is more important than speed.**
1. Read both sentences. If the second sentence has wrapped automatically onto a new visible line, do not insert an extra paragraph break.
1. Press Enter once to start a new paragraph, then type **A saved document can be opened again.**
1. Check that you have two paragraphs, not a separate paragraph for every visual line.

Use this observation table as a worksheet:

| Check | Your result | What success looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Insertion point located | Yes / Not yet | Typed text appears in the intended area |
| First two sentences entered | Yes / Not yet | Words and punctuation match the model |
| Only one intentional paragraph break | Yes / Not yet | The third sentence begins a new paragraph |
| No extra alignment spaces | Yes / Not yet | Ordinary single spaces separate words |

If a word looks wrong, correct it instead of erasing the entire passage. You will practice more precise correction in Activity 2.

### Activity 2: Correct characters, words, and punctuation (about 10 minutes)

**Goal:** Use navigation, Backspace, Delete, and replacement.

Type the following practice lines as shown. Each contains an intentional problem:

1. `The wokshop begins at nine.`
1. `Pleasebring a pencil.`
1. `The lesson starts on monday`
1. `We will meet at 8:00 a.m. tomorrow.`

Make four corrections:

1. Fix **wokshop** to **workshop** without deleting the entire line.
1. Add the missing space between **Please** and **bring**.
1. Capitalize **Monday** and add the missing period.
1. Change the time from **8:00 a.m.** to **9:00 a.m.** by replacing only the necessary part.

:::answer Show Activity 2 corrected text
1. The workshop begins at nine.
1. Please bring a pencil.
1. The lesson starts on Monday.
1. We will meet at 9:00 a.m. tomorrow.

The important result is that each change was targeted. You did not need to recreate any entire paragraph.
:::

### Activity 3: Select, copy, move, and recover (about 10–12 minutes)

**Goal:** Distinguish copying from moving, and practice Undo without risk.

1. In a new paragraph, type **Bring a notebook.**
1. Select exactly that sentence and choose **Copy**. Click or tap at the end of the document, start a new paragraph, and choose **Paste**.
1. Check that the sentence now appears twice: the original and its duplicate.
1. Select the duplicate sentence. Choose **Cut**, move the insertion point to a different position in the practice document, and choose **Paste**. The sentence should move rather than disappear.
1. Delete the moved sentence deliberately. Use **Undo** immediately and check whether it returns.
1. If available, try **Redo** and observe what action it reapplies. Use Undo again if needed to preserve your preferred text.
1. Finish by rereading the surrounding paragraphs for duplicated spaces or missing punctuation.

**Checkpoint:** Copy leaves the original text in place; Cut removes the selected source content; Paste inserts clipboard contents; Undo reverses a recent action when supported. If the pasted text looks unexpectedly styled, return to the original or use the app's plain-text paste option if available.

### Activity 4: Revise a short notice without retyping it (about 12–15 minutes)

**Goal:** Apply four edits to an existing document while preserving the rest.

If you completed Lesson 1, you may use a copy of that notice. Otherwise, type the **Community Workshop Notice** model from Section 5. Then make the following changes:

1. Replace **Thursday** with **Friday**.
1. Replace **2:00 p.m.** with **3:00 p.m.**.
1. Correct **a notebook and pen** to **a notebook and a pen**.
1. Insert **The session will last one hour.** after the first body paragraph's sentence about basic computer skills.
1. Read the full notice and check whether all four changes are present.

Use this revision checklist:

| Requested edit | Completed? | Evidence in final draft |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day changed | Yes / Not yet | Friday |
| Time changed | Yes / Not yet | 3:00 p.m. |
| Missing article inserted | Yes / Not yet | a notebook and a pen |
| Sentence added | Yes / Not yet | The session will last one hour. |

A successful revision keeps all other useful information in place. Do not remove the paragraph about accessibility accommodations. If the selected region accidentally included other words, undo the change and repeat it more carefully.

### Activity 5: Save and reopen the corrected document (about 10–15 minutes)

**Goal:** Demonstrate that a real revision was preserved, not merely visible on screen.

1. Name the practice document **community-workshop-notice-practice**, or give it another distinctive descriptive name.
1. If using a desktop editor, choose a known folder or configured storage location. If using Google Docs or another cloud editor, confirm the correct account and document name.
1. Save explicitly when needed. If automatic saving is supported, check its completion status rather than assuming success.
1. Before closing, write down which app/account and folder or location hold the item.
1. Make sure the revision includes **Friday at 3:00 p.m.** and that any save process has finished.
1. Close the **practice document**, then find and reopen it through the relevant document list or storage location.
1. Locate **Friday at 3:00 p.m.** and the added sentence **The session will last one hour.**
1. If either change is missing, stop and investigate the name, account, location, and save status rather than editing multiple copies blindly.

| Verification question | Your answer |
| --- | --- |
| What is the document's name? | __________ |
| Which application and account, if applicable, hold it? | __________ |
| Which folder or online location contains it? | __________ |
| Did you reopen the same document? | Yes / Not sure |
| Are the corrected day and time still present? | Yes / No |
| Is the added sentence still present? | Yes / No |

**Completion standard:** You reopened an identifiable stored document and confirmed at least two specific revisions. Looking at the unclosed editing window does not meet this standard.

### Activity 6: Make a separate practice version (about 8–10 minutes)

**Goal:** Learn to protect an original while creating a second version.

1. Reopen **community-workshop-notice-practice** if necessary.
1. Use the application's **Save As**, **Save a Copy**, **Duplicate**, or **Make a copy** function, whichever is appropriate.
1. Name the second item **community-workshop-notice-practice-revised**.
1. In the new copy, change the fictional meeting from **Friday** to **Saturday**.
1. Confirm the revised copy shows Saturday, while the original still shows Friday.
1. Check their titles and locations. If both now show Saturday, the second file may not have been created independently; review your app's copy workflow.

This exercise introduces a critical professional habit: preserve a known-good original when making a significant or experimental revision. It does not require elaborate naming systems; that is addressed later in *Document Organization*.

### Activity 7: Repeat the cycle with a different message (about 8–12 minutes)

**Goal:** Transfer the skill to a fresh situation without relying on the workshop example.

Choose **one** harmless, fictional scenario: a library reading-session notice, a short welcome letter for a volunteer group, or a reminder about a classroom supply list. Write three or four sentences. Deliberately replace one word, insert one missing fact, and correct one punctuation error. Save the document under a unique name, close it, and reopen it to confirm the final sentence remains correct.

When finished, explain the cycle in your own words: **I typed __________, changed __________, saved it in __________, and verified __________ after reopening.** This reflection demonstrates that you understand the method rather than merely following a particular app's buttons.

### Practice safety and accessibility

Do not test failure recovery by turning off power, unplugging equipment, disabling synchronization, deleting files, or forcing an app to crash. Those experiments can destroy real work. If a shortcut is difficult to use, select the equivalent visible command or an accessibility control. You may use screen magnification, a screen reader, a virtual keyboard, speech input, or another accessible method to complete the same learning goals. If working on an employer's or school's device, follow its file-storage and privacy rules.

## 8. Common mistakes and troubleshooting

Mistakes are an expected part of learning to edit. The priority is to recognize the symptom, identify a likely cause, and respond without damaging other work. Begin with a reversible action and confirm what changed.

### “I typed, but the words did not appear on the page.”

The insertion point may not be in the document. The keyboard input might be going to a search field, dialog, filename box, or another app. Click or tap inside the body of the document and try typing a harmless word. If the document is [[read-only|read-only]] or you lack edit permission, the app may prevent changes. Do not attempt to bypass ownership or permission controls; use a new file you own.

### “Typing erased the words I wanted to keep.”

You may have selected existing text and then typed, which replaced the highlighted selection. Use Undo immediately if appropriate, then click to position the insertion point without selecting nearby words. Some editors or input modes can also behave differently when overwrite or replace mode is enabled; inspect the editor's status if the problem persists.

### “Backspace deleted too much.”

A whole phrase may have been highlighted, or you may have held the key down too long. Stop, use Undo if available, and select a smaller passage. Do not keep deleting until the sentence looks right by accident.

### “My new line appeared in the wrong place.”

You may have pressed Enter in the middle of a paragraph, or the document may have wrapped text naturally. Check whether you intended a new paragraph. If not, remove the unneeded paragraph break carefully. Use the display's normal word wrapping rather than pressing Enter at every screen line.

### “The text I pasted has an unexpected font, link, or spacing.”

The clipboard may have copied formatting as well as words. Undo the paste if necessary, then use the app's text-only paste option if available. Do not try to correct a large pasted block by inserting dozens of spaces or manual line breaks. The *Formatting Text* lesson develops the relevant skills.

### “The shortcut did not do what the lesson says.”

Different applications, operating systems, keyboard layouts, and browser contexts interpret shortcuts differently. A web app may not use every desktop shortcut. Use the application's menu command, consult its help documentation, and confirm that the editing area has focus.

### “The file seems to have disappeared.”

Check the filename, folder, app, signed-in account, and any recent-files list. A document saved to a cloud account may not appear in a local Downloads or Documents folder. Another copy may have a similar name. Search within the appropriate account or authorized storage rather than assuming the file was deleted. Do not overwrite an older file while searching for the newer one.

### “I changed the document, but the old version reopened.”

You may have opened a duplicate, saved to a different folder, changed the wrong document, or closed before changes were stored. Compare names and storage locations. If the app provides version history or recovery tools, inspect them carefully. Do not assume a successful-looking screen proves the correct file received the update.

### “AutoSave was on, but the other device shows older text.”

Synchronization across devices may be delayed by internet connectivity, offline mode, account mismatches, or conflicts. Look for the service's sync status, confirm both devices use the same account and document, and avoid creating competing edits while uncertain. A locally visible change can be newer than the version currently available on another device.

### “I cannot edit or save this file.”

The file may be read-only, a protected attachment, in a restricted folder, or owned by someone else. If authorized, use the app's create-copy option and save to an appropriate location you can edit. Do not assume a permission problem can be solved by repeatedly clicking Save. Workplace and school policies may control where documents are stored.

### “The app closed unexpectedly before I saved.”

After reopening the app, check any offered recovery or restored-document options. Some applications keep recoverable information, but not every change is guaranteed. Look for the last confirmed saved version. Once you identify recoverable content, store it under a clear name if appropriate. The prevention habit is regular saving plus checking that the document was actually stored.

### Quick response guide

| Symptom | First safe action | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Text appeared in wrong field | Click the document body and test a harmless word | Typing more before checking focus |
| A sentence vanished during editing | Try Undo before unrelated edits | Rewriting the whole document immediately |
| New typing replaced old text | Clear selection and place insertion point carefully | Selecting more text than necessary |
| Pasted text looks strange | Undo, then use text-only paste if supported | Manual spacing to disguise formatting problems |
| Save seems incomplete | Inspect status, name, location, and connection | Closing immediately after an error |
| Old version opens | Compare file identity and saved versions | Editing both similarly named files at random |
| Read-only message appears | Use an authorized editable copy | Circumventing restrictions |
| App crash or lost work | Look for recovery and most recent saved copy | Assuming every change was recoverable |

### A simple prevention checklist

Before each important editing session, confirm that you have the correct document, the correct account or folder, and permission to edit. After a meaningful change, reread it. Before leaving, verify the save status. For high-value material, use your organization's approved backup and version-management procedures rather than depending on one working copy.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Dotted-underlined terms in the lesson can be selected to reveal a brief definition. This section collects the most important terms for review. Not every term has a separate MGU Encyclopedia article, and the lesson does not require external reading to understand the fundamentals.

- **Document:** Recorded information intended to be read, used, or kept.
- **Word processor:** An application for creating and editing written documents.
- **Draft:** A working version of writing that can still be revised.
- **Typing:** Entering characters into a computer or document editor.
- **Keyboard:** Physical or on-screen keys used to enter characters or commands.
- **Insertion point:** The position where new typed text will appear.
- **Focus:** The currently active control receiving keyboard input.
- **Character:** A letter, digit, punctuation mark, or other text symbol.
- **Space:** A blank separator between words.
- **Word wrap:** Automatic continuation of text onto the next displayed line.
- **Paragraph:** A group of related sentences or thoughts.
- **Paragraph break:** A division between paragraphs, often made with Enter.
- **Line break:** A move to another line that does not necessarily start a new paragraph.
- **Selection:** Text marked for replacement, copying, cutting, or another action.
- **Highlight:** The visual indication that text is selected, or a separate colored-marking feature depending on context.
- **Navigation:** Moving the insertion point around a document.
- **Backspace:** A key that usually removes the preceding character or a selection.
- **Delete:** A key or command that removes selected or adjacent material.
- **Shift:** A modifier key used for uppercase characters, symbols, or selecting text.
- **Caps Lock:** A toggle that ordinarily changes how letter keys produce capitals.
- **Keyboard shortcut:** A key combination for a command.
- **Insert:** Add text at a chosen position.
- **Replace:** Substitute new content for selected old content.
- **Edit:** Modify existing text to improve or correct it.
- **Undo:** Reverse a recent action when supported.
- **Redo:** Reapply a recently undone action when supported.
- **Clipboard:** Temporary holding area for copied or cut content.
- **Copy:** Duplicate selected content onto the clipboard.
- **Cut:** Remove selected content and place it on the clipboard.
- **Paste:** Insert clipboard contents at the chosen position.
- **Formatting:** The appearance or structured presentation of text, such as font, spacing, or emphasis.
- **Find:** Search a document for matching text.
- **Find and Replace:** Locate matching text and substitute new text.
- **Save:** Store the document or its changes.
- **Save As:** A command often used to save a file with a different name, location, or format.
- **Save a Copy:** A command for making a separate saved item in supported applications.
- **Duplicate:** Create another independent copy of an existing item.
- **AutoSave:** A feature that saves changes automatically when supported and active.
- **Save status:** A message or indicator showing the application's reported state of saving.
- **Filename:** A name that helps identify a stored document.
- **File extension:** A suffix such as `.docx` or `.odt` identifying a file format.
- **Folder:** A container used to organize files.
- **Local storage:** Storage on a device or directly accessible local medium.
- **Cloud storage:** Storage made available through an online service or account.
- **Synchronization:** Updating copies or records across connected devices or services.
- **Offline:** Not currently using a network connection to the relevant service.
- **Read-only:** A state where ordinary editing is restricted.
- **Version history:** A record of past document states, when the application provides one.
- **Recovery:** Restoring accessible work after an error or interruption, when possible.
- **Backup:** A separate protective copy of information.
- **Verify:** Check that an action, result, or stored version is actually correct.
- **Dictation:** Entering text through recognized speech instead of typing every character.
- **Accessibility:** Design and tools that support effective use by people with varied needs.
