## Lesson 3: Formatting Text

Words carry a document's message, but their appearance also affects how easily people can read that message. A clear notice, letter, or short report generally uses a readable font, a consistent size, and a small amount of emphasis to guide attention. This lesson teaches you how to change the appearance of text without accidentally replacing the words, changing an entire document, or making it harder to read. You will practice using common word processors and checking that your saved document retains the intended appearance.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 75–95 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lessons 1 and 2, *Creating a Document* and *Typing, Editing, and Saving*, recommended · **Practice equipment:** A computer with Microsoft Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice Writer, Apple Pages, or another word processor; a tablet or smartphone may be used with adapted controls

> **Essential idea:** Good formatting is a deliberate choice that makes information easier to understand. Use this cycle: **select the intended text → apply one formatting change → inspect the result → correct or undo if necessary → save and verify**. Formatting should support the message, not compete with it.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain the difference between a document's [[content|content]] and its [[text-formatting|text formatting]].
1. Select the correct text before changing its appearance, and recognize where new typing may inherit formatting.
1. Apply and remove [[bold|bold]], [[italic|italic]], and [[underline|underline]] deliberately.
1. Identify a [[font-family|font family]], choose a readable [[font-size|font size]], and recognize basic [[font-style|font styles]].
1. Use [[font-color|font color]] and [[text-highlight|text highlighting]] sparingly without depending on color alone for meaning.
1. Distinguish text-level formatting from [[paragraph-formatting|paragraph formatting]] and understand why structured [[heading-style|heading styles]] are preferable to merely enlarging headings.
1. Recognize the difference between [[direct-formatting|direct formatting]] and reusable [[style|styles]].
1. Remove unwanted appearance changes, including unexpected formatting introduced by pasted text.
1. Format the fictional community-workshop notice consistently in a supported word processor.
1. Check the appearance on screen, preserve the document, and reopen it to confirm the changes.

## 1. What is text formatting?

When you type a sentence, you create [[content|content]]: words, numbers, and punctuation that communicate a meaning. When you change how those characters look, you apply [[text-formatting|text formatting]]. A word can be made bold, italic, larger, smaller, or a different color without changing its spelling. In a [[word-processor|word processor]], these visual choices are generally stored with the document when the format supports them.

Compare the following sentence presented in two ways:

**Ordinary:** Please bring a notebook to the workshop.

**With emphasis:** Please bring a **notebook** to the workshop.

The wording has not changed. The second version places visual emphasis on one important item. That is the principal purpose of this lesson: use appearance to guide attention where doing so helps the reader.

### The difference between content, formatting, and layout

These three ideas work together but describe different decisions. Keeping them separate helps you choose the correct command.

| Part of a document | What it controls | Example | Usually covered in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Content** | The information in the document | “Workshop starts at 3:00 p.m.” | Creating; Typing, Editing, and Saving |
| **Text formatting** | The appearance of characters or selected words | Make “3:00 p.m.” bold | This lesson |
| **Paragraph formatting** | Arrangement of whole paragraphs | Align a paragraph or set line spacing | Headings, Lists, and Spacing |
| **Page layout** | Arrangement of the page or document area | Page margins and orientation | Later document-production tasks |
| **Document structure** | Meaningful organization of information | Title, headings, and sections | Headings, Lists, and Spacing |

Some commands overlap these categories. For example, a heading style may specify a font, size, spacing, and a structural role. A beginner does not need to memorize all distinctions immediately. The important question is: **Am I changing words, changing their appearance, or changing how the paragraph or page is organized?**

### What formatting can and cannot do

Formatting can make a document more readable, emphasize a warning, make a title visually distinct, or help readers scan information. Formatting cannot automatically make an inaccurate sentence true or a confusing instruction clear. If a workshop time is incorrect, changing the time to red does not fix the information. First correct the wording; then decide whether emphasis would be useful.

Good document presentation usually feels restrained. A short notice rarely needs five fonts, several competing colors, or a mixture of bold, italics, and underlining on every sentence. The goal is a hierarchy of attention: the reader sees what the document is about, notices key facts, and can read the remaining details comfortably.

### What counts as readable?

Readable means the intended audience can understand the text with reasonable effort. Consider the document's viewing environment, screen size, print size, language, accessibility needs, and purpose. A font size that feels comfortable on one device may be too small on another. Normal body text often uses a modest, consistent size, such as **11 or 12 points** in many desktop documents, but no single size is appropriate for every person, font, or situation.

Do not use very pale text on a white page for ordinary instructions. Do not make essential information depend only on a particular color being distinguishable. Use precise wording and, when helpful, bold labels. The best formatting is accessible to people who zoom the page, use a screen reader, print in grayscale, or read on a small display.

> **Remember:** You can produce a clear and professional document with just one readable typeface, a consistent body-text size, and a few carefully selected areas of emphasis.

### Your ongoing practice file

This lesson builds on the fictional community-workshop notice created in Lessons 1 and 2. If you have that practice document, open a **copy** of it. If you do not, use the short sample in Section 7. Never practice on an original résumé, employer's notice, school submission, or another person's document without making an independent copy or obtaining permission.

## 2. How do you select text for formatting?

Before you click a formatting command, you must identify **what the command should affect**. In many editors, formatting a [[selection|selection]] changes only the selected words. If nothing is selected, a command may change the appearance of text that you type next, or it may affect the current word or paragraph. This is why accurate selection is essential.

### Selection versus the insertion point

The [[insertion-point|insertion point]] is the place where new characters will appear. A selection is a range of existing content marked for an operation. You can usually recognize selected words by their colored background. If you select **Workshop**, then choose Bold, only that selected text should become bold. If you choose Bold with the insertion point inside an empty line, you may be preparing to type bold words rather than changing words that already exist.

To select a phrase with a mouse, place the pointer at the start of the phrase, hold down the mouse button, drag across the words, and release. To select with a keyboard, position the insertion point and use **Shift** with the arrow keys. You may also double-click a word in many desktop editors. Touchscreens often use a long press and adjustable selection handles. Behavior varies by device, application, and accessibility settings.

### Inspect the boundaries of your selection

Imagine this sentence:

> All volunteers should arrive by 2:45 p.m. for the workshop.

If you need the arrival time to stand out, select only **2:45 p.m.** and apply bold. Look closely for a stray preceding space, a missing period, or an accidentally selected part of the next sentence. Selecting more than intended can create an uneven appearance or cause an unwanted change when you later type.

| You want to format... | Suggested selection | What you should verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One important word | Select that word | No neighboring word changed |
| A short deadline | Select the entire time or date phrase | The time and punctuation remain correct |
| A complete sentence | Select the sentence, not the next paragraph | The selection ends in the right place |
| The whole practice document | Use Select All only while the document body has focus | You truly want every paragraph changed |
| Text you will type next | Position the insertion point, then activate a format | Turn off the format when the special phrase is finished |

If you accidentally select too much, click away and try again. Undo can reverse many unwanted changes. Do not repeatedly apply random controls in the hope that the text will return to normal.

### Format existing text or format while typing?

You can format a selection **after** typing or turn on a format **before** entering new text. Both approaches are valid. For beginners, **write first and format afterward** is usually easier to control. It lets you focus on the wording, then inspect where emphasis belongs. It also reduces the chance of accidentally continuing in bold or italic when ordinary text should resume.

For a brief experiment, place your insertion point at the end of a blank practice paragraph, turn Bold on, type **Important:**, turn Bold off, then type ** Please arrive early.** The first phrase should be emphasized while the second is ordinary text. If the entire sentence remains bold, select the extra portion and remove the bold attribute.

### When selecting everything is useful—and risky

[[select-all|Select All]] is helpful when you want to standardize the body text throughout a short practice file. However, it may also change titles, existing headings, footnotes, or other elements you intended to preserve. On a larger document, changing the [[body-text|body text]] through the appropriate style or template can be safer than selecting every character. This course introduces styles later in the lesson and develops them in the next lesson.

> **Practice checkpoint:** Before every formatting change, say: “I know which text is selected, I know what I want to change, and I can check whether it worked.”

## 3. How do you apply basic emphasis?

The three most familiar text-emphasis tools are [[bold|bold]], [[italic|italic]], and [[underline|underline]]. Their toolbar buttons often display the letters **B**, *I*, and an underlined U. A selected text range may show more than one format at once, but combining every effect usually makes a document harder to scan.

### Bold: make a key word stand out

**Bold** gives text a heavier visual weight. It can help readers notice a short label, a critical time, or a concrete action. For example:

> **Arrival time:** 2:45 p.m.

Using bold on a label may be more useful than bolding the whole paragraph. If everything is bold, nothing stands out by comparison. Bold can supplement a structural heading but does not, by itself, make that text an accessible heading.

To apply bold, select the intended text and use the Bold command on the toolbar or menu. On many desktop editors, **Ctrl+B** (Windows or common Linux configurations) or **Command+B** (Mac) toggles bold. A toggle means choosing the same command again usually removes it from selected bold text. If the selection mixes bold and ordinary text, inspect the result rather than assuming the entire range will be treated identically.

### Italic: distinguish a short phrase

*Italic* text is slanted. It is often used for certain titles, terms, or limited emphasis. For example:

> Read the *orientation guide* before attending.

Italics can become difficult to read when overused, especially in dense paragraphs or when the typeface has an unusual italic design. Use italic selectively; normal text should remain normal. On many desktop word processors, **Ctrl+I** or **Command+I** toggles italic for a suitable selection.

### Underline: understand the context

An [[underline|underline]] draws a line beneath text. It was historically a common method of emphasis, and it remains available in word processors. However, on-screen underlined words are frequently interpreted as [[hyperlink|hyperlinks]]. If you underline ordinary text that does not lead anywhere, readers may assume it is a clickable link. For a notice intended primarily for digital reading, bold is often a clearer choice for an important label.

Underline may still be appropriate when an assigned format specifically requires it or when the document's convention is clear. On many desktop word processors, **Ctrl+U** or **Command+U** toggles underline. Do not underline an entire document simply to make it look more formal.

### Compare emphasis choices

| Tool | Sensible use | Common overuse | Better habit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bold | Important short label or deadline | Every sentence is bold | Bold only what the reader must notice first |
| Italic | A limited term or title | Long paragraphs in italics | Return to ordinary text after the phrase |
| Underline | A required convention | Nonlinked words that look clickable | Prefer bold for general on-screen emphasis |
| Bold + italic | Rare, purposeful special emphasis | Combining effects automatically | Ask whether one effect is sufficient |
| All capitals | Short acronym or established label | Entire instructions in uppercase | Use standard capitalization for sentences |

### A simple three-step technique

1. Select the exact phrase **Workshop begins at 3:00 p.m.** in a disposable practice paragraph.
1. Choose Bold, then read the phrase and the words around it.
1. Choose Bold again while the same phrase is selected to remove the bold formatting, then repeat with Italic.

This is a reversible experiment. If the text does not change, confirm that you selected the document's text rather than a toolbar, page background, or search field. If more text changes than expected, undo the action and refine the selection.

### Why the next line sometimes stays bold

Formatting commands can remain active at the insertion point. If you finish a bold label, press Enter, and start typing, the new paragraph may inherit that format. This is not necessarily an application defect. It means the typing position still carries the active formatting. Turn Bold off, use the paragraph's normal style, or clear unwanted direct formatting before proceeding. A useful checkpoint is to type one short word and inspect it before entering a whole paragraph.

### Shortcuts are optional

Keyboard shortcuts can save time but are not required to pass the lesson. Toolbar buttons, menus, touch controls, and accessibility commands serve the same instructional objective. Some online editors reserve particular key combinations, and the appearance of their menus can change. Use the command's name when a shortcut behaves differently from the examples here.

## 4. How do you choose fonts and sizes?

A [[font-family|font family]] is a design for displaying a collection of characters. Examples include Arial, Calibri, Aptos, Times New Roman, and Georgia. They differ in shape, width, weight, and visual character. A font family may contain regular, bold, and italic variations. A [[font-size|font size]] controls the approximate scale of characters, commonly measured in [[point|points]] in desktop word-processing applications.

### Font choice supports the reading task

For most beginner practice documents, select one familiar, easy-to-read font. It is not necessary to install a new font or purchase a design package. Use a standard font already available in your application. A font's availability and exact appearance can vary across devices. When a recipient opens a document without your selected font, the application may substitute another one. Simple, widely available choices generally reduce surprises.

Two broad design families are often introduced:

- A [[serif|serif]] font, such as Georgia or Times New Roman, has small finishing strokes on many letterforms.
- A [[sans-serif|sans-serif]] font, such as Arial or Calibri, generally lacks those finishing strokes.

Neither category is universally more readable for every individual, medium, or assignment. The familiar typeface, size, spacing, and reader's needs matter more than a blanket rule that all serif or all sans-serif text is best.

### Font sizes and points

On many desktop systems, font size is expressed as **pt**, or points. As a rough reference, one typographic point is 1/72 inch in traditional page measurement, though displayed pixel size depends on device scaling and software. A 12-point font is typically larger than a 10-point font **within the same font family**, but different families can appear visually larger or smaller at the same nominal point size.

| Text role | Illustrative starting size | Why it might work | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary body paragraph | 11–12 pt | Common desktop-document range | Increase when audience or context requires |
| Short emphasized label | Usually the body size | Bold can distinguish the label | Larger is not always better |
| Simple visual title | About 16–20 pt for practice | Makes the title stand apart | Do not confuse appearance with a real heading style |
| Small footnote or caption | Often smaller than body text | Reduces visual competition | Avoid shrinking information until it is hard to read |
| On-screen text at high zoom | Keep the intended point size | Zoom enlarges the view temporarily | Zoom does not necessarily change saved or printed font size |

These are **practice examples, not institutional style requirements**. A form, school rubric, accessibility accommodation, or organization's template may specify different values. Do not insist on a particular size when the needs of actual readers call for another.

### Change the font or size on existing text

1. Highlight the phrase you want to change.
1. Find the [[font-menu|font menu]], usually a dropdown showing a font name.
1. Choose a readable font already installed or available in the editor.
1. Find the [[size-control|size control]], usually a number field or dropdown.
1. Choose a modest size, then inspect whether the text remains legible and properly positioned.
1. Check the neighboring words to verify that you changed the intended selection only.

If you select an entire practice paragraph and change the font, every selected character should generally adopt the new appearance. If only the next words you type change, the editor may have had an insertion point rather than a selection. If the document suddenly spans more pages, the new font, point size, or line spacing may have altered the text's physical dimensions. Review before proceeding.

### Zoom is not font size

[[zoom|Zoom]] changes how large the document appears on your screen. It usually does not change the actual font-size setting stored in the document or the size printed on paper. This distinction is useful for accessibility: you can make a document easier to see while working without altering every font size. Some devices also offer system-level display scaling, screen magnification, or accessibility text-size settings.

Try this comparison on a practice file: set a paragraph to 12 pt; increase the document zoom to 150%; inspect the size control again. It should still report approximately the same 12 pt size. Then return zoom to a comfortable level. If your app uses a different display mode or lacks the control, explain the concept without changing settings.

### Font choice should remain consistent

Switching between three fonts within a two-paragraph memo usually adds noise rather than clarity. Use one main font for body text. If you need visual distinctions, apply bold to a label or use an appropriate heading style. You can learn advanced type hierarchy later; an attractive result for this lesson can be modest and plain.

> **Decision rule:** Choose a font because people can read it, a size because it is comfortable in the expected medium, and emphasis because it communicates priority.

## 5. How do you use color and highlighting?

[[font-color|Font color]] changes the color of the characters themselves. [[text-highlight|Text highlighting]] places a colored background behind selected text in some word processors, similar to marking a passage with a highlighter. A document may also have a [[page-color|page color]] or other background appearance; that is a separate feature. This lesson addresses font color and selected-text highlighting, not page design.

### Use color sparingly

Color can help separate a short note from ordinary text, but it should not be the only way the reader knows something is important. For example, a required arrival time should be written explicitly—**Arrive by 2:45 p.m.**—rather than conveyed only by green or red text. Readers may be color-blind, print in grayscale, use a screen reader, or view the file on a device with different display settings.

A high [[contrast|contrast]] between text and background generally helps readability. Dark text against a light page is a familiar default. Avoid pale yellow letters on white or dark blue letters on black. If the text is part of an institution's template, follow its accessible color conventions rather than selecting colors randomly.

### Highlighting versus changing the font color

| Operation | What changes | Typical example | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Font color | The visible characters | Dark-red text for a short note | Text remains readable against background |
| Text highlight | Background immediately behind text | Highlight one practice phrase | Highlight is not obscuring the letters |
| Page background | The document or page background | A colored page | Usually outside this lesson's scope |
| Bold | Letter weight, not color | **Important:** | Meaning survives grayscale printing |

A highlight can be useful during drafting, but not every highlighted passage belongs in the final document. Before sharing work, inspect whether the highlighting still serves the audience. Some apps offer limited colors, and some export or printing paths may represent them differently.

### A controlled color experiment

1. On your **practice copy**, select the label **Practice reminder**.
1. If available, change its font color to a dark, readable color.
1. Read it against the page background and consider whether it is still visible in grayscale.
1. Return the label to the normal font color.
1. Select the same label, apply a light text highlight if supported, and inspect the result.
1. Remove the highlight again so your document ends in a restrained appearance.

If you cannot find a highlight tool, do not install software to complete the activity. Explain the difference between font color and highlight, and complete the rest of the task using Bold. That satisfies the principle of deliberate emphasis.

### Do not confuse text highlighting with selection

A [[selection|selection]] is usually a temporary editing state: it shows which words are currently targeted. Text highlighting is a formatting attribute deliberately attached to the text and may remain after you click elsewhere. If the colored background disappears when you click away, you may have seen only the selection, not an actual highlight. Test on a harmless phrase and verify after deselecting it.

### Color and document accessibility

Meaning must survive without color. Write **Registration closes Friday** rather than relying on a red Friday to signal urgency. When possible, use descriptive labels, consistent typography, and plain-language sentences. A screen reader may not announce a font's color or highlight by default, and an exported file may not reproduce every visual effect identically. The same principle applies to text that is both bold and colored: the visible style supplements, rather than replaces, the actual information.

## 6. How do you keep formatting consistent?

A document looks coherent when similar pieces of information are presented in similar ways. If one deadline is bold and another deadline of equal importance is ordinary text, the reader may assume the first is more important. Consistency therefore communicates a predictable pattern. A simple document needs only a few rules: ordinary body text stays ordinary; titles are visually distinct; essential labels are emphasized in the same way.

### Direct formatting versus styles

[[direct-formatting|Direct formatting]] means applying an appearance change directly to selected words or paragraphs, such as clicking Bold or choosing 14 pt. A [[style|style]] is a named collection of formatting properties that can be applied consistently and, in many applications, updated across the document. A [[character-style|character style]] governs selected characters in applications that support it; a [[paragraph-style|paragraph style]] typically governs a whole paragraph and can carry structural meaning such as Heading 1.

For example, you might manually make a title 18 pt and bold. That changes how it looks, but it does not necessarily mark it as an actual heading for navigation or assistive technology. Applying the editor's **Title** or **Heading 1** style may create more reliable structure, depending on the application. We will practice semantic headings and paragraph styles in the next lesson.

| Method | How it is applied | Good beginner use | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direct bold or italic | Select words and use a toolbar command | Emphasize a short label | Many separate changes are difficult to maintain |
| Direct font or size | Select text, choose font and point size | Observe how appearance works | Can create inconsistency in longer files |
| Character style | Apply a named text-level style, if supported | Reuse the same special text treatment | Not all editors expose styles the same way |
| Paragraph style | Apply a named style to a paragraph | Structure headings and body text | Must understand the role, not just appearance |
| Clear direct formatting | Remove local formatting overrides | Repair a pasted or accidentally styled phrase | May intentionally remove emphasis you wanted |

### Clean up inconsistent text

Imagine that you pasted a paragraph from a website. One sentence appears in a different font and an unexpected color. First, select only the inconsistent words. Look for a command such as **Clear Formatting**, **Clear Direct Formatting**, or **Remove Formatting**. In many applications, this returns selected text toward its surrounding or current style. The exact effect depends on the application's formatting model, the active style, and any copied content.

Do **not** assume that clearing formatting always restores the whole document to one fixed default. It may remove bold, italic, links, character-level effects, or other local changes you intentionally applied. Review the result immediately. If the wrong changes disappear, use Undo and select a narrower range.

### Paste without importing unwanted styles

[[paste|Pasting]] from a website, email, or another document may bring fonts, hyperlinks, sizes, or colors into your document. Where supported, **Paste as plain text**, **Keep text only**, or an equivalent option usually inserts the words without much of the source formatting. The exact command varies by program and platform. You can also paste normally, then remove unwanted formatting from the intended passage. Avoid applying changes to the entire document when only one inserted phrase is inconsistent.

### Where to find the controls in common applications

The general workflow is the same: **select text, open the formatting controls, apply a limited change, inspect, and save**. Exact buttons differ by application version, window size, subscription, operating system, and web or mobile interface.

| Application | Where to look for text controls | Beginner actions | Important note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Microsoft Word | **Home** tab; **Font** group or context toolbar | Bold, italic, underline, font, size, color, clear formatting | Word desktop and Word for the web can differ |
| Google Docs | Main toolbar; **Format → Text** menu | Emphasis, font, size, text color, highlight, clear formatting | Native Docs ordinarily saves online; confirm save status |
| LibreOffice Writer | Formatting toolbar or **Format** menu | Text emphasis, font, size, color; **Clear Direct Formatting** | A named style may remain after local overrides are cleared |
| Apple Pages | **Format** sidebar and text-formatting controls | Font, size, bold, italic, color and related attributes | Pages for Mac, iPad, and iCloud use different layouts |

Do not assume every application uses the same icon, dialog, or keyboard shortcut. A toolbar may be condensed when the window is narrow. Many apps also have menus or searchable Help commands. Use those alternatives if a button is missing.

### A short shortcut reference

| Goal | Often on Windows desktop | Often on Mac desktop | Alternative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bold selection or upcoming text | Ctrl+B | Command+B | Bold toolbar/menu command |
| Italic selection or upcoming text | Ctrl+I | Command+I | Italic toolbar/menu command |
| Underline selection or upcoming text | Ctrl+U | Command+U | Underline command |
| Undo a recent mistake | Ctrl+Z | Command+Z | Edit → Undo |
| Save a document, when manual saving is used | Ctrl+S | Command+S | File → Save |
| Remove unwanted local formatting | **Application-dependent** | **Application-dependent** | Search for Clear Formatting or Clear Direct Formatting |

Do not memorize the same clear-formatting shortcut for every application. For example, Google Docs and LibreOffice Writer support different shortcuts for this operation; use the program's labeled command if you are uncertain. Also be careful: keyboard shortcuts act on whatever interface currently has [[focus|focus]]. Confirm that the document, rather than a browser address bar or search box, is active.

### A practical document-formatting plan

For a one-page fictional notice, a sensible plan could be:

1. Use one readable font throughout the document.
1. Use a consistent body-text size such as 12 pt, if appropriate for the reader.
1. Make the document's title stand out; use an actual title or heading style if the application offers one.
1. Bold two or three labels such as **Date**, **Time**, and **Location**.
1. Leave explanatory sentences in ordinary text.
1. Avoid unnecessary underlining, multiple colors, or decorative effects.
1. Review the whole document, save, and reopen the correct copy.

These are decisions about communication. You should be able to explain why each choice helps readers rather than simply listing which buttons you clicked.

### Save and verify the visible result

Formatting is a document change. In a desktop editor, save the practice file. In a cloud editor, confirm the appropriate saving status and account. Close only when it is safe to do so, reopen the **same named practice document**, and check that two deliberately formatted items still appear as expected. This is the same verification habit learned in Lesson 2, now applied to appearance as well as wording.

## 7. Guided practice: Format a document

These activities build on Lessons 1 and 2. Use an **independent practice copy** of your document. If you do not have an existing file, use the model below. You can complete the exercises in any available word processor. If you use an on-screen keyboard, dictation, touch input, or accessibility software, the commands may look different; the objective is to apply and check the same appearance change. If you have no editor, work through the steps conceptually on paper, although **Activity 7 requires a saved and reopened digital document** for full completion.

### The practice notice: before formatting

Type or paste the following *fictional* notice as ordinary text. Use paragraph breaks only where they logically belong. Do not worry about its appearance yet.

> Community Workshop Notice
>
> The community center will host a beginner workshop about basic computer skills. The session will last one hour.
>
> Date: Friday
>
> Time: 3:00 p.m.
>
> Location: Community Room B
>
> Please bring a notebook and a pen. Participants may request reasonable accessibility accommodations through the center's usual contact process.

The goal is a well-organized, readable practice document, not an official announcement. Every detail here is invented. Do not provide a real contact address, telephone number, private identity, or organizational endorsement for the exercise.

### Activity 1: Save a formatting practice copy (about 6–8 minutes)

**Goal:** Protect the unformatted or earlier document before experimenting.

1. Open your previous practice notice or create a new document containing the model above.
1. Verify that the wording is correct before adjusting appearance.
1. Make a separate copy where the application provides **Save As**, **Save a Copy**, **Duplicate**, or **Make a copy**. Choose a method that creates a distinct editable item rather than merely renaming the original.
1. Name it **community-workshop-notice-formatting-practice**.
1. Record the application, account if relevant, and known storage location.
1. Open the new copy and confirm its name before doing any formatting.

| Check | What to record | Completion evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original protected | Original title: __________ | Original file or document remains available |
| New copy created | New title: __________ | Distinct name or storage item exists |
| Correct editing location | App/account/folder: __________ | You can identify the practice copy |
| Text ready | Yes / Not yet | Date and time are correct before formatting |

If your editor lacks a suitable copy feature, create a new document and paste the fictional notice into it, then save that new item under the practice name. **Do not overwrite the original in order to satisfy the task.**

### Activity 2: Bold the important labels (about 8–10 minutes)

**Goal:** Apply selective emphasis without changing wording.

1. Select only **Date:** in the notice. Choose Bold.
1. Select only **Time:** and choose Bold.
1. Select only **Location:** and choose Bold.
1. Click outside the selection, then inspect each line. The labels should stand out while **Friday**, **3:00 p.m.**, and **Community Room B** remain ordinary text.
1. If an entire line became bold, select the unintended part and remove Bold, or use Undo and try again.
1. Reread the three labels and values. Verify that you have not erased a colon or changed the facts.

**Expected appearance:**

> **Date:** Friday
>
> **Time:** 3:00 p.m.
>
> **Location:** Community Room B

### Activity 3: Practice bold, italic, and underline as reversible actions (about 8–10 minutes)

- Italic changes the appearance to a slanted form; it does not rewrite the sentence.
- Underline draws a line beneath the selected words; online, that can resemble a hyperlink.

## 8. Common mistakes and troubleshooting

Formatting mistakes are ordinary and usually reversible. The key is to identify the exact range affected before changing anything else. If an action unexpectedly alters the page, use Undo when available and correct the selection rather than applying several unrelated commands.

### “I clicked Bold, but nothing happened.”

The insertion point may be in the document without any existing text selected; Bold may now be active for text typed next. Or the toolbar, a dialog, or the browser may have [[focus|focus]] rather than the editing area. Highlight a short phrase in the body, try the Bold command, and deselect to inspect. If the selected text was already bold, the command may remove bold instead.

### “Everything after my label is bold.”

You may have left Bold active while continuing to type. Select the unintended bold words and turn it off, or return to the current paragraph's normal style. Do not erase the entire paragraph. If the app carries formatting across a paragraph break, turn off the format before proceeding with normal sentences.

### “I changed the entire document accidentally.”

You may have used Select All when you wanted only a phrase, or chosen a global style or font command. Undo immediately if appropriate. Reapply the change to the intended selection. If the original title and body had different styles, restore them deliberately rather than forcing everything to one size.

### “The font looks different from the one I selected.”

The font may not be available on the current device, and the editor may substitute another. Some fonts also have multiple weights and character designs. Choose a familiar available font and check the result; do not download untrusted font installers simply to match a cosmetic example.

### “My text became tiny or the document gained extra pages.”

A font-size change may have applied to the wrong selection, or the text width changed. Check the size control and the current zoom separately. A smaller zoom makes the document look smaller *on screen*; a smaller font-size value changes the text itself. Restore a readable size before continuing.

### “My pasted paragraph looks unlike the rest.”

The pasted content may contain [[source-formatting|source formatting]]. Select only the inconsistent passage and use a plain-text paste option, Clear Formatting, or the correct document style. Do not assume that copying text transfers only its letters. Inspect any hyperlinks and remove unintended ones through the editor's link controls when appropriate.

### “I cleared formatting and lost the bold labels.”

Clear Formatting can remove intentional emphasis as well as errors. Undo if the clearing step affected too much. Then limit the action to the troublesome phrase and reapply a consistent pattern. Clearing all direct formatting in a whole document is rarely necessary for a beginner's targeted repair.

### “The underline looks like a link.”

That is a common interpretation of underlined text on the web. For ordinary digital notices, prefer bold for emphasis unless the document has a reason to use underlining. If the text is truly a hyperlink, manage the link itself rather than assuming changing its underline affects the destination.

### “Color looks readable on my screen but disappears in a printout.”

Some printers are grayscale, displays vary, and very light colors may produce insufficient contrast. Remove unnecessary color and communicate important information with readable text and explicit labels. If the document must be printed, a print preview is useful; the separate *Printing Documents* lesson covers that workflow.

### “The formatting disappeared after I reopened the file.”

You may have opened an older copy, the save may not have completed, or a file format may not preserve all effects as expected. Check the document title, storage location, account, save status, and file format. Save an editable copy in a format supported by the application; do not overwrite the original while diagnosing the issue.

### “The heading looks large but does not appear in document navigation.”

You may have changed only its appearance with direct font size and bold. A genuine [[heading-style|heading style]] carries a structural role; apply the appropriate paragraph style if supported. The next lesson teaches that distinction more thoroughly.

### Quick troubleshooting guide

| Symptom | Likely explanation | Recommended response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entire paragraph changed | Too much text selected | Undo and select a smaller range |
| New typing stays italic | Italic remains active | Turn off Italic before continuing |
| Unwanted font in pasted text | Source formatting imported | Paste as plain text or clear local formatting |
| Text hard to read | Small size or low contrast | Choose a comfortable size and readable colors |
| Title has no navigation role | Direct formatting used instead of a style | Apply a relevant heading/title style |
| Reopened file looks old | Wrong copy or save/sync problem | Verify file name, account, location, and save status |

### A repeatable repair sequence

When something looks wrong, use this order: **stop → identify the text affected → undo if appropriate → choose the correct selection → apply one corrective action → inspect → save**. Avoid troubleshooting by selecting everything and clicking through many font, color, and style buttons. That approach can create more inconsistency than the original mistake.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

Terms with dotted underlines in the lesson can be selected for quick definitions in the MGU dashboard when this Markdown is rendered by the supported lesson interface. Some vocabulary entries may include a reference link when a suitable article has been verified. You do not need to memorize every name; focus first on the difference between **what the document says** and **how the words appear**.

- **Content:** The actual words, numbers, and information communicated in the document.
- **Text formatting:** Visual properties applied to text, such as bold, italic, font size, or color.
- **Selection:** Existing text marked for an editing or formatting command.
- **Insertion point:** The location where new typing is entered.
- **Bold:** Heavier-looking characters used for deliberate emphasis.
- **Italic:** Slanted characters sometimes used for titles or limited emphasis.
- **Underline:** A line beneath text; can resemble a hyperlink on screen.
- **Font family:** A named design for characters, such as Arial or Georgia.
- **Font size:** The scale of text, often expressed in points in desktop editors.
- **Point (pt):** A typographic unit commonly used for font sizes.
- **Serif:** Small strokes or extensions present in many traditional typefaces.
- **Sans-serif:** Typeface category that generally lacks serif finishing strokes.
- **Font color:** The color of the letters themselves.
- **Text highlight:** A color displayed behind selected words as a formatting effect.
- **Contrast:** The difference in appearance between text and its background that affects visibility.
- **Direct formatting:** Appearance changes applied directly to selected text.
- **Style:** A reusable set of formatting attributes associated with a named role.
- **Character style:** A reusable appearance definition for a character or selected range, where supported.
- **Paragraph style:** A style applied to a paragraph, sometimes providing document structure.
- **Heading style:** A paragraph style that marks text as a heading rather than merely making it large.
- **Clear formatting:** A command for removing some or all direct appearance overrides.
- **Word processor:** Software for writing, editing, and formatting documents.
- **Zoom:** A viewing adjustment that enlarges or reduces the apparent on-screen display without necessarily changing the saved text size.
- **Paste as plain text:** A way to insert copied words without carrying over much source appearance.
- **Hyperlink:** A clickable connection to a webpage or other destination.
- **Save:** Preserve the document or its latest changes for later use.
