## Lesson 6: Spellcheck and Proofreading

A document is not finished merely because its sentences have been typed and its headings look attractive. Readers need to understand its information, trust its details, and know what to do next. A missing letter can distract the reader, but an incorrect meeting time, duplicated sentence, or confusing instruction can cause a more serious problem. This lesson teaches you to combine built-in [[spellcheck|spellcheck]] and [[grammar-check|grammar checking]] with deliberate human [[proofreading|proofreading]]. You will continue using a fictional community-workshop notice developed in earlier Document Skills lessons, review it for several kinds of mistakes, and save a corrected version that you can reopen and inspect.

**Level:** Beginner · **Estimated study time:** 85–115 minutes · **Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–5, *Creating a Document*, *Typing, Editing, and Saving*, *Formatting Text*, *Headings, Lists, and Spacing*, and *Inserting Images and Tables*, recommended · **Practice equipment:** A computer with Microsoft Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice Writer, Apple Pages, or another word processor; a private practice document or blank page; no printer, purchase, or third-party proofreading service required

> **Essential idea:** A spelling or grammar suggestion is a clue, **not a final decision**. First determine what you intended to say. Use the application's review tools to find possible problems, then personally check meaning, facts, names, dates, numbers, layout, accessibility, and the saved file. The writer remains responsible for the final document.

### Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish [[editing|editing]], [[spellcheck|spellcheck]], [[grammar-check|grammar checking]], and [[proofreading|proofreading]].
1. Recognize common [[spelling-error|spelling errors]], [[typographical-error|typing mistakes]], and [[word-choice|word-choice errors]] in short documents.
1. Run or open a built-in spelling and grammar review in a familiar word processor without changing unrelated settings.
1. Interpret a [[suggestion|suggestion]] and decide when to **accept**, **ignore**, or **manually correct** it.
1. Explain why a correctly spelled word can still be the wrong word in a sentence.
1. Check common [[punctuation|punctuation]], [[capitalization|capitalization]], [[agreement|agreement]], and [[sentence-boundary|sentence-boundary]] problems.
1. Proofread a document systematically for meaning, sequence, repeated words, missing information, and consistency.
1. Confirm names, times, [[date|dates]], numbers, tables, and image descriptions using a trustworthy source rather than a guess.
1. Understand how [[proofing-language|proofing language]], dialect, and a [[custom-dictionary|custom dictionary]] affect spellcheck results.
1. Protect private information when using optional [[cloud-service|cloud-based]] editing assistants and collaborative tools.
1. Save a revised document under a sensible [[filename|filename]], reopen it, and perform a short final [[quality-check|quality check]].

## 1. What can spellcheck and proofreading do?

A word processor can help you prepare a letter, notice, report, application, or set of instructions. Once you have written a draft, you must look for problems. Some are mechanical: an extra letter, missing period, or inconsistent capitalization. Others involve meaning: a missing instruction, conflicting information, a vague sentence, or a table entry that says the wrong time. No single automated tool detects all these problems reliably.

[[editing|Editing]] is the broad process of improving written material. An editor might move a paragraph, remove repetition, replace a confusing sentence, or change the order of sections. [[proofreading|Proofreading]] is typically a focused final review to find remaining errors before the document is distributed. In beginner practice, you will often move between these tasks: you may notice a misspelling while revising a sentence or discover a confusing phrase during your final inspection.

[[spellcheck|Spellcheck]] compares words with available language rules, dictionaries, and other software methods to identify *possible* spelling mistakes. [[grammar-check|Grammar checking]] looks for *possible* grammatical, punctuation, or style problems. Some modern writing assistants use additional statistical or AI-based methods. Their suggestions can help, but they may misunderstand an unfamiliar name, regional expression, specialized term, or deliberately informal sentence.

### Four different kinds of review

| Review activity | Main question | Example | Who decides the final wording? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spellcheck | Is this word possibly misspelled? | `adress` may be intended as `address` | The writer |
| Grammar and punctuation check | Does this sentence follow an appropriate grammatical pattern? | `The classes starts` needs subject–verb agreement | The writer |
| Editing | Is the message clear, complete, and organized? | Move the registration instruction closer to the date | The writer |
| Proofreading | Are there remaining mistakes in the near-final document? | Check a repeated word, wrong time, or missing table heading | The writer |

These checks overlap. A sentence can be grammatically correct but hard to understand. A spellchecker may accept a real word that is incorrect in context. A document may contain no spelling mistakes yet direct readers to the wrong room. The purpose of proofreading is not simply to eliminate colored underlines; it is to ensure that the actual document communicates what you intended.

### Correct spelling does not guarantee correct meaning

Consider these sentences:

- **Intended:** “Please bring your **form** to the workshop.”
- **Wrong word:** “Please bring your **from** to the workshop.”

Both *form* and *from* are spelled correctly. Some grammar tools may flag the second sentence, but not every tool or document will. A person should examine the sentence and use context to decide what belongs there. These errors are often called [[real-word-error|real-word errors]].

Another example is a time: “The session begins at 1:00 p.m.” If the official schedule says 10:00 a.m., no spelling or grammar rule will expose the error. You need the source schedule or an approved reference.

### A helpful review order

A practical beginner workflow has five passes:

1. **Content:** Does the document say everything the reader needs?
1. **Organization:** Are headings, lists, and explanations in a sensible order?
1. **Language:** Are sentences clear, and have you reviewed spelling and grammar suggestions?
1. **Accuracy and accessibility:** Are names, facts, dates, tables, and alternative text correct and understandable?
1. **Final file:** Have you saved, reopened, and inspected the version intended for sharing?

If you change the content substantially during a late pass, repeat the affected checks. Editing can introduce new mistakes; a final review is most useful when performed on the version that will actually be distributed.

> **Remember:** A green or clean-looking review indicator means the application has not identified a particular problem. It does not certify factual accuracy, completeness, professionalism, or accessibility.

### Practice boundaries

For this lesson, use a fictional announcement or a non-sensitive document you are authorized to edit. Do not enter passwords, identification numbers, private employment information, patient details, or confidential client materials into an unfamiliar proofreading website. Do not ask another person to upload their document without permission. You can learn the essential skills using ordinary local features in a word processor.

## 2. How do you find and review spelling suggestions?

A basic word processor often marks a possible spelling problem using a wavy underline or an indicator in a review panel. The appearance is not universal. Red may be used for spelling in one program, while another uses a different color, symbol, or accessible announcement. Some applications also suggest corrections as you type. A marked word is not automatically wrong, and an unmarked word is not automatically right.

### A safe first test

Open a new practice document and type this invented sentence:

> `The comunity workshop has a helpful beginer guide.`

The words *comunity* and *beginer* are intentionally misspelled. Depending on the software, language settings, and enabled features, they may receive underlines or appear in a review pane. If the application does not mark them immediately, do not assume the feature is broken; review may be manual, disabled, or unavailable for the current language.

To inspect one flagged word, you can often right-click it, select it, or activate the accessible review command. The program may offer **community** or **beginner** as a candidate correction. Read the whole sentence before choosing. A suggestion is trustworthy only when it fits your meaning.

### Common actions in a spelling review

| Action | Usually means | Appropriate example | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Accept or Change | Replace the flagged item with the proposed wording | Correct `comunity` to `community` | Check that the new word fits the sentence |
| Ignore or Skip | Leave the current item unchanged, often for this occurrence or review | Leave a fictional brand name as written | The exact scope of Ignore varies by application |
| Ignore All | Skip matching occurrences for a session or review | A legitimate term repeated many times | Do not use to hide a genuine repeated mistake |
| Add to Dictionary | Teach a local or account-associated dictionary to recognize the word | An authorized specialized term you use regularly | Adding a mistake may conceal it later |
| Change All | Replace multiple matches at once where available | Fix a consistent misspelling in a controlled practice file | Review every replacement and avoid changing unrelated uses |
| Edit Manually | Close or bypass the proposed wording and type your own correction | Fix a misinterpreted person's name using the approved spelling | Use a verified source for unfamiliar names |

A [[custom-dictionary|custom dictionary]] can be useful for a legitimate name or technical term, but it should be used sparingly. You generally do not need to add an unfamiliar word just to complete one practice exercise. If the same legitimate word is repeatedly flagged, you can choose to ignore it while keeping a note to confirm its spelling.

### Open the review feature in different applications

Menus vary with device, software version, account, and document mode. The table provides likely starting points, not an assurance that every interface has identical commands.

| Application | Where to begin | What to look for | Beginner decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Microsoft Word | **Review** tab, often **Editor** or **Spelling & Grammar** | A review pane and possible spelling, grammar, or style suggestions | Inspect each suggestion before accepting |
| Google Docs | **Tools → Spelling and grammar** or contextual suggestions | A spelling-and-grammar review and proposed replacements | Confirm the word in context; review document language |
| LibreOffice Writer | **Tools → Spelling**; optionally automatic spelling check | Suggested spellings in a review dialog or on-screen markings | Check language and available dictionaries |
| Apple Pages | **Edit → Spelling and Grammar** or application spelling controls on Mac | Spelling suggestions, correction choices, and optional grammar settings | Verify the intended wording; menu names vary by device |

If an unfamiliar ribbon or panel appears, first locate the document and ensure that you are not selecting a destructive command. Review controls typically do not require any paid add-on for basic spelling, although the available grammar and writing-assistant features can differ by product, version, and subscription.

### Set the correct proofing language

A [[proofing-language|proofing language]] is the language or language variety a program uses when reviewing text. A document written in English may be checked using English (United States) or English (United Kingdom). Some terms differ legitimately, such as *color* and *colour*. A red underline on a valid regional spelling may indicate a language mismatch rather than an error.

If many ordinary words are marked at once, inspect the document's proofing language. Microsoft Word typically includes language settings under **Review**. Google Docs offers language selection through its document settings or **File → Language**, depending on interface. LibreOffice Writer has language controls associated with text, paragraphs, or document settings. Pages can use macOS spelling-language configuration. Do not change your computer's entire display language merely to correct one document.

| Situation | More reliable response | Less reliable response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common English words all appear wrong | Check proofing language and installed dictionaries | Replace the entire paragraph based on guesses |
| A proper name is underlined | Verify it from an approved reference | Accept the closest-looking unrelated name |
| An American or British spelling is flagged | Follow the document's chosen language convention | Switch spellings randomly throughout the page |
| A technical term is flagged | Confirm term, ignore if correct, possibly add deliberately | Assume all unfamiliar terms are mistakes |
| The checker finds nothing | Run a manual review and inspect settings if necessary | Assume the document is error-free |

### Accessible ways to review

Visual underlines should not be the only way to identify possible errors. Many applications provide review panels that can be navigated using a keyboard or screen reader. If you use assistive technology, look for an application-specific **Editor**, **Spelling**, or **Review** control and follow its announcements. Keyboard shortcut keys are not identical across all applications and operating systems; use the menu if you are unsure. Zooming the document or increasing display size can also make review easier without changing the words themselves.

> **Checkpoint:** You should be able to identify one flagged practice word, explain whether it is truly incorrect, choose a safe correction, and confirm that the surrounding sentence still makes sense.

## 3. How do you review grammar, punctuation, and word choice?

A [[grammar-check|grammar checker]] examines patterns in a sentence and may propose changes to grammar, punctuation, spelling, or writing style. Some applications separate these categories; others combine them in a single assistant. Treat the suggestions as prompts to consider rather than authoritative instructions. Language is shaped by purpose, audience, dialect, and context, and automated systems may favor one style even when another is appropriate.

### Distinguish several common problems

| Type of issue | Practice example | Reason to review | One appropriate correction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Subject–verb agreement | `The students is ready.` | Plural subject with singular verb | `The students are ready.` |
| Missing punctuation | `Bring a pen notebook and folder.` | Items are difficult to separate | `Bring a pen, notebook, and folder.` |
| Sentence boundary | `The workshop begins at noon it ends at one.` | Two independent clauses run together | `The workshop begins at noon. It ends at one.` |
| Incomplete instruction | `Please submit before.` | A deadline or object is missing | Check the approved source and add the missing information |
| Word-choice error | `Please save the form in the correct folding.` | A correctly spelled but unsuitable word | `Please save the form in the correct folder.` |
| Repeated word | `The the schedule is attached.` | Accidental duplication | `The schedule is attached.` |
| Unclear pronoun | `When Pat spoke to Lee, they agreed.` | The intended referent may be ambiguous | Clarify who agreed, if meaning requires it |
| Inconsistent point of view | `You should bring a pen. We must arrive early.` | The intended actor may shift confusingly | Make instructions consistent with the audience |

The best correction is the one that preserves intended meaning while improving clarity. In the incomplete-instruction example, the reviewer cannot invent a deadline. The correct action is to consult the original schedule or the person who supplied the details.

### Grammar, punctuation, and style are not identical

[[grammar|Grammar]] concerns how words and sentence parts relate. [[punctuation|Punctuation]] helps show sentence boundaries, lists, quotations, and other relationships. [[style|Style]] concerns choices such as formality, concision, or voice. For an announcement, a plain instruction may be clearer than a long, overly formal sentence.

Compare these two approaches:

- “It is hereby requested that all participants make themselves available to provide the necessary writing instruments.”
- “Please bring a pen.”

The shorter version is more direct for a simple workshop notice. However, a writing assistant should not be allowed to remove a necessary qualification, change a legal condition, or alter a technical meaning just to make a sentence shorter.

### A tool may suggest something that is not wrong

Some suggestions treat a passive sentence as undesirable, ask for a shorter sentence, or recommend a different level of formality. These are often [[style-suggestion|style suggestions]] rather than corrections of definite mistakes. Passive wording can be appropriate when the action matters more than the actor: “The entrance will be closed during repairs” may be clear for a notice. Regional vocabulary, community-specific language, names, and quoted material should not automatically be replaced with a tool's preferred wording.

Use a simple decision rule for every suggestion:

1. **Read:** What did you actually write? What does the tool propose?
1. **Compare:** Will the proposed text preserve the intended meaning?
1. **Decide:** Accept, reject, or create your own correction.
1. **Recheck:** Read the corrected sentence in its paragraph to catch a new problem.

Never accept a suggestion merely because it is offered prominently or appears with a confidence indicator.

### Numbers and punctuation in instructions

Instructional documents often contain details that need special attention: times, dates, room identifiers, registration steps, and contact information. A grammar checker can flag a missing comma without knowing whether “10:00 a.m.” should really be “11:00 a.m.” Do not assume syntactic correctness establishes factual correctness.

For times, use a clear convention consistently. Example: “Tuesday, 10:00 a.m.–11:30 a.m.” If a notice also has a table, make sure its times agree with the paragraph. Use **a.m./p.m.** when a time could be ambiguous, and consider whether a time zone is necessary for online sessions. For dates, avoid ambiguous numerical forms such as `03/04/2027` when readers may interpret month and day differently; write out the month if appropriate and verify the calendar date.

### Avoid automated overcorrection

- **Names:** A proper name may not appear in the dictionary and should be checked against a reliable reference.
- **Quotation:** Correcting somebody else's words may misrepresent the original; follow the document's quotation policy.
- **Technical wording:** A correct subject-specific term may be less familiar to a general grammar tool.
- **Dialect:** Do not treat a person's dialect as a defect. Select language appropriate to the document and intended audience.
- **Formal requirements:** If an agency or instructor requires exact terminology, use that wording rather than a paraphrase recommended by a tool.
- **Uncertain facts:** If the correct information is unknown, mark it for verification instead of silently making it up.

> **Remember:** A grammatical sentence can still be inaccurate, misleading, incomplete, or inappropriate for the audience. Grammar review is one part of quality control.

## 4. How do you proofread a document systematically?

Manual [[proofreading|proofreading]] means reading a document deliberately to find problems that have survived drafting and editing. A reader encountering the notice for the first time will not know what you intended to include; they will only see what is actually present. A systematic review helps you adopt the reader's perspective.

### Separate reading for meaning from reading for mechanics

On a first pass, focus on the message: What is this document for? Who needs it? What should they do? Are the instructions complete and in the right order? Resist stopping for every small comma if it prevents you from noticing that the document never states where the workshop occurs.

On a second pass, focus on wording: read sentence by sentence, look for repeated words, unnecessary phrases, misspellings, missing punctuation, and inconsistent terminology. You may find it useful to move your finger or pointer along each line, or use a read-aloud feature when appropriate. Slowing down changes how much attention you give to familiar text.

On a third pass, inspect the [[layout|layout]]: look at headings, paragraph spacing, lists, table columns, image placement, and any awkward page breaks. A document can be linguistically correct but visually difficult to follow. A heading stranded at the end of a page or a table cut off at the edge may confuse a reader.

### A repeatable five-pass proofreading method

| Pass | Focus | Useful question | Possible action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Purpose | Audience, completeness, instructions | Could a new reader act on this document? | Add or clarify missing information |
| 2. Language | Spelling, grammar, word choice | Does every sentence say what I mean? | Review suggestions and correct manually |
| 3. Facts | Names, locations, times, dates, numbers | Does the text agree with the approved source? | Verify and correct mismatches |
| 4. Presentation | Headings, lists, images, tables | Can readers easily find and understand key details? | Repair structure and spacing |
| 5. Final file | Saved version and expected appearance | Is this the exact document I intend to distribute? | Save, reopen, and recheck |

These passes can be brief for a one-page notice and longer for a report. Their purpose is to prevent the common habit of reading the same way repeatedly and overlooking the same mistake each time.

### Read slowly enough to notice repeated or missing words

The mind is good at recognizing familiar phrases, which can make it easy to overlook a repeated article or an omitted small word. Consider:

> “Please bring the the worksheet to workshop.”

An automated spelling tool may not flag *the* because both instances are valid words. Reading slowly reveals the extra *the* and the missing article before *workshop*. An appropriate correction is: “Please bring the worksheet to the workshop.”

Another common mistake occurs after copying or pasting. You may accidentally paste the same paragraph twice or leave an obsolete instruction underneath the revised one. Search for duplicate headings and repeated sentences, especially after major revisions.

### Reading aloud and listening to the text

Reading aloud can expose awkward transitions or missing words. If it is not possible or comfortable to speak, silently read each sentence carefully, use text-to-speech when available, or follow the text with a cursor. A built-in [[read-aloud|read-aloud]] tool can be useful, but the exact feature and supported languages vary. Do not send confidential writing to an unapproved external speech or AI service just to hear it read.

A natural pause in reading does not automatically identify the correct punctuation; use your understanding of the sentence and the writing convention. The purpose of hearing the text is to catch problems that are harder to notice visually, not to replace editorial judgment.

### Look at sentences in smaller pieces

If you find the page overwhelming, try one of these strategies:

1. Review one paragraph at a time and mark it complete.
1. Focus on one class of mistake per pass, such as names or punctuation.
1. Temporarily increase [[zoom|zoom]] without modifying the saved page design.
1. Use the document's headings to move between sections.
1. Compare a final copy with an earlier copy when significant changes were made.
1. Take a short pause between editing and proofreading so familiar mistakes are less easy to overlook.

Do not use a technique if it makes the document harder for you to read. For example, some people benefit from listening while reading; others prefer a quiet line-by-line check. Choose a method that is accessible and repeatable.

### Proofread from the reader's perspective

Imagine a person receiving a one-page notice from an organization. They may be using a phone, screen magnification, printed paper, a screen reader, or a translated copy. They may have limited time. Ask whether the document gives the most important information in plain terms and whether it uses terminology consistently. Consider whether an abbreviation is introduced before use. Make sure that any illustration or colored highlight is not the only place a key instruction appears.

A good final proofreading pass goes beyond looking for mistakes. It identifies avoidable uncertainty: a reader should not have to guess which day, which room, or which instructions apply.

## 5. How do you check accuracy, consistency, and accessibility?

Some of the most important proofreading problems are not spelling problems. They are [[accuracy|accuracy]] and [[consistency|consistency]] problems: the wrong date in a table, a title that differs between pages, the wrong capitalization of an organization, or a link that appears to lead somewhere other than its label.

### Verify names, dates, times, and numbers against a source

A [[source-of-truth|source of truth]] is the authoritative record for a particular detail. For a workshop, that might be the organizer's approved schedule or an internal notice template. You should not assume that the first version of the document is correct simply because it appears elsewhere in the draft. When there is a conflict, check the source rather than choosing whichever time appears more often.

| Item | What can go wrong | How to verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Event name | Name is abbreviated differently in two sections | Compare with the approved title |
| Date and day | Weekday does not match calendar date | Check the authorized schedule and calendar |
| Start and end time | Introduction and table disagree | Compare both with the approved session schedule |
| Location | Room number is mistyped or omitted | Confirm with an authorized location record |
| Contact address | One letter in an email or website link is wrong | Compare with a trusted original; do not invent |
| Amount or quantity | Table says 15 seats but paragraph says 50 | Verify the original figures |
| List of required items | A supply appears in text but not in list | Reconcile the two presentations |
| Filename or version | An old draft is mistaken for the approved copy | Inspect file name, location, and latest saved content |

For this lesson, all workshop details are fictional. A fictional reference sheet in the guided practice will provide the correct facts. The learner's task is to make the notice agree with that reference, not to invent plausible replacements.

### Develop a consistency rule

[[consistency|Consistency]] means applying a chosen convention throughout the document when the context calls for it. For example, if the title is “Community Digital Skills Workshop,” use that title consistently unless a shorter name is introduced. If times use `a.m.` in one place, avoid alternating among `AM`, `am`, and `a.m.` without a reason. A consistency rule does not mean every sentence must look identical; it means similar information is presented predictably.

Useful conventions for a short notice include:

- One spelling of the event name throughout.
- One date style and one time style throughout.
- Comparable capitalization for parallel list entries.
- The same terms for the same object, such as **workshop room** rather than switching among **studio**, **office**, and **lab** without explanation.
- Clear, descriptive headings instead of vague labels.
- Consistent labels for table columns, such as **Activity**, **Start time**, and **Duration**.

### Check tables as information, not just as shapes

In Lesson 5, you learned to insert tables and images. During proofreading, verify their *content*. Inspect every cell for spelling errors and factual mismatches. Check that the header row identifies the values beneath it and that rows are not shifted by an accidental paste. If a table says “Introduction — 10:00 a.m.” while the paragraph says the introduction begins at noon, the problem will remain until the actual schedule is confirmed.

A table's dimensions may also change when text is corrected. After replacing long words, review whether the rows remain readable and whether column headers still appear where expected. Avoid using spaces or tabs to simulate a table, and avoid hiding crucial information in a screenshot that cannot be read as text.

### Check image captions and alternative text

A [[caption|caption]] is a visible label or explanation associated with a picture or table. [[alternative-text|Alternative text]] describes meaningful visual content for readers who cannot perceive the image in the usual way. These serve related but different purposes. A caption may identify the subject or source, while alternative text communicates what a reader needs to understand about the image.

For a workshop notice, an image of a simple room entrance might have visible caption “Workshop entrance.” Its alternative text could say “Entrance to the workshop room with a large sign beside the door,” if those details are accurate and help people navigate. Do not claim that a picture shows something it does not. If the image is purely decorative, follow the application's supported decorative-image method when appropriate.

Review image descriptions after changing or replacing images. Old alternative text attached to a new image can be misleading, even when every word is spelled correctly. Similarly, check that captions, table descriptions, and headings do not contradict the objects they refer to.

### Check hyperlinks without exposing private information

A [[hyperlink|hyperlink]] may display readable words while leading to a different address. For a real document, verify the visible label and the intended destination before distribution. When appropriate, use a trusted preview or approved link check. Be careful with unfamiliar links and do not enter login details on an unexpected page. For training, you can use plain text or known official resources rather than following an unverified link.

### Review with accessibility in mind

An accessible document is more than text with no spelling errors. Check that headings are actual heading styles rather than merely large bold paragraphs, lists use list controls, and tables use real cell structures. Do not convey a requirement by color alone; for example, “Bring the items highlighted in green” is less useful than a clearly labeled **Required materials** list. Make sure important text is not trapped in an image.

Some word processors provide a separate accessibility checker. If available, it can identify issues such as missing alternative text or suspicious table structures, but it may not detect every problem. Use the checker as another tool, followed by your own inspection. This lesson focuses on a basic verification habit, not specialized accessibility certification.

### Respect privacy when seeking help from automated tools

Built-in spelling tools and optional AI writing assistants may have different data-processing arrangements. Whether data is processed locally or by an online service depends on the application, feature, settings, and organizational policies. **Do not assume that a built-in feature is always offline or that every suggestion tool has the same privacy protections.** For private or regulated material, follow organizational requirements and review relevant feature settings or disclosures before enabling cloud-based assistance.

In this lesson, you do not need to copy documents into third-party websites, upload private files, grant broad document access, or enable a new account feature. The educational skills can be practiced on invented content.

> **Checkpoint:** When you finish reviewing the notice, you should know which details were checked against an approved source, which writing conventions you used, and which layout or accessibility issues you inspected.

## 6. How do you complete a final review and save your work?

A final review is the last stage of document quality control before sharing, printing, or exporting. It should happen after you have made your corrections—not before. A final spelling check run on an old draft does not verify the content of a revised document.

### Step 1: Make a clean review copy when appropriate

If a document is shared, belongs to another person, or serves as a record, preserve the original when you are not authorized to overwrite it. Use the program's **Save As**, **Make a copy**, or equivalent feature when needed, and place the review copy in an authorized folder. For an individual practice exercise, a filename such as `community-workshop-notice-reviewed.docx` is descriptive. The exact extension depends on the word processor and selected format.

You do not have to create dozens of copies. Make a distinct version when doing so protects the original, supports review, or is required by the assignment. Avoid confusing filenames such as `final-final-newest-3`. A single clear title and a purposeful version label are more useful.

### Step 2: Finish the review in a fixed order

1. Confirm the intended audience and purpose.
1. Confirm the approved facts and required instructions.
1. Read the document for clarity and completeness.
1. Run the available spelling and grammar review, one suggestion at a time.
1. Manually read every paragraph, title, list, table, caption, and relevant alternative-text field.
1. Inspect the document's layout and any visible review comments or tracked changes.
1. Make sure no temporary placeholder or practice annotation remains in the copy intended for use.
1. Save the corrected document and verify that the application has completed any required sync or saving step.
1. Reopen the saved copy and check the beginning, middle, end, and any important tables or images.

For a cloud document, a title change or autosave indicator does not prove that collaborators see the same version under all conditions. Check the application's save or sync status, access permissions, and relevant version history when applicable. For a local document, reopen the actual file from the intended folder rather than relying solely on the still-open editing window.

### Step 3: Verify the final version, not the working memory

When you proofread a familiar document, you may remember what you intended to write rather than notice what was saved. Reopening breaks that habit. It gives you another chance to see what the reader will receive. Verify the filename, location, and visible content. If you change anything after reopening, save again.

| Before distribution | Confirm | If incorrect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Document identity | File name, folder, version, and intended audience | Open the correct copy and rename or move as permitted |
| Main message | Purpose, event title, and reader instructions | Edit the unclear or incomplete passage |
| Key facts | Names, dates, times, room, quantities, and destinations | Consult approved source, then correct |
| Language | Spelling, punctuation, terminology, and plain wording | Review and re-read affected sentence |
| Layout | Headings, lists, images, tables, and readable spacing | Repair layout and inspect again |
| Accessibility | Logical headings, table headers, image descriptions | Correct the relevant structure or text |
| Saving | Latest changes appear after reopening | Save or resolve sync status, then reopen |

### A short sign-off rule

You are ready to finish when you can answer **yes** to these questions: Does the document communicate its purpose? Are the key facts verified? Have language suggestions been reviewed rather than automatically accepted? Are headings, lists, tables, and images understandable? Is the saved file the correct version? If a required fact cannot be verified, do not label the document final; record the outstanding question and ask the responsible person.

> **Remember:** Saving is part of proofreading. A corrected page that exists only in an unsaved editing session is not a verified final document.

## 8. Common mistakes and troubleshooting

### “There are no red underlines, so everything is correct.”

Not necessarily. The review feature may be disabled, may use a different indication, may not support the language, or may fail to recognize the particular error. Even a fully functioning spellchecker cannot confirm event details or whether the document is complete. Run a manual review and check key facts against a source.

### “The suggestion is probably right because it came from the computer.”

An automated suggestion is not a verified correction. It may change a name, remove a necessary qualification, or alter the intended tone. Read the proposed replacement in context, check uncertain terminology, and reject suggestions that distort your meaning.

### “Every unfamiliar word should be added to the dictionary.”

A dictionary entry reduces future warnings, including for a word you mistakenly added. Use **Add to Dictionary** only when you have checked the word and understand why it should be accepted repeatedly. For a one-time unfamiliar word, ignoring it or checking a source may be better.

### “My program marks common words wrong.”

The wrong proofing language may be selected, the relevant language dictionary may be missing, or checking may be configured for part of the text only. Inspect the document or selection language. If a program has no suitable dictionary, continue manual proofreading rather than changing the content to satisfy an unsuitable checker.

### “Spellcheck worked for the paragraph but not the table.”

Some tools and editing modes review different parts of a document differently. Manually inspect table cells, text boxes, headers, captions, and image descriptions. Test the review tool's scope if a table appears to be omitted. Do not assume that an application examined every object.

### “I changed one sentence, but the old instruction remains elsewhere.”

A notice may repeat a time or location in the introduction, schedule, footer, or callout. Search for the old wording and check every occurrence. For important numbers, compare all repeated values with the approved source. After any global replacement, inspect each changed location.

### “My document's language has changed after accepting a suggestion.”

Some writing assistants offer rewrites for style or tone rather than corrections of clear errors. Use **Undo** promptly if a suggestion changes the meaning, voice, or required wording. Then make the smallest manual change needed. Keep the audience and approved terminology in mind.

### “I cannot find the spelling command in my application.”

Try the menu-bar or ribbon review area, search the application's Help command, or consult the official support reference near the end of this lesson. Commands vary among desktop, web, and mobile versions. You can still complete the proofreading activities by examining the text directly.

### “I lost the corrected version after saving.”

You may have edited a different copy, saved in an unfamiliar location, or closed before cloud synchronization completed. Check the current filename and file location, the application's recent-files list, and version history if available and authorized. Avoid repeatedly creating new copies until you identify which one contains your changes. Reopen the intended document and verify the actual text.

### “My grammar checker recommends changing dialect or quotation.”

Automated language tools are not authoritative judges of identity, dialect, or cited wording. Preserve accurate quotations and follow the intended document's language convention. When wording is unclear, edit for meaning while respecting the writer's voice. Do not assume the software's default style is the only legitimate one.

### “A grammar tool gives strange results on a sensitive document.”

First determine whether the feature is approved for the information being processed. Optional online assistants may transmit text for processing. Do not paste confidential information into an unfamiliar site, or bypass workplace rules to obtain suggestions. Use an authorized tool or a manual review process instead.

### A quick troubleshooting decision guide

| Symptom | Check first | Recommended next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No mistakes are flagged | Is checking enabled? Is the language appropriate? | Start manual review or open the application review command |
| Nearly every word is flagged | Incorrect language or missing dictionary | Correct proofing language or obtain authorized language support |
| Name repeatedly flagged | Is the spelling verified? | Ignore deliberately or add only after confirming |
| Suggestion changes meaning | Sentence context and intended audience | Reject or Undo; edit manually |
| Table still contains a wrong time | Approved schedule and every relevant cell | Correct the source mismatch, then verify full document |
| Edited version appears old | Filename, folder, save or sync status | Reopen the intended copy and check version history if available |
| A reviewer cannot understand a highlighted item | Is information communicated only by color or image? | Write an explicit text instruction and inspect accessibility |

> **Troubleshooting principle:** Find the actual cause of the problem. Removing a warning is not the same as correcting a document.

## 9. Essential vocabulary

The following terms are useful for everyday document review. Some words appear earlier in the lesson as selectable vocabulary tooltips. You do not need to memorize every technical definition before practicing; focus first on recognizing what each term means when it appears in your word processor.

- **Proofreading:** The deliberate review of a near-final document for remaining errors, inconsistencies, and presentation problems.
- **Editing:** Changing or reorganizing writing to improve its meaning, structure, accuracy, and clarity.
- **Spellcheck:** Software assistance that identifies words that may be misspelled, based on available language resources and software rules.
- **Grammar check:** Software assistance that proposes corrections to possible grammatical, punctuation, or style issues.
- **Suggestion:** A proposed replacement or change that a person should evaluate before accepting.
- **Proofing language:** The language or language variety used by a spelling or grammar checker.
- **Custom dictionary:** An application- or account-associated collection of additional words treated as acceptable.
- **Real-word error:** An incorrect word that is nevertheless spelled correctly, such as using *from* where *form* is intended.
- **Typographical error:** A mistake made in entering or arranging characters, including omitted or repeated letters or words.
- **Word choice:** The decision to use one word or expression instead of another, with attention to meaning and audience.
- **Grammar:** The structures and conventions that organize words into meaningful sentences.
- **Punctuation:** Marks such as commas, periods, apostrophes, and question marks that support meaning and sentence structure.
- **Capitalization:** The use of uppercase and lowercase letters according to the purpose and conventions of the writing.
- **Agreement:** The grammatical relationship among parts of a sentence, such as the match between subject and verb.
- **Sentence boundary:** The point where a sentence ends and another begins.
- **Style suggestion:** A proposed improvement to tone, concision, or other writing choices that may not correct a definite error.
- **Read aloud:** A feature or practice that makes text audible to help review how a sentence sounds.
- **Accuracy:** The degree to which a statement matches verified information or the intended facts.
- **Consistency:** Using the same valid convention or information in comparable places in a document.
- **Source of truth:** An approved, authoritative reference used to verify a particular fact or requirement.
- **Quality check:** A deliberate inspection to confirm that a document meets its requirements before use.
- **Layout:** The arrangement of text, headings, images, tables, and spacing on a page or screen.
- **Caption:** Visible explanatory wording associated with an image or table.
- **Alternative text:** Text that conveys the meaningful content or purpose of a visual object for readers who cannot perceive it normally.
- **Hyperlink:** A selectable reference that opens a destination such as a webpage or another document location.
- **Cloud service:** A computing service that processes or stores data on systems accessed across a network.
- **Filename:** The name assigned to a saved document file, often including a format extension.
- **Save:** A command or function that preserves current document content for later use.
- **Autosave:** A feature that saves changes automatically when supported and enabled.
- **Sync:** The process of updating copies of data across a device and a remote service.
- **Version history:** A feature that may record previous saved document states for review or recovery.
- **Reopen:** Open the saved file again to check what was preserved, rather than relying on the current editing window.
- **Ignore:** A review action that skips a flagged item without necessarily changing the underlying text.
- **Change All:** A review action that replaces all selected matches; its exact scope varies by application.
- **Undo:** A command that reverses a recent edit when the application retains the action in its history.
- **Proofing pane:** A panel or dialog displaying flagged issues and possible corrections.
- **Screen reader:** Assistive technology that communicates accessible text, controls, and document structure.
- **Accessibility:** Design and practices that help people with differing abilities use a document.
- **Heading style:** A structural label identifying a document section, not merely text made larger or bold.
- **Header row:** The row of a table containing labels for its columns.
- **Data table:** A structured set of rows and columns containing related facts or measurements.
- **Text alternative:** A meaningful text description or equivalent information for content that may otherwise be inaccessible.
- **Placeholder:** Temporary wording intended to be replaced with confirmed content before distribution.
- **Redline / tracked change:** A marked record of a proposed or completed edit in a review workflow.
- **Comment:** A review note attached to document content, often used to ask questions or request changes.
- **Audience:** The intended readers or users of the document.
- **Clarity:** The extent to which a reader can readily understand the intended message.
- **Concise writing:** Writing that communicates necessary information without avoidable excess.
- **Manual correction:** A change typed or applied by the writer instead of automatically accepting a proposal.
- **Verification:** Checking information or a saved result against a reliable reference or expected requirement.
- **Privacy:** Protecting confidential or personal information against unauthorized access or disclosure.
- **Document copy:** A separate version of a file created to preserve the original or support review.
- **Proofreading pass:** One planned round of checking for a particular kind of issue, such as facts or punctuation.
- **Time convention:** The chosen way to express hours and minutes, such as `10:00 a.m.`.
- **Zoom:** An on-screen magnification control used to change the viewing size without necessarily altering document formatting.
